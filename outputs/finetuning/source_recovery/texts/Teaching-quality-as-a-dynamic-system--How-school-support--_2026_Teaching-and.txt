Grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Person–Environment Fit Theory (PE Fit), this study uses largescale TALIS data and network analysis to examine how teacher motivation, basic psychological needs, and school
support jointly shape teaching performance. Self-efficacy in using digital tools/resources, distributed leadership,
and instructional leadership emerged as key bridge nodes linking individual and organizational processes, while
Self-efficacy in using digital tools/resources and Teaching self-efficacy functioned as proximal factors closely
connected to teaching-performance dimensions. Comparing three needs–performance configurations—High
Needs–Low Performance, Balanced Needs–Performance, and High Performance–Low Needs—revealed marked
structural differences, with digital self-efficacy consistently central but other salient nodes varying by fit level.
Several associations displayed non-linear turning-point patterns. These findings inform a Multi-Level Teacher Fit
Framework conceptualizing teacher development as a dynamic alignment process across teacher–job, teacher–environment, and job–environment fit.

1. Introduction
Teaching performance is commonly defined as enacted teaching
practice, including cognitive activation, learning support, and classroom
management (Ferguson & Womack, 1993). In this study, teaching performance is the focal construct and is operationalized using TALIS
subscales labeled as “teaching practices.” For clarity, we use teaching
performance consistently throughout the manuscript and use teaching
practices only when referring to the TALIS measurement labels. Prior
research suggests that teaching performance is shaped by resources
operating at multiple levels—individual, interpersonal, job-related, and
organizational (Chen, 2019). At the individual level, factors such as
personal characteristics, job satisfaction, and self-efficacy have been
shown to relate to variations in teaching performance (Kim et al., 2018;
Klassen & Tze, 2014). At the interpersonal level, social support, collegial
support, and trust have also been linked to teaching performance (Ju
et al., 2015; Wentzel et al., 2010). At the job level, working conditions—including job stress, workload, and task demands—have been
identified as important correlates of teaching performance (Abdulaziz
et al., 2022; Toropova et al., 2021). In addition, organizational-level
factors, such as organizational commitment, school climate,

distributed leadership, and organizational justice, have been shown to
shape teaching performance in meaningful ways (Hong & Matsko, 2019;
Ingersoll & Tran, 2023). Despite this growing literature, most empirical
studies have tended to focus on the effects of a single level—most
commonly individual-level factors—on teaching performance (Broder &
Dorfman, 1994; Hascher & Waber, 2021). Moreover, substantial differences across schools in terms of socioeconomic resources, policy environments, and organizational cultures suggest that the mechanisms
through which these factors relate to teaching performance are likely to
vary across contexts (Hargreaves, 1988).
In response, some scholars have proposed integrative approaches
that combine multiple dimensions of teaching performance into
comprehensive models. In particular, a growing line of research has
begun to draw on concepts from Self-Determination Theory (SDT) to
understand the determinants of teaching performance, conceptualizing
them as dynamic, interactive, and multi-level processes (Carson &
Chase, 2009; Chiu et al., 2024; Stupnisky et al., 2018). According to
SDT, teachers’ perceptions of school support—such as distributed
leadership, instructional leadership, and collaborative cultures—are
assumed to influence the satisfaction of their basic psychological needs,
thereby shaping motivation, professional commitment, and

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward
teacher burnout and psychological well-being. Among the many individual factors influencing teaching performance, basic psychological
needs—have been widely recognized as core psychological resources
underlying teacher motivation and professional functioning. When
teachers' basic psychological needs are met, they typically have more
psychological resources to invest in their students, and are therefore
more likely to enact supportive instructional behaviors—particularly
autonomy-supportive and structuring teaching performance (Wang,
Meng, et al., 2024). Nevertheless, these needs have remained relatively
underexamined in research on teaching performance (Collie et al., 2016;
Doménech-Betoret & Gómez-Artiga, 2014). According to Person–Environment (PE) Fit Theory, stress, reduced effectiveness, and negative emotional experiences are likely to emerge when individuals' needs
are misaligned with their abilities or working conditions (Arifani et al.,
2023; Polikoff & Porter, 2014). In teaching contexts, such misalignment
is particularly prevalent. When instructional demands substantially
exceed teachers' actual performance, experiences of frustration and
overload are more likely to occur. Conversely, when teachers' capabilities consistently exceed the challenges they encounter, teaching may
lose its sense of meaning and motivational value, potentially undermining engagement and contributing to burnout (Evers et al., 2017).
From this perspective, teacher professional development is not determined solely by the absolute level of performance or demands, but
rather by the degree of alignment between them. However, much of the
existing literature has focused on the fit between job demands and job
resources (Han et al., 2020; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2018), with far less
attention devoted to the more fundamental alignment between teachers’
psychological needs and their teaching performance—a mechanism that
directly shapes teacher motivation, emotional experiences, and teaching
performance.
Despite extensive research, a critical gap remains in understanding
the link between teachers' psychological needs and teaching performance, which shapes motivation and emotional experiences. Much of
the existing literature has focused on job demands and resources, but far
less attention has been given to the alignment between teachers' psychological needs and their teaching performance, which forms the
foundation for understanding teacher motivation and performance in a
more dynamic, interactive context. While Person–Environment Fit (PE
Fit) Theory highlights the importance of the alignment between individuals’ needs and their abilities or work conditions, this has seldom
been applied to explore how psychological needs align with teaching
performance in real-world teaching settings. Using network analysis, we
compare three needs–performance configurations—High Needs–Low
Performance, Balanced Needs–Performance, and High Performance–Low Needs—within the Shanghai sample of the Teaching and
Learning International Survey (TALIS), including 3976 teachers from
various subjects and grade levels. Guided by these considerations, we

2. Literature review and hypotheses
2.1. Theoretical framework
The theoretical framework is presented in Fig. 1. Its overall structure
is grounded in SDT, with core components including school support,
basic psychological needs, motivation, and teaching performance.
School support refers to the various resources, structures, and interpersonal relationships within the school environment that assist teachers in performing their roles effectively (Lam et al., 2010). According to
SDT, basic psychological needs refer to three fundamental needs that are
essential for fostering motivation and well-being: autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000). Autonomy for teachers refers to having the freedom to make decisions about their instructional
decisions, rather than feeling controlled by external pressures (Niemiec
& Ryan, 2009); competence involves teachers feeling effective and
confident in their teaching, rather than feeling ineffective or incapable
(Marshik et al., 2017); and relatedness refers to teachers feeling connected and supported by their students, colleagues, and the school
community, rather than feeling isolated (Sheldon & Filak, 2008). When
these needs are satisfied, individuals are more likely to experience
positive emotions, engage in intrinsically motivated behaviors, and
report greater well-being (Katz & Moè, 2024; Moè & Katz, 2022).
Furthermore, the needs are defined as evolved and species typical, and
are thus thought to be universally relevant within all people and all
cultures. With respect to motivation, the framework distinguishes between extrinsic motivation and intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci,
2000). Extrinsic motivation refers to teachers teaching for external rewards, such as money, recognition, or career advancement (Deci et al.,
1999). Intrinsic motivation refers to teachers engaging in teaching
because they enjoy the process and find it fulfilling, rather than for
external rewards (Ryan & Deci, 2024). Finally, teaching performance is
conceptualized as the ultimate outcome within the framework, shaped
by multi-level interactions among teachers’ basic psychological needs
and motivational processes.
In this study, SDT and PE Fit theory are conceptualized as complementary frameworks rather than alternative explanations. SDT primarily addresses why teacher motivation emerges by emphasizing the
satisfaction of three basic psychological needs. When these needs are
fulfilled, teachers are more likely to experience autonomous forms of
motivation, which energize engagement and persistence in teaching
activities. However, SDT alone does not fully explain when such motivation can be effectively translated into high levels of teaching performance. Performance is defined as the effectiveness with which teachers
carry out their instructional duties, operationalized by self-reported
teaching performance indicators (Steinberg & Kraft, 2017). For clarity,
we use teaching performance to refer to this construct throughout the
manuscript. PE Fit theory provides this additional explanatory layer by
focusing on the alignment between teachers' psychological needs and
their enacted teaching performance. From a PE Fit perspective, motivation is most likely to function optimally when teachers' needs are
adequately matched with their performance-related demands and opportunities. In contrast, when needs and performance are misaligned—such as when psychological needs are high but performance
capacity is constrained, or when performance demands exceed teachers’
experienced needs—motivation may fail to translate into effective
teaching practice (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005).

organizational culture (Fotheringham et al., 2022).

3. Method
3.1. Sample
This study used publicly available, de-identified data from the OECD
Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2024, which follows OECD technical standards for confidentiality and protection of
personal information. The present study examined teachers from
Shanghai, China, with a final analytical sample of 3976 teachers (Mean
age = 39.50 years). Because demographic characteristics have been
shown to shape teachers’ professional attitudes and turnover intentions,
gender, age, and educational attainment were included as control variables. The sample comprised 972 male teachers (24.44%) and 3004
female teachers (75.56%), a distribution consistent with broader patterns observed in the teaching profession. Participants reported an
average of 16.2 years of teaching experience (SD = 10.70), ranging from
0 to 48 years, reflecting a diverse mix of novice and highly experienced
teachers. Most teachers held a graduate degree (77.60%), while 22.40%
reported non-graduate qualifications.

2.5. Hypotheses
Grounded in SDT and PE Fit, this study examines the network of
teaching performance and related factors and tests whether the network
differs across levels of needs–performance fit. Within SDT framework,
autonomy and motivation are conceptualized as proximal influences on
teaching performance, whereas school support is expected to exert its
influence through more distal pathways. Network analysis offers a
suitable methodological tool for examining these pathways, as it enables
the simultaneous modeling of multiple interrelations rather than relying
on single linear associations. Prior research has shown that autonomy
and self-efficacy are key psychological mechanisms associated with
instructional behavior, coping with teaching challenges, and professional development. From a network perspective, these variables may
not only relate to teaching performance but may also function as central
nodes that integrate information across the system. PE Fit further suggests that psychological mechanisms may exhibit different configurations of coordination and coupling depending on the degree of
needs–performance alignment. Teachers with high perceived fit may
demonstrate more coherent and strongly connected resource networks,
whereas those with lower fit may exhibit fragmented or weakly connected patterns. Accordingly, the three needs–performance configurations are treated as potential moderators of the network structure in the
present study. The hypotheses below articulate expected structural
tendencies rather than directional effects.

4.1. Preliminary analyses
Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the bivariate
associations among school support, basic psychological needs, motivation, and teacher motivation. The results of the Pearson correlation
analysis are summarized in Supplementary materials, Table S1. Overall,
school support showed small to moderate positive correlations with
indicators of basic psychological needs, teacher motivation, and teaching performance. While several associations reached moderate levels (e.
g., r ≈ 0.03–0.34), many correlations were weak or negligible, suggesting that the relationships between school support and these outcomes are not uniformly strong.
To classify teachers according to their needs–performance alignment, we first conducted paired-samples t tests to examine differences
between basic psychological needs and teaching performance. Teachers
showing a statistically meaningful discrepancy were classified as
exhibiting a needs–performance mismatch, whereas those with no
meaningful discrepancy were assigned to the Balanced Needs and Performance Group (BNPG) (N = 778). It is important to note that this
classification logic is theoretically grounded in the Three-Channel Model
of Flow Theory proposed by Csikszentmihalyi and Csikzentmihaly
(1990). Within this model, needs–performance fit is conceptualized as
the relative difference between teachers’ psychological needs and their
performance. We employed a theory-guided continuous difference-score
approach. Both psychological needs satisfaction and teaching performance composite scores were standardized, and for each teacher a difference index was computed. The direction and magnitude of this index
were then used to assign teachers to three theoretically defined
needs–performance configurations: High Needs–Low Performance
Group (HNG; z ≥ +0.5 SD), Balanced Needs–Performance Group (BNPG;
− 0.5 SD < z < +0.5 SD), and High Performance–Low Needs Group
(HPG; z ≤ − 0.5 SD). Person-centered approaches such as latent profile
analysis could also be used to derive empirical groupings; however, our
goal was not to identify data-driven latent classes but to operationalize
the three conceptual channels of flow. It is also noteworthy that the
±0.5 SD thresholds were not intended to capture an optimal or “true”
categorical boundary. Rather, they served to translate the theoretical
structure of the Three-Channel Model into operational groups suitable
for network comparison. Robustness checks using alternative cutoffs
(±0.3 SD and ±0.7 SD) produced highly similar network patterns,
indicating that the main structural findings were robust across reasonable threshold choices.
Descriptive statistics and one-way ANOVAs were conducted for the
three groups (see Supplementary materials, Table S2). The results
showed that all variables differed significantly across groups, with Fvalues ranging from 10.24 to 1081.47 (all ps < 0.01) and corresponding
partial η2 values between 0.005 and 0.347, indicating effect sizes from
small to moderately large. Overall, the HNG group obtained the highest
scores on indicators such as school support, teacher motivation, and
teaching performance, whereas the HPG group showed the highest
levels of basic psychological needs. The BNPG group generally fell between the two, forming a clear gradient pattern. These findings indicate
that the three needs–performance configurations demonstrate meaningful distinctions on key variables, thereby providing a solid statistical
basis for subsequent network comparisons.

3.3. Data analysis
Network analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 4.5.1) to
visualize relations among variables and compare network structures
across needs–performance alignment groups. We estimated and visualized cross-factor networks using ggmModSelect (default estimator) and
the qgraph and bootnet packages; this choice was made because recent
work suggests LASSO regularization may be less accurate in largesample settings. In the networks, nodes represent variables and edges
represent partial correlations controlling for all other variables; purple
edges indicate positive relations and red edges indicate negative relations, with thicker edges indicating stronger absolute associations.
We assessed node importance using centrality indices—strength,
closeness, and betweenness—and evaluated accuracy and stability via
nonparametric bootstrapping (confidence intervals for edge weights and
centrality) and case-dropping bootstraps to compute the centrality stability coefficient (CS). Following common guidelines, CS ≥ 0.25 indicates acceptable stability, >0.50 good stability, and ≥0.70 excellent
stability. These procedures provide a basis for robust comparisons of the
“school support–basic psychological needs–teacher motivation–teaching performance” network across needs–performance alignment groups.

4.2.2. Centrality estimation
We computed bridge strength to identify nodes that were most
strongly connected to nodes in other communities (Fig. 2, right). The
80th percentile of bridge strength (i.e., the top three nodes) was used as
the cutoff for selecting the strongest bridge nodes. In this study, the node
with the highest bridge strength was Self-efficacy in using digital tools/
resources (C2), followed by Distributed leadership (S1) and Instructional leadership (S2). This pattern suggests that, even without direct
links to the teaching performance, C2, S1, and S2 showed the strongest
cross-community connections and thus functioned as structural hubs in
the network. Consequently, these findings do not support Hypothesis 2,
which predicted that teaching autonomy would be the central nodes in
the network.
4.2.3. Network accuracy and stability
The accuracy of the estimated edge weights was assessed using 95%
bootstrapped confidence intervals (CIs). The bootstrap results showed
relatively narrow 95% CIs, indicating high accuracy of the network estimates. The stability of the centrality indices was evaluated using the
centrality stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) obtained by repeatedly
estimating the network on subsets of the data, beginning with 100% of
the sample and progressively reducing the proportion of cases. A CScoefficient of at least 0.25 is considered sufficient for meaningful
interpretation, whereas values above 0.50 are preferred. In the present

5. Discussion
5.1. Core characteristics in the relationships between teachers’ teaching
performance and its associated factors
Many prior studies treat teaching performance as a single construct
(Spooren et al., 2007; Vo et al., 2025). Our network results challenge this
view by showing clear differentiation among its subdimensions. In
particular, Collaborative practices/activities (T1)—an organizationally
embedded component—was positioned at a greater distance from the
more individual-level dimensions of teaching performance (T2–T4).
This pattern supports work suggesting that teaching performance spans
both individual and organizational elements (Ronfeldt et al., 2015;
Vangrieken et al., 2017; Vembye et al., 2023) and aligns with the idea of
a continuum from individual to organizational dimensions (Churchward
& Willis, 2019; Wiswall, 2013). Within this differentiated structure, we
further identified distinct proximal correlates for the four
teaching-performance dimensions. Teaching self-efficacy (M1) emerged
as the closest correlate of collaborative practices; self-efficacy in using
digital tools/resources (C2) primarily anchored core teaching practices
and technology use; and teaching autonomy (A2) functioned as the key
proximal correlate for assessment and feedback practices. This differentiated pattern can be meaningfully interpreted within the SDT
framework. These variables were positioned in the immediate neighborhood of the teaching-performance nodes, indicating that they are
more directly embedded in the local functional architecture of teaching
behaviors. SDT posits that teachers' practices arise from the situated
coordination of multiple psychological resources, with different teaching tasks activating different motivational and efficacy-related processes
(Darling-Hammond, 2021). Accordingly, collaborative work appears to
rely more on general teaching self-efficacy, technology-intensive instruction aligns more with digital self-efficacy, and assessment/feedback
aligns more with autonomy. Notably, both M1 and C2 are
efficacy-related, and prior evidence links teacher self-efficacy to teaching performance (Duan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026). Our study extends this literature by showing that these links remain after accounting
for the broader network, suggesting that efficacy holds a structurally
influential position across multiple teaching processes. Teaching Autonomy also functioned as a proximal correlate (Jerrim et al., 2023; Lin
& Gao, 2023), consistent with TALIS's evolving view of autonomy as
participation in decision-making rather than simple independence
(Narayanan et al., 2024; Vangrieken et al., 2017). In our network,
teaching autonomy demonstrated robust connections with other psychological and contextual variables, reinforcing its structural importance. Even within a network containing more distal organizational
factors, autonomy remained relatively stable and consistently influential, echoing previous findings that autonomy represents a durable
motivational resource for teachers (Jerrim et al., 2023; Reeve & Cheon,
2016).
Through the application of centrality estimation in network analysis,
the present study further illuminated the structural roles of self-efficacy
in using digital tools/resources (C2), distributed leadership (S1), and
instructional leadership (S2) as bridge nodes within the teachingperformance network. These positions should not be read as direct
intervention priorities; rather, they indicate hub-like roles that connect
otherwise weakly linked parts of the system and may function as
context-sensitive structural pivots. Prior research has reported relatively
weak associations between teacher self-efficacy and teaching performance (Fauth et al., 2019; Lazarides et al., 2021), possibly due to differences in measurement level and contextual specificity (Burić & Kim,
2020; Daumiller et al., 2021; Holzberger et al., 2013). Whereas generalized measures of professional self-efficacy capture broad assessments
of competence, the TALIS indicator for digital-tool self-efficacy provides
a more task-specific assessment that aligns more closely with the challenges teachers encounter in concrete teaching practices. This task
specificity may help explain why C2 emerged as a structurally influential

4.3.3. Network comparison
The networks corresponding to the three needs–performance configurations exhibited both similarities and differences (see Fig. 3).
Permutation-based network comparison tests were conducted to
examine group differences. The network structure invariance tests
indicated significant structural differences among all three networks (all
ps < 0.01), suggesting that the specific patterns linking school support,
basic psychological needs, teacher motivation, and teaching performance differed systematically across levels of needs–performance
alignment. This finding supports Hypothesis 3b. Correlations among the
edge-weight vectors showed moderate structural similarity between the
HNG and BNPG networks (r = 0.57) and between the HNG and HPG
networks (r = 0.55), whereas the similarity between the BNPG and HPG
networks was very high (r = 0.92). Differences in global strength were
non-significant between the HNG and BNPG networks (ΔS = 0.40, p =
.72), but significant between the HNG and HPG networks (ΔS = 5.43, p
< .01) and between the BNPG and HPG networks (ΔS = 5.03, p < .01).
These results suggest that the overall connectivity of the network is
higher in the group with lower need satisfaction, providing no support
for Hypothesis 3c. The summed absolute edge weights (global strength)
were 6.14 (HNG), 6.80 (BNPG), and 7.00 (HPG), indicating a slight increase in overall connectivity as need decreases below the level of
teaching performance, although the magnitude of these differences was
generally small. Edge-specific comparisons revealed 27 significant differences between the HNG and BNPG networks, 19 differences between
the HNG and HPG networks, and 16 differences between the BNPG and
HPG networks, providing partial support for Hypothesis 3d. Detailed
edge-level results are presented in Supplementary materials, Table S7.
Systematic shifts in the direction and magnitude of associations with
teaching performance were observed across the three needs–performance configurations. Turning-point patterns were the most prevalent (47
edges), indicating that when needs and performance were mismatched
(i.e., in the HNG and BNPG groups), the associations between relevant
factors and teaching performance tended to exhibit similar directional
patterns before reversing in the HPG group. A second group of edges
(24) exhibited no discernible trend, suggesting that their relationships
with teaching performance remained relatively stable regardless of
needs–performance alignment. In contrast, increasing trends (19 edges)
and decreasing trends (15 edges) reflected monotonic changes across
configurations. Specifically, increasing trends indicated that associations became more positive as need levels decreased and performance
levels increased, whereas decreasing trends indicated that associations
weakened—or, in some cases, shifted toward negative—as needs
increased and performance decreased. Taken together, the prominence
of turning-point patterns underscores the extent to which
needs–performance fit shapes the functional relationships among predictors of teaching performance.

argument advanced by van Ameijde et al. (2009), who suggested that

for examining how school support, psychological needs, and motivation
relate to teaching performance; while context may shape effect sizes, the
mechanistic patterns and the proposed framework are intended to be
applicable and testable across countries. Overall, these findings highlight the context-dependent structural roles leadership practices may
play within the broader teaching-performance system.
Beyond the core proximal factors, several noteworthy and largely
unanticipated patterns also emerged. First, school-support variables
were positioned at greater structural distances within the network,
suggesting that their influence on teaching performance may depend
more heavily on teachers' subjective perceptions and autonomy in utilizing support, rather than on the mere availability of external resources.
This aligns with prior arguments that institutional supports must be
psychologically translated into teacher-perceived resources in order to
exert meaningful effects (Biswas et al., 2022; Harris & Sass, 2011).
Surprisingly, Career Motivations (M2)—often linked to teaching performance in prior studies—did not appear as a proximal correlate in our
network, contrasting with the view that career motivation directly
drives instructional behavior (Bardach & Klassen, 2021). One plausible
explanation is that in highly institutionalized educational systems such
as China's, external accountability structures and evaluation mechanisms may attenuate the direct behavioral expression of career motivations (Wang, Xing, et al., 2024b). As a result, their influence may be
subsumed by more task-specific psychological resources—such as
self-efficacy or autonomy—within the network (Kuhn et al., 2022).
Equally interesting is that Student–teacher relations (R1) and Trust in
school (R2)—the two indicators of relatedness—showed relatively weak
associations with the teaching-performance nodes when compared with
M1, A2, and C2. This suggests that teachers' perceived relatedness is less
directly embedded in the immediate instructional processes. Rather than
contradicting SDT, this configuration extends variable-centered findings
by illustrating that the structural visibility of psychological needs may
differ across contexts and analytic approaches (Bouten et al., 2025; León
& Liew, 2017). It is also possible that the TALIS indicators capture
relational climate and institutional trust more than SDT-relatedness as
subjective need satisfaction, which may partly attenuate these links
(Harvey et al., 2022). Moreover, because relatedness was measured
entirely through teacher self-reports, its reliability may be influenced by
role expectations or contextual pressures, which could attenuate its
correspondence with actual teaching practices (McGrath & Van Bergen,
2015).

This suggests that teachers’ needs–performance states do not alter the
overall activation level of the teaching-performance system but instead
reshape how resources are interconnected within the system (Jaggy
et al., 2025).
Across all three networks, Self-efficacy using digital tools/resources
(C2) and Teaching self-efficacy (M1) consistently functioned as proximal
factors, regardless of teachers' needs–performance alignment. This
pattern implies that, irrespective of fit, self-efficacy serves as a “foundational psychological resource” that enables the mobilization of other
instructional supports (Overbaugh & Lu, 2008; Ross et al., 2025).
Self-efficacy in teaching, especially when mediated by digital tools and
resources, appears to be a critical component for teachers' effective
teaching practices across different needs–performance configurations.
In contrast, Distributed leadership (S1) and Instructional leadership (S2)
demonstrated more context-sensitive roles. Although leadership variables exhibited bridging functions in the overall network, their positions
shifted toward the periphery in the high-needs, low-performance (HNG)
group, indicating weaker connections to specific teaching practices
(Bellibaş et al., 2021; Hosseingholizadeh et al., 2023). Within the PE fit
framework, this pattern can be interpreted as follows: When teachers'
demands exceed their performance, leadership practices may be
perceived as additional burdens or external directives, making them less
likely to be internalized as meaningful instructional resources. Only
when needs are less than or equal to performance—such as in the BNPG
or HPG groups—do these leadership practices become more easily integrated into teachers’ day-to-day instructional systems and function as
supportive resources (Sinakou et al., 2022).
Comparisons across the three needs–performance configurations
revealed that, although the overall level of network connectivity
remained relatively stable, several key nodes and connection patterns
shifted systematically with changes in fit. In the high-needs group
(HNG), teaching self-efficacy (M1) played a more central role in its
connections to teaching performance dimensions, whereas in groups
with more balanced or higher performance levels (BNPG, HPG),
Distributed leadership (S1) and Instructional leadership (S2) became
more prominently connected. These findings suggest that a network's
“core nodes” do not necessarily align with a particular group's “sensitive
nodes,” highlighting the configuration-dependent nature of intervention
targets and challenging one-size-fits-all interpretations of centrality (Lu
et al., 2025). Edge-level comparisons further demonstrated nonlinear
patterns, with several associations reversing in direction or weakening
as needs–performance alignment changed. Such “turning-point reorganizations” cannot be detected through traditional variable-centered
analyses and are only visible through a configurational approach
(Ebersold et al., 2019). For example, the association between Career
motivations (M2) and Professional satisfaction (M3) was more coherent
in groups with better needs–performance alignment, but weakened or
shifted direction under extreme high-needs or high-performance conditions. This pattern suggests that the coupling between motivational
and emotional resources is not linear but varies according to contextual
factors such as perceived task demands, available resources, and
teachers' regulatory strategies (Tafesse, 2022). A similar reorganization
emerged between Teaching practices–general/core (T2) and Career
motivations (M2), where both the magnitude and sign of the association
shifted across configurations. Together, these findings point to a central
mechanism: the coupling between motivational and emotional resources
is not structurally stable but is highly sensitive to teachers' momentary
fit between demands and performance (Sato & Cárcamo, 2024). Since
“performance” was operationalized using self-reported teaching practices, the grouping captures needs–performance alignment rather than
strict needs–skills fit. Given potential self-report bias, these configurations should be interpreted as exploratory alignment profiles, not
objective performance rankings. Theoretically, both SDT and PE fit
frameworks emphasize the contextual functionality of resources, proposing that the same resource may operate differently under varying
needs–performance combinations. Our results support this perspective,

5.2. Differences in the relationships among teaching performance and its
associated factors under three ‘need-performance’ configurations
To our knowledge, this is the first study to employ a PE fit–informed
network approach with a large teacher sample to compare structural
differences in teaching performance networks across distinct
needs–performance configurations. Prior research has rarely differentiated teachers by their level of needs–performance alignment when
examining instructional processes (Klassen et al., 2012; Nava et al.,
2018), instead treating needs, motivation, and teaching behaviors as
largely independent constructs. The network comparisons revealed no
significant differences in global strength across the three groups, yet
several edges differed systematically in magnitude and configuration.

Taken together, these findings suggest that teaching performance is
not shaped by linear effects of motivation or ability, but rather by the
structural organization of multiple psychological resources under
different levels of needs–performance alignment. The nonlinear patterns
observed in this study highlight meaningful variation in how teachers
function across levels of need satisfaction—variation that would be
difficult to detect using traditional linear approaches (Gilbert et al.,
2021; Zhang et al., 2026). Importantly, the turning-point and reversal
patterns identified here refer to within-network changes in associations
across configurations, rather than developmental shifts or changes in
causal direction at the individual level. These shifts in sign or magnitude
reflect reconfigurations in how variables are embedded within the
broader system under different alignment conditions, and should not be
interpreted as true reversals in the causal influence of any single factor
on teaching performance. We also acknowledge that the emergence of a
high-needs group may be partially shaped by methodological factors,
such as self-selection or social desirability biases (Currie, 2021; ElSayary, 2023; Lauermann & ten Hagen, 2021). In the Shanghai context,
such effects may be further amplified by a high-stakes, performance-oriented school culture and strong normative expectations for
teachers to demonstrate commitment, care, and professional competence (Yu & Luo, 2025). Under these conditions, some teachers may
overreport need satisfaction or relational climates in ways consistent
with role ideals, which could contribute to the observed high-needs
pattern. The specific estimates may be context-bound, but the analytic
logic—examining how resource systems reorganize across

tripartite, multi-level view of PE fit by distinguishing Teacher–Job,
Teacher–Environment, and Job–Environment Fit, and—within the
TALIS context—operationalizes Job–Environment Fit as school-level
organizational and leadership supports that structurally enable core
teaching and instructional improvement. It further adds a developmental lens by situating these fits across interconnected career stages
(entry, adaptation/consolidation, retention/exit), treating fit as an
ongoing process of adjustment rather than a static attribute. Finally,
informed by configuration-sensitive network patterns, it argues that
teacher development reflects the dynamic coordination of multiple fits
under different needs–performance profiles, thereby motivating testable
questions and more targeted approaches to professional development.
6. Implications
Effective professional development could focus on a small set of
structurally proximal resources that can directly activate teaching performance, rather than attempting to intervene on all factors simultaneously. This study highlighted three stable proximal resources in the
teaching performance network—Teaching self-efficacy (M1), Teaching
autonomy (A2), and Self-efficacy in using digital tools/resources (C2).
Because they are structurally close to teaching-performance dimensions,
teacher education and school school-based professional development
may prioritize them. M1 can be strengthened through modeling, feedback, peer learning, and professional learning communities that build
mastery in authentic contexts. Expanding A2 in instruction, management, and assessment may increase agency and engagement. The
prominence of C2 supports providing sustained, context-embedded
digital training for daily instructional use. C2, Distributed leadership
(S1), and Instructional leadership (S2) also served as cross-dimensional
bridging factors, suggesting that improving digital efficacy and participatory, instruction-focused leadership may create broader, system-wide
connections between personal and organizational resources, even
though causal claims cannot be made.
Based on the configuration-sensitive network results, instructional
support should be framed as alignment-sensitive resource activation,
rather than a uniform “high vs. low performance” approach. Across all
three networks, teaching self-efficacy (M1) and digital self-efficacy (C2)
consistently appeared as proximal resources, suggesting that practicebased professional development should prioritize building teachers' efficacy through modeling, feedback, guided practice, and sustained,
classroom-embedded support for using digital tools. In contrast, leadership resources (S1/S2) were configuration-sensitive: in the HNG
group, leadership was more peripheral and less connected to teaching
practices, implying that teachers under low fit may experience leadership inputs as pressure rather than support; therefore, supports for HNG
should focus on direct capacity-building (coaching, scaffolding,
performance-building routines) rather than leadership-driven initiatives. When needs and performance were more aligned (BNPG/HPG),

7. Limitations and future directions

The authors declare that they have no known competing financial
interests or personal relationships that could have appeared to influence

Several limitations should be noted. First, networks were estimated
from cross-sectional data, so temporal ordering and causality cannot be
inferred; the “reconfigurations” reflect differences across teacher groups
(between-person variation), not within-teacher developmental change.
Longitudinal or experience-sampling studies are needed to test network
stability and configuration replicability over time. Second, some TALIS
measures use different time frames, which may introduce noise; future
work should better align measurement windows or use synchronized
multi-wave designs. A limitation is that the estimates are based on
Shanghai teachers and may not generalize to other educational contexts.
Future research should test the replicability of the identified profiles and
network patterns in other regions/countries, ideally using multiinformant or observational indicators and formal checks of measurement invariance and network replicability. Third, teaching performance
is context-dependent, and future studies should include broader
contextual indicators (e.g., school resources, policy environments, professional learning cultures). Adding stable teacher characteristics (e.g.,
personality, instructional styles) and using multi-informant data or SEM
may reduce common-method bias and improve explanation. This indicator was assessed via self-report and may be subject to role expectations
