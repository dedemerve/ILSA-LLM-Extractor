A B S T R A C T  
Digital literacy (DL) is an important capacity for students’ learning in a rapidly changing world. 
However, tension exists between the theoretical conceptualizations of DL as a multidimensional 
construct and empirical studies reporting unidimensional DL scores. Also, little is known about 
how DL may vary among different age cohorts, and whether and at which age do performance 
gaps emerge with respect to gender. The focus of this research is to develop a test appropriate for 
measuring DL performance at different ages and a comprehensive DL assessment framework has 
been adopted for this purpose. Using data from three age cohorts of students (one from primary 
schools and two from secondary schools), the dimensionality of DL and performance differences 
are examined. Comparisons of unidimensional and multidimensional item response models sug­
gest the measured DL to be a unidimensional construct. The results also show that secondary 
school students obtained higher levels of DL compared to primary school students. A gender gap 
in DL is found among secondary school students. There is also a need for further research to 
understand through longitudinal studies the emergence of the gender gap in DL performance.

2. Background 
In this section, we review the research literature that underpinned our study: theoretical and empirical studies related to the 
definition and dimensionality of DL, DL performance of children in different age groups, and methodologies in assessing DL. 
2.1. Dimensionality of DL 
2.1.1. Definition of DL 
There are many labels for the knowledge, skills and attitudes required for adequate functioning in a digital society, including digital 
literacy, ICT literacy, media literacy, new literacy, digital competence, etc. There is a lot of overlap among the many definitions in the 
literature, and the exact definitions for these terms are highly contentious (Ferrari, Breˇcko, & Punie, 2014). In recent years, the term 
digital competence is increasingly used to refer to a similar set of skills, due to the launch of the Digital Competence Framework 
(DigComp) series of documents by the European Commission. Noting that DL is often used as a synonym for digital competence, Ferrari 
et al. (2014) documented the differentiation between DL and digital competence as deemed appropriate by the Director General 
Education and Culture of the European Commission. Accordingly, DL refers to “the skills required to achieve digital competence. It is 
underpinned by basic skills in ICT and the use of computers to retrieve, assess, store, produce, present and exchange information, and 
to communicate and participate in collaborative networks via the Internet” (p. 8f). On the other hand, as one of the eight key com­
petences needed for lifelong learning, digital competence goes beyond the DL skills to include an important focus on attitudes and 
mindset. 
In this paper, we focus on the assessment of learners’ knowledge and skills in digital functioning. Hence, we use the term DL (or DL 
competence), and adopt the definition used by Law, Woo, de la Torre, and Wong (2018, p. 6): 
"Digital literacy is the ability to access, manage, understand, integrate, communicate, evaluate and create information safely 
and appropriately through digital technologies for employment, decent jobs and entrepreneurship. It includes competences that 
are variously referred to as computer literacy, ICT literacy, information literacy and media literacy." 
2.1.2. Dimensionality in DL frameworks 
DL is usually conceptualized as a multidimensional construct in both assessment and curriculum frameworks. The International 
Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) arguably provides the most well-known framework for the assessment of DL in 
adolescents. It defines DL as “an individual’s ability to use computers to investigate, create and communicate in order to participate 
effectively at home, at school, in the workplace, and in the community” (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Duckworth, & Friedman, 2019a, p. 
16). The DL framework adopted in the ICILS 2013 study comprised seven aspects placed within two strands: “collecting and managing 
information” and “producing and exchanging information”. In ICILS 2018, the framework was modified to include eight aspects 
organized into four strands, with two aspects in each strand. The four strands are: understanding computer use (aspects: foundations of 
computer use and computer use conventions), gathering information (aspects: accessing, evaluating and managing information), 
producing information (aspects: transforming and creating information), and digital communication (aspects: sharing information and 
using information responsibly and safely) (Fraillon et al., 2019a). 
The European Commission has put sustained efforts into developing its comprehensive Digital Competence Framework (DigComp) 
to guide the development of educational programs in Europe. The DigComp describes the knowledge, skills and attitudes underpinning 
digital competence in five competence areas (Ferrari et al., 2014) and can thus also be interpreted as a comprehensive DL framework. 
The latest version at the time of writing is DigComp 2.1, which allows us to distinguish five areas of DL: information and data literacy, 
communication and collaboration, digital content creation, digital safety, and problem solving (Carretero, Vuorikari, & Punie, 2017). 
These five areas are further broken down into 21 finer-grained competences, making DigComp a more detailed and comprehensive 
framework (see Table 1). Law et al. (2018) further showed that the DigComp framework is broadly applicable across geographical 
regions, although the DL competences required for competent participation in today’s networked world may differ, conditional on the 
usage context as well as the technological infrastructure and devices available, which are often dependent on the socioeconomic

Other assessment frameworks tend to have narrower or similar coverage as those in ICILS and DigComp. For example, the

collaborating and producing.” (NCSL, 2017) In Singapore, the Digital Media and Information Literacy Framework emphasizes “a 
fundamental appreciation of the benefits, risks and possibilities that technology can bring and how online platforms and digital 
technologies work”, “a basic understanding of how to use information responsibly”, and “the know-how for safe and responsible use of 
digital technologies.” (Government of Singapore, 2019, p. 3) Hong Kong’s Education Bureau stresses the responsible use of infor­
mation, the identification of information needs, the access of relevant information, their evaluation, organization and processing, the 
production of digital content, the recognition of the roles of information providers, as well as of how reliable information can be 
obtained (Education Bureau, 2018, p. 2). A somewhat narrower definition of DL was adopted by the Assessment and Teaching of 21st 
Century Skills (ATC21S) project, which situates DL in the context of collaboration in digital environments, focusing on students’ 
consumer and producer skills, and their skills in developing social and intellectual capital in networks (Wilson, Gochyyev, & Scalise, 
2017). These few examples suggest the existence of a multitude of national and international frameworks for DL, all of which comprise 
multiple dimensions or competences. 
2.1.3. Dimensionality in empirical studies 
While theoretical conceptualizations of DL in curriculum or assessment frameworks are multidimensional with distinct compo­
nential competences, published empirical studies generally report significant overlaps across dimensions, and empirical DL assessment 
studies have mostly reported DL as a unidimensional construct. For example, there were two strands in the ICILS 2013 assessment 
framework, yet multidimensional models showed latent correlations close to 1 between both dimensions such that only one overall DL 
score was reported (Gebhardt & Schulz, 2015). The ICILS assessment framework was modified to four strands, and computer and 
information literacy was again found to be best described using a single scale (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Duckworth, 2020). 
Although the conceptual framework of PIAAC PS-TRE captures several key dimensions, only one overall score has been reported 
(Organisation for economic Co-operation and development OECD, 2016). Aesaert, van Nijlen, Vandelrinde, and van Braak (2014) in 
their study found that a unidimensional model fit the collected DL data better than a model with multiple factors; many cross-loadings 
occurred when a two-dimensional model was estimated. Claro et al. (2012) aimed to measure three dimensions but they found that 
information and communication performance did not split into two dimensions; self-reported awareness of ethical issues appeared as a 
separate factor but was not based on students’ performance. 
In contrast, the “Learning in Digital Networks” assessment designed to measure four DL dimensions indeed found that a multi­
dimensional model provided a better fit to the data than a unidimensional model (Siddiq et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2017). However,

2.2. Individual DL development 
2.2.1. Progression of DL 
Little knowledge exists currently about the development of DL over time and across different ages. Most existing studies have 
focused on college or secondary school students (e.g., Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Duckworth, 2019b; Katz, 2007); there is 
fewer research on DL among primary school students (Aesaert et al., 2014; Siddiq et al., 2016). Comparisons of the performance levels 
among primary and secondary school students (e.g., Kim et al., 2019) are particularly rare. Yet, what students can do with digital 
technologies may differ based on their age and/or experience. For example, it is plausible that some DL competences such as un­
derstanding computer use might be prerequisites for other DL competences, such as data literacy, which could then be the foundation 
for the development of even other DL competences. Moreover, older students may have more experience in using digital technologies, 
and their DL performance should reflect that. Other factors such as students’ cognitive and socioemotional competences, the ability to 
take the perspectives of others and to make moral judgments, also develop as students get older and go through puberty (Bjorklund & 
Causey, 2018; van der Graaff et al., 2014). Growth of these competences may influence DL performance, which develops both in formal 
and informal learning contexts (Meyers, Erickson, & Small, 2013). Home contexts may also be more influential among younger 
students who may have had less exposure to structured school education (Siddiq & Scherer, 2019). 
Previous research indeed suggests that older cohorts of students tend to outperform younger students (Australian Curriculum, 
Assessment and Reporting Authority [ACARA], 2018; Law, Yuen, Shum, & Lee, 2007; Kim et al., 2019). However, several studies also 
found significant overlaps among students of different grade levels (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Law et al., 2007). Moreover, some studies 
reported no significant difference or even lower average DL performance of an older age cohort when compared to a younger cohort of 
students (e.g., Lazonder, Walraven, Gijlers, & Janssen, 2020; Spisak, 2018). These cohort differences do not imply that students’ DL 
would not improve as they get older. In fact, Lazonder et al. (2020) found that the average DL performance for each of two cohorts of 
elementary school students in the Netherlands improved annually over the three-year period of the study. However, the older cohort of 
students (sixth grade) did not perform better than the younger cohort of students (fifth grade) when both cohorts were assessed at the 
first time of measurement, and some individual students did not improve over the three years. A possible reason could be that students 
are exposed to digital technologies at ever younger ages, such that cohort differences in DL performance may not always occur despite 
age-related growth in DL. Other factors, such as cognitive abilities and social contexts, may also play a role. It was one important aim of 
the current study to examine whether DL performance differences exist between cohorts of primary and secondary school students and 
the extent to which the students’ performance scores overlap across different age cohorts. 
2.2.2. Student background and DL performance 
The progression of DL cannot be fully understood without an inspection of students’ gender. For example, boys appear to be more 
confident about their DL than girls based on self-reports (e.g., Gebhardt, Thomson, Ainley, & Hillman, 2019; Lau & Yuen, 2015), but 
performance-based assessments draw a different picture. Research using data from the 2018 ICILS showed that female secondary 
students performed better than male students (Fraillon et al., 2019b), and an in-depth analysis of data from 14 countries that 
participated in the 2013 study concluded: “Female students performed relatively better on tasks that involved communication, design, 
and creativity, and male students generally performed relatively better on more technical tasks.” (Gebhardt et al., 2019, p. 69) 
Moreover, Siddiq and Scherer (2019) in a meta-analysis found that across studies girls on average performed better than boys, though 
the effects were small overall. In addition, their study revealed that the gender differences appeared to be larger in primary schools and 
less pronounced in secondary schools. However, only six of the 46 correlations that these researchers examined stemmed from studies 
with primary school students; even fewer studies had included samples from both primary and secondary students. Hence, the evi­
dence base on gender differences in students’ DL performance across age cohorts remains scarce and fragmented. 
In summary, these findings suggest that female students perform somewhat better in terms of DL. However, a dearth of research 
with samples from primary school students prevents robust conclusions about the role of gender at different ages. Studies comparing 
primary and secondary school students’ performance levels and differences by gender are especially rare. Yet, it is important to un­
derstand whether cohort-specific gaps in DL performance, if any, are due to gender in order to prevent these gaps from widening or 
even to emerge. This study consequently aimed to examine possible performance differences due to students’ gender, and whether 
these gaps were moderated by age cohort. 
2.3. Measurement of DL 
2.3.1. Assessment format 
Decisions about assessment format and item types are critical when assessing DL. Many researchers have used students’ self-

reported efficacy as a proxy for DL, often measured using a Likert scale (e.g., Lau & Yuen, 2014). However, judgments of one’s own DL 
are influenced by self-perceptions and may not always reflect a person’s actual level of DL (Aesaert et al., 2014; Rohatgi, Scherer, & 
Hatlevik, 2016). For example, recent studies of high school students in Israel (Porat, Blau, & Barak, 2018) and the U.S. (Spisak, 2018) 
reported that students overestimated their own DL competences. Thus, conclusions drawn from self-reported DL can have severe 
shortcomings, such as the reinforcement of gender stereotypes, and the use of educational measures that may perform differently for 
students from disadvantaged backgrounds (Aesaert et al., 2014). 
Other studies have overcome the limitations of self-reports by employing performance-based assessments that require students to 
demonstrate their DL. Performance-based DL assessments are likely to provide more accurate reflections of students’ DL competence as 
they do not rely on self-perceptions. These assessments may use constrained response formats (such as multiple-choice items; e.g., 
Hatlevik, Scherer, & Christophersen, 2017), tasks in purpose-built digital environments usually mimicking existing software appli­
cations at a lower level of complexity (e.g., Fraillon et al., 2019b), or fully authentic assessment tasks in real software environments 
that adopt real-life scenarios (e.g., Eshet-Alkalai & Chajut, 2009). Restricted response formats can be less suitable for capturing DL 
performance in authentic contexts as students cannot adopt problem-solving strategies available in real-life situations, such as 
exploring a software environment for clues to find the solution. Partial knowledge, unfamiliarity with the user interface for a particular 
application used in such items, or difficulties with text comprehension can affect students’ DL score. 
Assessments that require students to complete tasks in purpose-built or real-world digital environments may be more authentic 
than constrained response formats (Wirth, 2008). Rather than answering questions that require the reproduction of knowledge, test 
takers have to complete open-ended tasks that are more likely to reflect real-life situations and demonstrate deep understanding 
(Stecher, 2010). Participants in these assessments may need to demonstrate problem-solving skills and they usually have to show that 
they can adapt to situations they might encounter in digital environments, such as planning a trip to an unknown destination using the 
Internet or creating visualizations using a multimedia software application (Eshet-Alkalai & Chajut, 2009; Law et al., 2007). 
Yet, software applications differ in interface design and the same DL may be accomplished in very different ways depending on the 
nature and sophistication of the devices and software applications used (Law et al., 2018). Although assessments that closely reflect 
real-life situations may be particularly appealing, the use of specific software packages can produce bias. Reliance on a particular 
software package can favor students who have more experience with the same software application (Fraillon, 2018). In addition, 
assessments that adopt simulated or authentic software applications tend to include few relatively large assessment tasks (e.g., Law 
et al., 2007; Lazonder et al., 2020). However, within a reasonable amount of time students can only complete a very limited number of 
such tasks, which is problematic insofar as familiarity with the task context can have a much bigger effect on the performance in an 
assessment than it would have if there were multiple small tasks to complete. Thus, developing an age-appropriate assessment using 
authentic software applications can be more challenging as different tasks may be required, but only the inclusion of at least some 
identical measures across those instruments enables researchers to put students on the same scale that allows valid performance 
comparisons. 
2.3.2. Age-appropriateness 
Developing age-appropriate instruments that can produce comparable DL scores remains a challenge in the assessment of DL across 
age cohorts. Existing assessment frameworks do not address the development of DL over time, and many previous assessments of DL 
are limited to identifying and evaluating information, as well as technical skills (Siddiq et al., 2016). Moreover, only few researchers 
have developed DL assessments to compare DL performance levels across different age cohorts (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Lazonder et al., 
2020). Assessments suitable for primary and secondary school ages are particularly rare (e.g., ACARA, 2018). 
The possible influences on the progression of DL outlined earlier also pose challenges for the development of age-appropriate 
assessments. If some DL competences are prerequisites for other DL competences, then it may be impossible to measure advanced 
DL competences at very young ages. By the same token, very basic DL competences may not differentiate performance among older 
individuals as almost everyone should have developed them by a certain age. Furthermore, younger students may have less accu­
mulated experience with digital technologies. The same task could be much harder to complete when students are younger (e.g., due to 
differences in cognitive abilities or fewer experiences with digital devices), such that assessment items need to be age-appropriate and 
potentially different for students at different ages. It is therefore no surprise that there is a lack of assessment instruments that allow 
comparisons of the DL performance at primary and secondary school levels in the extant research. The present study had to address this 
research gap by developing a reliable assessment instrument based on a comprehensive DL assessment framework that is appropriate 
for assessing DL at primary and secondary school levels in order to be able to compare DL performance across age cohorts and to 
examine the effects of gender. 
2.4. Summary 
This paper reports on the development of a DL performance assessment suitable for measuring DL for different age cohorts based on 
a comprehensive assessment framework. The study was designed to answer four research questions (RQs). RQ1: Do the empirical data 
support the theoretical conceptualization of DL as a multidimensional construct? RQ2: Does the instrument produce reliable and 
unbiased DL performance scores that can be compared across primary and secondary school age cohorts? RQ3: (a) Are there DL 
performance differences between primary and secondary school students, and (b) whether and how do the performance scores of 
students in different age cohorts overlap? RQ4: (a) Are there performance differences due to students’ gender, and (b) do these gaps 
differ for students at different ages? The following section describes how the assessment was developed, the samples used, and the 
analysis methods adopted.

4. Method 
This research adopted a cross-cohort panel design to examine performance differences among students in three different age co­
horts, including one cohort of primary school students (Primary 3) and two cohorts of secondary students (Secondary 1 and Secondary 
3) in Hong Kong. The current analysis reports on the development of the DL assessment instrument and cross-cohort differences in 
students’ DL performance using data from the first wave that was conducted in the 2018/2019 school year. 
4.1. Instrumentation 
The development of the current DL test was largely informed by the DigComp 2.1 framework (Carretero et al., 2017), onto which all 
items were mapped as it provided detailed guidance on the various aspects of DL that a comprehensive assessment needs to cover. 
Based on the detailed descriptions for each competence area and subareas that the DigComp framework specifies, and provided with 
sample tasks and items from other assessments such as ICILS, test developers were asked to design items for most of the subareas. It was 
further decided that item formats adopted should primarily be the constrained response type. This choice had the advantage that a 
larger number and wider range of items could be administered, reducing potential bias that could occur when using only a few large 
tasks, which brings large dependencies across items. However, instead of largely text-based, multiple-choice formats, engaging visual 
stimuli and interactive items were incorporated to mimic realistic scenarios that students might encounter in their daily lives. Thus, the 
current assessment was different from merely knowledge-based or self-assessments, and from assessments using authentic or simulated 
software applications. 
A common item format was multiple-choice with two variants, where students either selected the correct answer by clicking on a 
radio button, or where they could select more than one answer using checkboxes (see Fig. 1). A similar yet more interactive format was 
the drop-down menu, where students selected one response from a list of options integrated into a visual image (see Fig. 2). We also 
developed drag-and-drop items where students clicked on a response and then dragged it into a response grid (see Fig. 3). Some short 
response questions asked students to write one or a few words (see Fig. 4). Finally, a rare format was represented by click-image items 
where students clicked on a particular position in a screenshot to indicate what they believe reflects the requested action (see Fig. 5). 
The research team initially generated 133 items in English language that were then reviewed according to the following criteria: (1) 
appropriateness of the language used and ease of comprehension for primary and secondary school students; (2) appropriateness of 
content and context for these students; and (3) implementability on the customized Open edX platform, which was used for data 
collection. Fifty-two items that did not satisfy one or more criteria were dropped, resulting in a total of 81 items for use in the pilot 
study. Each of these items was reviewed with respect to its suitability for measuring the respective competence area for which it had 
been developed. Although the remaining 81 items addressed all five key competences defined in the DigComp, six subcompetences 
were not covered (see Table 1). These six subcompetences represent areas that are difficult to assess without using extended tasks. As 
the study was conducted in Hong Kong where students may use either English or Chinese, these 81 items were also translated into 
Chinese. After piloting the items, some items were revised and a total of 80 items was retained for the main study assessment 
instrument. 
The researchers subsequently assembled three test forms, one for each student age cohort, based on the appropriateness of contexts 
and the time that students had spent on each item during the pilot study. The test forms were assembled so that 90% of the students in 
each cohort would be able to complete the test within the time limit (i.e., 50 min). The three booklets comprised 45, 50, and 50 items 
for Primary 3, Secondary 1, and Secondary 3 students, respectively (see Table 2), including some common items required to equate the 
test forms. This test design placed all students on the same scale and made students’ performances across different cohorts comparable. 
4.2. Sample 
The data collection procedure strictly followed the research ethics policy of the administrating institution. Primary and secondary 
schools located in four district areas in Hong Kong were sampled—18 primary schools and 14 secondary schools participated in the 
study, and the materials were administered to 715, 705, and 569 students in Primary 3, Secondary 1, and Secondary 3, respectively.1 
Students used desktop computers, laptops, or tablets (depending on the devices provided by the schools); and were given the option to 
complete the test in English or in Chinese. The student samples are summarized in Table 3.

4.3.2. Test quality (RQ2) 
Items with a low discrimination were discarded because of their lower ability to differentiate between students of different DL 
competence. In addition, item invariance is a fundamental requirement in measurement as only invariant items enable valid per­
formance comparisons across groups (Holland & Thayer, 1988). That is, to ensure that an assessment is “fair” and unbiased across 
groups of students, we conducted item-level invariance tests. The absence of so-called differential item functioning (DIF) provides a 
piece of evidence that supports the construct validity of the DL assessment. DIF assessment was carried out within the IRT framework to 
ensure that the test scores were comparable across genders (boys vs. girls). Items exhibiting different difficulties for different groups 
should be removed from the scale. The DIF assessment followed a the three-step scale purification procedure described by Holland and 
Thayer (1988): (1) Assess DIF for all items, one at a time, while treating all other items as anchors; (2) adopt those possibly DIF-free 
items as the new anchors and assess all items again; and (3) repeat the previous steps until two successive iterations yield the same set 
of DIF items. 
The analysis only assessed uniform DIF as it is usually difficult to provide meaningful interpretation for nonuniform DIF (relative to 
uniform DIF; Dib, Tamam, Terzi, & Hobart, 2017; Salehi & Tayebi, 2012). An item was said to have a substantial DIF when (1) the 
difference between the item difficulty estimates for different groups was significant at the 0.05 nominal level, and (2) the DIF size was 
larger than 0.50 (Le, 2009). An R script was written to carry out the entire DIF assessment procedure and we subsequently examined 
the quality of the retained items by computing the root mean square deviation (RMSD) statistic. The criterion of RMSD > 0.10 was used 
to screen out poorly fitting items (Yamamoto, Khorramdel & von Davier, 2013). Test reliability was assessed subsequently by means of 
the expected a posteriori (EAP) reliability estimates (Adams, 2005). 
4.3.3. Performance differences 
Finally, the purified scale was used in further analyses to examine cohort differences in DL performance (RQ3), and the extent to 
which student gender explained the variance in DL scores (RQ4). Analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted for cohort and 
gender as factors, including interactions between these two factors. A significance level of p < .05 was applied to highlight performance 
differences. Violin plots were used to examine overlaps in the performance scores among the three cohorts.

5.2. Fairness and reliability of the instrument 
Answering RQ2 was necessary to ensure that the unidimensional DL measure was reliable and produced comparable scores for 
different groups of students prior to addressing RQ3 and RQ4. Further analysis of the 2PLM showed that 6 out of 80 items had poor 
discrimination power (i.e., αj < 0.35). Of the remaining 74 items with acceptable item discrimination, one item was detected to have 
very different difficulties for boys and girls (see Fig. 6). The item difficulty was −0.37 for boys and 0.18 for girls, respectively, which 
means the item favored boys. It is not entirely clear why boys find the item easier, but it might be because boys are more familiar with 
such a situation or have encountered such a screen before, and that they know better when professional technical help is needed. 
Hence, this item was discarded from the final item pool. Therefore, 73 items were retained in the item pool to produce the DL per­
formance scores for all students. 
The parameter estimates of the 73 items are displayed in Fig. 7 (with items assigned to the same originally theorized sub­
competence shown as different numbers). The figure shows no clear pattern for items of different DL components. This result suggests 
that items supposed to measure different subcompetences did not cluster together in terms of difficulty or discrimination, so there was 
no bias of the unidimensional measure in this regard. Furthermore, the RMSD was between 0.007 and 0.077, indicating that the quality 
of the DL test was very good. 
Included in the three booklets were 42, 47, and 43 of the retained test items for cohorts 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The EAP reliability 
estimates for the booklets were 0.84, 0.86, and 0.84, respectively, indicating good precision of the measurement. Thus, performance 
scores could be estimated for all students for further analysis. 
5.3. Students’ DL performance 
5.3.1. DL performance across age cohorts 
Table 5 shows the mean DL scores and their standard deviations for different groups of students. The overall DL growth trend was 
clear: secondary students achieved higher scores than primary students, and cohort 3 students slightly outperformed cohort 2 students

distributions had large overlaps indicating, for example, that a number of primary school students outperformed some students in 
secondary grades (RQ3b).

6. Discussion 
6.1. Quality and dimensionality of the test 
6.1.1. Tension between the theoretical conceptualization of DL and empirical assessment results (RQ1) 
This study aimed to develop a reliable DL performance assessment for three age cohorts. The assessment that was developed 
measured a broader range of DL aspects than most previous studies which made it particularly suitable for examining the dimen­
sionality of DL. In particular, a goal of this research was to examine whether the instrument measured the five components that were 
conceptualized in the assessment framework, or if DL should be conceived of as a unidimensional literacy as several empirical studies 
have suggested (e.g., Aesaert et al., 2014; Gebhardt & Schulz, 2015). The findings of unidimensional and multidimensional IRT an­
alyses align with other research on DL, most notably the ICILS (Gebhardt & Schulz, 2015), according to which specific DL competences 
are highly correlated. Accordingly, DL can be conceived of as a more general literacy. 
Yet although our analysis results have pointed to DL as a unidimensional construct, further empirical evidence is needed to make 
this a definitive claim. First of all, the number of test items for some of the competence areas such as digital content creation and 
problem solving have relatively fewer items. Moreover, it is not clear that the dimensionality of the competences would remain the 
same (i.e., unidimensional) at higher levels of performance proficiency. In our next round of data collection, which will be carried out 
on the same student sample but two years after the first data collection, we hope to be able to explore this dimensionality issue further. 
However, there are important implications if DL can be taken as a unidimensional construct for the purpose of assessment. First of 
all, this would suggest that educators aiming to assess DL may not have to develop multiple sets of questions for each of the DL 
competence areas. That is, unless a specific competence/subcompetence area is of particular interest, few items per area could be

educational emphasis on a particular competence area could have spillover effects on an individual’s development on other DL di­

digital problems, such that it is difficult to differentiate them in empirical assessments. Future research could explore the dimen­
sionality of assessment items to illuminate further on this question. 
6.1.2. Test quality and comparability of the test scores across age cohorts (RQ2) 
Another question pertained to the quality of the test and its usefulness for assessing DL at different ages. The final performance test 
included 80 items, most of which adopted constrained response formats. This decision made it possible for the researchers to 
administer a larger number of items in a relatively short amount of time. It can also reduce the impact of students’ familiarity with a 
particular task context that could affect students’ performance in a DL assessment with only a few large tasks (Fraillon, 2018). Despite 
rigorous piloting prior to the field work, six items were discarded from the final DL measure due to low discrimination power; one item 
was dropped because of gender DIF. Hence, new items may need to be developed to replace those items. Moreover, the longitudinal 
study design may require that some relatively easy items be replaced as completing them should be even easier when students are older 
in subsequent data collections. Technological changes and software developments could also demand the revision of some assessment 
items, and this issue will be explored in the future. However, it is worth noting that these revisions are likely less costly than if a new 
assessment environment had to be developed to replace an outdated software platform, as could be the case when implementing 
performance assessments using authentic or simulated software applications. 
The current study shows that it is feasible to develop DL assessments suitable for primary and secondary students. The fact that only 
one item was biased with respect to gender provides support for the validity of the assessment and suggests that the construct was fair 
and generalizable across students from different genders. The psychometric properties of the final set of items suggest that the 
assessment is of very good test quality, and the three test forms used to assess students in the three age cohorts are highly reliable. This 
finding lends evidence to the assessment’s usefulness for measuring DL. The existing assessment could be the starting point for 
developing a more comprehensive item pool that test developers adopting the DigComp framework could draw on. The development of 
additional items measuring DL at younger or older ages than those included in the present study is another task for the future. 
6.2. Factors associated with DL development 
6.2.1. Performance differences due to cohort membership (RQ3) 
It came without surprise that secondary school students fared better than primary school students (RQ3a). This result indicates that 
DL develops over time, and experiences with digital technologies in early and middle adolescence may be important elements in this 
development (Lazonder et al., 2020). However, the violin plots showed huge overlaps in the distributions of performance scores of 
primary and secondary students (RQ3b). Though this was not a new finding (Kim et al., 2019; Law et al., 2007), in an era when in­
dividuals rely heavily on the use of technology for educational and career success as well as personal and social well-being, it is

and parents identify youths with needs for additional DL development and to provide them with support. Future studies could be

hours for using digital technology built into school curricula could contribute to a more equal development of DL (Zhu, Yang, MacLeod, 
Yu, & Wu, 2019). 
6.2.2. Performance differences due to gender (RQ4) 
This research further examined whether any variation in students’ DL performance could be explained by their gender. The analysis 
found no statistical differences in male and female primary school students’ DL performance but significant gaps between both genders 
at the secondary school level. This result stands in contrast to the findings from a meta-analysis according to which gender differences 
may decline with increasing age (Siddiq & Scherer, 2019). However, Siddiq and Scherer identified only few studies with primary 
school samples and reliable conclusions about the development of the gender gap were not possible. The current analysis, using data 
from primary and secondary students in the same educational context, therefore contributes new knowledge that puts a question mark 
behind that earlier finding. Although the world region where an empirical study was conducted does not appear to moderate the 
association between gender and DL (Siddiq & Scherer, 2019), the current findings also raise the question about what might have 
happened for Hong Kong students during the second half of primary schooling that may have caused the emergence of such a per­
formance divide between male and female students’ DL. The results also prompt the question of how home socialization and school 
education can prevent the emergence of this divide. One explanation of boys’ lower performance may be a lower concern for education 
and academic learning as well as more interest in playing games as they grow older, rather than engaging in digital activities that have 
more potential for the development of a general DL (e.g., Lau & Yuen, 2014; Ünlüsoy, de Haan, Leseman, & van Kruistum, 2010). In 
addressing the gendered DL gap, we suggest that educators and researchers should focus on how digital self-efficacy and motivations to 
use digital technologies vary between boys and girls. Further research is needed to understand the effects of gender differences in the 
access and usage of digital devices. In particular, longitudinal data are required to better understand the development of DL over time, 
and the students who participated in this study will participate in another wave during the 2020/2021 school year. 
6.3. Limitations and future research 
A few limitations of this study need to be addressed. First, we have only examined DIF based on gender. Although it was an 
advantage to have a bilingual assessment, it was not possible to test for language DIF because most students chose the Chinese version 
and the sample of students who responded to the English test was too small for an examination of DIF. Moreover, we discarded a few 
students who took the Chinese test but indicated that they speak English at home because their raw scores were significantly lower than 
those of other students. They probably did not understand the questions, suggesting that researchers should beware of the potential 
effect of language proficiency on DL performance. Another lesson that could be learned from this situation is to allow students to 
switch between different languages whenever they wish. Unfortunately, this could make it much harder to examine language-related 
DIF. 
Another limitation is that we primarily used constrained response items for an efficient administration of the assessment. This 
approach minimized possible context effects that larger tasks could have. However, students use fully functioning software applica­
tions in their daily lives. Future research could explore how DL can be measured across age cohorts through using their own digital 
devices. Is it possible to develop assessment software that can track what students do on their own digital devices, and then employ 
computational methods to infer their DL competence? More research is also needed to understand how familiarity with certain 
technologies might influence students’ performance in DL assessments. Although the current assessment was conducted on laptop and 
tablet devices, and even though students in this study could choose between different web browsers, this information was not recorded. 
In addition, future studies could pay attention to the differences between DL performance and self-reported DL. Further investi­
gation is also needed to provide more evidence on the validity of the test and to eliminate items that might be redundant. In this regard, 
it is noteworthy that the developed assessment, though comprehensive, did not cover all of the 21 subcompetences in the DL 
assessment framework (see Table 1). Furthermore, performance was compared across three age cohorts, but the data were cross- 
sectional. As technology develops rapidly and is increasingly used by ever younger individuals, a cross-cohort longitudinal study is 
necessary to fully understand DL development. Prior research suggested that DL develops linearly, but individual and cohort differ­
ences exist (Lazonder et al., 2020). 
7. Conclusion 
This study has developed an age-appropriate assessment instrument measuring DL across three cohorts of primary and secondary 
school students. IRT analyses showed that the measure was essentially unidimensional, of high reliability, and comparable across 
students of different gender. This result may be considered as evidence for the construct validity of the assessment. The results also 
demonstrate that there is a tension between the theoretical conceptualizations of DL as a multidimensional construct and research 
according to which distinct dimensions of DL can hardly be identified empirically. For unidimensional constructs, assessments could be 
developed to be more economical in terms of the number of items used to assess DL, particularly if in the end only a unidimensional 
score will be reported. 
The results also suggest performance differences between different age cohorts; differences were also found between male and 
female students in secondary schools. Future longitudinal research is needed to scrutinize why this difference appeared for the older

explore performance differences among students at different ages and, in conjunction with other information, could help schools 
identify how school factors and teaching support students’ DL development. Finally, the developed items could be adopted by other 
researchers for the assessment of DL based on the DigComp framework (Carretero et al., 2017).
