Research has shown that both gender and family socioeconomic status (SES) are important determinants of
adolescents’ physical activity (PA). This study expands our current knowledge by exploring the moderating role
of family SES on gender differences in adolescents’ out-of-school PA. We also examine whether this moderating
effect varies across countries with different levels of gender inequality. Based on existing empirical studies and
theoretical framework, it is hypothesized that 1) gender differences in out-of-school PA would be smaller among
adolescents from higher SES families due to them being more likely to share egalitarian gender norms, and 2) this
SES gradient in gender differences in adolescents’ out-of-school PA would be more salient in countries with lower
levels of gender inequality. A total of 316,657 15-year-old adolescents in 50 countries from the 2015 Programme
for International Student Assessment (PISA) survey were analyzed. Our multilevel negative binomial regression
models show that the gender gap in out-of-school PA is mitigated among families with higher SES, but this
moderating role of family SES is less noticeable in countries with higher levels of national gender inequality. Our
findings suggest the importance of egalitarian gender culture at both individual and macro-level in understanding gender disparities in adolescents’ PA.

1. Introduction
Engaging in physical activity (PA) during the adolescent stage provides diverse benefits such as improved physical and mental health
(Hallal et al., 2006), healthy body weight (Elinder et al., 2011),
increased self-perception (Stein et al., 2007), better academic performance (Michael et al., 2015; Owen et al., 2018), and better friendship
(Rachele et al., 2014). Given that PA during adolescence is an important
predictor of PA in later life (Tammelin et al., 2003), it also has life-long
influences on individuals’ lifestyles and well-being. In this context, a
large body of literature has tried to identify sociodemographic factors
associated with adolescents’ PA.
Previous research has found that male adolescents are more likely to
be physically active than female adolescents (Luque-casado et al., 2021;
Ricardo et al., 2022; Vilhjalmsson and Kristjansdottir, 2003). A study
comparing European and North American countries has suggested that
the gender gap in PA might be a sociocultural product of existing societal
gender norms, specifically the perception of PA as masculine behavior

(de Looze et al., 2019). Due to the prevailing gender norms, parents
might respond differently to their sons and daughters’ inclinations to be
physically active. Indeed, male adolescents are more likely than females
to perceive parental facilitation of PA (Welk et al., 2003), while female
adolescents tend to feel social pressure that prevents them from
engaging in PA (Cowley et al., 2021; Spencer et al., 2015). In addition,
parental support of PA is associated with more active engagement in PA
only for male children (Telford et al., 2016). These findings collectively
suggest that the gender gap in adolescents’ PA might be the outcome of
the gendered socialization process.
Another line of research has documented that adolescents from
higher socioeconomic status (SES) families are more likely to engage in
PA compared to those from lower SES families (e.g., Bann et al., 2019).
There are several possible ways through which family SES influences
adolescents’ PA (Stalsberg and Pedersen, 2010). Given that some activities require equipment, parental financial resources may play a role
in facilitating or discouraging their children’s participation in such activities. In addition to parental financial resources, parental cultural

resources might also affect adolescents’ PA. Highly educated parents
might possess better understanding of the health advantages of children’s PA (Stalsberg and Pedersen, 2010). It has been found that adolescents are more likely to engage in PA when their parents have greater
perceived importance of PA on children (Kahn et al., 2008).
Despite this, less is known about how gender and family SES jointly
influence adolescents’ PA. Based on the argument that gender differences in PA are socially generated by gender norms to which adolescents
and their parents adhere, gender differences in adolescents’ PA might be
more salient among lower SES families compared to higher SES families.
Parents with higher SES are likely to hold more egalitarian gender norms
(Brewster and Padavic, 2000; Du et al., 2021), which refer to an inclination towards more liberal gender roles (Pandian, 2019). In contrast,
those with more traditional gender role attitudes might distinguish
personal traits into binary categories, either masculine or feminine,
considering women as weak and vulnerable and men as active (Harrison
et al., 1981; Larsen and Long, 1988). Accordingly, they might be more
likely to adopt behaviors and attitudes in line with the prescribed
gender-role stereotypes (Rotter and O’Connell, 1982). It can thus be
expected that parents with higher SES might encourage their sons and
daughters to participate in PA to similar degrees, while their counterparts from lower SES backgrounds, who are more likely to have traditional gender role attitudes, socialize and motivate their sons and
daughters differently in terms of PA. Previous studies also suggest that
individuals’ gender role attitudes are established at early ages (Larsen
and Long, 1988; Reis and Wright, 1982) and adolescents from more
affluent families tend to have more liberal gender role attitudes (Filler
and Jennings, 2015; Kulik, 2002). Thus, they might be able to more
actively challenge prevailing gender stereotypes that being active is
considered masculine (Spencer et al., 2015).
However, the moderating role of family SES in gender differences in
adolescents’ PA might vary across countries depending on the macrolevel contexts in which individuals and families are situated. It has
been theorized that micro-level agencies, such as individuals or households, are nested in macro-level units, such as a community or society, so
their power is discounted by macro-level gender inequality (Blumberg,
1984; Fuwa, 2004). According to this theoretical framework, the potential role of highly educated parents’ cultural resources and egalitarian gender attitudes in encouraging female adolescents’ PA
participation might be suppressed by macro-level gender inequality. If
this is the case, the moderating role of family SES is expected to be
stronger in more gender equal societies, while it is less likely to be
noticeable in gender unequal societies.
Taken together, we attempt to address the following research questions: (1) Are gender differences in out-of-school PA among adolescents
smaller among families with higher SES compared to lower SES families? (2) If so, is the moderating role of family SES constrained in societies with traditional gender contexts? We utilize individual-level data
for 15-year-old adolescents from 50 countries included in the 2015
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) survey. This
individual-level data is combined with multiple sources of country-level
data to estimate multilevel negative binomial regression models to
answer our questions. To preview our results, we find evidence that
gender differences in adolescents’ out-of-school PA are smaller among
families with higher SES compared to lower SES families. Our analyses
also demonstrate that the moderating role of family SES is getting
weaker as national gender inequality increases.
The brief outline of the present study is as follows. In the next section, we introduce the data, measures, and analytical strategy used for
this study in detail. Then, both descriptive and multivariate results are
presented. Next, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of
our findings. Finally, we conclude with the limitations of our study and
suggestions for future research.

2. Methods
2.1. Data and sample
This study uses individual-level data of adolescents from the 2015
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) survey conducted by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) (Database: https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/201
5database). PISA has collected data on 15-year-old students mostly
attending upper secondary schools across many countries every three
years since 2000. We utilize data from the PISA 2015 because the most
recent wave, the PISA 2018, does not contain information on adolescents’ PA.
Originally, a total of 483,465 adolescents from 73 countries and
economies participated in the survey. This study is utilizing data from
the subset of cases (N = 54) that contain information on adolescents’ PA,
our dependent variable. Four cases were further excluded from our
analytic sample due to missing data on either national gender inequality
(Chinese Taipei, Hong Kong, and Macau) or neighborhood urbanicity
(Sweden). Thus, the final analytic sample consists of 316,657 15-yearold adolescents in 50 countries after the listwise deletion. We have
conducted a supplementary analysis including those four countries with
imputed values, but the results remain stable. In addition, the 2015 PISA
surveyed four provinces (Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, and Guangdong) to
represent China, which may raise concern on national representativeness. Accordingly, additional analysis was performed after excluding
China, but it hardly changed the results. The full results for these supplementary analyses are available in Table A1 in the online Appendix.
2.2. Measurements
2.2.1. Out-of-school PA
Our dependent variable is the self-reported number of days in the
past seven days adolescents participated in out-of-school vigorous PA.
The current study focuses on out-of-school PA for several reasons. Adolescents’ PA can be broadly distinguished into two categories: in-school
and out-of-school PA (Gidlow et al., 2008; Johnson et al., 2021; O’Neill
et al., 2016). While both PA can be beneficial to adolescents, in-school
PA is more likely to be determined by physical education (PE) provided by the school curriculum, whereas families can be more influential
in shaping out-of-school PA. Given that our goal is to explore the potential role of family SES in gender differences in PA, out-of-school PA is
the more suitable dimension for our study. On top of that, adolescents’
in-school PA levels are generally much lower than out-of-school PA
levels (Gidlow et al., 2008), which suggests that there might be more
room for differences in PA by gender and family SES during
out-of-school time (Bann et al., 2019).
PISA asks adolescents about the number of days during the past
seven days that they engaged in moderate and vigorous out-of-school PA
separately. In the PISA survey, vigorous PA is defined as activities lasting
at least 20 min that make respondents sweat and breathe hard, such as
running, cycling, aerobics, soccer, and skating. Moderate PA in contrast
includes walking, biking to school, or climbing stairs, which usually
captures adolescents’ PA in daily lives rather than intended PA. Thus,
moderate PA could be potentially influenced by diverse structural factors. For instance, adolescents in developing countries are more likely to
walk or ride a bicycle for commuting to and from school compared to
their peers in developed countries, possibly due to varied distance to
school or infrastructure for public transportation (Uddin et al., 2019).
Although both moderate and vigorous PA are beneficial to adolescents,
research has shown that vigorous PA might provide more benefits to
adolescents than moderate PA (Owens et al., 2017). In addition, it has
been reported that gender differences in PA are greater for vigorous PA
than for moderate PA (Bann et al., 2019). For these reasons, this study

Qatar, the Gini index is not available from the World Bank. Alternatively, we have utilized the data from the OECD for New Zealand (https

Statistics Singapore, 2017) and Qatar (Ministry of Development Planning and Statistics Qatar, 2015).
Table 1 presents weighted summary statistics of all variables in our
analysis for 50 countries. Following a previous study (de Looze et al.,
2019), we categorized the sample countries into three groups according
to the tertiles of GII index: low, medium, and high. A group of countries
with high GII implies relatively higher level of gender inequality.
Out-of-school PA is the lowest in Brazil, followed by Costa Rica,
Singapore, and France, while Iceland shows the highest levels of
out-of-school PA, followed by Poland, United States, and Denmark. In
terms of national gender inequality, most European countries, including
Slovenia, Switzerland, Germany, Denmark, and Austria, are characterized by comparably lower levels of gender inequality. Latin American
societies, including Dominican Republic, Brazil, Colombia, and Peru,
show relatively higher levels of gender inequality. Besides, Qatar stands
out with the highest level of gender inequality.

2.2.2. Family SES
PISA offers an index of “Economic, Social and Cultural Status
(ESCS)” indicating the overall level of adolescents’ family SES. This
index is a composite measure of parental education, parental occupation, and educational home possessions including the number of books
and other resources available in adolescents’ homes. Because the ESCS
index was originally scaled to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one across countries, it facilitates cross-national comparisons.
We conducted a sensitivity analysis with the individual components of
ESCS measure, but the results remained stable regardless of which
component was used (see Table A3 in the online Appendix).
2.2.3. Gender Inequality Index
To capture the overall level of gender inequality of a country, we use
the Gender Inequality Index (GII) provided by United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) (2015). The GII was introduced in 2010 by
the UNDP to overcome limitations of two prior indices, the Gender
Empowerment Measure (GEM) and the Gender Development Index
(GDI), and replaced them (Buvinic et al., 2014; Van Staveren, 2013).
Since then, it has been widely adopted by researchers as an indicator of
societal-level gender inequality (e.g., Borgonovi and Han, 2021; Pepin
and Cohen, 2021). As a composite index measure of gender inequality in
a given society, GII takes into account gender disparities in three dimensions: reproductive health, empowerment, and the labor market.
The values of GII range from zero to one, with higher scores indicating
greater gender inequality (Buvinic et al., 2014; de Looze et al., 2019).
2.2.4. Control variables
We include a total of six control variables to explore gender differences in PA, net of the potential covariates. Four variables are adolescents’ individual characteristics: in-school PE, academic performance,
school grade, and neighborhood urbanicity. At the country-level, we
control for the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita and the Gini
index.
With regards to the individual-level control variables, adolescents
were asked to report, on average, how many days they attend PE classes
per week during the school year. This variable ranges from zero to seven
days. Participating in PE classes is known to be positively correlated
with adolescents’ PA (Uddin et al., 2020). We try to examine the net
effects of national gender inequality and family SES on gender differences in adolescents’ out-of-school PA after controlling for in-school PE.
Second, as adolescents who prioritize academic achievements are likely
to be limited in out-of-school PA (Howie and Pate, 2012), we control
adolescents’ test scores on mathematics that PISA administered. We
utilize mathematics scores because it could be more suitable for
cross-national comparisons due to the universal nature of mathematics
(Kankaraš and Moors, 2014). Originally, the mathematics scores are
scaled to have a mean of 500 points and a standard deviation of 100
points. To facilitate interpretations, we have rescaled the mathematics
scores to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. Third,
given that PISA surveyed 15-year-old adolescents regardless of their
current school grade, it is necessary to consider what school grade the
adolescents were at the time of the survey. Although the majority of our
analytic sample was either 10th grade (about 53%) or 9th grade (about
32%) students, it varies across countries. Accordingly, we control for

2.3. Analytical strategy
Because this study examines how individual-level and country-level
characteristics are jointly associated with adolescents’ PA, we estimate a
series of multilevel models. The dependent variable, adolescents’ out-ofschool PA, follows a Poisson distribution, which is a discrete probability
distribution representing the number of events during a given time
period. While exploring the data, it was found that the distribution of the
dependent variable has greater variance than the mean (i.e., overdispersion), which violates the distributional assumption of Poisson data
that the mean is equal to the variance. Accordingly, the negative binomial regression models are more suitable for our analysis because they
adjust for Poisson overdispersion (Hilbe, 2011; Marshall et al., 2011).
We estimated the overdispersion parameter alpha, which helps us to test
the overdispersion assumption, from our specified models (Piegorsch,
1990). As can be seen in Table 2, the alpha was greater than zero in all
analytical models and statistically significant, which suggests that
negative binomial regression models are more appropriate than Poisson
models in our data.

3. Results
3.1. Descriptive results
Before estimating the multilevel models described above, we begin
by providing descriptive results on gender differences in out-of-school
PA across countries. Fig. 1 visualizes cross-national variation in
gender differences in out-of-school PA. The Y-axis represents gender
differences (a male-minus-female) in the adolescents’ self-reported
number of days in the past seven days adolescents participated in outof-school PA. We arranged countries in descending order concerning
the gender gap in out-of-school PA. It is not surprising that male adolescents are more likely to participate in out-of-school PA than female
adolescents in every country included in our analysis. All differences are
statistically significant at the alpha level of 0.05. This is consistent with a
previous study analyzing different sets of countries (de Looze et al.,
2019). The magnitude of gender differences varies considerably across
the countries, with the largest gap found in Japan. In contrast, the gap is
the smallest in Finland.
It is also noteworthy that gender differences in out-of-school PA vary
jointly by family SES and national gender inequality, as we hypothesized. Fig. 2 depicts the male-minus-female difference in adolescents’
weekly out-of-school PA by family SES and national gender inequality.
Here, gender differences in adolescents’ out-of-school PA are portrayed
across tertiles of family SES (low, middle, and high), separately for
countries with low and high gender inequality. As shown in the left
panel of Fig. 2, the family SES gradient in gender differences in out-ofschool PA is noticeable for countries with low gender inequality, with
smaller gender gaps in higher SES families. However, the corresponding
pattern is weaker in countries with high gender inequality, as shown on
the right panel of Fig. 2. This suggests that gender differences in adolescents’ PA might be abated among privileged families from genderegalitarian societies.

Now, let’s turn to our final model specified in the analytical strategy
section. In Model 5, we further include both individual and country-level
control variables to examine whether the results obtained in Model 4

the control variables, the more PE classes adolescents attended in
school, the more adolescents engage in out-of-school PA. Those in higher
school grades engage less in out-of-school PA. In addition, academic
performance is negatively associated with adolescents’ out-of-school PA.

Adolescents from urban cities are less likely to participate in out-ofschool PA than their peers from non-urban areas. At the country-level,
both logged GDP per capita and Gini index are not statistically significantly associated with adolescents’ out-of-school PA. Finally, the results
show that including control variables in the model does not significantly
change the effects of focal variables.

Three major conclusions surfaced from this study. First, consistent
with previous studies (Bann et al., 2019; de Looze et al., 2019; Ricardo
et al., 2022), female adolescents are less likely to engage in out-of-school
PA than male adolescents across all countries included in the analysis.
However, gender differences in out-of-school PA were smaller among
adolescents from higher SES families compared to their counterparts as
we hypothesized. This extends the findings from prior research by
showing that gender differences in PA might be mitigated among adolescents from privileged families. As explained in the introduction, these
patterns are likely partly driven by the fact that parents with higher SES
tend to hold more egalitarian gender role attitudes (Brewster and
Padavic, 2000; Du et al., 2021), and their children might be naturally
inclined to have similar gender norms (Filler and Jennings, 2015; Kulik,
2002). In addition, parents with higher SES are usually more likely to
possess awareness and knowledge of recommended guidelines for their
children’s PA (DeBastiani et al., 2014), which may translate into higher
levels of parental support of children’s PA. Accordingly, combined with
their liberal gender role attitudes, they might similarly encourage their
sons and daughters to be physically active, resulting in smaller gender
differences in adolescents’ PA among advantaged families. This finding
could help us further develop the theoretical arguments of gendered
socialization and family learning regarding adolescents’ PA.
Second, although gender differences in adolescents’ out-of-school PA
were ameliorated among families with higher SES, this pattern was more
salient in countries with lower levels of gender inequality. In other
words, the moderating role of family SES in shaping gender differences
in adolescents’ out-of-school PA was constrained in more gender unequal countries. This finding is consistent with our hypothesis based on
the theoretical and empirical argument that the power of individuals’
resources and ideology in gender dynamics are limited in more gender
unequal societies (Blumberg, 1984; Fuwa, 2004). While this theoretical
framework has been extensively used to explore gender inequality
across diverse outcomes such as men and women’s housework (Knudsen
and Wærness, 2008), mental well-being (Van de Velde et al., 2013), and
sports event attendance (Lagaert and Roose, 2018), it has been rarely
applied to the literature on gender differences in adolescents’ PA.

constrained in gender unequal societies. In addition, it is also noteworthy that female adolescents from lower SES families are least likely
to engage in out-of-school PA, which suggests that they might be least
likely to benefit from PA.
4. Conclusion
It has been well established that male adolescents tend to engage in
PA more frequently than female adolescents. Previous studies have also
shown that adolescents from privileged families are more likely to
participate in PA than their counterparts. However, less attention has
been paid to how these two factors jointly shape adolescents’ PA. This
study tries to fill this gap in our knowledge by exploring the moderating
role of family SES on gender differences in adolescents’ out-of-school
PA. Furthermore, the cross-national comparative nature of the PISA
2015 data allows us to examine whether the moderating effect of family
SES varies across countries with different levels of gender inequality.
8

S. Lee and Y. Lim

Social Science & Medicine 305 (2022) 115062

However, a recent cross-national comparative study has hinted at the
possibility of applicability with findings that gender gaps in adolescents’
PA are moderated by macro-level gender inequality (de Looze et al.,
2019). Building on and extending this line of research, our findings
suggest that the moderating role of egalitarian gender attitudes of parents with higher SES would be either realized or suppressed by social
structures. These results indicate that higher levels of national gender
inequality might prevent parents with higher SES from fostering or
conveying more egalitarian gender norms to their children, which
makes female adolescents less physically active than male adolescents
even among privileged families. Alternatively, it might be the case that
female adolescents from higher SES families are more likely to be
discouraged from participating in PA than their male counterparts due
to social pressures stemming from prevailing traditional gender culture
in a society, even though they inherit gender egalitarian ideology from
their parents.
Third, it is important to pay attention to the finding that female
adolescents are generally less likely to engage in out-of-school PA than
male adolescents across every country and family SES groups although
the degree of gender gap is varied. Considering the diverse benefits of PA
for adolescents (Esteban-Cornejo et al., 2015; Hallal et al., 2006;
Michael et al., 2015; Owen et al., 2018), these results indicate that female adolescents are more likely to be disadvantaged in multiple aspects
of life as a result of insufficient out-of-school PA compared to male adolescents. This is particularly relevant to female adolescents from lower
SES families residing in gender unequal countries given their lowest
participation in out-of-school PA. A possible alternative way to
compensate for the lack of out-of-school PA for female adolescents is to
actively participate in PA during school hours. Because our data does not
provide a comparable measurement for in-school PA, we cannot directly
test whether this is the case. However, a previous study has shown that
secondary school adolescents’ in-school PA is generally much lower than
out-of-school PA (Gidlow et al., 2008), which implies that female adolescents’ relative deprivation of out-of-school PA is not likely to be
compensated during school hours. Despite this, some evidence has
suggested that in-school PE may promote adolescents’ out-of-school PA
through enhanced knowledge and motivation (Wang and Chen, 2020).
Thus, educational policymakers might want to further develop PE programs to facilitate adolescents’ out-of-school PA, especially focusing on
promoting female adolescents’ PA to address rampant gender differences. Our study informs that it might be helpful for educational policies
and school teachers to socialize adolescents with gender egalitarian
ideologies in order to diminish gender gaps in PA.
There are several limitations in this study worth mentioning. Firstly,
we need to be cautious in interpreting our results because there could be
uncontrolled national characteristics that might explain the observed
effects of national gender inequality. Although we included countries’
economic development and income inequality as control variables in our
models, there is still a chance of omitted-variable bias as in the case of
other cross-national research (Yu, 2015). Especially, there could be
some other country-level cultural factors that might explain gender gaps
in adolescents’ PA, which are usually hard to measure (Bann et al.,
2019). To address this issue, we have estimated country-fixed effects
models, which is comparable to our multilevel models. In country-fixed
effects models, between-country heterogeneity could be controlled for
(Bol et al., 2014; Lee, 2017). This approach could serve as a useful tool to
test the robustness of results on cross-level interactions observed from
multilevel analysis (Yu, 2015). As seen in Table A5 in the online Appendix, results were consistent with the findings from our multilevel
models, lessening the concern about the omitted country-level
characteristics.
Second, although the PISA 2015 provides a great opportunity to
examine the relationship between family SES and adolescents’ PA across

many countries with varied macro-level gender inequality, it does not
contain exact measurements of parents’ gender ideology. This means
that we need to rely on family SES as a proxy for parents’ gender ideology rather than using a direct measurement of it. Given the close
connections between individuals’ SES and their gender ideology, we
believe that family SES functions as a reasonable proxy for gender ideology. However, future studies would benefit from collection of more
direct measurements of parents’ and adolescents’ gender ideologies to
explore gendered patterns of adolescents’ PA and their impact on
diverse aspects of their lives.
Lastly, our analysis uses a self-reported number of days in the past
seven days adolescents participated in out-of-school PA, which may not
accurately capture individuals’ exact levels of PA. For example, some
may exercise for 2 h on a single day, while others work out 20 min for
three days per week. Although the former performs more PA than the
latter in absolute time, it cannot be captured in this study base on our
measurement. However, this measurement of weekly PA has been
widely used in a large body of previous studies (e.g., Bann et al., 2019;
Brewer and Kimbro, 2014; de Looze et al., 2019). Moreover, there is a
reliability concern with the self-reported weekly PA measure (Bann
et al., 2019). It might be hard for adolescents to accurately memorize
and count their PA records of the past week (Sirard and Pate, 2001).
Despite this, given that the large-scale cross-national data on adolescents’ PA is limited, we believe our study still provides valuable
contribution in the literature. Future cross-national studies could benefit
from utilizing less biased measure of adolescents’ PA. For example, some
recent studies analyzed the Harmonized European Time Use Survey to
explore adolescents’ daily activities in Finland, Spain, and the United
Kingdom (Gracia et al., 2020, 2022). This survey provides more detailed
diaries of activities with 10-min time slots for a randomly chosen day of
the year. Although the number of countries is small, it was found that
gender differences in exercising activities vary across three countries
(Gracia et al., 2022), indicating that macro-level factors could be
influential for gender gaps in adolescents’ PA. It would be beneficial to
collect multilevel time-diary data on adolescents’ daily activities from
large number of countries for future studies (Gracia et al., 2020). More
ideally, the joint use of subjective and objective (i.e., devise-assessed)
measures of adolescents’ PA needs to be developed (Bann et al., 2019).
Despite these drawbacks, the present study provides empirical evidence that family SES and societal gender inequality might jointly shape
gender differences in PA. This may suggest the importance of egalitarian
gender culture at both individual and macro-level in understanding
gender disparities in adolescents’ PA. In the era of growing global
concerns about adolescents’ insufficient PA and gender gaps therein
(Guthold et al., 2020), it is our hope that our study motivates further
academic investigations on this topic and stimulates more active discussions among health and educational policy makers to alleviate
pervasive gender inequality in adolescents’ PA.
