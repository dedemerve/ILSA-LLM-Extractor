Abstract
Much of the existing educational research has focused on affluent Western societies.
Despite comprising a broad swathe of the world population, less work has focused
on lower middle-income economies such as the Philippines. Perhaps part of this
reason is the lack of high-quality data in such contexts. PISA provides a potential
solution to this problem. The Philippines participated in PISA in 2018 for the first
time and scored lowest in reading out of 78 participating economies. This study was
rooted in the bioecological model and simultaneously considered the roles of personal characteristics, proximal processes, and contextual factors to identify the most
important predictors of reading achievement in the Philippine context. We used the
2018 PISA Philippine data, which consisted of 7233 15-year-old adolescent participants. Machine learning was used to select important variables. Results revealed
the 26 top predictors of reading achievement out of 52 variables. The most critical
predictors in order of importance included reading difficulty self-concept, socioeconomic status, grade repetition, school belonging, and fixed mindset among others.
Hierarchical linear modelling broadly indicated similar results with small to moderate effect sizes. Theoretical and practical implications were discussed.
Keywords PISA · Bioecological model · Philippine education · Reading
achievement · Machine learning approach

1 Introduction
Educational research is dominated mainly by studies conducted in high-income
Western societies with relatively less work conducted in lower middle-income
economies such as the Philippines. This observation has led scholars to observe
that much of social science and educational research is WEIRD, that is, Western,
educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic societies (Henrich et al., 2010).
Though there are many reasons why educational scholarship is mainly drawn
from affluent Western societies, perhaps one of the most significant barriers to
conducting research in less affluent and non-WEIRD contexts is the lack of highquality data.
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) provides a potential
solution to this problem of lack of high-quality educational data. PISA has become
increasingly accepted across the globe. Its increasing popularity is manifested in
the growing number of countries participating in the assessment exercise, from
44 countries in 2000 to 78 in the most recent round completed in 2018 (OECD,
2019a). One of the primary advantages of PISA is that it provides rich data that
could be an invaluable resource for participating countries to understand their educational system more deeply (OECD, 2019a). Studies utilizing PISA data from low
and middle-income economies have emerged in the past few years (e.g., Ahmadi
et al., 2020; Bernardo, 2021, 2022; Haw et al., 2021; King et al., 2022; Lounkaew,
2013; Perera & Asadullah, 2019; Thien et al., 2015; Wills & Hofmeyr, 2019; Zuze
& Reddy, 2014). However, these studies are still relatively scarce and much remains
to be learned.
For example, a systematic review of PISA studies in its first decade showed
that the USA, Australia, Germany, the UK, and Ireland had the most number of
studies published on PISA (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018). In the same way, Pons’
(2017) literature review of PISA’s policy impact also focused on rich or Western countries. Hence, even with the growing number of non-Western and low- to
middle-income economies that have joined PISA, the benefits of the data generated were still underutilized by the non-Western lower middle-income countries.
In this study, we focused on the Philippines which is the ­13th most populous
country in the world and represents 1.4% of the world’s population. To date, there
are around 27 million Filipino students in the K-12 setting (Department of Education, 2021). The Philippines joined PISA for the first time in 2018 and scored
lowest in reading out of the 78 participating countries and regions. The country’s
dismal performance in PISA exposed an educational crisis (World Bank Group,
2020). Driven by the low rankings, the government has campaigned for aggressive reforms to boost the quality of its basic education system (Department of
Education, 2019). However, research that could empirically support these reforms
seemed lacking. As in many instances, the only reference made to PISA in the
government’s discussions concerned the low rankings of Filipino students but no
specific mention of which factors to focus on has been made (Bernardo, 2020;
Department of Education, 2019; San Juan, 2019).

Several studies have emerged since the Department of Education’s (2019)
national report, leveraging the PISA data in understanding what factors explain
the country’s performance and may predict future performance in PISA. However, many of these studies focused on few variables that could only moderately
predict reading achievement (Bernardo, 2022; Haw et al., 2021; Trinidad, 2020).
Perhaps the only comprehensive study on the Philippine PISA dataset is a report
done by the World Bank (2020) which looks at the policy implications of the
PISA findings. For example, it noted the percentage of Filipino students who
achieved the minimum level of proficiency in reading and also provided other
useful information such as comparing the percentage of advantaged vs. disadvantaged students who expected to complete tertiary education. This landmark report
provided important baseline information and various recommendations for policy
intervention.
However, the report was a policy paper and did not present the relative importance of the known predictors and the magnitude of their association with reading
achievement. The report also did not indicate how the different variables are related
to each other, which we address in this study through the use of the bioecological
model. These types of information have critical implications for strategizing interventions especially with the Philippines’ limited financial resources. Given the scarcity of published research using the Philippine PISA data, there is a need to complement the available knowledge with a systematic and comprehensive attempt to
unpack what the PISA dataset could offer. Such study does not only help in advancing scholarly understanding but also in promoting evidence-based policymaking in
the Philippine context.
One crucial challenge for a comprehensive investigation using PISA data is
accounting for the volume of predictors, indicating the multidimensionality of factors influencing educational outcomes. The volume of predictors becomes a concern
especially in educational research that often relies on classical statistical approaches.
Traditional statistics was designed to handle parsimonious models and is less useful
when handling a large number of variables (Breiman, 2001b; Yarkoni & Westfall,
2017). An alternative approach that has gained traction among researchers is the use
of machine learning approaches (Breiman, 2001b; Jacobucci & Grimm, 2020; Martínez-Abad et al., 2020).
We aimed at addressing the abovementioned gaps by leveraging the publiclyavailable PISA data to understand how different variables such as personal characteristics, proximal processes, and contextual factors were associated with Philippine
students’ achievement. We also explored the advantage of machine learning technique, specifically a random forest algorithm, to identify the most critical set of
factors that could predict reading performance in the Philippine setting, given that
PISA 2018 focused on reading as the key domain (OECD, 2019b).
1.1 Programme for international student assessment (PISA)
PISA is a triennial assessment program that measures 15-year-old adolescents’
reading, mathematics, and science literacy. PISA’s focus of assessment changes

every three years to complete a cycle of reading, mathematics, and science,
respectively. In 2018, the assessment focus was on students’ reading literacy. Its
unique characteristic was that, rather than focusing on curricular proficiency targets, its cognitive assessments measured what students could do with what they
knew in responding to real-world problems. The assessment results were published with the participating countries ranked according to their performance.
This “performance ranking” strategy was meant to use peer pressure as leverage
in pushing for better education outcomes among participating countries (OECD,
2019a).
Aside from the cognitive assessment, PISA also included a background questionnaire for schools and students. The school questionnaire gathered information about school background, resources, constraints, staffing, and curricular
focus. Meanwhile, the student background questionnaire collected information
about the students’ demographic data, non-cognitive (i.e., motivation, dispositions, practices) variables, and metacognitive learning strategies. It is important
to emphasize that these constructs were theory-driven and have been found in
past studies to be critical predictors of achievement (Lee & Shute, 2010). PISA
linked these school and student data with the cognitive assessment data to provide insights on what factors were positively and negatively associated with the
countries’/regions’ performance (OECD, 2019b). Hence, the publicly available
PISA data offered helpful information (e.g., best policy practices from high-performing countries) that could be used in formulating policies that impact a country’s/economy’s educational development.
For more than two decades, researchers have used PISA to build the knowledge base for policymaking and understanding reading achievement factors in
different contexts (Anderson et al., 2010; He et al., 2019; Lee & Stankov, 2018).
Consistent with PISA’s comparative intent, many studies were interested in patterns across participating countries/regions (e.g., Chiu & Chow, 2015; King
et al., 2022; J. Lee & Stankov, 2013), within or between geographical settings
(e.g., Martins & Veiga, 2010; Reparaz & Sotés-Elizalde, 2019; Yan et al., 2021),
or among high-performing countries/regions (e.g., J. Lee, 2014; Yu et al., 2015).
Past studies have also used country-level data to examine country-specific predictors and assess intervention impacts (Jakubowski & Jakubowski, 2021; Ma
et al., 2021; Marôco, 2021; Tire, 2021). However, as mentioned earlier, many
studies utilizing PISA were conducted in affluent and Western economies. Existing research done in non-Western context is dominated by studies from wealthy
countries/regions (e.g., China, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Macau, and Hong
Kong). PISA studies from low- to middle-income economies have recently burgeoned (Ahmadi et al., 2020; King et al., 2022; Perera & Asadullah, 2019; Thien
et al., 2015; Zuze & Reddy, 2014). Nevertheless, they are still underrepresented.
Hence, the PISA dataset could be especially relevant for lower middle-income
and developing economies such as the Philippines, where relatively little educational research has been conducted. Moreover, it provides an opportunity for
these countries to engage with this publicly available data and inform any educational interventions, which rarely happens in most countries (Anderson et al.,
2010; Auld & Morris, 2016; Morsy et al., 2018; Pons, 2012).

1.2 The Philippine setting
The Philippines joined PISA for the first time in 2018. The country tailed behind
the 78 participating countries and regions and was ranked the lowest in reading and
second to the lowest in mathematics and science. An imminent educational crisis
had already been detected from the patterns of poor performance in earlier national
and international large-scale assessments (Bernardo et al., 2008; Department of
Education, 2019). The dismal performance highlighted the severe condition of the
country’s education system. The Philippines’ Department of Education (DepEd:,
2019) cited the critical situation as a basis for campaigning for a four-point agenda
towards the quality of education set to boost the country’s quality of basic education: (1) K-12 review and update, (2) improving the learning environment, (3) teacher’s upskilling and reskilling, and (4) engagement of stakeholders for collaboration
and support. However, DepEd seemed to have launched the campaign without the
benefit of the research that examined factors that contribute to the Philippines’ PISA
performance. Consequently, the four-point agenda presented to the public seemed
to lack specific directions or foci of intervention for the country’s education system.
There are a few existing studies that have been conducted on the Philippine
PISA data (Bernardo, 2022; Cordon & Polong, 2020; Haw et al., 2021; Trinidad,
2020; Trinidad & King, 2022; World Bank Group, 2020). For example, Trinidad
(2020) looked at the role of gender and socioeconomic status on Filipino students’
reading achievement. Another study looked at how different teaching styles were
associated with reading achievement (Haw et al., 2021). Yet, these studies have
focused only on a few specific variables. Hence, they did not provide comprehensive information that could help Philippine policymaking. Perhaps the World Bank
Group’s (2020) country report is the most comprehensive and in-depth study examining the various predictors of student achievement in the Philippines. It attributed
the Philippine’s dismal PISA performance to several factors: sub-par educational
attainment, proficiency due to socio-economic status, school disciplinary climate,
and positive attitudes towards learning driven by gender and socio-economic
gaps. The Report also examined other factors (e.g., quality of instruction, learning
time, etc.) related to the PISA performance and suggested several critical areas for
intervention.
Though extremely useful for policymaking, the World Bank Report did not examine the relative strength or effect size associated with the different PISA variables.
Examining the effect sizes of the different predictors and comparing them against
each other is important given that schools and educational systems have to contend
with limited resources. Identifying the most important predictors of reading proficiency might provide useful information for potential variables that could be targeted
by interventions that would yield the greatest returns. A prominent example of such
an undertaking is Hattie’s (2008, 2023) work on Visible Learning which attempted
to identify the variables that had the most predictive power in terms of learning and
achievement. This type of work is useful because it allows us to see the “forest”
and not just the “trees" as all the variables exist in relation to each other rather than
in isolation and comparing their relative strength provides added value. Hence, we

1.3 Factors influencing reading achievement: Bronfenbrenner’s
process‑person‑context‑time (PPCT) model
1.3.1 Utilizing PISA background variables
As previously mentioned, the PISA framework (OECD, 2019b) integrated students’
cognitive information (i.e., assessment performance) with various demographic and
background variables associated with reading performance to inform policymaking. These variables include the following: family background (e.g., socioeconomic
status and gender), home interactions (e.g., language used at home and emotional
support from parents), non-cognitive and metacognitive factors (e.g., motivation,
attitudes, and beliefs), teaching (e.g., stimulation of reading), learning (e.g., time
spent in learning the language of test), school demographics (e.g., location and
ownership), school resources (e.g., number of teachers), and school climate (e.g.,
competitiveness). The OECD carefully selected these variables given prior studies
documenting their close associations with reading literacy and achievement (OECD,
2019b). What follows are broad examples of these variables with their theoretical
and empirical underpinnings. A full presentation of the PISA variables used in this
study and sample empirical evidence is provided as supplementary material (see
Table S1).
Socioeconomic status (SES) has been widely recognized as a crucial predictor
of student achievement (Berger & Archer, 2018; Berkowitz et al., 2017). SES determines an individual’s access to educational resources, which accounts for disparities in achievement (Giambona & Porcu, 2015; King et al., 2022; King & Trinidad,
2021; Yeung et al., 2022). For example, access to ICT resources is linked to one’s
family SES (e.g., Giambona & Porcu, 2015; Tan & Hew, 2018). Gender also persistently appears in research as a variable of interest in understanding achievement
gaps. Research findings indicate that females generally have an academic achievement advantage (Voyer & Voyer, 2014). This female advantage becomes more pronounced in reading literacy (Acar-Erdol & Akin-Arikan, 2022; Chiu & McBrideChang, 2006; Zuze & Reddy, 2014). Other student factors such as early childhood
education were also found to influence reading achievement (Chen et al., 2021).
Motivation, attitudes, and metacognitive characteristics are essential in predicting
student achievement (Hattie, 2008). These were considered malleable characteristics
and are potential levers for educational enhancement (OECD, 2019b). For example,
researchers have highlighted student engagement as a pre-eminent predictor of reading achievement (Appleton et al., 2008; Cho et al., 2019; Guthrie & Klauda, 2015).
They suggested that an engaged reader actively chooses and selects specific learning strategies to understand a text (Guthrie & Klauda, 2015). Researchers have consistently demonstrated that learning strategies and metacognition strongly correlate
with reading achievement (Artelt & Schneider, 2015; Brunner et al., 2018; Cai et al.,
2019).

Researchers have also shown that school and classroom climate could influence
student achievement (Daily et al., 2019; Davis & Warner, 2018). For example, Daily
et al. (2019) found that a positive school climate was one of the strongest determinants of academic achievement. Studies have also reported that school climate factors explain significant variance in students’ academic progress above and beyond
demographic characteristics (Davis & Warner, 2018; Hopson & Lee, 2011). More
specific to reading achievement, Meng et al.’s (2016) study showed four teacher factors related to reading achievement regardless of cultural context: the teachers as a
person, their classroom environment, teachers’ stimulation of learning, and teaching
strategies. Furthermore, negative experiences in school, such as the experience of
bullying, were also found to impact reading performance (Yu & Zhao, 2021).
Studies that examined PISA’s background variables and their influence on student
achievement have burgeoned since the first cycle of PISA (for a systematic review,
see Hopfenbeck et al., 2018). Family socio-economic status and gender were widely
examined in past research (e.g., Chiu & Chow, 2015; Chiu & McBride-Chang,
2006; Gamazo & Martínez-Abad, 2020). Other scholars focused on non-cognitive
variables arguing that these are more malleable and conducive for intervention
which may have more influence on student achievement (Bernardo et al., 2021; Lee
& Stankov, 2018; Yu et al., 2015). Others emphasized teacher support and practices
as a probable explanation of variances in student achievement (Haw & King, 2022;
Yan et al., 2021).
However, these studies have usually only focused on one or two critical variables but did not comprehensively examine how the different variables in the PISA
assessment influence achievement. Furthermore, from a policymaking perspective,
the analysis of these variables was geared toward identifying transferrable best practices and important factors for high achievement (Elliott et al., 2019). Researchers
argued that such analysis and utilization of the PISA data might generate a fractionated body of work that does not explicitly acknowledge critical interactions among
these variables (see Elliott et al., 2019; Lee & Shute, 2010). Elliot et al. (2019) suggested that these factors should not be evaluated separately but as a network or an
interactive system.
1.3.2 Bronfenbrenner’s process‑person‑context‑time (PPCT) model
Bronfenbrenner’s (2005; see also Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2007; Skinner et al.,
2022) bioecological model of human development provides a useful framework
to help us understand the role of different variables in predicting reading achievement. According to the bioecological model, human development is influenced by
four principal factors: proximal processes, person characteristics, context, and time
(PPCT). The model’s unique assertion was that these factors are not just additive
but form an interactive system that influences human development. The four factors
interact, bringing about the individual’s growth over time (Bronfenbrenner, 2005;
Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2007). The PPCT model may also be applied in literacy
development “because it offers a comprehensive explanation of literacy experiences,
demonstrating how developing readers’ individual characteristics transact with both
proximal and distal influences to craft their literacy ecology” (Jaeger, 2017, p. 164).

Though OECD did not explicitly use the bioecological framework, many of
the variables in PISA address elements in Bronfenbrenner’s bioecological model.
Hence, the PPCT model may be a sound theoretical framework for understanding
the various factors that impinged on student reading literacy as represented in
their reading assessment achievement. However, it is important to note that PISA
data’s cross-sectional nature made it impossible to employ the complete theoretical framework especially its temporal element. Experts have suggested that a partial model involving proximal processes, person characteristics, and context (PPC
model) may be used when longitudinal data is not present (Ashiabi & O’Neal,
2015; Jaeger, 2017; Tudge et al., 2016).
We only provided a broad definition of each factor in the PPC model due to
space considerations. As previously mentioned, these variables were theorydriven and chosen by the OECD for their known associations with reading literacy and achievement (see OECD, 2019b). Hence, we did not intend to replicate
OECD’s comprehensive literature review but rather to classify these variables
within the PPC model. The full details of these variables and sample empirical
evidence are provided as supplementary file (see online supplementary file 1,
Table S1).
1.3.2.1 Proximal processes Proximal processes refer to the person’s interaction
with the immediate environment—putting a premium on the individual as an
agent of development. Such processes involve person-to-person interaction over
an extended time but may also include interacting with objects and other activities (Ashiabi & O’Neal, 2015; Bronfenbrenner, 2005). These processes are the
main impetus for development because it is by interacting with the environment
that individuals develop their worldview and realize their potential (Tudge et al.,
2009). PISA captured these interactions in the individual’s reading-related attitudes, motivation, and strategies. In reading literacy, Jaeger (2017) underlined
the facilitative role of relationships with parents, teachers, and peers as important
proximal processes. The PISA questionnaire operationalized these by including
teaching practices for reading, parental involvement, and school interpersonal
relationships.
1.3.2.2 Personal characteristics Bronfenbrenner’s (2007) bioecological framework foregrounds the role of individuals as agents of their own development. As
such, their individual characteristics interact with their environment and experiences. These characteristics include those that may encourage or discourage
reactions from the environment (e.g., age and gender), genetic and psychological
resources (or liabilities) that affect optimal engagement, and active behavioral dispositions that could either enable or prevent the individual’s learning engagement
(Bronfenbrenner, 2005; Hayes et al., 2017; Jaeger, 2017). These characteristics are
best represented in PISA by the students’ personal (e.g., age and gender), dispositional (e.g., enjoyment of reading), school-focused attitudes (e.g., competitiveness), and well-being variables (e.g., positive emotions).

1.3.2.3 Context Bronfenbrenner’s model emphasizes that the person’s development is nested within different layers of contextual systems: microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystems (Hayes et al., 2017; Tudge et al., 2009,
2016). Tudge et al., (2009, 2016)suggested that evaluating the varying influence of
two microsystems or two macrosystems is enough to assess the context’s impact.
For brevity, we only focused on micro- and macrosystems (i.e., proximal and distal
contexts) that may have equivalent variables in the PISA questionnaire framework.
The microsystem refers to the proximal environment that may immediately
influence the individual. For example, the home, classroom, and school are the
primary facilitators of literacy (Jaeger, 2017). Bronfenbrenner and Morris (2007)
highlighted two aspects of microsystems that have strong associations with cognitive development: “On the constructive side are objects and areas that invite
manipulation and exploration, whereas instability, lack of clear structure, and
unpredictability of events undermine the developmental process” (p. 815). These
aspects may be represented by several PISA variables—home learning resources,
classroom resources and environment, and school climate. We also included
length of early education and repetition as these are parental and school decisions
that may influence the students’ literacy development.
The macrosystem is defined as the overarching patterns of a sociocultural group or sub-groups (King, 2022; King et al., 2018; King & McInerney,
2014; Rosa & Tudge, 2013). It includes the individual’s cultural values and supporting socio-economic conditions and opportunities (Ashiabi & O’Neal, 2015).
The impact of macrosystems on one’s development is evaluated in terms of group
differences (Tudge et al., 2009). It may also include opportunities provided to or
taken away from individuals due to group membership (Elliott et al., 2019; Jaeger, 2017). One’s SES or social class is one of the factors often associated with
the macrosystem. SES or social class was not directly measured in PISA but represented by an index of economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS). ESCS was
derived from parents’ highest educational status, highest occupational status, and
general wealth. OECD (2019a) also gathered data about immigration status as
this is a central issue that determines educational opportunities.
Several prior studies have utilized Bronfenbrenner’s model in investigating
the influences of different PISA background variables on country-specific student achievement (Allen et al., 2021; Areepattamannil, 2014; Pitsia, 2022; Wang
et al., 2022; Yu & Zhao, 2021). However, these studies were relatively rare. We
aimed to fill the abovementioned gap by employing the PPC model as the guiding
framework of our study.

1.4 Classical statistical and machine learning approaches
The factors associated with reading achievement are complex and multi-dimensional. This posits a challenge for classical statistical approaches. First, classical statistics relies on model-data fit, requiring parsimonious or simple models
that tend to overlook other critical variables (Breiman, 2001b). Second, classical

statistical methods mostly assume linear relationships (Ryo & Rillig, 2017).
Classical statistical methods could handle variable interactions, but they rely on
the researchers to define these interactions in the model design (Ryo & Rillig,
2017). Hence, considering the interactions among several variables might prove
to be quite challenging (Hong et al., 2020).
Researchers have suggested machine learning algorithms as an alternative
approach in studies investigating the simultaneous influence of several variables
or factors (Hong et al., 2020; Ryo & Rillig, 2017). There are two key features
found in most machine learning algorithms that support this type of research
goal. First, machine learning algorithms were designed to internally handle
issues related to many variables (e.g. multicollinearity and overfitting) which
often leads to parsimonious models via the selection of important variables
(He et al., 2019; Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Second, machine learning algorithms employ non-parametric techniques, such as decision trees, that intuitively
approximate interaction effects (Hong et al., 2020). These features have made
machine learning a viable alternative to traditional statistical methodologies,
and it is increasingly being applied in several domains of scholarly research,
such as agriculture (Patel & Patel, 2014), neurology (Bzdok, 2017), and medicine (Lu & Ishwaran, 2018).
In the past decades, researchers have also applied machine learning in educational research, especially with large-scale international assessments such as
PISA (Gamazo & Martínez-Abad, 2020; He et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022). For
example, using decision trees, Gamazo and Martínez-Abad (2020) investigated
the factors linked to academic performance in the PISA 2018 worldwide data.
They found socioeconomic factors at the root of the decision tree, indicating
that SES had the most impact. The finding echoed the OECD report (2019a)
regarding socioeconomic factors impacting academic performance across countries and schools. Several studies have also used machine learning approaches
in analyzing country-specific factors influencing achievement. Gabriel et al.
(2018) investigated the relationships between Australian adolescents’ psychological characteristics and mathematics literacy in the PISA 2012 dataset. The
study found complex interactions between the adolescents’ self-efficacy, math
anxiety, and socioeconomic position concerning Australian adolescents’ performance in math. In Germany, Lezhnina and Kismihók (2022) explored German
adolescents’ attitudes regarding information and communications technology
(ICT) and its association with science and math achievement using PISA 2015
and 2018 data.
The use of machine learning approaches in PISA has also emerged in Asian
contexts, though much of this work has been in affluent Asian societies such as
Singapore (Dong & Hu, 2019), Hong Kong SAR (Wang et al., 2022), and Korea
(Chung et al., 2021). Recently, studies from lower middle-income economies such
as Guatemala, Paraguay, and Senegal (Delprato et al., 2022), Vietnam (Musso
et al., 2020), and the Philippines (Bernardo et al., 2021, 2022) have also contributed to the literature in understanding key predictors of literacy using machine
learning approaches. However, these studies are relatively under-represented,
adding to the observation that educational research utilizing machine learning is

1.5 The present study
The present study investigated the most influential factors that could predict Philippine adolescents’ reading literacy using a machine learning approach. Drawing
on the PISA database, we grouped the variables into three categories: proximal
processes, personal characteristics, and context (PPC model). These categories
were based on a modified version of Bronfenbrenner’s PPCT model (Ashiabi &
O’Neal, 2015; Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2007; Tudge et al., 2009, 2016). Figure 1 below shows the conceptual framework of our study.

2 Methods
2.1 Data and measures
We utilized the Philippine PISA 2018 dataset, which consists of 7233 15-year-old
adolescent students. These students were selected through a complex two-stage
sampling method. About 187 schools of various types were first sampled across
the Philippines. Students within these schools were subsequently sampled. PISA

included weights in the published dataset to account for the two-stage sampling.
The current study used these weights in drawing out classical statistical inferences (i.e., correlation and regression estimates) to ensure unbiased and accurate
estimates (OECD, 2009).
2.1.1 Outcome variable
PISA 2018’s main assessment focus was reading, so we used the students’ reading
scores in PISA 2018 as the outcome variable. It is important to note that the student’s performance scores are estimates of their ability based on a limited number of
questions. To cover a specific range of assessment items within a limited time, PISA
administered a rotated test booklet wherein each booklet contains blocks of assessments from the PISA item pool (Rutkowski et al., 2010). The country’s general performance was estimated from the combined scores from these test booklets. This
approach presupposed that the PISA assessment was meant to draw the performance
score at the country or population level (OECD, 2009). However, the PISA dataset
included individual performance to aid researchers in secondary-data analysis. PISA
estimated the students’ performance scores from the population’s ability distribution
based on the individual’s performance in the assigned test booklet. Ten plausible
values were provided to indicate the estimates of what the individual student might
reasonably have from the assessment (OECD, 2009). These plausible values were
scaled to have a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100 using the Rasch model.
We followed the PISA data analysis procedures by conducting individual analyses
for each plausible value and aggregating the results as a final step (OECD, 2009).
2.1.2 Predictors
We selected predictors from the school-level and student-level variables in the Philippine PISA 2018 dataset. The school principals provided school-level information
(e.g., location, ownership, staffing, resources, etc.) through the school questionnaire. Meanwhile, the student questionnaire consisted of demographic (e.g., ESCS
and home resources), reading-related (e.g., understanding texts), dispositional (e.g.,

We excluded variables that have 100% missing information. Furthermore, we
removed the PISA index variables used as input to create another index to avoid
redundancy. For example, we did not include the index of the highest occupational
status of parents (i.e., HSEI) because it was used to derive the ESCS index. Finally,
we removed the variables that did not show a statistically significant correlation with
reading achievement from our preliminary analysis. The exclusion reduced the predictors to 53 variables composed of simple questionnaire indices, scaled variable
indices, single item, and composite variables. We classified these variables using the
bioecological model (see Fig. 1). We presented the full description of the predictors
as an online supplementary material (see Table S1).
2.2 Analytic strategy
2.2.1 Preliminary analysis
After removing the variables that met our exclusion criteria, we analyzed the missing value using 80% missingness as a threshold per case (Martínez-Abad et al.,
2020). All cases had less than 25% missingness, so all 7223 observations were
retained. We conducted multiple imputation procedures using mice package in R
(van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshorn, 2011). This computational package used a
multivariate imputation by chained equations method (MICE), which was found to
provide unbiased estimates of missing data in large-scale assessments (Grund et al.,
2020). Afterwards, we summarized the descriptive statistics and examined the correlations between the predictors and outcome variables. The statistical estimates
were computed following the PISA data analysis manual guidelines using several
statistical packages in R (see Long, 2021; Lumley, 2021; Robitzsch & Oberwimmer,
2019). For heuristics purposes, we used Gignac and Szodorai’s (2016) guidelines in
evaluating effect size (i.e., 0.10 is small, 0.20 is the typical effect size, and 0.30 is
the relatively large effect).
2.2.2 Primary analysis
We conducted our primary analysis in two steps. First, we examined the relative
importance of each predictor using machine learning approach. We created a regression model with 52 background variables simultaneously predicting individuallevel reading achievement (Model 1). We then streamlined the model by selecting
only the variables with the most significant predictive impact on Philippine reading
achievement (Model 2). Next, we utilized the Model 2 predictors to construct a hierarchical linear model and identified their parameter estimates.
2.2.2.1 Step 1: Random Forest machine learning (RF) We used Random Forest
machine learning algorithm using ranger (Wright et al., 2021) and randomForest
(Liaw & Wiener, 2022) packages in R. RF is a non-parametric regression estimation
generalized from a collection of randomized decision trees built from boot-strapped
samples (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). One advantage of RF over other machine learn-

13

Understanding Filipino students’ achievement in PISA: The…

1105

ing algorithms is that it can handle different data types and does not require any data
transformation. Researchers generally summarize the random forest algorithm as follows (see, for example, Biau & Scornet, 2016; Gregorutti et al., 2017):
(1) Split the dataset into a training set and a test set;
(2) From the training set, draw a bootstrapped sample with the same size as the
original dataset;
(3) From the bootstrapped sample, build a decision tree;
(4) Build several decision trees where each tree comes from a random and independent bootstrapped sample;
(5) Generalize the final estimates by averaging the result of all the individual decision trees;
(6) Cross-validate the prediction results with the test (unobserved) set; and
(7) Reiterate the process until optimal accuracy or stopping constant has been met.
Following the PISA guidelines, we conducted random forest analyses for the ten
plausible values of reading achievement and aggregated our results to generate the
final estimates of our model. We did not use the replicate weights as random forest do not generate parameter estimates. We analyzed our model with the following
steps: (1) evaluation of model accuracy and validity, (2) quantifying and ranking the
importance measures of the predictors, and (3) cross-validating the prediction model
and empirically selecting the most important predictors.
2.2.2.2 Accuracy and validity RF’s primary concern is prediction accuracy. The
model validity is not only ascertained by how it fits the observed data but also presupposes that it can still accurately predict the outcome given a new data set. The
algorithm internally tests this prediction on “new data” by using about three-fourths
of the bootstrap sample to train the algorithm and test the prediction with the remaining one-fourth of the sample (Archer & Kimes, 2008; Lu & Ishwaran, 2018). Instead
of model-data fit, RF algorithms report two heuristics for model goodness: accuracy
and validity. First, random forest reports the error rate (i.e., root mean squared error
or RMSE) of the prediction vs. unobserved sample to indicate the model’s accuracy
performance. Lower RMSE means higher prediction accuracy. Second, RF reports
the percentage of the variance explained by the prediction on the unobserved sample
­(R2) to indicate the model’s overall effect. We evaluated our regression model using
the said two metrics.
2.2.2.3 Variable importance In lieu of providing parameter estimates and significance testing, RF a variable importance measure for each predictor in the regression model. Variable importance allows the identification of predictors with the
highest impact on the outcome variable (Biau & Scornet, 2016; Lu & Ishwaran,
2018). One way in RF determines variable importance is by quantifying the
decrease in the model’s accuracy. The algorithm returns the value that shows the
percentage of prediction inaccuracy if the values of the variables in the unobserved
set were permuted (Breiman, 2003). According to Breiman (2003), less important

variables would not create a substantial inaccuracy to the prediction even if their
values are permuted. In contrast, variables with greater importance are expected
to increase prediction error if their values are permuted. Hence, the higher the
decrease in accuracy resulting from the permuted value of the predictor indicates
its greater importance in the prediction model. In this study, we examined the
importance measure of each predictor using the random forest’s mean decrease in
accuracy heuristic and ranked them from the most important to the least important.
2.2.2.4 Cross‑validation and variable selection Having 52 predictors in the
regression model makes noise in identifying the most important predictors, especially when they are very close regarding their importance measures. To eliminate
this noise and to streamline our model to the most important predictors of reading achievement, we conducted a tenfold cross-validation procedure. The crossvalidation procedure sequentially tests the crucial variables and their performance
in the model’s prediction accuracy. The prediction error is expected to improve as
the number of important predictors accumulates. “Noise” predictors have relative
importance but do not substantially improve the model’s prediction performance.
Variable importance measures allow the identification of “true predictors” or those
with the highest impact on the outcome variable (Biau & Scornet, 2016; Lu &
Ishwaran, 2018). Finally, we created our streamlined model (Model 2) using the
predictors selected by the tenfold cross-validation procedures. We reported the
prediction estimates and the importance measures of the predictors.
2.2.2.5 Step 2: hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) Next, we conducted an HLM
analysis of the final model. A central component of classical statistics is the generation of parameter estimates that provide vital information for model interpretation. In contrast, machine learning algorithms do not offer parameter estimates.
Hence, despite its known advantages, interpreting the results of machine learning
may be challenging, especially for those more familiar with the classical approach.
This second step aimed at providing comparative information (i.e., parameter estimates and standard errors) from classical statistical methods in view of easier
interpretation. We utilized bifiesurvey package in R (Robitzsch & Oberwimmer,
2019) to compute these estimates. Following the PISA analysis manual’s procedures (OECD, 2009), we used the 80 replicate weights included in the dataset,
separately computed the parameter estimates of the 10 plausible values of reading
achievement and aggregated them for the final estimates.

3.2 Primary analysis
3.2.1 RF models
3.2.1.1 Model 1 We estimated ten prediction models, regressing all 53 variables to
the ten plausible values of reading achievement using a random forest algorithm. We
fixed each algorithm to grow 1000 decision trees randomly. We followed the suggestion of the random forest fine-tuning procedure to test 17 variables at each level
in the decision tree to reach optimal prediction accuracy. We computed Model 1’s
final estimates by aggregating the results from the ten prediction models. Results
showed that the final prediction model explained 64.19% of the variances in student
reading achievement in the unobserved sample with a 2227.06 error rate ­(R2 = 0.66;
RMSE = 47.19). The individual variable importance scores of the predictors and their
ranking are presented in Table 1.
3.2.1.2 Model 2 The variable rankings in the above model allowed for selecting the
top most important variables. Some studies have typically selected the top 10 or 20
important variables. However, these numbers seem arbitrary and have not been given
empirical underpinning. In this study, we empirically determined the top most important reading achievement predictors via cross-validation procedure. We conducted
five iterations of a tenfold cross-validation procedure to select only those predictors
that substantially affect the model’s accuracy performance. The cross-validation procedure reported the model’s error rate when the predictors were sequentially added
to the model (in order of importance). The results of the cross-validation procedure
were included in the online supplementary material (see Figure S1 in supplementary
material 1). Results showed that the prediction model’s accuracy performance was
optimal at 26 predictors showing the lowest error rate of 2280.44 (RMSE = 47.75).
The succeeding predictors no longer significantly contributed to the model’s accuracy.
Hence, we selected the top 26 variables as the best predictors of reading achievement.
Complete results of the cross-validation procedure are found in the online supplementary materials (see Figure S1 in supplementary material 1).

ranked performance of Model 2 predictors.
Results showed that the critical predictors adequately represented the three factors in the bioecological model: personal characteristics, proximal processes, and
context. The personal characteristic predictors consisted of 12 variables: self-concept of reading difficulty, fixed mindset, work mastery, reading enjoyment, mastery
goal, resilience, eudaimonic well-being, expected job in the future, attitude towards
learning activities, self-concept of reading competence, general fear of failure, and
perceived difficulty of PISA. The machine learning algorithm identified five proximal processes: time spent on online reading, summarizing metacognitive strategies, emotional support from parents, learning time in the language of test, and time
spent in leisure reading. Lastly, nine contextual variables were identified to have
a close association with reading achievement: socioeconomic status, grade repetition, school belongingness, discriminating school environment, being bullied, ICT
resources at home, classroom disciplinary climate, shortage of educational material,
and student-to-computer ratio.
The final model indicated that personal characteristics and contextual variables
dominated Philippine adolescents’ reading achievement predictors. Among the personal factors, three variables were negatively correlated with reading achievement:
self-concept of reading difficulty, fixed mindset, and perceived PISA difficulty. Selfconcept of reading difficulty had the highest impact in the predictive model. Results
further showed that socioeconomic status, grade repetition, and belongingness were
the primary contextual predictors of achievement in the sample, echoing the trend
found in prior studies. Furthermore, among the variables under proximal processes,
the time spent on online reading was found to have the highest impact, seconded by
summarizing metacognitive strategy and parental emotional support. The proximal
process variables primarily consisted of the amount of time spent on learning (i.e.,
online reading, leisure reading, and language class). The importance ranking of each
variable indicated the decrease in accuracy performance of the whole model when
the variable at hand is perturbed. Hence, in general, the results suggested the interdependence of the variables within the model.

4 Discussion
Our study investigated the important predictors of reading achievement among Philippine adolescents using the PISA 2018 data. We used a modified model of Bronfenbrenner’s bioecological framework as the lens in our inquiry. According to the bioecological model, three interacting factors simultaneously impinge on the person’s
development: personal characteristics, proximal process, context, and time (PPCT
model). However, since PISA data is cross-sectional, we only used the first three
factors (i.e., the PPC model) to analyze the Philippine sample. We examined 53 predictors using the random forest machine algorithm to identify the most important
variables associated with reading achievement. Results suggested that 26 out of the
53 identified variables critically contribute to the prediction model. Consistent with
Bronfenbrenner’s bioecological framework, results showed that these predictors
consisted of personal characteristics (12 predictors), proximal processes (five predictors), and contextual variables (nine predictors). The machine learning algorithm
intuitively took into account the interactions among these variables, making the
approach supportive of the bioecological model’s theoretical assumptions. Results
were consistent with the findings from prior studies (World Bank Group, 2020).
Several empirical insights may be drawn from these findings that could provide
“knowledge for learning” and “knowledge for policy” for Philippine educational
policymakers (Pons, 2012). First, our results underscored the crucial role of contextual factors affecting reading achievement. The model’s top three most critical
contextual predictors were family socioeconomic status, grade repetition, and school
belongingness. It is noteworthy that these important contextual variables were
mainly related to the school environment and socioeconomic conditions either at
the student or school level. This finding is generally consistent with past studies,
which suggested these two influence student achievement (Chiu & Chow, 2015; Delprato et al., 2022; Trinidad, 2020). SES as the second topmost important contextual
predictor is not surprising as this has been consistent in the research literature and
Philippine findings (Liu et al., 2020; Sirin, 2005; World Bank Group, 2020). Moreover, SES has been found to explain gaps in motivation, attitudes, learning engagement and even the school climate leading to gaps in academic achievement (Ahmadi
et al., 2020; Bernardo, 2022; King et al., 2022; Shin & So, 2018).
More importantly, SES does not only influence the individual student but also
has critical implications for the school. Ideally, schools are supposed to equalize socioeconomic disparities (Chiu & Khoo, 2005; Huang, 2010). However,
socioeconomically disadvantaged students in the Philippines tend to go to socioeconomically disadvantaged schools which lack these equalizing resources leading to further achievement gaps (Huang, 2010). Scholars have suggested that an

13

increase in school resources is associated with greater academic achievement
(e.g., Zuze & Reddy, 2014). The World Bank report (2020) has reiterated this
point. Consistent with prior research, our findings underscored the importance
of leveraging school material resources and school climate to address potential
gaps in achievement brought about by socioeconomic status (World Bank Group,
2020). Particularly, our findings reiterate the crucial impact of providing disadvantaged schools with ICT facilities and developing a rigorous ICT curriculum
(World Bank Group, 2020).
The need for ICT in schools highlights a second point from the evidence. Findings suggest that the abovementioned contextual predictors are related to the
proximal processes available to students. Results suggested that the amount of
time spent for learning and reading, whether in a formal class or leisure activities,
were essential predictors of reading literacy. Specifically, online reading emerged
as the most critical variable that predicts Philippine adolescents’ reading achievement. This finding is crucial as PISA 2018 has extended its operationalization
of reading literacy to encompass digital reading (OECD, 2019b). Some studies
even have considered PISA 2018 an assessment for digital reading literacy (Hu
& Yu, 2021). Hence, the findings emphasized an aspect of the Philippine reading
curriculum that may need attention if optimal reading literacy is desired. However, a digital reading curriculum may only be operationalized with adequate ICT
facilities.
Another top predictor under the proximal process category is metacognition.
PISA 2018 included three metacognitive strategies in the questionnaire: identifying
sources of information, summarizing, and understanding and remembering (OECD,
2019b). Our results indicated that among the metacognitive reading strategies, summarizing had the most impact on Philippine adolescent’s reading achievement. This
predictor has an essential role not only for reading achievement but also for other
vital factors that influence it. For example, researchers have found associations
between metacognitive strategies and academic self-concept (Roebers et al., 2012).
Research has also emphasized that the most effective way of facilitating a strong
belief about one’s competence is through mastery experiences (Bandura, 1994). In
reading, such mastery experiences may include exposure to various metacognitive
reading strategies (Unrau et al., 2018). Also, it is important to note that researchers have shown a reciprocal relationship between metacognitive strategies and motivational factors such as self-efficacy and self-concept (Bandura, 1994; Ferla et al.,
2009), as well as mastery goals (King & McInerney, 2016). Furthermore, knowledge of metacognitive strategies may mediate the relationship of other variables
(e.g., reading enjoyment) with reading comprehension, which is crucial in PISA performance (Miyamoto et al., 2019).
The abovementioned associations lead to the third critical point. Evidence
revealed that the final model’s top predictor was self-concept of reading difficulty
which aligned well with prior studies that suggested self-concept as having significant impact on achievement (Guo et al., 2015; Habók et al., 2020; Marsh, 1984;
Marsh & Martin, 2011; Retelsdorf et al., 2014). It is crucial to underscore that
this variable is a negative type of self-concept which may inhibit reading achievement. Our findings then may have revealed that negative self-concept of reading

competence is one of the critical factors associated to the Philippine adolescent’s
reading achievement in PISA. In contrast, research has suggested that self-concept
of reading competence is a topmost predictor of achievement in some high-performing Asian countries was also (Wang et al., 2022, 2023a).
Reading competence self-concept, along with other motivational and attitudinal
constructs are considered malleable factors that could be addressed by intervention
programs (OECD, 2019b). For example, studies have shown that intervention programs on reading self-concept may facilitate higher academic outcomes (e.g., Rhew
et al., 2018; see also Lazowski & Hulleman, 2016 for a meta-analytic review). Such
intervention may happen in the proximal experiences of the learners such as teaching learning strategies, encouragement or feedback (Bandura, 1994; Craven et al.,
1991; McInerney et al., 2012). As there is a reciprocal causality between competence and self-concept, helping students increase their academic competence have
been also shown to increase their self-beliefs (Hornery et al., 2014; Marsh & Martin, 2011; Parker et al., 2014). More importantly, researchers have emphasized that
the most effective intervention happens overtime by cultivating competence through
mastery activities (Bandura, 1994; Bandura & Schunk, 1981; Choi, 2005). Hence,
when adequately addressed, reading self-concept could potentially steer the students’ reading outcomes (OECD, 2019b). The findings suggested higher chance of
optimal reading achievement when education interventions paid attention to proximal and distal processes that increase reading competence self-concept.
Finally, the foregoing discussion demonstrates the interplay between personal
characteristics, proximal processes, context, and time as asserted by Bronfenbrenner’s (2005) bioecological framework. The results suggested that the top predictors
associated with reading achievement are inextricably related such that these predictors cannot be addressed in isolation from the other. For example, and as we laid out
in this discussion above, reading competence self-concept (personal characteristic)
is effectively formed through mastery experiences which include learning metacognitive reading strategies (proximal process). From the perspective of digital reading,
mastery experiences entails prolonged exposure to different digital or online texts
(time). In turn, such exposure may only be provided if ICT resources are available
at home or school (context). Such interplay indicate that interventions may need to
collectively address these three dimensions to effectively facilitate better reading
outcomes.
These findings have critical implications for Philippine policymakers in understanding the factors surrounding reading achievement and drawing potential policy
interventions based on this information. First, PISA was primarily designed to provide information that could help address the achievement gap predicted by socioeconomic contexts. Hence, the first critical consideration for policymakers might
still revolve around ensuring socioeconomically disadvantaged students have
access to educational resources that will strengthen and facilitate reading literacy.
As socioeconomically disadvantaged students also tend to define the school-level
socioeconomic condition, policymakers may need to prioritize providing disadvantaged schools with material resources, especially ICT resources and connectivity.
The World Bank Group (2020) report has adequately built a case for the need for
such policy intervention. This could be a possible point of involvement of various

Philippine stakeholders, which the Department of Education intends to tap (Department of Education, 2019).
Second, addressing socio-economic factors such as lack of material resources
may not necessarily translate to improving reading literacy. Researchers from developing countries have underscored the essential role of improvements in the day-today learning experience in optimizing academic outcomes (Ganimian & Murnane,
2016; Glewwe & Muralidharan, 2016). The predictive model suggested developing
students’ motivation and attitude towards reading and improving mastery experiences in reading as potential critical areas that policymakers may need to pursue
side-by-side learning resources. Perhaps the most critical area in the curriculum that
may need special attention is the acquisition of metacognitive learning strategies
and mastery experiences. It may also help to review not only how the curriculum
promotes reading literacy, but also how the curriculum facilitates motivation and
promotes positive attitudes and self-beliefs. Although several studies have suggested
that motivation and attitudes are malleable, it is crucial to consider that steering
them is not necessarily easy. For example, the self-concept of reading competence
is an outcome of reading engagement and motivation across the students’ formal
years of studies. Hence, DepEd’s K-12 curriculum review may need to examine how
learning experiences and interactions in school and classroom are designed to build
up the enjoyment of reading, a growth mindset, and mastery goals leading toward
positive self-beliefs which may further facilitate reading competence.
Third, our findings found several negative experiences in Philippine schools
impacting students’ reading performance corroborating the World Bank report
(2020) on the status of Philippine school climate. Alarmingly, “stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination” was found to be one of the highest predictors of reading achievement. Twenty percent (20%) of the sample reported they had repeated
a grade at least once. Moreover, about 68% are above the OECD mean by more
than one standard deviation in terms of experience with bullying. These numbers
go against the government’s current policies on retention and child protection policy (Department of Education, 2012a, 2012b ). Given that the management of the
Philippine education system is still largely centralized (Bernardo & Garcia, 2006;
de Guzman, 2007), consistency in the implementation of the DepEd’s retention and
promotion policy as well as zero-tolerance for bullying in various school regions
may need to be revisited.
Although our study did not highlight the role of teachers, implementing intervention strategies to optimize the students’ personal characteristic and proximal processes are primarily the teacher’s responsibility (OECD, 2020; e.g., Ma et al., 2021;
Wang et al., 2023b; Yan et al., 2021). Moreover, schools also depend on teachers to
ensure classroom and school climate. However, most Philippine teachers reported
that handling the learning environment is one of their weakest competency. This
weakness is not adequately addressed by the typical massive training for Philippine
teachers (World Bank Group, 2016). Furthermore, these trainings typically focus on
content knowledge and teaching skills (San Antonio et al., 2011). Hence, this could
be an avenue where teachers could be upskilled and updated (Department of Education, 2019; World Bank Group, 2020).

It is important to clarify that the above evidence was based on cross-sectional
data for which causal relationships cannot be established. Another crucial piece of
information that needs clarification is that Philippine participants’ reading performance in PISA 2018 was below the OECD average and ranked the lowest. Our evidence provided information about within-country predictors that may enable or disable reading performance based on this performance. This critical information had to
be considered in interpreting our findings. Hence, the implications drawn from the
evidence were tentative and, at best, exploratory. We encourage the readers to confer with other countries/regions to understand what could optimize the Philippines’
reading outcomes completely. For example, a top predictor of reading achievement
across all PISA 2018 participants is identifying the credibility of sources as a metacognitive strategy (Gamazo & Martínez-Abad, 2020). Researchers and policymakers could use this information, considering differences in the education system and
economy, to further reflect on what could optimize Philippine reading achievement
and outcomes.

5 Conclusion
5.1 Contributions
Our study presented two notable contributions. First, the current research is one of
the few studies that used 2018 PISA Philippine data to provide empirical insights for
research and policymaking in the Philippines. Prior studies that used the PISA data
have selected few variables based on a limited set of theoretical frameworks (e.g.,
Chiu & Chow, 2015; Haw et al., 2021; Hopson & Lee, 2011; Yu & Zhao, 2021).
From a policymaking perspective, these studies are limited as they do not provide
a forest-level view of the substantive predictors. Informed by Bronfenbrenner’s bioecological framework, our study is perhaps one of the few that systematically and
comprehensively investigated the complex combination of personal characteristics,
proximal processes, and contextual variables that may interact with each other and
simultaneously influence Filipino adolescents’ reading achievement.
Second, a key feature of our study was the use of a machine learning algorithm in
identifying important predictors of reading achievement. As mentioned earlier, one
challenge in using PISA data is the large number of variables that could not be easily handled by classical statistics. To address this, researchers maximize the model’s
parsimony by selecting a few variables. However, doing so limits the accuracy of the
model as it excludes other variables that may have potential substantive influence
on the outcome. The use of machine learning algorithms allowed us to consider as
many variables as possible using the PISA dataset.
Another methodological contribution that emerged from our study is the potential complementarity of the machine learning approach with traditional statistics. Despite its known advantages, a clear trade-off in using the machine learning
approach is its interpretability. The absence of statistical estimates could render the
interpretation of machine learning algorithms challenging, especially for those who
are not yet familiar with the approach. Our HLM analysis provided supplementary

13

information that could mitigate this challenge. However, the machine learning algorithm was also able to complement our HLM analysis by removing potential noise
in PISA’s complex data and drawing a parsimonious model that traditional statistics
are designed to handle. Researchers may leverage this complementarity to introduce
methodological refinements in future studies (Wang et al., 2022, 2023a).
Our study extended the empirical literature that underpins the bioecological framework of human development from the perspective of reading literacy.
Moreover, it underscored the utility of the machine learning approach in answering research questions involving high- and multi-dimensional data. By situating our
study in the Philippines, our study extended the research literature in bioecological
models and machine learning research in non-WEIRD contexts, which are still relatively under-represented.
5.2 Caveats, limitations, and directions for future research
We acknowledge several limitations in our current study. First, the cross-sectional
nature of the PISA dataset prevented us from making any causal conclusions. This
also prevented us from fully capitalizing on Bronfenbrenner’s PPCT model, which is
more appropriate for longitudinal studies that take temporality into account (Tudge
et al., 2016). To address these constraints, we tried to avoid using language that may
imply causation. Future researchers could conduct experimental and longitudinal
studies.
Second, our study is specific to the reading context. This was because PISA
2018 focused on the reading domain. Hence, our results may not be generalizable
to mathematics and science, which could be assessed in future PISA cycles. We also
acknowledge that our study relied on the variables measured by PISA. Subsequent
studies may extend our findings by adding other potential predictors in the model.
We are aware of the criticisms against PISA on methodological and epistemological-philosophical grounds (Araujo et al., 2017; Zhao, 2020). However, despite these
limitations, PISA remains to be one of the largest and most international among educational assessments. Many countries which are typically absent from the scholarly
literature on education participate in PISA. Hence, PISA could serve as a valuable
source of information for these economies. We recommend more in-depth explorations of the PISA dataset across different countries, particularly in lower middleincome economies. Qualitative studies that would complement quantitative perspectives are also needed to develop a more complete picture of the different factors that
could play a role in their reading achievement.
5.3 Summary
Little systematic research has been conducted on Philippine adolescents’ reading
achievement, partly because of the scarcity of high-quality data. In this study, we
leveraged the public availability of the PISA dataset to understand the roles of various personal characteristics, proximal processes, and contextual factors in predicting achievement. Given the Philippines’ dismal performance in PISA, educators and

high concentration of less advantaged students, designing learning experiences that
