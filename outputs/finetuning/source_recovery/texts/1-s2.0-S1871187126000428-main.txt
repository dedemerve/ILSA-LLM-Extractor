The relationship between creativity and emotional health is multifaceted, with evidence suggesting both positive and negative associations. To better understand these complex patterns, this
study considers openness, a key personality trait closely related to creativity, as an explanatory
factor linking creative competence and emotional outcomes. Using data from the 2022 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), this study examined potential curvilinear
relationships between creativity competence (creative thinking test scores and self-efficacy in
creative thinking) and emotional health (emotional regulation and psychosomatic symptoms),
exploring how different facets of openness might explain these complex associations. The results
revealed a potential U-shaped relationship between creative thinking scores and psychosomatic
symptoms, with both high and low creativity levels associated with increased symptoms, while
emotional regulation showed no obvious curvilinear patterns. Further investigation of openness, a
key personality trait associated with creativity, revealed the opposing effects of its two facets:
Openness to intellect positively predicted emotional health, while openness to art showed
negative associations. These relationships were moderated by both gender and cultural individualism, with males and individuals from individualistic cultures exhibiting stronger negative
emotional outcomes associated with openness to art. These findings enhance our understanding
of the creativity–emotion relationship by highlighting the distinct roles of two facets of openness
and the importance of individual and cultural contexts in shaping these associations. They also
offer nuanced implications for education and adolescent development, emphasizing that fostering
creativity should be accompanied by cultivating emotional well-being within supportive learning
environments.

1. Introduction
Creativity has long been recognized as having a complex and nuanced relationship with emotional health. On the one hand,
extensive research has highlighted the benefits of creative engagement, showing that creative activities can enhance positive
emotional states, alleviate stress, and support psychological resilience (Acar et al., 2020; Conner et al., 2016; Fiori et al., 2022; Puig
et al., 2006). Consequently, creative expression is widely utilized in therapeutic interventions aimed at promoting mental health and
emotional regulation (Jensen et al., 2016; Khalil & Demarin, 2024; Leckey, 2011; Williams et al., 2018). On the other hand, creativity
has been associated with heightened psychological vulnerability, particularly among highly creative individuals. Empirical studies
have indicated elevated rates of emotional distress and mental disorders among eminent artists and writers, giving rise to the

well-known mad genius hypothesis (Liu et al., 2025). This apparent paradox underscores the need for more nuanced research into the
conditions under which creativity promotes or undermines emotional health. The personality trait most closely linked to creative
thinking is openness (Dollinger et al., 2004), which provides a potentially important lens for understanding why creativity sometimes
supports well-being and at other times increases vulnerability.
This relationship becomes especially significant during adolescence—a developmental stage marked by rapid cognitive, emotional,
and social changes alongside increased susceptibility to psychological distress (Clarke et al., 2021; Nightingale & Fischhoff, 2002).
Adolescence represents a critical period in which both creativity and emotional health play pivotal roles in determining personal
development, academic achievement, and future life trajectories (De Mézerville, 2019; Morrish et al., 2017). However, inconsistencies
in the literature regarding the impact of creativity on emotional health, as well as the limited understanding of the mechanisms driving
these divergent outcomes, highlight the need for further research.
To address these gaps, the current study uses data from the 2022 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a largescale international dataset representing 15-year-old students from 81 countries and regions. This representative sample allows for a

of the creative personality trait—specifically openness—relate to adolescents’ emotional health. This study distinguishes between two
facets of openness—openness to intellect and openness to art—and clarifies their distinct associations with emotional outcomes.
Furthermore, it explores the moderating effects of gender and cultural contexts (individualism vs. collectivism) on these relationships,
providing a more refined understanding of how demographic and cultural factors influence the creativity–emotion link.
2. Literature review
2.1. Positive relationship between creativity and emotional well-being
Extensive empirical research has supported the notion that creativity generally fosters positive psychological outcomes, significantly enhancing emotional health. Creative engagement has been consistently associated with increased positive affect, reduced
stress, improved resilience, and enhanced adaptive coping strategies (Acar et al., 2020; Conner et al., 2016; Fiori et al., 2022). Acar
et al.’s (2020) meta-analysis found a moderate positive correlation (r = 0.22) between creative behaviors and emotional well-being,
suggesting that routine creative activities—ranging from leisure pursuits (e.g., cooking and crafting) to structured problem-solving
tasks—can boost positive emotions, life satisfaction, flourishing, and personal growth (Conner et al., 2016). For instance, creativity
has been shown to buffer stress and enhance resilience during challenging periods, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, by encouraging
positive emotional states (Fiori et al., 2022). Thus, creativity may serve as a protective psychological resource, transforming stressors
into opportunities for adaptive personal development (Simonton, 2000).
Moreover, creative activities have demonstrated therapeutic value through their capacity to facilitate emotional regulation.
Fancourt et al. (2019) categorized emotional regulation strategies in artistic creative activities into three broad types—avoidance,
approach, and self-development—each contributing positively to mood stabilization and mental health. In particular, artistic
expression, which has proven effective in managing emotional distress, is being increasingly integrated into therapeutic interventions,
either as adjunctive clinical therapies or as recommended daily activities (Bungay & Clift, 2010; Jensen et al., 2016; Williams et al.,
2018). For instance, creative arts therapies have effectively improved emotional health among breast cancer patients by alleviating
negative emotions and fostering positive experiences (Puig et al., 2006). Similarly, expressive writing activities, such as blogging, have
been reported to enhance emotional health by providing an outlet for creativity, self-expression, and emotional release (Hibsch &
Mason, 2020).
The positive emotional impact of creativity can also be understood through its role in promoting self-actualization and personal
fulfillment. According to Maslow’s (1967) theory of self-actualization, engaging in creative expression is integral to realizing an individual’s full potential, thus fostering a sense of purpose and psychological fulfillment. In support of this perspective, Amabile et al.’s
(2005) organizational research demonstrated a reciprocal relationship wherein creativity promotes positive affect, while positive
emotions further enhance creative performance. Additionally, creativity has been linked to long-term health benefits, such as
improved resilience and increased longevity. For example, Turiano et al. (2012) observed that older adults with higher creativity levels
experienced significantly reduced mortality risk, suggesting creativity’s potential as a protective factor for overall health and
well-being.
2.2. The duality of creativity and emotional health
Despite substantial evidence highlighting the beneficial impacts of creativity on emotional health, researchers have acknowledged
a more complex, dual relationship (Abraham, 2014). For the majority of individuals in the general population, differences in creative
thinking are meaningfully related to emotional health: Those with lower levels of creativity tend to report poorer well-being, whereas
engaging in creative activities can enhance individuals’ creativity and, in turn, promote greater psychological resilience and life
satisfaction (Fiori et al., 2022). Thus, for most people, cultivating creativity through active participation is generally beneficial for their
mental health (Hansen et al., 2023). However, the mad genius hypothesis proposes that highly creative individuals, particularly
eminent artists and writers, may simultaneously experience elevated vulnerability to psychological disorders, including mood disorders, schizophrenia spectrum conditions, and substance abuse (Carson, 2011; Jamison, 1989; Liu et al., 2025). Jamison’s (1989)
influential research found that prominent creative individuals often demonstrated peak productivity during episodes of emotional
instability, with a substantial proportion undergoing clinical treatments for affective disorders.

However, it is critical to note that the mad genius hypothesis primarily addresses highly eminent, exceptional creators, whose
experiences may not be broadly generalized across the population of creative individuals (Simonton, 2019). Furthermore, the relationship between creativity and psychopathology appears to be highly domain-specific: Artists and writers, for example, are at higher
risk of psychological distress than scientists or mathematicians (Simonton, 2014a). Simonton (2014b) further argued that the relationship between creativity and well-being is contingent upon measurement methods, the creativity domain, and the level of creative
achievement considered.
Synthesizing these perspectives, recent work has proposed a curvilinear relationship whereby well-being tends to be highest at
moderate levels of creativity, while both low and exceptionally high levels of creativity are associated with an increased likelihood of
psychopathological symptoms (Abraham, 2014; Acar et al., 2017; Kinney & Richards, 2014). This curvilinear hypothesis offers a
theoretical reconciliation of the conflicting findings involving both positive and negative associations. Consequently, the current study
adopts this nuanced perspective, hypothesizing that while creativity positively relates to emotional health among most individuals,
this relationship may become negative at exceptionally high levels of creativity.
Supporting this complexity, findings from nonclinical populations have further indicated divergent relationships between creativity and psychological distress. Baas et al.’s (2016) meta-analysis revealed a weak, negative correlation between creativity and
depressive mood together with a stronger positive association between creativity and risk of bipolar disorder. These findings suggest
that even within normal-range populations, creativity’s emotional impact is multifaceted, warranting careful consideration.
2.3. Emotional health: functional and outcomes dimensions
Emotional health is a multifaceted concept that includes emotional regulation strategies and the outcomes of emotional distress.
Researchers on adolescent emotional health have often highlighted two distinct dimensions: functional emotional health, measured by
emotional regulation, and emotional health outcomes, indicated by psychosomatic symptoms. Emotional regulation has been defined
as the ability to effectively manage and respond to emotional experiences, particularly in the face of stress or frustration (Nyklíček
et al., 2010, 1–9; Organisation for Economic Co-operation & Development [OECD], 2024a).
Emotions inherently serve adaptive functions, guiding attention, motivating action, and facilitating individuals’ navigation of
challenging environments (Oatley, 1998). In adolescence, emotional regulation assumes critical developmental importance because of
the numerous emotional and social challenges that adolescents encounter. Effective emotional regulation enables adolescents to
manage stress proactively, cope constructively with anger, and maintain psychological balance and composure during frustrating
situations. These skills are foundational not only for immediate psychological health but also for long-term social and occupational
effectiveness (OECD, 2019). Empirical studies have consistently linked effective emotional regulation strategies to positive mental
health outcomes, underscoring emotional regulation’s pivotal role in adolescent psychological development (Hu et al., 2014).
Conversely, difficulties in emotional regulation have been shown to mediate the relationship between perceived stress and subsequent
psychosomatic symptoms, highlighting the integral role that emotional regulation plays in overall emotional well-being (Teixeira
et al., 2021).
The outcomes of emotional health are reflected in psychosomatic symptoms that represent physical manifestations of underlying
emotional distress. Psychosomatic symptoms commonly experienced by adolescents include recurrent headaches, abdominal pain,
fatigue, and other somatic complaints directly related to psychological stressors and emotional dysregulation (Kelly et al., 2010;
Kinnunen et al., 2010). Such symptoms are particularly concerning because of their significant impacts on adolescents’ daily functioning, quality of life, and long-term mental health (Kohlmann et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2017).
Psychosomatic symptoms often serve as critical indicators of deeper emotional or psychological difficulties, even though adolescents—or their caregivers—may not immediately explicitly recognize these underlying issues (Santalahti et al., 2005). Research has
indicated that psychosomatic symptoms in adolescents can be exacerbated by adverse social experiences, such as bullying, and that
they frequently impose considerable burdens on both individuals and health services (Kelly et al., 2010; Natvig et al., 2001). Given
their clear implications for adolescent mental health and development, psychosomatic symptoms provide a meaningful and practically
significant indicator of the outcomes of overall emotional health (Bauer et al., 2005; Li et al., 2020).
2.4. Creative personality: openness
It is possible that third variables may affect the relationship between risk of mental illnesses and creativity (Baas et al., 2016;
Damian & Simonton, 2015). In particular, creative personality traits, such as openness, are suspected to influence both creative
competence and emotional health. This dual influence highlights the need to examine how the structure of openness contributes to
both creativity and emotional health, shedding light on why creativity has been linked to both positive and negative emotional
outcomes.
Openness, a core dimension of the Big Five personality traits, has consistently been identified as the trait most strongly associated
with creativity (Dollinger et al., 2004; Ostendorf & Angleitner, 1994). This link has been well documented across various domains,
including both artistic and scientific creativity (DeYoung, 2014; Kaufman et al., 2014). Openness encompasses a broad range of
characteristics, including imagination, curiosity, aesthetic sensitivity, and a preference for novelty and variety, making it a central
construct in understanding creative personality (Feist, 1998; Lubart et al., 2013). However, it is the nuanced structure of this trait that
can enable critical insights into its complex relationship with creativity and emotional well-being. This paper focuses on two distinct
but correlated facets of openness: openness to art and openness to intellect.
The facet of openness to art reflects cognitive engagement with perception, fantasy, aesthetics, and emotions (DeYoung et al.,

2012). This facet aligns closely with artistic creativity, as it emphasizes experiential and sensory processes, such as imagination,
curiosity, and receptivity to novel ideas (Kaufman et al., 2014). By contrast, openness to intellect represents cognitive engagement
with abstract and semantic information, primarily through reasoning and analytical thought (DeYoung, 2014). This facet is more
closely associated with scientific creativity, as it emphasizes logical reasoning and divergent thinking. Together, these facets reflect a
dual-process framework. Artistic creativity tends to draw more heavily on the experiential and associative processes linked to
openness, such as perceptual, aesthetic, and implicit learning mechanisms, whereas scientific creativity relies more on the deliberate
and analytical processing associated with intellect and divergent thought (Kaufman et al., 2014).
Given this structural complexity, openness likely plays a pivotal role in explaining the intricate relationship between creativity and
emotional health. This differentiation helps clarify why prior studies have reported both positive and negative associations. Openness
to art, with its emphasis on emotional and sensory engagement, may heighten individuals’ susceptibility to emotional instability,
particularly among artistic populations in which intense affective responses are more common (Baas et al., 2016). Conversely,
openness to intellect, with its focus on analytical reasoning, may serve as a buffer against emotional distress by facilitating cognitive
regulation and problem-solving. This distinction aligns with evidence suggesting that artistic creativity is more likely to be associated
with depressive mood, whereas scientific creativity may have neutral or even positive associations with emotional health (Baas et al.,
2016; Kaufman et al., 2014).
Moreover, the possibility of these facets having opposing impacts on emotional health provides a plausible explanation of the
inconsistent findings regarding the overall relationship between openness and subjective well-being (Hayes & Joseph, 2003). While
previous studies have shown that personality traits, including openness, account for substantial variance in well-being (DeNeve &
Cooper, 1998), the lack of a significant direct relationship between openness and well-being in some studies may have resulted from
the offsetting effects of these two facets of openness. For instance, the positive influence of openness to intellect on well-being could be
counterbalanced by the potentially negative influence of openness to art, resulting in a net-zero association at the aggregate level.
Gender and cultural contexts may act as critical moderators in the relationship between openness and emotional well-being.
Research has consistently shown significant gender differences in emotional experience, regulation, and creativity. For instance,
women tend to engage more deeply with emotional experiences, making them more susceptible to the emotional fluctuations associated with artistic creativity, while men often rely on cognitive control and rational problem-solving, which are more aligned with
scientific creativity (Kaufman, 2001; Kaufman et al., 2010; Roothman et al., 2003).
Evidence from PISA 2022 has further highlighted how openness to art is a highly gendered trait, with girls scoring significantly
higher than boys across OECD countries (OECD, 2024a). Similarly, cultural dimensions, such as individualism and collectivism,
substantially influence emotional expression and creativity. Individualistic cultures, which emphasize self-expression and emotional
authenticity, may amplify the emotional demands of creative pursuits, while collectivistic cultures, which prioritize emotional restraint and social harmony, may better support the regulation of emotional challenges (Mesquita, 2001; Triandis, 2018). These
theoretical and empirical insights underscore the importance of considering gender and cultural orientation as potential contextual
moderators in understanding the complex interplay between creativity, openness, and emotional health.
2.5. The present study
While the relationship between creativity and emotional health has been widely examined in previous research (e.g., Abraham,
2014; Hansen et al., 2023; Kinney & Richards, 2014), the findings remain mixed, indicating complex dynamics involving both positive
and negative associations. Adolescence represents a crucial developmental stage, making it essential to investigate how creativity
relates to adolescents’ emotional health at a population level; however, empirical evidence based on large, cross‑cultural adolescent
samples remains scarce. Furthermore, while openness has been established as a key personality foundation of creativity, the distinct
influences of the two correlated facets of openness examined in this study, openness to art and openness to intellect, on emotional
health have not been systematically examined. The current study addresses these gaps by drawing on the PISA 2022 results—a large,
representative dataset concerning adolescents.
To better understand these dynamics, the present study examines the two facets of openness and their distinct roles in the
creativity-emotion relationship. By distinguishing openness to art from openness to intellect, we aim to clarify their differential
contributions to the emotional consequences of creativity. Specifically, we hypothesize that whether creativity promotes or hinders
emotional health depends on the relative influence of these two facets of openness. Building on this foundation, this study further
examines whether gender and cultural orientation (individualism vs. collectivism) moderate these associations, thereby illuminating
the contextual conditions under which creativity supports or undermines adolescents’ emotional health.
Based on the reviewed theoretical and empirical literature, we propose two hypotheses:
• H1: The relationship between creativity and emotional health is curvilinear, with adolescents who exhibit moderate levels of
creativity reporting higher emotional health compared to those with either low or high levels of creativity.
• H2: Openness to intellect is positively associated with emotional health, whereas openness to art is negatively associated with
emotional health. These associations may be moderated by gender or cultural (individualism vs. collectivism) contexts.

3. Method
3.1. Data
This study utilized data from PISA 2022 (available at https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/), which was administered by the OECD.
PISA assesses the cognitive performance and attitudes of 15-year-old students (N = 613,744) internationally. Creative thinking was the
innovative domain in PISA 2022, and 64 of the 81 participating countries and economies implemented the creative thinking assessment as part of their national study. The current investigation focused on data drawn from the PISA 2022 creative thinking assessment
and the associated student questionnaire, which included measures relating to students’ creativity-related beliefs, emotional regulation, and psychosomatic symptoms. The specific variables analyzed in this study are detailed below, and the corresponding questionnaire items are provided in the appendix.
3.2. Measurements
3.2.1. Creativity competence
Creative Thinking Test Scores. Creative thinking was operationally defined as “the competence to engage productively in the
generation, evaluation, and improvement of ideas that result in original and effective solutions, advances in knowledge, and impactful
expressions of imagination” (OECD, 2024a). In PISA 2022, students’ creative thinking was measured through open-ended tasks in four
domains: written expression, visual expression, social problem-solving, and scientific problem-solving (OECD, 2023). Responses were
scored by trained coders, with cross-country reliability checks. The rubrics focused on appropriateness, diversity of ideas, originality,
and improvement (OECD, 2023). To support reliable population-level inference and cross-country comparability, 10 plausible values
(PVs) were generated for each student’s creative thinking score. The precise content of the item is not publicly available, as PISA items
are protected to maintain test security. However, the OECD has released several sample items that illustrate the structure and format of
the creative thinking cognitive items (OECD, 2024a).
Creative Self-Efficacy. Students reported their confidence regarding their creative abilities through the PISA scale titled Creative
Thinking Self-Efficacy (Module ST334). This scale comprised 10 items evaluating students’ perceived abilities to engage creatively
across various tasks (e.g., “Coming up with creative ideas for school projects” and “Inventing new things”). Students responded on a 4point Likert scale ranging from “Not at all confident” to “Very confident.”
3.2.2. Creative personality: openness
Openness to Intellect. Students’ openness to intellectual and cognitive engagement with creativity was measured using the
Creativity and Openness to Intellect scale (Module ST340). This index included 10 items (e.g., “I like games that challenge my
creativity” and “I enjoy thinking about NEW ways to solve problems”), which were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (“Strongly disagree”
to “Strongly agree”).
Openness to Art. This dimension captured students’ aesthetic sensitivity and reflective openness through five items (e.g., “I enjoy
creating art” and “I reflect on movies I watch”) from the Openness to Art and Reflection scale (Module ST341). Responses employed a
4-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree.”
3.2.3. Emotional health
Emotional health was assessed through three constructs: two aspects of emotional regulation (stress resistance and emotional
control) and psychosomatic symptoms.
Stress Resistance. Students’ capacity to handle stress effectively was measured through the Stress Resistance scale (Module
ST345) consisting of 10 items (e.g., “I remain calm under stress” and “I get nervous easily”). Responses were provided on a 5-point
scale (“Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree”), with certain items reverse-coded prior to scaling.
Emotional Control. This construct assessed students’ abilities to regulate negative emotions, such as anger and frustration. The
Emotional Control scale (Module ST313) comprised 10 items (e.g., “I keep my emotions under control” and “I get mad easily”), which
were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (“Strongly disagree” to “Strongly agree”).
Psychosomatic Symptoms. Frequency of psychosomatic symptoms was measured using nine items (e.g., “Headache” and
“Stomach pain”) from Module WB154, with responses ranging from “Rarely or never” to “About every day” (5-point scale).
All the index values of self‑report constructs described above, including creative self‑efficacy, openness to intellect, openness to art,
and the three emotional health indices, were derived from students’ responses to the PISA 2022 context questionnaires. All questionnaire‑based indices were scaled by the OECD using official PISA 2022 procedures (OECD, 2024b). And item parameters were
estimated through multi‑group IRT calibration applying the generalized partial credit model (GPCM), with senate weighting (balanced
weighting ensuring equal contribution of each participating country) to ensure cross‑country comparability. The full set of OECD‑released item parameters and scaling documentation is available at https://stat.link/v6uq1n.
3.2.4. Background information
Gender. Student gender was coded dichotomously (0 = female, 1 = male).
Individualism–Collectivism. Cultural orientation at the national level was operationalized using Hofstede’s (2011) cultural
dimension of individualism, with country-specific data sourced from the Culture Factor Group (https://www.theculturefactor.com/
country-comparison-tool). The index ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating individualistic societies that prioritize

independence, personal achievement, and self-interest and lower values indicating collectivistic societies that emphasize group
cohesion, loyalty, and interdependence. Country‑level scores for Brunei Darussalam, Kosovo, and Macao (China) were not available in
Hofstede’s dataset; therefore, these economies were excluded from analyses involving this indicator.
Covariates. Gender (coded 0 = female, 1 = male) and students’ socioeconomic background were included as control variables.
Socioeconomic status was represented by the Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS) index provided by PISA.
3.3. Data preprocessing
The three emotional health outcomes (emotional control, stress resistance, and psychosomatic symptoms) were administered as
optional modules in PISA 2022. Consequently, availability varied by country/economy for reasons inherent to the PISA survey design
rather than student item nonresponse. Valid responses were available for 430,289 students from 61 countries/economies for emotional
control, 292,466 students from 42 countries/economies for stress resistance, and 112,798 students from 14 countries/economies for
psychosomatic symptoms. Because these gaps reflected structural missingness by design (i.e., non-administration of an optional
module), we did not extrapolate outcomes for countries that did not field a given measure. At the same time, missingness concerning
predictor variables was generally limited (see Table 1), whereas outcome missingness was both substantial and structural. For this
reason, we based our missing-data strategy on outcome availability rather than on the availability of predictors.
For each outcome, we restricted analyses to the countries/economies that administered the questionnaire, and we conducted all
preprocessing and imputation within that subset. Within the outcome-specific domains, the remaining student-level missingness was
addressed using multiple imputation in SPSS 27 with fully conditional specification and predictive mean matching (five donors). The
imputation models were specified around the focal outcomes and included all variables used in the analyses, including predictors and
control variables, to preserve model congeniality and to maximize the use of the available information.
A total of 10 iterations were performed per imputation, and 10 complete datasets were generated. Each of the 10 PVs for the
creative thinking test was analyzed with one corresponding imputed dataset. The PISA student weight (W_FSTUWT) was included as an
analysis weight during imputation to maintain representativeness, ensuring that students or countries with larger samples proportionally contributed greater influence. The national individualism index was included as an auxiliary variable but was not imputed.
This approach accommodated both continuous and categorical variables and produced imputations that were consistent with the
survey’s design constraints.
3.4. Analyses
Five regression models were estimated to address the two hypotheses. Models 1–3 tested H1, which predicted a curvilinear association between creativity and emotional health. Model 1 served as the baseline specification in which the three emotional health
outcomes (stress resistance, emotional control, and psychosomatic symptoms) were regressed on creative thinking test scores and
creative self‑efficacy, controlling for gender and ESCS. Model 2 extended this baseline by adding quadratic terms for both creativity
variables to test the hypothesized U-shaped relationships. Model 3 further examined contextual variation by incorporating interaction
terms between each creativity variable and gender and national individualism, thereby testing whether the creativity–health link
differed across individual and cultural contexts.
Models 4 and 5 addressed H2, which focused on the distinct contributions of the two openness facets, openness to intellect and
openness to art, to emotional health and their moderation by gender and culture. Model 4 simultaneously included both openness
facets, controlling for gender and ESCS, to estimate their independent main effects on emotional health. Model 5 then added interactions between each openness facet and gender as well as cultural individualism to test whether these relationships varied between
individuals and across sociocultural contexts. Collectively, Models 4 and 5 offered a facet‑level analysis of openness and a test of the
contextual contingencies proposed in H2.

Before the substantive analyses, we examined the data hierarchy by computing null-model intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs)
for each emotional health outcome. Although the ICCs were relatively low (ranging from 0.001 to 0.002), we nonetheless employed
three-level multilevel regression models with students nested within schools and schools nested within countries. This decision followed Huang’s (2018) recommendation that multilevel modeling should not be bypassed simply because of low ICCs. The design
effect, which determines the inflation of standard errors due to clustering, depends not only on the ICC but also on the average cluster
size. With large cluster sizes typical of PISA data, even small ICCs can substantially inflate Type I error rates if the nested structure is
ignored. Accordingly, we adopted multilevel modeling to appropriately account for the hierarchical sampling design, with PISA
student weights (W_FSTUWT) incorporated to ensure population-representative estimates.
All continuous variables (e.g., creative thinking test score, creative self‑efficacy, both openness facets, ESCS, and emotional health
outcomes) were standardized to z‑scores to facilitate interpretation and comparability and mitigate multicollinearity in models
including interaction and quadratic terms (Enders & Tofighi, 2007). All interactions and polynomial terms were computed from these
standardized variables. Regression models were estimated using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) in R with its default estimation
method of restricted maximum likelihood (REML), first separately across all 10 imputed datasets and then pooled following Rubin’s
(1987) rules.

4. Results
4.1. Descriptive statistics and correlations
Table 1 summarizes the descriptive statistics and correlations among the key variables. Due to PISA’s matrix sampling design, the
sample sizes varied across measurement scales (see the sample sizes in Table 1). The correlation analysis revealed modest associations
between creative thinking test scores and both openness facets (openness to intellect, r = 0.106; openness to art, r = 0.050). By
contrast, creative self-efficacy showed stronger relationships with openness to intellect (r = 0.529) and openness to art (r = 0.394). The
correlations between creativity competence (test scores and creative self-efficacy) and emotional health indicators were generally
small. Notably, psychosomatic symptoms correlated weakly with creative thinking test scores (r = 0.051) and showed no significant
correlation with creative self-efficacy (r = .003).
The regression results are summarized in Table 2. In Model 1, creative self‑efficacy positively predicted both emotional control (β =
0.076) and stress resistance (β = 0.102) and negatively predicted psychosomatic symptoms (β = − 0.030). By contrast, creative
thinking test scores showed weak linear effects: non‑significant for emotional control, negative for stress resistance (β = − 0.023), and
positive for psychosomatic symptoms (β = 0.027).
Model 2 introduced the quadratic terms to assess nonlinear associations. The quadratic effects of creative self‑efficacy were small
and inconsistent across outcomes, indicating predominantly linear relations across outcomes. By contrast, the quadratic terms of test
scores were significant for emotional control (β = 0.012), stress resistance (β = 0.008), and psychosomatic symptoms (β = 0.025),
supporting an U‑shaped pattern between creative performance and emotional health indicators.
Model 3 examined the moderating roles of gender and national individualism. The interaction effects were primarily observed for
creative thinking test scores. Gender (coded 0 = female, 1 = male) significantly moderated the associations of test scores with
emotional control (β = 0.050) and stress resistance (β = 0.029), suggesting stronger benefits of higher creative performance for male
students with respect to these positive outcomes. Individualism also moderated the associations of test scores with emotional control (β
= 0.030) and psychosomatic symptoms (β = − 0.020), indicating that higher cultural individualism enhanced the positive link between
creativity and emotional well‑being.
To examine the nonlinear relationships between creative thinking and emotional health, we plotted emotional indicators against
creativity levels. Creative test scores were divided into 100 percentiles, and average scores for emotional control, stress resistance, and
psychosomatic symptoms were calculated for each percentile. The third chart in Fig. 1 shows evidence of a U-shaped relationship
between creative thinking test scores and psychosomatic symptoms: Students with moderate creativity levels reported fewer psychosomatic symptoms than those with lower or higher creativity scores. However, no strong U-shaped patterns were observed for the
emotional regulation indicators of emotional control and stress resistance (see the first and second charts in Fig. 1).
4.2. Dual impacts of openness to creativity

intellect positively predicted emotional control (β = 0.080) and stress resistance (β = 0.115) and negatively predicted psychosomatic
symptoms (β = − 0.030). By contrast, openness to art negatively predicted emotional control (β = − 0.036) and stress resistance (β =
− 0.090) and positively predicted psychosomatic symptoms (β = 0.081). These patterns indicated divergent emotional correlates of
aesthetic versus intellectual openness.
Model 5 examined potential moderators. Gender significantly interacted with openness to art. Being male amplified its negative
associations with emotional control (β = − 0.037) and stress resistance (β = − 0.029) and its positive association with psychosomatic
symptoms (β = 0.043), indicating stronger adverse emotional outcomes among males high in openness to art. For openness to intellect,
gender significantly moderated its association with stress resistance (β = 0.023), whereas the interactions with emotional control and
psychosomatic symptoms were not significant.

5. Discussion
5.1. The complicated relationship between creativity and emotional well-being
Our findings provided partial support for the hypothesized curvilinear relationship between creativity and emotional health.
Specifically, the analysis revealed a potential U-shaped relationship between creative test scores and psychosomatic symptoms,
indicating that students with either very low or very high creativity levels experienced more psychosomatic symptoms than those with
moderate creativity levels. By contrast, creative self‑efficacy, the belief in one’s creative capability, displayed a predominantly linear
and positive relationship with emotional health. The divergent patterns between the two indicators of creative competence may have
partly stemmed from the way in which creativity was assessed: objective test performance versus self‑reported belief.
The objective indicator, creative thinking test scores, revealed a clear quadratic relationship with emotional health. Moderate
levels of creative performance were linked to positive emotional health, whereas both very low and very high creative test scores were
associated with heightened psychosomatic symptoms. This pattern suggested that moderate creative engagement may promote
cognitive flexibility and a sense of mastery that supports emotional balance. By contrast, striving for excessive creative may become
psychologically taxing: The cognitive effort, uncertainty, and evaluative pressure inherent in high‑level creative thinking may deplete
an individual’s emotional resources and induce stress, reflecting the well‑recognized double‑edged sword of creativity (Chen et al.,
2024).
Creative self‑efficacy, the belief in one’s creative capacity, showed predominantly linear associations with emotional health. Unlike
actual performance, self‑efficacy reflects a stable cognitive appraisal rather than the emotional intensity of creative engagement.
Consistent with self‑determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000), higher perceived efficacy fostered competence and control, leading to
steady emotional benefits rather than curvilinear fluctuations. Methodological factors may have contributed to this difference. Because
creative self‑efficacy and emotional health were measured concurrently through self‑reports, shared method variance and insufficient
effort in responding may have attenuated the nonlinear patterns (DeSimone et al., 2018; Meade & Craig, 2012; Wang & Hau, 2024).
Given the 2‑h duration of the testing session for the PISA assessment, response fatigue may have encouraged overly consistent, linear
answers, partly explaining why the expected quadratic form was less evident for creative self‑efficacy than for objective creative
performance.

Moreover, the varying strengths of the curvilinear relationships between creative test scores and different aspects of emotional
health can be attributed to their distinct characteristics. As a process-oriented dimension, emotional regulation showed a weaker
curvilinear relationship compared to psychosomatic symptoms. This aligned with Baas et al.’s (2016) findings that creativity demonstrates varying associations with different psychological outcomes, ranging from weak negative correlations with depressive mood
to stronger positive associations with risk of bipolar disorder. In our study, while psychosomatic symptoms represented concrete,
outcome-focused indicators of emotional distress, emotional regulation reflected an internal, ongoing coping process. This fundamental difference may have partly accounted for the preliminary indication of a U‑shaped pattern between creativity and psychosomatic symptoms; however, this interpretation should be viewed as tentative, warranting further examination in future studies.
5.2. Divergent roles of openness: cognitive protection and emotional vulnerability
Our study further clarified the complex role of the Big Five personality trait of openness in adolescent emotional health by distinguishing specifically between two of its key facets: openness to intellect and openness to art (DeYoung, 2014; Kaufman et al., 2014).
Consistent with prior research, we found that these two aspects exerted distinct—and sometimes opposing—effects on emotional
health outcomes. Openness to intellect, involving cognitive exploration and intellectual curiosity, positively predicted emotional
regulation and negatively predicted psychosomatic symptoms. This aligned with evidence linking intellectual openness to cognitive
flexibility, problem-solving skills, and effective emotional coping (DeYoung et al., 2009; Nusbaum & Silvia, 2011). Therefore,
openness to intellect may offer protective cognitive resources, enhancing emotional stability and resilience.
Conversely, openness to art—characterized by emotional sensitivity, aesthetic appreciation, and heightened imagination—was
associated negatively with emotional regulation and positively with psychosomatic symptoms. These results supported existing evidence linking this trait to emotional intensity, increased psychological vulnerability, depression, and positive schizotypy (DeYoung
et al., 2012; Eldesouky, 2012). Artistic openness, while fostering creativity and originality, thus appeared to elevate susceptibility to
emotional distress.
This dual-facet model of openness provides a robust theoretical explanation for the potential U-shaped creativity–psychosomatic
symptoms relationship observed in our study. Students who were low in creativity because of low intellectual openness might have
lacked essential cognitive resources for emotional coping, while highly creative students who were rich in openness to art might have
experienced greater emotional reactivity, with both scenarios potentially increasing psychosomatic symptoms. This interpretation
aligns closely with DeYoung’s (2014) paradoxical simplex structure that positions intellect and apophenia at opposite ends of cognitive
exploration, with each possessing distinct emotional implications.
Our findings also revealed the moderating effects of gender and individualistic cultural orientation on the relationship between
openness to art and emotional health outcomes. Specifically, male students and students from individualistic cultural backgrounds
experienced worse emotional regulation and more psychosomatic symptoms when exhibiting high openness to art. Moreover, they
experienced stronger positive effects of openness to intellect on emotional regulation.
These patterns may reflect the socialization of creativity and emotional expression across genders and culture. Previous research
has shown that males and individuals from individualistic cultures are generally more encouraged to demonstrate creative thinking
and independent expression than females and those from collectivistic backgrounds (Goncalo & Staw, 2006; Paek & Bao, 2023). In
South Korea, for example, teachers tend to perceive creative boys as independent and agentic, whereas creative girls are valued for being
polite and compliant (Paek & Bao, 2023). This difference may partly be explained by the confidence gap whereby males often overestimate and females often underestimate their creative performance (Vieira et al., 2025). While such gendered and cultural expectations may foster creative risk‑taking and openness among males and individualists, they can also create psychological tension. For
males, creative expression is often encouraged as a form of self‑assertion, yet they are simultaneously expected to remain rational,
competitive, and emotionally controlled, reflecting the masculine socialization process described by Pleck (1995).
Similarly, in individualistic contexts in which uniqueness and self‑expression are valued, individuals may experience feelings of
isolation or psychological pressure when their creative pursuits receive limited social recognition or when competition is intense
(Hofstede, 2011). These conflicting demands between expressing creativity and managing emotions may help explain why high
openness to art is associated with poorer emotional regulation and more psychosomatic symptoms in these groups.
5.3. Moderate safety and the price of creativity
The potential U-shaped relationship between creative thinking test scores and psychosomatic symptoms revealed a provocative
pattern: Students with moderate creativity levels enjoyed a relative safety zone in terms of emotional health, reporting fewer psychosomatic symptoms compared to their lower- and higher-creativity peers. This finding challenges the assumption that creativity is an
unqualified good in education, suggesting instead that both low and exceptionally high creative competence may come with emotional
costs.
At moderate levels of creativity, emotional balance appeared to be optimized, likely because these students benefited from the
cognitive flexibility and problem-solving abilities associated with openness to intellect without the emotional vulnerabilities linked to
openness to art. By contrast, highly creative students who tended to exhibit elevated openness to art experienced intense emotional
sensitivity, heightened aesthetic engagement, and increased psychosomatic symptoms. This aligned with research suggesting that
while openness to art fosters originality, this often comes at a cost, including greater emotional intensity and susceptibility to mood
disorders (DeYoung, 2014; Eldesouky, 2012). Conversely, students with low creativity may lack the cognitive resources provided by
openness to intellect, which supports emotional control and stress resistance (DeYoung, 2014; Nusbaum & Silvia, 2011).

These findings invite a broader reflection on the nature of well-being, particularly in the context of creativity. They challenge the
simplistic notion that functioning well means feeling good (Ericsson, 2009) and highlight the tension between personal growth and
emotional comfort. The psychosomatic symptoms experienced by talented students in our study reflected the no pain, no gain nature of
creative development. This parallels findings in expertise research in which activities crucial for developing exceptional skills across
domains—from music to chess—are often reported as the least enjoyable (Ericsson et al., 1993; Vittersø et al., 2009). Similarly, the
emotional toll observed in highly creative students suggests that exceptional abilities may require sacrifices in emotional well-being,
raising the question of how to support these individuals without stifling their potential.
5.4. Implications for education and adolescent development
The findings of this study yield several actionable recommendations for educators, school psychologists, and policymakers.
First, the findings suggest that high levels of creativity may carry emotional costs. Rather than aiming to maximize creativity for all
students, educators should differentiate between moderate and exceptional levels of creative performance. For students who already
demonstrate very high creativity, emotional monitoring and support should be integrated into creative learning activities to prevent
emotional overload while sustaining creative development.
Second, the results highlight the importance of distinguishing between different facets of openness in educational and psychological research. Openness to intellect appears to function as a cognitive resource that supports emotional regulation, whereas
openness to art is associated with greater emotional sensitivity. This distinction may be valuable not only for understanding the
relationship between creativity and emotional health, but also for research in other domains where openness plays a role.
Third, educators should be attentive to gender and cultural differences in how creativity relates to emotional health. For male
students and those from individualistic cultural backgrounds, normalizing emotional challenges associated with creative engagement
and reducing excessive evaluative pressure may be particularly beneficial. Creating classroom climates that value emotional wellbeing alongside creative expression could help mitigate the emotional costs observed in these groups.
At last, the consistent positive association between creative self-efficacy and emotional health underscores the importance of
fostering students’ belief in their creative capabilities. Unlike objective creative performance, creative self-efficacy showed stable
emotional benefits without evidence of emotional costs. Educational practices that emphasize constructive feedback, mastery experiences, and confidence in creative ability may therefore support students’ emotional well-being while encouraging continued creative
engagement.
5.5. Limitations and future directions
Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, while the PISA dataset provides a large and diverse sample that enables valid
population‑level inferences across educational systems, its design does not permit clear conclusions at the individual student level.
This is because PISA primarily targets system‑level evaluation rather than measuring individuals per se. Future research should
therefore examine the relationships among creativity, openness, and emotional health through different lenses, using other large‑scale
assessments or smaller‑scale studies specifically designed to capture individual differences and developmental mechanisms. In
addition, experimental approaches may complement such efforts by providing a more dynamic understanding of these associations.
Second, because the present study is based on cross‑sectional data, it cannot address potential reciprocal (bidirectional) relationships between creativity and emotional health. While our analyses focused on the direction from creativity to emotional health,
future studies employing longitudinal designs or structural equation modeling (SEM) are needed to test reciprocal effects more
rigorously.
Third, while our creative competence measures included both objective test scores and self-reported efficacy, the assessment of
emotional well-being relied primarily on self-report measures, which may have been subject to social desirability bias and cultural
response styles. Future research would benefit from incorporating multiple assessment methods, including behavioral observations
and physiological indicators.
Finally, given that our sample consisted of adolescents, the generalizability of our findings to other age groups remains uncertain.
Future research should examine whether similar patterns exist across different developmental stages and populations.
6. Conclusion
This study provides large‑scale evidence that the relationship between creativity and adolescents’ emotional health is complex and
multifaceted. This study found preliminary evidence of a U‑shaped association between creative thinking performance and psychosomatic symptoms, suggesting that both very low and very high levels of creativity may be linked to increased emotional problems,
whereas individuals with moderate levels of creativity appear most adaptive.
Examining the personality foundations of these patterns, we observed that openness to intellect served as a cognitive protector,
promoting emotional health, whereas openness to art emerged as an emotional risk factor, related to heightened psychosomatic
symptoms and reduced emotional regulation. The contrasting roles of these openness facets help explain why creativity may contribute
to both emotional enrichment and vulnerability. Furthermore, gender and cultural individualism shaped these dynamics, intensifying
the emotional costs of openness to art, particularly among males and individuals from more individualistic societies.
