The role of information and communication technology (ICT) in adolescent development is considered a doubleedged sword because it can both meet the needs of adolescents and cause potential damage to them. Previous
studies primarily relied on variable-centered approaches and failed to reveal the heterogeneity among groups
concerning problematic technology use and ICT literacy. This study employed a person-centered approach to
identify distinct subgroups and examined their associations with smartphone use content types. Using a longitudinal design, we investigated 1275 Chinese adolescents (Mage = 14.22 years, SD = 1.23) twice in two years.
Latent class analysis and regression mixture model were implemented. Six classes were identified. Less use of
entertainment and more use of information-seeking and learning types of smartphone content a year earlier were
predictors of well-adjusted group membership. The heterogeneity of ICT use among adolescents found in this
study emphasizes the importance of personalized policy advice.

Introduction
With the rapid popularization of modern communication technology
worldwide, an increasing number of researchers point out the importance of effective use of information communication technology (ICT)
for adolescents to adapt to the future of society (van Laar, van Deursen,
van Dijk, & de Haan, 2017). Among many ICTs, smartphones are the
most popular devices for adolescents to connect to the Internet in the
United States and China (CNNIC, 2021; Rideout, Peebles, Mann, & Robb,
2022). The increasing reliance on ICT is seen by some as a price that
must be paid to adapt to the information society, and policymakers and
educators are often caught in the dilemma of restricting adolescents’ ICT
use to prevent problematic ICT use and encouraging ICT self-efficacy.
Although the Ministry of Education of China encourages primary and
secondary schools to construct digital campuses to improve students’
ICT skills, it prohibits students from bringing mobile smart devices such
as smartphones into the campus (Ministry of Education of China, 2018,

2021). Different from the contradictory situation in education policy, no
empirical studies have directly examined the relationship between
problematic smartphone use (PSU) and ICT self-efficacy, especially the
potential individual differences in the relationship between the two are
not clear. In this study, we used a person-centered approach to examine
the relationship between PSU and ICT self-efficacy among Chinese adolescents. Furthermore, we used a longitudinal design to test the predictive effect of adolescents’ smartphone use content types one year
earlier on the membership of PSU and ICT self-efficacy classes. This
study will help to elucidate the relationship between adolescents’ PSU
and ICT self-efficacy. Further, the identification of individual differences
in the relationship between PSU and ICT self-efficacy will facilitate the
development of more evidence-based and differentiated educational
policies (rather than simply restricting or encouraging ICT use without
guidance).

ICT self-efficacy

PSU and ICT self-efficacy

Self-efficacy refers to a belief in whether an individual’s ability can
achieve the desired goal (Bandura, 1977). ICT self-efficacy refers to a
belief in whether an individual can use ICT to solve problems and meet
needs (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Duckworth, & Friedman, 2020). For
adolescents, ICT self-efficacy is a significant predictor of their ICTrelated academic performance and ICT competences. Aesaert and colleagues proposed the extended digital competence model, positing ICT
self-efficacy as pupil-level characteristic that could improve students’
ICT competences (Aesaert, Van Braak, Van Nijlen, & Vanderlinde,
2015). Supporting this model, Grade 8 students’ ICT self-efficacy scores
were significantly and moderately correlated with their computer and
information literacy in the International Computer and Information
Literacy Study (ICILS) 2018 (Fraillon, Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, &
Duckworth, 2020). Additionally, adolescents’ ICT self-efficacy is related
to their intention to use ICT and their emotions during the use of ICT.
The higher the ICT self-efficacy of 9th graders, the more frequently they
used ICT for learning and recreation (Rohatgi, Scherer, & Hatlevik,
2016). Wilfong (2006) also found that college students’ ICT self-efficacy
has a negative effect on their anxiety and anger during computer use.
Therefore, ICT self-efficacy is important for adolescents to use ICT
enjoyably and effectively to meet their needs and to develop ICT competences to improve their future performance in society.

ICT self-efficacy and problematic ICT use are both theoretically and
empirically related to ICT use. In the theory of self-efficacy, Bandura
(1982) proposed that mastery experience was the most effective way to
build self-efficacy, implying that adolescents must have sufficient ICT
use experience in order to shape their ICT self-efficacy. Pathway models
of PSU also regard smartphone use as an antecedent for PSU (Billieux,
Maurage, & Lopez-fernandez, 2015; Busch & McCarthy, 2021). Several
large-scale studies have found a significant positive association between
ICT use and ICT self-efficacy among 15-year-olds (Chen & Hu, 2020) and
8th graders (Hatlevik, Throndsen, Loi, & Gudmundsdottir, 2018).
Moreover, 51 studies in a systematic literature review reported a positive correlation between the frequency of smartphone use and PSU
(Busch & McCarthy, 2021). It seems that there is a positive correlation
between PSU and ICT self-efficacy, as both are strongly associated with
ICT use. However, no prior research has directly examined the relationship between PSU and ICT self-efficacy. When looking beyond PSU,
the limited studies focusing on the relationship between ICT self-efficacy
and other problematic ICT use have not yet yielded consistent findings.
For example, some studies found a positive correlation between ICT selfefficacy and problematic Internet use (Ceyhan & Ceyhan, 2008; Shi,
Chen, & Tian, 2011), while one study found a negative correlation between ICT self-efficacy and addiction to social networking sites (Wu,
Cheung, Ku, & Hung, 2013). More research on the relationship between
ICT self-efficacy and problematic ICT use (especially PSU) is needed to
address the current gap in the literature.
Up to now, studies that obtained inconsistent results have used
variable-centered approaches (e.g. Ceyhan & Ceyhan, 2008; Shi et al.,
2011; Wu et al., 2013). The variable-centered approaches describe the
statistical relationships between ICT self-efficacy and problematic ICT
use that are considered to be invariant for all individuals within a
sample. Such approaches ignore the heterogeneity among individuals
and make it difficult to distinguish differences in the relationship between variables across subgroups (Collins & Lanza, 2010). For example,
the relationship between ICT self-efficacy and PSU may differ qualitatively (not just quantitatively) across subgroups. The variable-centered
approaches cannot distinguish between subgroups, increasing the likelihood of obtaining false-negative results. Even when positive results are
obtained, they may be more sample-dependent, with limited reproducibility and ecological validity (Bryan, Tipton, & Yeager, 2021). This may
increase the inconsistent results in the literature and bog down policymaking, as is currently the case with ICT self-efficacy and problematic
ICT use studies. In contrast, person-centered approaches divide heterogeneous individuals into subgroups based on the pattern of a combination of variables (Collins & Lanza, 2010; Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018),
which helps to address current research and policy controversies.

Problematic smartphone use
Problematic smartphone use refers to excessive and uncontrollable
use of smartphones that results in impaired social functioning and psychological and behavioral problems (Billieux, 2012). Previous studies
have not reached sufficient consensus on the dimensions of PSU; however, some dimensions are agreed upon by most researchers. Excessive
use refers to the use of smartphones too frequently or in inappropriate
situations, and this excessive use usually interferes with the adaptive
functioning of individuals, such as academic and work performance, or
interpersonal interactions (Billieux, 2012). Tolerance refers to the difficulty in controlling or reducing smartphone use, or even the need to
increase smartphone use to gain satisfaction, although the individuals
are aware that they are using their smartphone too frequently (Gutiérrez, de Fonseca, & Rubio, 2016). Withdrawal refers to the negative
emotional and behavioral responses of individuals when they are unable
to use their smartphones, such as anxiety (Busch & McCarthy, 2021).
Adolescence is a period of high PSU prevalence (Gutiérrez et al., 2016).
PSU in adolescents shows an upward trend between the ages of 10 and
18, and is expected to peak and begin to decline after the age of 18 (Lai
et al., 2022). Some researchers believed that the asynchronous development of the brain’s social-emotional and cognitive control systems
may make mid-adolescence a period of high vulnerability to PSU
(Marciano, Camerini, & Morese, 2021; Steinberg, 2005). The correlation
between PSU and various negative outcomes has been widely recognized in the research literature. Among children and adolescents, systematic literature reviews have found that PSU increases risk for mental
health problems such as anxiety, depression, and perceived stress, as
well as sleep quality and quantity (Mac Cárthaigh, Griffin, & Perry,
2020; Sohn, Rees, Wildridge, Kalk, & Carter, 2019). A large body of
evidence supporting the association of PSU and lower professional or
social performance has also accumulated (Busch & McCarthy, 2021;
Fischer-Grote, Kothgassner, & Felnhofer, 2019). For example, Lai and
colleagues found that PSU significantly predicted higher severity of
depression and anxiety symptoms in adolescents (Lai et al., 2022). For
fourth and eighth graders, parent-child relationship and peer relationship were also negatively associated with PSU (Zhang et al., 2021). In
conclusion, most studies on PSU have been conducted on the age groups
of adolescents because of their high susceptibility to PSU, and PSU has
detrimental effects across a wide variety of adjustment outcomes.

Smartphone use content types as influencing factors of PSU and ICT selfefficacy
A large number of theoretical models and empirical studies have
explored the factors influencing PSU and ICT self-efficacy, but the factors influencing subgroups of PSU and ICT self-efficacy remain unclear.
Among the many potential influencing factors, the role of smartphone
use content types on the differentiated outcomes resulting from smartphones has received attention from researchers. For example, the uses
and gratifications theory suggests that individuals choose different
media use contents due to the existence of different needs, which in turn
leads to different outcomes (Katz, Blumler, & Gurevitch, 1973). This
theoretical view has been developed to the needs-affordances-features
perspective in the era of smartphone popularity (Karahanna, Xu, Xu,
& Zhang, 2018). In addition, the interaction of person-affect-cognitionexecution (I-PACE) model and pathway models of PSU have also
emphasized the key role of specific motive for using and use content
types in the development and maintenance of a specific Internet-use

The present study
This study had two specific purposes. The first purpose was to
explore the relationship between PSU and ICT self-efficacy. This study
used a person-centered approach to identify the subgroups of PSU and
ICT self-efficacy among adolescents. The second purpose was to explore
the predictive effect of different smartphone use content types on
different subgroups. In this study, five smartphone use content types (i.
e., entertainment, interpersonal, information-seeking, utility, and study)
were selected based on previous studies (Bian & Leung, 2015; Jiang &
Zhao, 2016). We examined whether smartphone use content types one
year earlier predicted subgroup membership. Due to the novel and
exploratory nature of this study, we did not develop specific hypotheses.

Measures
Problematic smartphone use
A smartphone addiction proneness scale developed by Kim, Lee, Lee,
Nam, and Chung (2014) for adolescents was used to measure PSU in
Wave 2. This scale has gained popularity among researchers (Harris,
Regan, Schueler, & Fields, 2020). We used a revised Chinese version
scale (Huang, Lai, Xue, Zhang, & Wang, 2020), which consists of 16
items and includes four dimensions: (1) disturbance of adaptive functions (4 items, α = 0.85, e.g. “I have a hard time doing what I have
planned [study, do homework, or go to afterschool classes] due to using
smartphone”); (2) withdrawal (4 items, α = 0.87, e.g. “I get anxious and
restless when I am without a smartphone by my side”); (3) tolerance (4
items, α = 0.89, e.g. “I try cutting my smartphone use time, but I fail”);
and (4) virtual life orientation (4 items, α = 0.78, e.g. “Using a smartphone is more enjoyable than spending time with family or friends”).
Students were asked to rate how often the described events had occurred
using a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = never to 4 = always. A
higher score indicated a higher tendency of PSU. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.95. Complete list of items and confirmatory factor
analysis results are presented in the Supplementary Material 1.

Procedure
Data collection in Wave 1 was conducted at schools, and data
collection in Wave 2 was conducted online. We contacted 12 basic education schools from four cities in Central China and obtained permission from the school administrators to enter the campus to recruit
participants. We randomly selected 30 classes from these schools and
distributed participant recruitment advertisements to all students in
these classes. The formal invitations were sent to students who
expressed interest in participating in the study, including themselves
and their legal guardians. In Wave 1, adolescents completed paper
questionnaires in the classroom independently and took the parent
questionnaire home to be filled in by one of the parents. In Wave 2,
influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic, the link to the online questionnaire was sent by teachers to the WeChat (a popular communication
application that is easily accessible to Chinese adolescents) groups for
each of their classes. All procedures performed in this study were in
accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and
its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study was
approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the State Key
Laboratory of Cognitive Neuroscience and Learning, Beijing Normal

Smartphone use content types
A mobile phone use patterns questionnaire developed for Chinese
youth was adapted to measure smartphone use content types in Wave 1
(Huang et al., 2022; Jiang & Zhao, 2016). This questionnaire consists of
20 items and includes five dimensions: (1) entertainment (5 items, α =
0.82, e.g. “Watch movies, TV series, anime, variety shows, etc.”); (2)
interpersonal (4 items, α = 0.75, e.g. “Send and receive text messages, or
chat using QQ, WeChat, etc.”); (3) information-seeking (4 items, α =
0.81, e.g. “Use search engines to find information”); (4) utility (4 items,
α = 0.76, e.g. “Use mobile phone utilities [such as calculators, alarm
clocks, and schedule management apps]”); and (5) study (3 items, α =
0.65, e.g. “Use a mobile phone to learn online courses”). Students were
asked to rate how often the described smartphone use content types
were used with a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = never to 4 = very
frequently. A higher score of subdimension indicated higher use frequency of specific smartphone content type. Complete list of items and
confirmatory factor analysis results are presented in the Supplementary
Material 1.

Results
Classes of PSU and ICT self-efficacy
Fit indices (i.e., AIC, BIC, a-BIC, entropy, VLMR-LRT, BLRT, and
minimum class size) of LCA models are presented in Table 2. AIC, BIC,
and a-BIC declined consistently from two- to eight-class models with
significant p-values for BLRT, which is not an uncommon situation in
practice (Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). However, models with seven or
more classes have smaller decreases in the ICs for each additional class,
suggesting that the six-class model might be the “elbow” of point of
“diminishing returns” in the model fit (Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018).
The entropy of all models was greater than the standard recommended
by a previous study (Lubke & Muthén, 2007), and there was little difference between them. The VLMR-LRT p-values of the seven-class model
became insignificant, indicating that the seven-class model did not fit
significantly better than the six-class model. In the absence of a significant difference in the fit of the two models, the more concise model
should be accepted. Additionally, the six-class model has appropriate
minimum class proportion (9%), size (n = 111), and interpretability,
avoiding the over-extracted and potentially unstable class solutions
(Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018; Yang, 2006). Considering the statistical
criteria, parsimony, stability, and interpretability of the model, the sixclass model was selected.
In the six-class model, the probabilities of participants being assigned
to a single latent class identified by LCA were high (> 96%). We assigned
each participant to the class associated with the highest posterior
probability (Jung & Wickrama, 2008). We plotted the means of 29 items
and two variables in the six-class model in Figs. 1 and 2 respectively. We
also conducted analyses of variance to determine whether the six classes
had different ratings for each dimension of the two variables (Welch’s
ANOVA was conducted because Levene’s test showed that the variance
was not homogeneous among groups). The parameter estimates and
within-class indicator means are presented in Table 3. Class 1, which
contained 21% of the sample (n = 265, 52% female), was labeled the
high problem-medium efficacy group. The members in this group had
high PSU and medium ICT self-efficacy. Class 2, which contained 16% of
the sample (n = 204, 60% female), was labeled the low problemmedium efficacy group. The members in this group had low PSU and
medium ICT self-efficacy. Class 3, containing 13% of the sample (n =
171, 51% female), was labeled the maladjusted group. The members of
this group had high PSU and low ICT self-efficacy. This indicated that
they have not made good use of ICT but also suffered many negative
effects from smartphones. Class 4, which contained 9% of the sample (n
= 111, 53% female), was labeled the well-adjusted group. The members
of this group had the least PSU and highest ICT self-efficacy, which
indicated that they were well adapted to the information society. They
not only avoided the negative impact of excessive smartphone use but
also had the highest levels of confidence in using ICT. Class 5, which
contained 15% of the sample (n = 196, 41% female), was labeled the
medium problem-medium efficacy group. Both the PSU and ICT selfefficacy of members in this group were medium. Class 6, which contained 26% of the sample (n = 328, 56% female), was labeled the
moderately-adjusted group. Even though the members of this group had
high ICT self-efficacy, their PSU level was medium. Compared with the
well-adjusted group, their adaptability to the information society was
moderate rather than excellent.

Statistical analysis
We conducted descriptive statistics and missing value analysis in
SPSS (version 25) and SPSS Missing Value Analysis module. In this
study, the average missing rate for each variable was 0.88% in Wave 1.
Little’s MCAR (missing completely at random) test showed that the data
of Wave 1 was not missing at random (χ 2 = 40.140, df = 20, p = 0.005).
In Wave 2 online survey, we set up a reminder for missing responses.
Except for the missing data due to participants’ unresponsiveness to the
online survey, there were no other missing values in this survey. We used
maximum likelihood (ML) estimation procedures to estimate missing
item-level data (Little & Rubin, 2020). Then, we identified classes and
their predictors by latent class analysis (LCA) and regression mixture
model (RMM) in Mplus 7.4 (Muthén & Muthén, 2015). First, we conducted LCA to identify subgroups of adolescents with similar patterns
across the 16 PSU items and 13 ICT self-efficacy items. We used the full
scores for all 29 items as ordered categorical indicators of LCA and
included gender and SES as covariates. Two- to eight-class models were
conducted in sequence. We added additional classes until there was no
obvious improvement in model fitting or conceptual improvements,
such as the too-small proportion of classes or theoretically meaningless
classes. Comparing six statistical criteria (i.e., Akaike information criterion [AIC], Bayesian information criterion [BIC], sample-size adjusted
Bayesian information criterion [a-BIC], Vuong-Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test [VLMR-LRT], Bootstrap likelihood ratio test [BLRT],
and entropy), we evaluated the appropriate number of classes (Nylund,
Asparouhov, & Muthén, 2007; Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). The

Classes of PSU and ICT self-efficacy
Through latent class analysis, we identified six classes of PSU and ICT
self-efficacy among Chinese adolescents. Overall, only 9% of the entire
sample belonged to the well-adjusted group. This suggests that the
majority of Chinese adolescents are not adjusting well to the information
society, underlining the need for large-scale interventions. The six
classes differed significantly in the total and dimension scores of PSU
and ICT self-efficacy. It was easy to see from the scores of different
subgroups of adolescents on PSU and ICT self-efficacy that, although
there was a relationship between PSU and ICT self-efficacy, there was a
great deal of individual variation in the relationship between them. The
case in point is that among the two high ICT self-efficacy groups, the
moderately-adjusted group had a high degree of PSU and the welladjusted group had a low degree of PSU. These findings suggest that
dependence on ICT is not a price that adolescents have to pay to adapt to
the information society. Through education and intervention for adolescents, their ICT self-efficacy can be improved, and PSU can be prevented at the same time.
In addition, most adolescents (78%) do not belong to either the welladjusted group or the maladjusted group. According to the theory of selfefficacy and the pathway models of PSU (Bandura, 1982; Busch &
McCarthy, 2021), ICT self-efficacy and the dependence on ICT are
gradually formed with the increase of ICT use experience. Therefore,
other groups (e.g. the moderately-adjusted group) may be transitional
forms between the well-adjusted group and the maladjusted group.
During adolescence, individuals may change from members of one
group to another. The research on transformation probability and its
predictors among different groups will provide inspiration for policy
recommendations.
It is difficult to compare our findings with those of previous personcentered studies in this field, as we did not find any LCA studies on PSU
and ICT self-efficacy. As for previous studies using variable-centered
approaches in this field, the inconsistent findings from them may be
sample-dependent and difficult to generalize to other samples. Even if
the samples of previous studies had individual differences similar to
those in the present study, it is impossible to determine. Since this study
has found the heterogeneity of ICT use among adolescents, the policy
recommendations based on evidence from studies using variablecentered approaches should be treated with caution.

Predictors of PSU and ICT self-efficacy classes
With the least PSU and highest ICT self-efficacy, the well-adjusted
group seems to be the desirable group from a public policy perspective. The results of RMM with the well-adjusted group as reference
category using a three-step ML approach are presented in Table 4. An
odds ratio (OR) > 1 indicates a lower probability that subjects with
higher scores on this variable are identified into the reference category.
On the contrary, OR <1 indicates a higher probability that subjects with
higher scores on this variable are identified into the reference category.
The results showed that adolescents who used the entertainment
smartphone content type more frequently one year earlier were less
likely to belong to the well-adjusted group compared to the high
problem-medium efficacy group, the maladjusted group, the medium
problem-medium efficacy group, and the moderately-adjusted group
(OR1 = 3.09, p1 < 0.001; OR2 = 2.84, p2 < 0.001; OR3 = 2.16, p3 =
0.004; OR4 = 2.11, p4 = 0.003). More frequent use of the informationseeking smartphone content type one year earlier positively predicted
the probability of adolescents belonging to the well-adjusted group
rather than the high problem-medium efficacy group, the low problemmedium efficacy group, and the maladjusted group (OR1 = 0.60, p1 =
0.034; OR2 = 0.61, p2 = 0.044; OR3 = 0.51, p3 = 0.012). More frequent
use of the study smartphone content type one year earlier positively
predicted the probability of adolescents belonging to the well-adjusted
group, rather than the maladjusted group and the medium problemmedium efficacy group (OR1 = 0.58, p1 = 0.048; OR2 = 0.57, p2 =
0.029). Other detailed results of RMM are presented in the Supplementary Material 2.
Discussion

Predictors of PSU and ICT self-efficacy classes

This study used a person-centered approach to explore the classes of
PSU and ICT self-efficacy and the relationship between class memberships and smartphone use content types among adolescents. We identified six different subgroups in a sample of Chinese adolescents. This
finding suggested that adolescents who used smart devices very
frequently did not necessarily have higher confidence when performing
ICT tasks. That is, using more does not necessarily mean using better. We
also found predictors for different subgroups from smartphone use domains. The heterogeneity of ICT use among adolescents found in this
study emphasizes the importance of personalized policy advice.

Various smartphone use content types significantly predicted the
classes of PSU and ICT self-efficacy. Previous studies have found that ICT
use for entertainment is both a risk factor for specific Internet-use disorders (Billieux et al., 2015; Brand et al., 2016; Busch & McCarthy,
2021), and a positive predictor of ICT self-efficacy (Chen & Hu, 2020;
Rohatgi et al., 2016). This may have led researchers to disagree on the
pros and cons of ICT use for entertainment among adolescents. This
study identified a subgroup of adolescents with low PSU and high ICT
self-efficacy through an individual-centered approach and found that

Implications, limitations, and future directions
The most enlightening aspect of this study is the use of a personcentered approach to finding significant individual differences in the
relationship between adolescents’ PSU and ICT self-efficacy. Previous
researchers and policymakers have conflated these two concepts from
the fact that they are closely related to the frequency of ICT use;
therefore, they consider problematic ICT use an inevitable price to be
paid for adolescents to adapt to the information society. Some of them
argue that ICT use among adolescents should be restricted to prevent
problematic use, but others advocate that adolescents should be allowed
to use ICT excessively to cultivate their information literacy. People with
two different perspectives were caught in a debate that had left educators, adolescents, and their parents at a loss. This study helps clarify the
perceptions of the pros and cons in ICT use and demonstrates that it is
feasible for adolescents to tend toward the pros and avoid the cons in ICT
use. The maladjusted group, which represents 13% of the sample, deserves the attention of researchers and policymakers. Their highest PSU
levels and low ICT self-efficacy mean that they not only bear potential
risks associated with ICT (e.g. adaptive dysfunction induced by problematic ICT use), but also have difficulty using ICT for their development
and social progress. Reducing the proportion of this subgroup among
adolescents will be a challenge for researchers. In addition, this study
uses longitudinal data to explore the different effects of smartphone use

likely to belong to this subgroup. This finding bridges a gap in previous
studies and helps to coalesce consensus among researchers. The same is
true for the ICT use for information-seeking: previous research has
shown that it is positively associated with both ICT self-efficacy and PSU

content types. Unlike draconian policy recommendations that set uniform limits on the amount of time adolescents can use their smartphones, the findings of this study suggested that educators and parents
focus on the types of content adolescents use on their smartphones. As
Orben (2022) suggests for digital diet approach, guidelines for smartphone use time should be combined with use content, and should be
more flexible and personalized. Future research could continue to
deepen and examine the quantitative relationships between different
types of smartphone use and adolescents’ developmental outcomes.
Although this study presented several significant findings regarding
adolescents’ ICT use, some limitations need to be considered. First, this
study was not based exclusively on longitudinal data. Thus, we must be
cautious about drawing causal conclusions from our findings (e.g.,
directing adolescents to use their smartphones less for entertainment
and more for information-seeking increase the probability of belonging
to a well-adjusted group). Further research needs to examine the stability of the classes over time and the dynamic development of PSU and
ICT self-efficacy. A strict causal relationship between influencing factors
and classes should also be tested by experimental studies. Second,
although our sample was Chinese adolescents living in rural, suburban,
and urban areas, our sample size limited our ability to explore ICT use
among adolescents from different contexts. Further studies should be
conducted in other contexts and with a more representative sample.
Third, we used a single method (survey) to collect data from two
different sources (adolescents and their parents), which might be
affected by social desirability and common method bias. In further
studies, a multi-informant approach to data collection, such as peer
nomination and teacher reports, is needed.
