Abstract
Contextual factors have been identified as greatly influencing students’ reading performance. However, the collaborative influence of key contextual factors on students’ reading performance is still elusive and warrants further exploration. Based
on Walberg’s educational productivity theory and Bronfenbrenner’s ecological system theory, emphasizing that learning in humans can only be understood by considering the influence of multiple factors combined into a unit or system, the current
study sought to identify the optimal factor set of key contextual factors that collaboratively influences fourth-grade students’ reading performance. In this study, data
from 183,428 students from 61 countries/regions were extracted from the progress
in international reading literacy study 2016 dataset. First, a support vector machine
(SVM) was adopted to classify the contextual factors influencing high-performing
(students whose reading score is above 550) and low-performing (students whose
reading score is below 475) students. Second, SVM recursive feature elimination
(SVM–RFE) was applied to identify the key contextual factors capable of differentiating the two student cohorts. The findings indicate that 20 key contextual factors
selected from 106 contextual factors at the student, family and school levels collectively differentiate high- and low-performing students, providing implications for
future teaching and learning on elementary school students’ reading performance.

Introduction
Reading performance equips students with the basic abilities to acquire knowledge
and meet important learning goals, which are essential for students’ future development (Graham et al., 2017). In learning how to read, elementary school students are
expected to acquire basic reading skills (e.g., Araújo & Costa, 2015), and fourthgrade students are at an important transition point (Mullis et al., 2017). In the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS), an international macro survey, reading performance is regarded as “an important ability that is essential not
only for individuals but also for society” (Mullis et al., 2006, p. 103). The PIRLS
collects information about fourth-grade students’ experiences in the process of reading. Since 2001, it has assessed their reading performance every five years. Because
the learning context is considered as a significant determinant of students’ reading
performance (e.g., Hu, 2014), a large number of contextual factors in the PIRLS
framework (Mullis et al., 2017) are included in various contexts related to elementary students’ reading at the student, family and school levels. Based on the PIRLS,
some studies focus on different contextual factors that affect students’ reading performance in the fourth grade (e.g., Cordero et al., 2017; Law, 2009; Mullis et al.,
2004; Park, 2011), including the influence of students’ attitude towards reading on
their reading performance at the student level (e.g., Gnaldi et al., 2005); the influence of the socioeconomic background on students’ reading achievement at the
family level (e.g., Mullis et al., 2004); and the influence of school resources, composition and education policies on students’ performances (e.g., Jing et al., 2015;
Machin et al., 2013; Tramonte & Willms, 2010) at the school level.
Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) ecological system model is widely used to explore the
influence of contextual factors on children (e.g., Eriksson et al., 2018; Leonard,
2011; Wiium & Wold, 2009). The model emphasizes how a child’s environment
affects the way the child grows and develops (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). In addition,
Walberg’s (1984) educational productivity model emphasizes the influence of contextual factors on humans’ complex learning. The model considers nine factors as a
unit, which are categorized into three categories: student aptitude, instruction and
environmental factors (Walberg, 1984). Both Bronfenbrenner’s ecological system
and Walberg’s educational productivity acknowledge that students’ learning cannot
be achieved based on a single factor and suggest that learning achievement can only
be obtained when the effect factors are combined as a unit or system (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Fraser et al., 1987; Walberg, 1984). Previous studies have analyzed the
importance of contextual factors from a certain perspective; however, few studies
have explored influential factors from the perspective of the collaborative influence
of contextual factors (Tse & Xiao, 2014). Based on the theoretically based rationale and previous literature, this study therefore attempts to explore the collaborative
effect of key contextual factors on fourth-grade students’ reading performance by
examining a large number of contextual factors, especially through the benchmark
of an international survey—the PIRLS.

Literature review
The impact of student‑level contextual factors on students’ reading performance
A large number of studies have revealed the significant influence of contextual factors on students’ academic performance (e.g., Chen et al., 2021; Creemers & Kyriakides, 2010). It has been found that gender is an influential factor. Generally, females
perform better than males in terms of language abilities (e.g., Berninger et al., 2008;
Burman et al., 2008). Student aptitude, referring to students’ ability to meet the
next learning goal (Gustafsson & Balke, 1993), serves as a strong factor influencing
student academic performance. In addition, motivation, which is partly affected by
attribution belief (Law, 2009), is an important component of students’ aptitude. Students’ positive perceptions of their reading abilities may motivate them to engage in
learning-related activities and further improve their academic performance (Gnaldi
et al., 2005). Attitude is another important factor. A study focusing on the PIRLS
2016 found that students who hold negative attitudes towards reading have lower
reading aptitude (Mullis et al., 2017). Moreover, the reading performance scores
of highly engaged students are generally above the international mean score of the
PIRLS (Kirsch et al., 2002).
The impact of family‑level contextual factors on students’ reading performance
At the family level, the socioeconomic status (SES) of a family can greatly influence
children’s development, including their reading performance (e.g., Chen et al., 2019;
Cheung et al., 2017; Tramonte & Willms, 2010). SES is an overall index for analyzing a family’s status, and it can be represented by indicators such as family wealth,
the home literacy environment (HLE) and parents’ educational level and occupational status (Authors, 2019a; Creemers & Kyriakides, 2010). For instance, students
who possess a better family socioeconomic background tend to acquire better cognitive abilities and achieve higher reading performance (Jerrim & Micklewright,
2014). An excellent HLE has been identified as positively affecting students’ reading
performance (e.g., Caro et al., 2013). In general, parents’ educational level and occupational status can reflect the HLE to some extent (e.g., Tramonte & Willms, 2010;
Van Bergen et al., 2016) and thus affect students’ reading performance. For example,
compared with mothers with a low educational level, mothers with a higher educational level tend to ask more questions to encourage their children to think during
the shared-reading process (Huebner & Meltzoff, 2005). Additionally, the number of
books at home is a significant indicator of the HLE (Van Bergen et al., 2016). The
PIRLS results revealed that the high reading performance of students is related to a
certain number of books at home in all countries/regions participating in the PIRLS
(Mullis et al., 2012). Moreover, families with more cultural resources can construct
a better literacy environment for their children (Caro et al., 2013).
In addition to the SES of a family, early literacy activities are found to be closely
associated with students’ reading performance. Active interaction with parents helps
young children learn reading skills and promotes their language ability (Bowman

The Impact of School‑level Contextual Factors on Students’ Reading Performance
Numerous studies focusing on reading performance have emphasized the impact
of school-level contextual factors, including school type (e.g., Machin et al., 2013),
teaching quality (Myrberg, 2007), school resources and the school learning environment (e.g., Creemers & Kyriakides, 2010). Good teachers are considered school
resources and have a long-lasting effect on students’ academic performance (Hammerness et al., 2005). Well-educated teachers positively affect student academic
performance (Greenwald et al., 1996). Teachers’ subject knowledge and pedagogical knowledge are combined to affect teaching performance, which, in turn, affects
students’ reading performance. Furthermore, instructional quality reflects teacher
ability, which is an important factor in students’ reading performance development
(La Paro et al., 2004). In addition, there is an interaction between the quality and
quantity of instruction, which indicates that more exposure to reading leads to reading growth (Lien, 2017). However, there are some attributes that limit instruction in
class. Classroom disciplinary problems, mainly caused by a lack of classroom management (Marsh, et al., 2012), are important contextual factors in teaching because
classroom chaos negatively influences students’ academic performance and achievement (Seidel & Shavelson, 2007).
The adapted model and research questions of this study
According to the well-founded ecological system model (Bronfenbrenner, 1979)
and educational productivity model (Walberg, 1981), learners have great difficulty
attaining a high level of achievement without help from their social context. The
ecological system model includes five systems: the microsystem, the mesosystem,
the exosystem, the macrosystem and the chronosystem. Based on PIRLS questionnaires, the microsystem and the exosystem are the emphasized levels in the PIRLS
context. The microsystem refers to the small, immediate environment the child lives
in, and the exosystem includes people and places that the pupil may not interact
with but that still have a large effect on the pupil (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). However, the microsystem and the exosystem do not focus on individual status, which
is also emphasized in the PIRLS context. Regarding Walberg’s educational productivity model, a single factor cannot influence learning outcomes, and progress can
be achieved only by taking into account multiple factors as a unit (Walberg, 1984).
This theoretical model emphasizes the unit impact of contextual factors on learning,
and contextual factors are identified as influencing one another; in turn, these factors collaboratively affect the development of individuals (e.g., Alivernini, 2013).

In Walberg’s framework, nine factors that improve student ability from the affective, behavioral, and cognitive perspectives are sorted into three categories: student
aptitude, instruction and environmental factors (Walberg, 1984). Based on Walberg’s (1981, 1984) theoretical model of educational productivity, contextual factors mainly include student motivation, reading engagement, family socioeconomic
background, early literacy activities, school instruction and teacher quality, which
are categorized at the student, family and school levels in the PIRLS context (Mullis
et al., 2006). In addition, according to direct influence and indirect influence, these
factors can be categorized into the exosystem and the microsystem, respectively
(Bronfenbrenner, 1979). Therefore, taking these two theories into consideration, an
adapted theoretical framework is adopted in this study to explore the effect of contextual factors on fourth-grade students’ reading performance at the student, family
and school levels.
In the PIRLS context, 4 international benchmarks are applied to assess participants’ reading performance: the low benchmark (score above 400), intermediate
benchmark (score between 475 and 550), high benchmark (score above 550) and
advanced benchmark (score above 625) (Mullis et al., 2017, p. 52). Fourth-grade
students at or above the high benchmark are considered to be equipped with a good
logical ability; they can locate and distinguish important information, make references, interpret and integrate information and recognize the use of some language
features (Mullis et al., 2017). In contrast, students below the intermediate benchmark need extra help in reading; they can make only straightforward inferences
and interpret obvious information (Mullis et al., 2017). Thus, there is a reading
performance gap between these two cohorts of students, which influences equity
and equality (Alivernini, 2013). Therefore, these two cohorts of students (students
whose reading scores are above 550 and those whose reading scores are below 475)
are recognized as high- and low-performing students in this study, respectively. In
addition, among studies investigating the influence of contextual factors on students’
reading performance, the analysis of the collaborative effect of contextual factors is
still insufficient. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the collective impact of the
optimal contextual factor set (key contextual factors) on high- and low-performing
fourth-grade readers based on the latest PIRLS 2016 dataset. Thus, a powerful classification machine learning approach—support vector machine (SVM)—and the
SVM recursive feature elimination (SVM–RFE) techniques, as an extension of the
SVM, were employed in this study to differentiate these two cohorts of students and
to identify the collaborative effect by selecting the key contextual factor set from a
total of 106 contextual variables/factors, respectively. The proposed hypotheses in
this study are as follows: contextual factors can effectively differentiate high- and
low-performing fourth-grade readers using the SVM algorithm, and the optimal
feature set selected by SVM–RFE can collaboratively influence students’ reading
performance and satisfy the model adapted from the ecological system theory and
educational productivity models, revealing that the adapted model can be used in the
PIRLS context. To test these hypotheses, two questions are discussed:
(1) Can contextual factors distinguish high-performing from low-performing fourthgrade readers with the SVM approach?

Methodology
Data
The data were extracted from the latest public dataset of the PIRLS 2016 (URL:
https://​timss​andpi​rls.​bc.​edu/​pirls​2016/​inter​natio​nal-​datab​ase/​index.​html), including
data from 50 countries and regions and 11 benchmarking entities. Regarding PIRLS
2016, “550 assessment is equivalent to PIRLS in scope and reflects the same conception of reading as PIRLS, but in addition to passages in common with PIRLS
it includes some less difficult texts” (Mullis et al., 2017, p. 50). Thus, the scores
acquired in PIRLS and PIRLS Literacy can be assessed with the same benchmark
and reveal students’ performance to the same extent. Sixty-one countries and regions
took part in the PIRLS 2016. Of these 61 countries and regions, Iran, Morocco and
South Africa took the PIRLS Literacy assessment, while the other 58 countries and
regions took the PIRLS assessment. Finally, 183,428 fourth-grade students from 61
countries and regions, including 73,870 students whose performance met the high
benchmark (550) and 109,558 students whose performance was below the intermediate benchmark (475), were selected as the samples.
Variables/Factors
The PIRLS 2016 used five plausible values to represent students’ overall reading
performance. According to the Data Analysis Manual of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), “using one plausible value or five plausible
values does not truly make a substantial difference on large samples” (OECD, 2009,
p. 48). Because the same scaling of plausible values is used in the PISA and PIRLS,
in this study, the first plausible value (ASRREA01) was randomly selected to represent students’ reading score, which was also applied in previous studies (e.g., Chen
et al. 2019; Gorostiaga & Rojo–Álvarez, 2016). The dependent variable of the study
was the reading test scores of the target students in PIRLS 2016: the high benchmark (score of 550) and the intermediate benchmark (score of 475) (Mullis et al.,
2017, p. 52). The independent variables/factors can be analyzed at three levels: the
student, family and school levels. Student-level factors were extracted from the student questionnaire (see “Appendix A”); family-level factors were extracted from
the student and home questionnaires (see “Appendix B”); and school-level factors
were extracted from the student, teacher, school and curriculum questionnaires (see
“Appendix C”).

Methods of analysis
Reasons for choosing the SVM approach
In processing large datasets, traditional analysis approaches such as multiple linear regression (e.g., Xiao et al., 2019) and hierarchical linear modeling (e.g., Lam
& Lau, 2014) might encounter difficulties in interpreting and estimating data with
different variables because of the complex interactions between predictor variables
and multicollinearity problems (Strobl et al., 2009). Therefore, three typical nonparametric data mining approaches—classification and regression tree (CART), random forest (RF) and SVM—are gaining popularity since they are not affected by
multicollinearity and the interactions between variables (e.g., Wu et al., 2008). In
addition, the process of constructing the possible model is totally data driven (e.g.,
Gabriel et al., 2017; Wei et al., 2018).
As a nonparametric method, CART is excellent in interpreting the complicated
interactions among a great number of variables (e.g., Breiman, 1984) by automatically detecting the interaction/moderation effects between variables while providing a straightforward interpretation of the results (e.g., Alivernini, 2013; Alivernini
et al., 2016; Breiman, 1984; Liu & Ruiz, 2008; Sanzana et al., 2015). Therefore,
CART has been employed to analyze datasets based on the PIRLS (e.g., Alivernini,
2013; Finch et al., 2016) and the PISA studies (e.g., Alivernini et al., 2016). However, in selecting optimal trees, internal performance measures cannot be provided
based on the training data in CART (Wu et al., 2008). Since there are many subsets
in CART, it might be affected by problems of overfitting, in which the algorithm
fits the training data too well, thereby potentially increasing the overall error, which
frequently occurs in searching too many feature subsets. In addition, even small
changes in the dataset can influence the tree because of the hierarchical splitting
structure (Kuhn, 2008).
An RF is a group of unpruned regression trees. It helps to select the best subset of
the variables to build the tree model. RF can select the best subset of variables and
can improve the performance of trees via ensemble methods (e.g., Sanzana et al.,
2015). However, it still faces the same overfitting problem as CART (Qiao & Jiao,
2018), and the generalization error for forests might increase with an increasing
number of trees (Breiman, 2001).
First introduced by Vapnik and Chervonenkis in 1963 (cited from Cortes &
Vapnik, 1995), SVM has been successfully utilized in several academic fields as a
machine learning approach (e.g., Wu et al., 2008) and was recently applied in largescale international databases such as PISA studies (e.g., Chen et al., 2021; Gorostiaga & Rojo–Álvarez, 2016) and PIRLS studies within a single country (Dong &
Hu, 2019). First, SVM is likely to avoid overfitting by perfectly setting a model to
one portion of a dataset without generalizing the results to the remaining datasets,
which demonstrates the excellent generalization property of SVM (Li & Sun, 2011).
Second, SVM is unlikely to be affected by small changes because the hyperplane
is relatively stable once it is constructed (James et al., 2013; Qiao & Jiao, 2018).
Third, the classification effect of SVM is notable for its high accuracy and robustness (Dosenbach et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2013) when dealing with a large amount

SVM modelling
As an SVM library, the free package LIBSVM (URL: https://​www.​csie.​ntu.​edu.​
tw/​~cjlin/​libsvm/) has been developed for the easy access of SVM users, and it has
gained popularity in machine learning (Chang & Lin, 2011). LIBSVM trains a dataset to acquire the optimal model and then utilizes it to process a testing dataset. In
the SVM model, some indicators are measured to assess the model’s classification
ability (Baldi et al., 2000), and the seven most common indicators are the accuracy
score (ACC), sensitivity score (SEN, also called the recall score), specificity score
(SPE), precision score and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC)
curve (AUC). These indices can all be used to assess the quality of the model, but
they reflect the information from different perspectives.

The ACC score is already a good indicator with which to evaluate the measurement of classification performance, but the combination of these seven indicators
can better assess the performance of the model (e.g., Pham et al., 2016). The AUC

value the model quality.
In addition, to ascertain the key contextual factors relevant to the reading performance of elementary students, the combined SVM–RFE technique was utilized
in this study. As an extension of the SVM, the SVM–RFE technique has been
reported to rank all factors with respect to their weight on the classification (Guyon
et al., 2002). This approach is an embedded feature selection technique that simultaneously integrates the training process with multiple feature search. SVM–RFE
removes the factors with the smallest weight and then builds a new model at each
iteration. This process continues until all factors are eliminated. The model outputs
a descending ranking list of all the factors with regard to their significance for the
classification. However, SVM–RFE also has limitations. The factors selected might
not obtain a good classification effect for the testing data, possibly due to overfitting. To avoid overfitting and improve the classification performance of the model,
SVM–RFE cross-validation (SVM–RFE–CV) can be built to select the optimal
factor set. Cross-validation is applied to assess how well the results of a statistical
analysis can be generalized to an independent dataset to evaluate the stability of the
constructed model. Compared with SVM–RFE, SVM–RFE–CV has better robustness to noise (Strobl et al., 2009) and better classification performance with regard
to the testing set (Zhang et al., 2013).
Data analysis procedure
The raw data were preprocessed in three steps: cleaning, imputation and normalization. In the data cleaning process, samples with more than 20% missing data were
first deleted, which is a typical percentage used in deleting invalid samples (Xia
et al., 2013). Subsequently, the remaining missing data were imputed with the nearest-neighbor mean value as suggested in machine learning-based analysis (Gorostiaga & Rojo–Álvarez, 2016) using the Python 3.5 computer program. Finally, the
data were normalized using the centering and variance scaling principle.

Then, the data were analyzed with Python 3.5 in three steps. First, an SVM with
a linear kernel function was utilized to process the SPSS dataset, including all 106
factors. This SVM could identify whether the two student cohorts could be distinguished with these contextual factors and to find the best parameters with which
to classify the two classes. Second, SVM–RFE was applied to sort the 106 contextual factors with regard to their weight on the model. It eliminated the factors with
the smallest weight and repeated the process until all the factors were eliminated.
Third, SVM–RFE–CV was used to select the optimal factor set, which can achieve
an excellent classification effect with the lowest number of factors (key factors). In
k-fold cross-validation, the difference in size between the training dataset and the
resampling subsets decreses, and the bias of the technique decreases as the size
increases. When choosing k in k-fold cross-validation, the choice is usually 5 or
10, but there is no formal rule (Kuhn & Johnson, 2013). Typically, fivefold validation can achieve good prediction performance (James et al., 2013), and it has been
widely used (e.g., Mou et al., 2014). Therefore, to assess the reliability of cross validation, a fivefold cross-validation method was used in this study to process the total
high-order dimensional data of 183,428 students based on the PIRLS 2016 with a
high computational load. The data were randomly divided into five sections. Four
and nine sections were used as the training data, respectively, and the remaining section was utilized to construct the SVM model. Moreover, the optimal set of factors
could be outputted through visualization.

Results
In this study, the popular linear kernel function was applied to train the SVM model.
After parameter optimization with the fivefold cross-validation method, the optimized SVM model with a total of 106 contextual factors reached a relatively high
classification accuracy of 81.94% (SEN = 73.00%, SPE = 87.97%, precision = 0.8036
and AUC = 0.8049). In an SVM model in which all predictions are correct, these
measurements will obtain a value of 1; in contrast, for a randomly built classification model, these scores will be approximately 0.5. In general, scores of indicators
above 0.8 are regarded as good, and scores of indicators above 0.7 are regarded as
fair (Xia et al., 2013). Therefore, through the fivefold cross-validation methods, the
SVM model was verified to effectively differentiate the two cohorts of target students. The first research question was addressed, and a solid foundation was constructed to address the second research question. Through SVM–RFE, a ranking list
of the 106 factors was outputted in descending order.
Gorostiaga and Rojo–Álvarez (2016) found that the minimum number of factors
in the optimal factor set was generally between 20 and 30. The optimal factor set
in another study conducted by Chen et al. (2019) also included 20 factors, which
confirmed the findings of Gorostiaga and Rojo–Álvarez (2016). In this study, a comparison of the accuracy scores of the top 20 (79.92%), top 25 (80.30%) and top 30
(80.53%) factors showed that there was only a minimal increase in accuracy after
the top 20 factors. The 20th factor was within the smooth curve range, showing that
the accuracy score tended to be stable after the top 20 factors. This finding indicated

Discussion
In this study, contextual factors were found to effectively differentiate high- and
low-performing fourth-grade students with respect to reading performance with the
constructed SVM model. In addition, the20 identified key contextual factors work
collaboratively to influence fourth-grade students’ reading performance through
SVM–RFE. These findings align well with the hypotheses. The results not only
reveal that the adapted model is suitable in the PIRLS context but also indicate that
the ecological system theory and educational productivity model can be combined
with the PIRLS contextual factors, revealing the importance of these two theories.
The innate correlations of 20 key contextual factors at the student, family and school
levels and their collaborative impact on student reading performance are elaborated
below.
Gender and motivation
For children’s reading performance, gender is an influential factor. Fourth-grade
female students perform better than their male counterparts, which is in line with
previous studies (e.g., Reilly, 2015). In addition to gender, readers’ motivation
greatly affects their reading performance. In this study, two key contextual factors—
“students like talking about what they read with other people” (ASBR06A) and “students like reading when a book helps them imagine other worlds” (ASBR06H)—
were identified as important indicators of reading motivation. As shown in Table 2,
high-performing children obtained lower mean scores for these two factors (1.41 and
1.40, respectively). This result indicates that students with higher reading motivation
acquire higher reading performance than those with lower reading motivation. This
finding is consistent with previous studies (e.g., Areepattamannil et al., 2010; Wang

Socioeconomic background and early literacy activities
The socioeconomic background of families is a well-established topic in explorations of the factors affecting students’ academic performance. Most previous studies focused only on the influence of the environment on students’ development. For
example, a stimulating environment, such as having a certain number of books at
home, is essential for children to develop their cognitive and verbal abilities (e.g.,
Rindermann et al., 2011) and receptive and expressive vocabulary (Raikes et al.,
2006). The present study tries to explore the innate correlations between different
socioeconomic background factors. It analyses this hot topic from the following
aspects: the number of books at home and family wealth status. The findings of this
part not only indicate that cultural resources at home can affect students’ reading
performance but also demonstrate the correlation between the home literacy environment (HLE) and family socioeconomic background: family socioeconomic background further affects students’ reading performance by influencing the HLE, which
is elaborated below.
In this study, in the optimal factor set, “the number of children’s books at home”
(ASBH14) ranks 6th out of all 106 factors, fully indicating its importance. This factor is an important component of the HLE and is greatly affected by family socioeconomic background. In addition, family socioeconomic background can be reflected
by many factors. “The frequency of speaking ‘language of test’ at home” (ASBG03)
and “the test language” (ITLANG) (ranking 3rd and 9th, respectively) are two influential indicators of children’s family socioeconomic status, which affect the HLE
and thus influence students’ reading performance. In this study, students who are at
the high benchmark have a lower mean score (1.54) than those below the intermediate benchmark (1.89) for “The frequency of speaking ‘language of test’ at home”
(ASBG03), indicating that students who usually use the language of the test at home
tend to achieve better reading performance.
In addition to factors related to the HLE, some family wealth indicators also indirectly reflect a family’s socioeconomic background, including “the existence of a
computer or tablet” (ASBG05A) and “Internet connection” (ASBG05D). In this
study, these factors positively influence students’ reading performance. As shown
in Table 2, high-performing students demonstrated mean scores of 1.03 and 1.04 for
these three factors (ASBG05A and ASBG05D, respectively), while low-performing
students’ mean scores were 1.19 and 1.26, respectively. “The frequency of students’
absence from school” (ASBG06) is also an important factor, ranking second out of
106 factors, which indirectly reflects children’s family socioeconomic background

According to the above discussion, family socioeconomic background affects

socioeconomic background and can also affect students’ reading performance. Thus,

With regard to early literacy activities, in this study, the factors “parents read
books with children” (ASBH02A) and “parents write numbers with children”
(ASBH02P) were positively associated with children’s reading performance. These
factors are directly related to children and belong to the microsystem in the ecological model, greatly influencing children’s development. Students with a higher
degree of parental engagement in reading activities achieve a higher mean score on
the PIRLS 2016 test. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that
parents’ involvement in early literacy activities is fundamental for children’s growth
in language and cognitive abilities (Song et al., 2013; Weizman & Snow, 2001).
Another important indicator is “how long was your child in pre-primary educational
programs altogether” (ASBH05B), which ranked 16th out of all 106 factors. Pre-primary education experiences such as kindergarten are also a microsystem factor that
might improve students’ reading performance and even have a long-lasting effect
on students. Moreover, students’ performance when they begin primary education
reflects their family socioeconomic background and early literacy activities, which
distinguishes students with extreme levels of science performance (Chen et al.,
2019).
From the above discussion, factors belonging to family socioeconomic background and early literacy activities are correlated and affect each other, working collaboratively to influence students’ reading performance.
Factors limiting instruction and teachers’ role
At the school level, two key factors identified belong to the “quality of instruction”
in Walberg’s (1981, 1984) educational productivity model and the exosystem in
Bronfenbrenner’s ecological system model (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). There are many
factors that limit instruction. From the school questionnaires, two limiting factors
were identified: “absenteeism” (ACBG14B) and “cheating” (ACBG14D). These two
factors are identified as negative factors influencing students’ reading performance.
In addition to factors that limit instruction, teachers play an important role in children’s academic performance. In this study, “Teachers at my school are fair to me”
(ASBG12D) and “My teacher does a variety of things to help us learn” (ASBR01H)
reflect teachers’ importance, selected into the optimal feature set. Teachers play
an important role in student academic achievement. They can motivate students
by providing high-quality reading instruction (Saskia et al., 2019). Teacher–student interactions and teacher guidance of student learning have been identified as
influential factors (e.g., William et al., 2009). However, previous studies focusing

Conclusions
In the PIRLS 2016 dataset, factors influencing fourth-grade students’ reading literacy can be analyzed at the student, family and school levels. Most previous studies
focused only on a single factor or a few factors but did not explore the combined
influence of contextual factors. Therefore, by combining ecological system theory
and the educational productivity model, this study tries to narrow this research gap
and explores the collective effect of the contextual factors based on the PIRLS 2016
database.
In the above discussion, the hypotheses are effectively addressed. Factors in the
optimal factor/feature set indeed have innate correlations and are intertwined. They
not only influence other factors at the same level but also influence other contextual
factors at other levels, which shows that contextual factors work together to influence students’ reading performance. The results show that in dealing with a large
amount of data with dozens of complex factors, the SVM can effectively distinguish
students above the high benchmark from those below the intermediate benchmark,
offering a valuable perspective for exploring similar situations. Then, SVM–RFE is
utilized to rank the factors at three levels, and SVM–RFE–CV is used to select an
optimal factor set that can achieve relatively high ACC and AUC scores with the
lowest number of factors. This step further identifies the weight of selected key factors on the model, revealing the significance of key contextual factors in the optimal
factor set. The results show that ecological system theory and the educational productivity model are suitable in the PIRLS context and reveal that contextual factors
play an important role in children’s development.
Overall, this study further emphasizes the importance of ecological system theory and the educational productivity model. This study contributes by exploring, for
the first time, the collaborative effect of key contextual factors on the reading performance of fourth-grade students using a machine learning method in the PIRLS
context. The findings have implications not only for students, parents, and teachers

future studies regarding the collaborative effect of contextual factors.

Limitations of this study
Among the limitations of this study, the identified key contextual factors are all
descriptive factors, and this study is exploratory. The results can only present the
collaborative effect and innate correlations but cannot provide cause-effect conclusions or predictions. Therefore, cause-effect conclusions of the relationships
between different factors might be further revealed by combining more objective
survey questions in future PIRLS studies. In addition, although this study fully
addressed the research questions based on the PIRLS 2016 with the machine learning approach, findings of this study could not be representative of that of other studies related to the PIRLS, which is a limitation and should be mentioned here. In
future studies, it is still encouraged to utilize other novel machine learning-based
approaches to explore the PIRLS data from multiple perspectives for the latent and
intriguing information to provide insightful suggestions for reading education and
reading assessment. The purpose of this study is to explore the collaborative effect
of the key contextual factors on students’ reading performance to improve students’
reading performance. Therefore, it provides an informative and reliable description
of the phenomenon, which can narrow the research gap and provide information for
future research.
