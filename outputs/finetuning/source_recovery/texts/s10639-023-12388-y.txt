Relationship between students’ ICT interactions
and science achievement in PISA 2018: The case of Türkiye

Abstract
In any field, effective teaching and learning are greatly aided by information and
communication technology, or ICT. ICT integration in education has received significant attention in educational system reforms, which emphasized it as an essential
component in improving the learning process. Educational organizations have been
particularly concerned about how ICT use would affect students’ academic achievement (Alhusein, 2021). Since information and communication technology (ICT) is
so prevalent in today’s digital world, the current study has focused on the connections between ICT and Turkish students’ science performance on the PISA 2018
assessment. To assess the impact of ICT use on students’ science performance, this
study used structural equation modeling (SEM) to analyze PISA 2018 data (Türkiye, n = 6890, 186 schools) while taking the students’ socioeconomic status into
consideration. Results showed that: (i) ICT use in and outside of the classroom was
found to be statistically significantly correlated with science achievement; (ii) there
was also a statistically significant correlation between science achievement and students’ attitudes toward ICT. This research can increase theoretical understanding of
technology, learning, and cultural context as well as actual applications of ICT in
science classes that consider the differences between ICT effects.
Keywords Confirmatory factor analysis · ICT · Science achievement · PISA ·
Structural equation modeling (SEM)

1 Introduction
Information and communication technology (ICT) use in education has been
hailed as an important feature of effective teaching and learning in recent years
because of rapid developments in ICT that require students to be fully equipped
to prepare them for a life in the digital age. It entails enhancing teaching and
learning with a variety of ICT tools and technologies, including digital media,
smartphones, tablets, laptops, and computers. In order to improve the quality of
education, ICT is also being used in teaching and learning procedures more and
more. For example, interactive learning can be provided by using simulations and
virtual labs so that students can have an opportunity to experiment and explore
scientific concepts in a more hands-on way.
Many reforms in educational systems have concentrated on the integration
of ICT in education as an essential step toward a more successful learning process (Fiş Erümit & Keleş, 2021). Moreover, ICT literacy and the ability to use
and interact with ICT are core skills in the twenty-first century (Bellanca, 2010).
According to Lennon et al. (2003), students’ attitudes, interests, and ability to use
digital technology to access, organize, and evaluate knowledge as well as share
newly created knowledge with others and the larger society, are all considered
indicators of their ICT literacy.
This skill is not limited to the students’ utilization of ICT in their classrooms
but includes their usage of ICT outside the classroom as well. As a result, encouraging students to interact with ICT is crucial to helping them become more successful learners. Better learning has frequently been linked to ICT integration in
teaching and learning practices, wherein ICT can improve learning environments
and give students constructive feedback (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018; Papanastasiou
et al., 2003).
Some studies show that students who use ICT for school-related tasks, such as
writing, show better academic achievement than their peers (Zhang et al., 2016).
However, other research revealed a negative correlation between students’ literacy test scores and ICT use at home and in the classroom based on an analysis
of PISA 2016 results (Kunina-Habenicht & Goldhammer, 2020). Furthermore,
the results of various assessments, including TIMSS (Papanastasiou, 2002), PISA
2006 and PISA 2000 in science achievement (Papanastasiou et al., 2003), and
mathematics in the NAEP (Zhang et al., 2016), also support these findings in a
similar way. Nonetheless, it is a noteworthy fact that these studies argue for a
need for closer inspection of the causes behind this negative association between
ICT usage and the students’ performance. Some studies cite the use of contentspecific software (Papanastasiou et al., 2003) or the utilization of ICT for remedial classes, which might explain these results (Kunina-Habenicht & Goldhammer, 2020; Zhang et al., 2016).
Given that 15-year-olds worldwide live in a more digital age and that technology plays a significant role in influencing students’ performance, the Organization
for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) has made sure that the Programme for International Students Assessment (PISA) has also been concerned

1.1 Theoretical framework
Multiple theories are incorporated into this study. The cultural constructivist learning theory (Cobern, 1993), in which students actively participate in the creation of
their culturally situated knowledge, influences how students use technology in their
cultural setting, as examined in this study. For instance, students might employ technology in their cultural setting in various ways. Hence, students generate knowledge
using the ICT that is accessible to them in their settings to succeed academically in
this study.
The second theory, termed the self-determination theory (SDT), connects the
cognitive evaluations of students’ science performance to the non-cognitive elements that underpin students’ interactions with ICT (Ryan & Deci, 2000). It has
to do with why people make the decisions they do in the absence of distractions
and other influences. This theory offers a helpful perspective for examining the ICT
factors of the current study. PISA assesses a student’s perceived autonomy, competency, and interest in utilizing ICT for social interactions and other purposes, as well
as comfort with it. SDT requires three psychological elements: relatedness, competence, and autonomy. According to Vansteenkiste et al. (2020), these needs are
typically universal, meaning that they affect everyone regardless of circumstances or
situational variables.
On the other hand, depending on the situation, culture, or experience, some
demands could be more pressing than others at a given moment. SDT pinpoints
three innate demands that, when met, permit ideal function and growth. When using
ICT, a student who exhibits high competence is more in charge and proficient. A
student has high autonomy when they wish to use ICT independently and make their
own decisions. PISA measures high relatedness as the frequency of ICT use in social
interactions among students, which is an indication of how well they relate to others.
The third, social cognitive theory (SCT) (Bandura, 1986), forms the basis of this
study and attempts to explain how people control their behavior in order to perform
and sustain goal-directed activities over an extended period of time. Consequently,
how students engage with ICT and set goals may be significantly influenced by their
level of confidence in their ability to execute an action. This theory, which holds
that people learn in a dynamic, social environment through interactions with their
surroundings that are influenced by their prior experiences, is pertinent to the topic
under investigation.
Finally, this study used the ecological framework (Zandvliet, 2012) to emphasize the role of teachers and the importance of the learning ecosystem in technologically advanced settings, which has implications for the use of ICT in the classroom.
The ecosphere, sociosphere, and technosphere are the three overlapping domains of
influence in the framework. The physical environment and surrounds of a student

2 ICT in science education
There is a correlation between ICT integration and the science performance of students. Studies have revealed that integrating ICT tools and instructional technology
into science curricula can help students grasp scientific ideas and principles, which
can boost their performance in the subject (Isman et al., 2007; Ghavifekr & Rosdy,
2015). ICT tools can provide students with a more interactive and dynamic learning
environment, which can enhance their engagement and motivation. Students may
perform experiments, evaluate data, and study scientific topics with a more handson approach by utilizing simulations, virtual laboratories, and other ICT tools. This
can lead to a deeper understanding and better retention of scientific knowledge.
Additionally, ICT tools can offer students access to a greater variety of materials
and information, which can aid in their development of a more thorough knowledge
of scientific concepts. Online databases, e-books, and scientific journals are easily accessible through the internet, allowing students to explore and learn beyond
the classroom. Consequently, the utilization of ICT tools and technology in science
education can positively impact students’ science achievement by providing new
avenues for learning, improving the effectiveness of teaching, and promoting collaboration and communication among students. Therefore, some studies highlight
the significance of investigating the students’ degree of proficiency with ICT and
confidence in using it, as it influences their understanding and engagement during
learning. Students who have access to computers both at home and at school have
higher levels of scientific literacy, according to Papanastasiou et al. (2003). Papanastasiou et al. (2005) found similar results, indicating that while students who had
access to computers at home demonstrated higher levels of scientific literacy and
achievement, the availability of ICT in schools did not significantly predict scientific
literacy.
In studies about students’ confidence and comfort in ICT use, research highlights the need to investigate students’ comfort in using ICT as computer anxiety or being unfamiliar with ICT use might hinder students’ ability to focus on
the learning tasks (Papanastasiou et al., 2005). Some studies have shown that students in the US who are comfortable and familiar with using computers to write
papers using word processing software have higher scientific literacy, but these
results were not consistent as the same data set from Germany did not provide

The relationship between students’ use of ICT and their science performance
was investigated in recent studies. Research has demonstrated that the utilization
of ICT can enhance students’ scientific performance. For example, students who
used ICT in scientific classes performed better than those who did not, according to Dodmani’s (2019) study. Rohatgi et al., (2016) conducted a meta-analysis
that provided evidence for this finding, showing that science achievement was
positively impacted by ICT use in numerous studies. Moreover, the utilization of
specific ICT tools has been found to be particularly effective in improving science achievement. For example, Miller et al.’s (2018) study noted that the use of
virtual labs in science classes led to higher levels of science achievement compared to traditional lab classes.
However, studies have also indicated that ICT use may have adverse effects
on science performance. For example, according to Kunina-Habenicht and Goldhammer (2020) and Skryabin et al. (2015), ICT use both within and outside of
school was linked to poorer levels of science achievement. In addition, students
who reported using computers in their classroom more frequently had lower academic achievement than their peers, while students with higher scores reported
using computers less in their classrooms. Some authors argue that increased use
of ICT tools might reduce time spent on school-related tasks and cause lower
achievement (Papanastasiou et al., 2005). However, studies have also indicated
that ICT use may have adverse effects on science performance. For example,
although ICT use is a strong predictor of academic achievement, its impact on
students’ academic achievement in science and mathematics at school was found
to be limited (Delen & Bulut, 2011), based on a study of Turkish PISA results
from 2009. These results were confirmed by a recent study by Meng et al. (2019),
which reached a similar conclusion.
The effect of ICT usage in education should consider the effect of many variables that take account of how learning happens, such as how educators use
it, the learning objective being addressed, and how students’ learning is being
assessed (Papanastasiou et al., 2003; Vrasidas & McIsaac, 2001). Accordingly,
research has demonstrated a positive relationship between ICT use in education
and academic achievement. This relationship highlights the need for teachers to
emphasize higher-order thinking skills in their instruction in order to challenge
their students, as well as their ability to use and teach technology with proper
support (Papanastasiou et al., 2003). More importantly, the research methods
being used to analyze these outcomes are essential in determining the correlation between those different variables (Papanastasiou et al., 2003). Overall,
there is a complex link between students’ utilization of ICT and their science
performance. The type and degree of ICT use are still up for debate, even though
using ICT can boost science achievement.

2.1 Education system in Türkiye
Commencing with the 2012–2013 academic year, compulsory education in Turkey
consists of 12 years, split into three phases. Primary school, which includes grades
1 through 4, is the initial level. The age range it covers is 66 months to 10 years
old. The second stage is middle school, which is for students in grades five through
eight and ranges in age from ten to fourteen. High school, which consists of grades 9
through 12, is the last stop before going to college. Kids go to high school between
the ages of 14 and 18. Middle and high schools may have different types of schools,
such as religious. However, all students take the same science, math, and ICTrelated courses in regular public schools. In addition, students in public religious
schools take different types of science, math, and ICT-related courses. As for public
vocational schools, students take similar courses at different levels. Science education courses are included in grades 3–8, which are at the primary and middle school
levels. Mathematics education courses are included in grades 1 to 12, which are primary, middle, and high school levels. Courses related to ICT are included in grades
1 to 10, which are primary, middle, and high school levels (MEB). This indicates
that math, science, and ICT education are considerably covered in the education system in Türkiye (MEB (n.d.)).
PISA 2018 Türkiye report (MEB, 2019) states that among the 15-year-old students who participated in the study, 43.7% attended Anatolian high schools, 31.1%
attended Anatolian vocational and technical high schools, and 13.7% attended Anatolian religious high schools. PISA 2018 Türkiye sample includes 11.2% of students
enrolled in science high schools, social sciences high schools, multi-program Anatolian high schools, and Anatolian fine arts high schools. Just 0.3% of pupils proceed
to complete their secondary schooling.

2.2 PISA: Programme for international student assessment
The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) runs the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), a global research project
that assesses 15-year-old students’ reading, math, and science knowledge in threeyear cycles to help them overcome challenges in real life (OECD, 2019a, b, c, d,
e; MEB, 2019). The 15-year-old group that continues their formal education is the
subject of PISA research. Every study cycle, a weighted area is chosen, and that area
is subjected to thorough analysis. Reading skills were selected as the focus area in
the PISA 2018 research. Each PISA survey sees an increase in the number of participating countries. The study included participation from 32 countries in 2000, 11
countries in 2002, 41 countries in 2003, 57 countries in 2006, 65 countries in 2009
and 2012, and 72 countries in 2015. Respondents to the PISA 2018 survey came
from 79 different nations.
Türkiye has been participating in the PISA survey since 2003 (MEB, 2019). In
PISA applications in 2018, a stratified random sampling method is used to select the
school sample. School type, Statistical Regional Units Classification (SRUC), Level

2.3 Science performance of Türkiye in PISA 2018
PISA defines science performance as a 15-year-old’s ability to use scientific knowledge to recognize questions, create novel information, describe scientific occurrences or events, and obtain conclusions regarding scientific problems that are supported by evidence. (OECD, 2019a, b, c, d, e). Over their average scores, the 79
countries that took part in PISA 2018’s scientific performances were evaluated. The
average scores of 79 countries taking part in the PISA 2018 science examination
vary between 336 and 590. The average score in the science of 79 countries joining
the PISA was 458, and the average score in the science of 37 OECD countries was
489. Türkiye is one of the 79 countries participating in PISA 2018. It was placed
39th among 79 countries and 30th among 37 OECD countries. Türkiye is above the
science average of 458 with an average score of 468 among the 79 countries that
participated in PISA 2018. Then again, its science score (468) is lower than the average score (489) of the 37 OECD countries. On the other hand, MEB (2019) reported
that the average science score of Türkiye varies between 424 and 488 in PISA
between 2006 and 2018. In addition, according to the OECD (2004), Türkiye’s average score in 2003 was 434, which was below the average score of 29 OECD nations,

in. On the other hand, the average science score, which tended to increase between
2006 and 2012, declined in 2015, as happened with the math score. In the PISA
2018, Türkiye ‘s average science score reached its highest level since 2006. Based
on the results of PISA 2015 and 2018, when compared to PISA 2015, Türkiye had
the largest average gain in science Türkiye scores (MEB, 2019).

3 Methodology
In order to investigate how ICT use affects science achievement among students in
Türkiye according to PISA 2018, this study controls for socioeconomic status (SES)
using structural equation modeling (SEM).
3.1 Data collection
This study uses secondary data from participants in the PISA 2018 database’s ICT
Familiarity Questionnaire and Student Questionnaire from Türkiye (OECD, 2019a,
b, c, d, e). The secondary data set used in this study was collected by the OECD
using the ICT Familiarity Questionnaire and Student Questionnaire in the PISA
2018 assessment. The secondary data were from 6890 15-year-old students from
186 schools in Türkiye who participated in PISA 2018 (MEB, 2019). The PISA
2018 includes nine ICT-related subscales.
3.2 Measures
The research questions were addressed by means of measures of students’ attitudes
toward ICT, ICT use in school, ICT use outside of school, and their science achievement in PISA 2018.
3.2.1 Interest in ICT and perceived competence
The PISA 2018 ICT questionnaire (OECD, 2017) included the students’ ICT interest
scale (IC013, INTICT), their perceived competence in ICT usage (IC014, COMPICT), and their perceived autonomy related to ICT usage (IC015, AUTICT).
Students were asked to rate how much they agreed or disagreed with each of the
constructs on a 4-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = agree, and
4 = strongly agree). The Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient and the McDonald’s coefficient omega for the INTICT scale are .866 and .866, respectively; the
COMPICT scale is and.860, respectively; and the AUTICT scale is .880 and .879,
respectively.
3.2.2 Availability and usage of ICT
After the PISA participants had finished the student survey, the ICT familiarity
questionnaire (ICQ) was given to them (OECD, 2017). One international option that
countries may choose to use is the ICQ. It asked if students used ICT for a range of
purposes and if it was accessible to them both at home and at school. To evaluate
the availability of ICT at the home scale (IC001, ICTHOME) and the school scale
(IC009, ICTSCH), students were asked if they had access to different types of technology both at home and at school. Responses fell into three categories: 1 (yes, and

I use it); 2 (yes, but I don’t use it); and 3 (no). The Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient and the McDonald’s coefficient omega for the ICTHOME scale are .778 and
.782, respectively, and the ICTSCH scale are .818 and .820, respectively.
Students were questioned about how often they used technology for schoolrelated tasks (IC011, USESCH), extracurricular activities (IC008, ENTUSE), and

3.2.3 Subject‑related ICT use
During classroom lessons, questions about the use of digital devices for the assigned
subject were put to the students (IC150, ICTCLASS). The response categories were
(1 = no time, 2 = 1–30 minutes a week, 3 = 31–60 minutes a week, 4 = more than
60 minutes a week, and 5 = I do not study this subject). The Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient and the McDonald’s coefficient omega for the ICTCLASS scale are
.863 and .853, respectively.
3.2.4 Science achievement
PISA 2018 reported student performance using “plausible values” to account for
measurement error. These are arbitrary values chosen at random from the distribution of estimates of proficiency (OECD, 2019a, b, c, d, e). PISA 2018 offers a set of
ten plausible values for science achievement. In PISA 2018, the science plausible
values are labeled PV1SCIE through PV10SCIE. The correct parameter estimates,
and standard errors were computed using Mplus’s TYPE = IMPUTATION command (Muthen & Muthen, 1998–2019). All analyses involving science achievement
scores were performed using each of the ten plausible values.

4 Secondary data analysis
Our secondary analysis involved data collected in PISA 2018. Secondary data analysis is the process of examining previously collected data for new purposes (Khine
et al., 2023). To develop new hypotheses or provide answers to research issues, secondary data might be evaluated. The benefit of using secondary data is that it makes
higher-quality and substantially more accessible data, like PISA and TIMMS data,
available. Beyond the ethical benefit of not having to gather data from specific individuals, secondary data also saves a ton of time, money, effort, and other resources.
Additionally, data from large samples can be of greater quality and be more accurate
in representing the population (Tripathy, 2013).
Before evaluating the measurement and structural models, the data were examined for missing values. Descriptive statistics were used, which included an

13

estimation of univariate normality, to look at the skewness and kurtosis of the items.
Convergent and discriminant validity were investigated in order to assess the measurement model using CFA.
The study model was then assessed using the Mplus Version 8.3 software and the
structural equation modeling approach (Muthen & Muthen, 1998–2019). The robust
maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator was employed to control the non-normality
(skewness or kurtosis) in the data set. Due to the hierarchical nature of the PISA
2018 data, the proper parameter estimates, standard errors, and chi-square values
were generated using Mplus’s TYPE = COMPLEX option and chi-square values
(Muthen & Muthen, 1998–2019). In the present study, the values reported are therefore Satorra-Bentler rectified (SB-: Satorra & Bentler, 2010). The student weight,
W_FSTUWT, and school ID, CNTSCID, in the PISA dataset were used as weighting and clustering variables to address the unequal probability of selection and the
non-independence of students (Liou, 2021). To manage missing data, the full information maximum likelihood estimation (FIML) option in Mplus was used (Muthen
& Muthen, 1998–2019).
Gender was utilized as the grouping variable in a multi-group SEM to assess if
the indirect impact affected both males and females equally. Differences in the path
coefficients are checked against zero, and the SEM is determined for the two gender
groups (Rohatgi et al., 2016). The following recommendations by Kline (2016) were
used to evaluate the model fit of the hypothesized path model: comparative fit index
(CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) greater than or equal to.90, root mean square
error of approximation (RMSEA) less than or equal to.08, and standardized root
mean square residual (SRMR) less than or equal to.10 for an acceptable model fit.

5 Results
5.1 Measurement model
The measurement model was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis with
Mplus Version 8.3 (Muthen & Muthen, 1998–2019). The CFA results are shown in
Table 2, which shows that the measurement model had a good model fit (df = 1758,
CFI = .930, TLI = .925, SRMR = .044, and RMSEA = 0.032). Since all t-values were
greater than 1.96, the standardized factor loadings of all the items ranged from.527
to.845 and were statistically significant (p < .05). This demonstrates that all measurement model constructs have appropriate validity.
5.2 Convergent validity
According to Fornell and Larker (1981), to evaluate the convergent validity of the
assessment items, it is necessary to look at the average variance extracted (AVE),
composite reliability (CR) of each construct, and item reliability for each measure.
Hair et al. (2017) proposed that the value of CR should be more than 0.70 to
achieve high internal consistency and reliability. In Table 2, the composite reliability
(CR) of the constructs is reported as follows: ICTHOME (.858), ICTSCH (.863),
ICTCLASS (.874), ENTUSE (.880), HOMESCH (.910), USESCH (.927), INTICT
(.864), COMPICT (.835), and AUTICT (.874).
The final convergent validity criterion for each construct is the average variance

6 Discussion
Using a secondary analysis of PISA 2018 data, the current study investigated the
effect of ICT usage both inside and outside of the classroom on students’ achievement in science. The construct validity of the student questionnaire for Turkish
students was validated by confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation
modeling. ICT use outside school and in school was noted to be statistically significantly linked to science achievement (H1, H2, H3, H5, H6). Fernandez-Gutierrez et al. (2020) and Cristia et al. (2017) supported the conclusion and argued

that ICT usage at school has a positive effect on science performance. The finding
is also supported by Wittwer and Senkbeil (2008), who identified a positive association between school performance and owning a computer at home.
A statistically significant association between students’ attitudes toward ICT
and science achievement (H8, H9) was found in the present study, which was supported by Abdulla et al. (2015) and Daud et al. (2019). However, no statistically
significant relationship existed between perceptions of student autonomy in using
ICT, the availability of ICT at school, and science achievement (H4, H7). This is
supported by Wittwer and Senkbeil (2008), who argued that for most students,
ICT has a minor or no effect on their academic success.
A statistically significant correlation between students’ interest in ICT and
their use of it both inside and outside of the classroom was found by the study
(H10, H12, H13). Students’ assessments of their ICT proficiency were found to
be significantly correlated with their use of ICT outside of the classroom (H16,
H19).
ICT use both inside and outside of schools was found to be statistically strongly
correlated with students’ perceived autonomy regarding ICT use (AUTICT) (H24,
H26, H27, and H28). The connection between ICT availability at school, the use of
digital devices for activities in school, and students’ perceptions of their autonomy
in relation to ICT usage (H25, H29) was not found to be statistically significant from
this analysis. The association between interest in ICT and their perception of ICT
autonomy (H30) was found to be statistically significant. Similarly, the research by
Park and Weng (2020) and Fraillon et al. (2014) found that students’ interest, perceived ICT competence, and autonomy had a favorable impact on their academic
performance.

However, no significant relationship was revealed that links ICT availability at
school, digital device usage for activities inside schools, usage of digital devices
outside of schools for schoolwork, and students’ interests in ICT (H11, H14, H15).
Furthermore, there was no connection between students’ utilization of ICT in class
and how they perceive their ICT proficiency (H17, H18, H20, H21).
Using digital devices for recreational activities outside the school (ENTUSE) was
statistically significantly correlated with students’ science performance, students’
interest in ICT (INTICT), and students’ perceptions of their competence in using
ICT (COMPICT) (H2, H13, H19). This result was contradicted by Juhaňák et al.
(2018), whose study indicated that in all of the areas investigated, student usage of
ICT for leisure activities while not in school (ENTUSE) was not significant. Likewise, the availability of ICT in schools (ICTSCH) and students’ perceived ICT competency (COMPICT) failed to demonstrate any discernible differences in terms of
academic achievement (Juhaňák et al., 2018).
6.1 Summary and conclusion
The study is significant since the data used are taken from the PISA 2018 database,
a large-scale assessment of high quality involving 600,000 students, which represents around 32 million 15-year-olds that study in private and public schools in the
79 countries partaking in it. Because this study involved only Türkiye data, it is
suggested that further investigation should be extended to include data from other
countries, especially China and OECD (the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development) countries that took part in PISA (the Programme for International Student Assessment) 2018 because China has the highest score in science
performance.
The present study specifies ample awareness of the present Türkiye educational
practices and highlights revisions of policies and curriculum to advance students’
ICT (information and communication technology) use in schools and at home and
students’ science outcomes. Put another way, now that we have a better understanding of the nuanced relationship that exists between students’ use of ICT and their
academic success, we can adjust and adjust how technology is used in classrooms
with the goal of minimizing any unfavorable effects and maximizing any benefits for
students’ science achievement.
Educators and stakeholders are encouraged to take the initiative to design and
develop a curriculum to enhance students’ attitudes toward ICT. In conclusion, most
of the ICT constructs: ICTHOME (use of ICT at home), ICTSCH (the availability of
ICT at school), ICTCLASS (subject-related ICT use during lessons), ENTUSE (use
of technological devices for leisure activities), HOMESCH (use of technological
devices outside of school for schoolwork), USESCH (use of technological devices
for activities in school), INTICT (interest in ICT), COMPICT (perceived ICT competence), and AUTICT (perceived autonomy related to ICT use) in PISA 2018 affect
science achievement and thus must be addressed while designing and developing
curriculum by educators and policymakers.
