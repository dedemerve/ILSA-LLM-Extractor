ABSTRACT

This study explores cross-­national patterns of self-­regulated learning (SRL) and their association with mathematics achievement
among students in six high-­performing East Asian education systems and the OECD average, based on PISA 2022 data from approximately 35,000 students. Contrary to the common assumption that high-­performing systems universally exhibit strong SRL,
the findings reveal notable intra-­regional differences. Korea and Singapore demonstrate high levels of behavioural control and
proactive engagement, whereas Chinese Taipei, Hong Kong (China), and Macao (China) show more limited SRL profiles despite
achieving impressive academic results. These disparities indicate that structural discipline alone is insufficient to cultivate deep
learning; instead, culturally mediated factors significantly influence SRL development. Drawing on socio-­cognitive and cultural-historical theories, the study advocates for a systems-­level reconceptualization of SRL as both a teachable competency and a culturally embedded practice. The findings highlight the need for pedagogical interventions that are tailored to individual learners'
needs, ongoing professional development for educators centred on SRL principles, and educational policies that foster learner
autonomy and agency. The study also emphasises future research directions, including longitudinal studies on SRL development
and the role of digital learning environments, which can mediate and enhance SRL among students.

1   |   Introduction
Self-­regulated learning (SRL) is defined as the proactive, strategic, and metacognitive processes through which learners set
goals, monitor and manage their cognition and motivation,
reflect on outcomes, and adapt their strategies to optimise academic achievement. In recent decades, SRL has emerged
as a central construct in the field of educational psychology,
demonstrating consistent associations with enhanced academic
engagement, resilience, and performance, particularly in mathematics, a domain that demands sustained cognitive effort and
problem-­solving proficiency (Hudesman et al. 2011; Wang and
Sperling 2020). Empirical research supports the effectiveness
of SRL-­focused interventions—emphasising goal setting, planning, self-­monitoring, and motivational regulation—in fostering independent learning behaviours and improving academic

outcomes (Elhusseini et al. 2022; Schunk and DiBenedetto 2020).
Furthermore, the execution of SRL has been demonstrated to
be closely associated with executive functions, including working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. As
demonstrated in the research by Rutherford et al. (2018), these
functions are critical for successfully navigating complex mathematical tasks.
While extant literature on the subject—largely based on
Western educational contexts—supports a strong positive association between SRL and academic success, comparatively less
attention has been paid to how SRL manifests within East Asian
education systems. These contexts, despite their globally recognised academic excellence, are shaped by cultural norms that
diverge significantly from Western individualistic paradigms.

2   |   Literature Review
2.1   |   SRL and Academic Achievement
SRL is widely conceptualised as a dynamic and cyclical process
through which learners set goals, deploy learning strategies,
monitor their progress, and reflect on outcomes to enhance
subsequent performance (Schunk and DiBenedetto 2020). A
substantial body of empirical research has established a positive
association between students' self-­regulatory capacities and academic achievement across subject domains, with particularly
strong effects observed in cognitively demanding areas such as
mathematics.
Evidence from large-­scale quantitative studies and meta-­analytic
syntheses consistently indicates that SRL is a significant predictor of academic performance. For example, Li et al. (2018) synthesised two decades of research and demonstrated that multiple
phases of self-­regulation, including forethought, performance
control, and self-­reflection, were meaningfully associated with
academic outcomes among Chinese learners. Similarly, Rosário
et al. (2013) reported that students' use of SRL strategies was
positively related to mathematics achievement, even after accounting for grade level and study time.
Prior research further suggests that the strength of the SRL–
achievement relationship varies across instructional contexts
and learning environments. In technology-­mediated settings,

metacognitive strategies such as planning and self-­monitoring
have been identified as particularly strong predictors of learning
performance (Lai and Hwang 2021). Likewise, studies of flipped
mathematics courses indicate that students who engage more
actively in self-regulatory behaviours—especially pre-class
preparation and post-­class reflection—tend to achieve higher
academic outcomes than their less self-­regulated peers (Sun
et al. 2018).
Motivational components of SRL play an important role in sustaining this relationship. Empirical modelling studies show
that motivational beliefs, including self-­efficacy and intrinsic
interest, contribute to academic achievement indirectly by supporting students' sustained engagement and effective use of cognitive and metacognitive strategies (Muwonge et al. 2019).
Despite the robust evidence linking SRL to academic achievement, opportunities for developing and enacting self-­regulation
are not evenly distributed across educational systems. Structural
constraints (e.g., limited formative feedback, teacher-­centred
instructional practices, and insufficient instructional scaffolding) may attenuate the extent to which students' self-­regulatory
capacities translate into academic success, particularly in high-pressure or online learning contexts (Seban and Urban 2024;
Vishwakarma and Tyagi 2023). Recent scholarship therefore
emphasises the importance of context-­sensitive instructional designs that explicitly support students' SRL in order to maximise
its contribution to academic achievement.

2.2   |   PISA Framework and Its Role in
Understanding SRL Across Contexts
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
is a large-­scale assessment programme that has gained international recognition. The objective of the assessment is to evaluate the abilities of 15-­year-­old students to apply knowledge in
real-­world contexts, particularly in mathematics, science, and
reading. The PISA mathematics framework places considerable
emphasis on problem-­solving, strategic reasoning, and critical
thinking. These elements exhibit a close alignment with core
SRL processes, including planning, metacognitive awareness,
and adaptive regulation (Stacey 2015).
Although PISA does not explicitly measure SRL or cultural variables, its item design necessitates self-­regulatory competencies,
especially in tasks requiring students to independently structure and monitor their problem-­solving approaches. These embedded SRL demands serve as a valuable proxy for examining
cross-­national differences in students' regulatory behaviours.
A comparative analysis indicates that nations that prioritise
inquiry-based, reflective pedagogies—such as Finland and
Canada—frequently exhibit a propensity to cultivate more
robust SRL competencies. These competencies have been
demonstrated to be associated with elevated PISA performance
(Liang 2010). Conversely, educational systems that prioritise
rote memorization and exam performance may inadvertently
hinder opportunities for SRL development.

contexts, PISA data provide a unique lens through which to

2.3   |   Cultural Dimensions of SRL in East Asian
Contexts
Despite the region's consistent high performance on the PISA,
East Asian education systems are embedded within sociocultural frameworks that depart markedly from Western individualistic models of learning. These systems are rooted in
Confucian heritage, emphasising diligence, respect for hierarchy, collective harmony, and academic perseverance. These
values influence students' engagement with SRL processes in
distinct ways (Tan 2018; Thornton 2014). For instance, although
students may exhibit proficient metacognitive abilities and considerable motivation, behaviours such as seeking assistance or
independently challenging instructional norms are frequently
impeded by apprehensions regarding face-­saving and deference
to authority.
Such systemic and cultural constraints can result in a reliance
on teacher-­directed instruction and performance-­oriented study
habits, which may foster procedural fluency but limit autonomous learning and creative problem-­solving (Cheng et al. 2023;
Jang et al. 2023). The preponderance of high-­stakes examinations further reinforces these tendencies, thereby narrowing the
scope for exploratory learning and reflective thinking (Ee and
Seng 2008; Ho 2017).
Recent reform initiatives across East Asia have begun to acknowledge these limitations, advocating for pedagogical shifts
that blend cultural continuity with SRL-­supportive practices.
These include the introduction of scaffolded autonomy, formative feedback mechanisms, and inquiry-based instructional
models designed to nurture metacognitive engagement and self-directed learning. In order to design effective and culturally resonant interventions, it is imperative to comprehend the cultural
dynamics that facilitate SRL in these contexts.

3   |   Theoretical Framework
3.1   |   SRL and Mathematics Achievement
SRL, as conceptualised in Zimmerman's (2000) cyclical model,
provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how
learners actively manage cognitive, motivational, and behavioural processes to achieve academic success. In mathematics, as an academic domain characterised by abstract reasoning,
cumulative knowledge structures, and frequent problem-­solving
demands, SRL is particularly salient for explaining individual
differences in achievement.
Zimmerman's model delineates SRL as a recursive process
encompassing three interrelated phases: forethought, performance, and self-­reflection. During the forethought phase,
learners set achievement goals, select strategies, and activate
European Journal of Education, 2026

motivational beliefs such as self-efficacy. These processes
influence task engagement and persistence, which are critical for sustained effort in mathematically demanding tasks.
The performance phase involves the enactment of cognitive
and metacognitive strategies, including problem-solving
heuristics, self-monitoring, and time management, all of
which directly affect the accuracy, efficiency, and flexibility
of mathematical reasoning. The self-­reflection phase entails
evaluative judgements of performance outcomes and causal
attributions, which in turn inform future strategy selection
and goal adjustment (Zimmerman 2000).
Within this framework, mathematics achievement is conceptualised not merely as an outcome measure but as the cumulative result of iterative regulatory processes. Empirical
research has consistently demonstrated that students who
effectively deploy SRL strategies exhibit higher levels of mathematical understanding, adaptive problem-­solving, and long-term achievement growth (Chen et al. 2018). Motivational
constructs, particularly self-­efficacy, play a foundational role
in this process by shaping learners' willingness to engage
with challenging mathematical content and to persist in the
face of difficulty (Schunk 1989). High self-­efficacy has been
associated with strategic flexibility, deeper metacognitive engagement, and greater resilience, whereas low self-­efficacy is
linked to avoidance behaviours and superficial strategy use
(Bol et al. 2015; Fauzi and Widjajanti 2018). Intrinsic motivation further supports sustained cognitive engagement and
conceptual understanding, reinforcing the effectiveness of
SRL processes in mathematics learning (Ryan and Deci 2000).
Taken together, this theoretical perspective positions SRL as a
multidimensional mechanism through which learners translate
motivational resources and strategic behaviours into measurable mathematics achievement. The framework thus provides a
conceptual basis for examining how specific SRL components
are differentially associated with mathematics outcomes across
educational contexts.

3.2   |   Cultural and Instructional Determinants
of SRL in East Asia
Although the core processes of SRL are theoretically universal,
their enactment and effectiveness are shaped by cultural norms
and instructional environments. In many East Asian education
systems, collectivist values and Confucian traditions emphasise effort, perseverance, and social responsibility in academic
pursuits, often framing learning as a moral obligation to family
and society rather than as an expression of individual interest
(Tan et al. 2021). Within such contexts, SRL strategies may be
employed primarily to meet external performance expectations,
potentially limiting the internalisation of autonomous regulatory processes (Jang et al. 2023).
Instructional practices frequently reflect these cultural orientations. Teacher-­centred pedagogy, standardised curricula, and
high-­stakes examinations—common features in systems such
as China, Japan, and South Korea—have been associated with
strong procedural competence and high average achievement
levels. However, these structures may constrain opportunities

4   |   Research Methods
4.1   |   Data Source
This study draws on data from the mathematics cycle of PISA
2022, focusing specifically on six East Asian economies that
have demonstrated notable success in this domain: Singapore,
South Korea, Japan, Hong Kong (China), Macao (China), and
Chinese Taipei. Collectively, these regions encompass approximately 35,000 students sampled from a total pool of 690,000
participants across 81 economies worldwide (see Table 1).
Singapore, Hong Kong, and Macao exclusively sampled students
from academic programmes, whereas Japan, South Korea, and
Chinese Taipei included both academic and vocational tracks.
This distinction is indicative of systemic diversity within East
Asia, a factor that may exert influence on both SRL behaviours
and mathematics achievement outcomes.

4.2   |   Measures and Variables
PISA 2022 offers eight plausible value sets for mathematics
achievement, including one overall score and four domain-specific scores—such as Change and Relationships, Quantity,
Space and Shape, and Uncertainty and Data—as well as three
process-­specific domains (Employ, Formulate, Interpret). The
present study focuses on the overall mathematics plausible values (PV1MATH–PV10MATH) to assess general performance
levels in a comprehensive manner.
Two constructs central to SRL, both closely aligned with
the framework of lifelong learning, were operationalised to
capture students' self-­regulatory capacities in mathematics.
These constructs reflect observable learning-­related dispositions and behaviours as measured in PISA 2022, rather than
latent regulatory processes or causal mechanisms. The first
construct, learning control and self-monitoring, represents
students' reported use of metacognitive strategies to regulate
their learning activities. This construct was measured using
10 Likert-­scale items from question ST309 (e.g., ‘I stop to think
before I act’), with response options ranging from strongly
disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). The items emphasise key
dimensions of self-regulation in problem-solving contexts,
including planning ahead, monitoring ongoing performance,
and adjusting behaviour in response to task demands. As
such, this construct captures students' perceived capacity for
cognitive and behavioural regulation during learning, rather
than direct evidence of strategic effectiveness.
The second construct, proactivity toward learning, measures
sustained effort and persistence. The responses were assessed
using eight items from question ST293 (e.g., ‘I actively participate in group discussions’). These responses were aggregated
into the Mathematics Proactive Learning Behaviour Index
(MATHPERS), which exhibited strong internal consistency
(α = 0.82). This index is indicative of adaptive engagement and
goal-­directed effort, which align with the competencies necessary for success both within and beyond formal learning
environments.
Furthermore, the Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS)
index is incorporated as a contextual variable. The ESCS index
is a composite measure derived from parental education, occupation, income, and access to cultural resources. The incorporation of ESCS enables the investigation of socioeconomic
influences, thereby facilitating a more nuanced comprehension
of systemic factors that influence SRL behaviours and educational outcomes.

4.3   |   Data Process and Analysis
A comparative analytical framework is employed to contrast the
behaviours exhibited by students using SRL with mathematics
achievement between East Asian economies and the OECD average. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, are employed to quantify cross-­system differences. To
investigate how SRL strategies influence achievement across the
entire performance spectrum, quantile regression models are
applied, focusing on the 25th and 75th percentiles. This technique captures differential relationships at lower and higher
achievement levels, offering insights into how SRL supports
both struggling and advanced students.
Models are estimated separately for each system to account for
context-­specific educational factors, with ESCS included as a covariate to control for socioeconomic variation. The analysis incorporates sampling weights (W_FSTUWT, W_FSTR1-­80) and the
multiple plausible values framework, ensuring statistical precision
and compliance with PISA's rigorous reporting standards.
This methodological approach is comprehensive in nature and
aims to elucidate the strength and nature of the relationship
between SRL and mathematics achievement across diverse systems and performance levels. The findings of this study provide
valuable implications for policy and pedagogical practice in fostering equitable, self-­regulated learners.

5   |   Results
5.1   |   Students' Controlling of Learning
and Self-­Monitoring Behaviours
The study examines students' self-­reported learning control and
self-­monitoring behaviours across six East Asian education systems and the OECD average. In line with SRL frameworks, the
indicators analysed here—conscientiousness, impulsivity, and
distractibility—are not conceptualised as stable personality traits.
Rather, they are treated as behavioural tendencies that reflect key
European Journal of Education, 2026

components of self-­monitoring and learning control, including response inhibition, attentional regulation, and evaluative checking
during learning activities (Zimmerman 2002; Pintrich 2000).
Specifically, the PISA questionnaire items capture students' habitual approaches to regulating their actions and attention in
academic contexts. Representative items include careful verbal
regulation (e.g., ‘I am careful with what I say to others’), evaluative checking behaviours (e.g., ‘I carefully check my homework
before turning it in’), and spontaneous responding indicative of
lower response inhibition (e.g., ‘I say the first thing that comes to
my mind’). Collectively, these items provide behavioural proxies for students' capacity to monitor and control their learning-related actions rather than dispositional traits.
A comparative analysis of these indicators reveals substantial
regional variation in learning control behaviours (see Figure 1).
Students in Korea reported the highest levels of conscientious
regulation, with 78.8% indicating that they were careful in their
verbal expressions. Singaporean students displayed a similar
pattern, with 72.6% reporting careful communication. These
patterns may be interpreted as consistent with educational
environments that emphasise accuracy, deliberation, and behavioural regulation in academic and social interactions.
In contrast, students from Hong Kong and Macao exhibited higher
levels of impulsive responding, with 67.0% and 66.9% of students,
respectively, reporting that they tend to say the first thing that
comes to mind. From an SRL perspective, such responses suggest
comparatively weaker response inhibition and attentional control,
which are central components of self-­monitoring processes. These
differences point to variation in how learning control behaviours
are emphasised or enacted across systems, though the present data
do not permit causal inferences regarding their origins.
Students from Chinese Taipei occupied an intermediate position within this spectrum. Approximately 62.6% reported exercising caution in verbal communication, and 63.2% indicated
that they carefully check their homework before submission.

where such behaviours are less prevalent.

6   |   Conclusions and Discussions
6.1   |   Uneven Profiles of SRL Across
High-­Achieving Systems
This study reveals pronounced cross-­system variation in profiles of SRL and mathematics achievement among selected East
Asian education systems and the OECD average. Korea and
Singapore consistently exhibit elevated levels of conscientiousness and self-­monitoring, patterns that are positively associated
with high mathematics performance. These findings lend support to established theoretical perspectives that position SRL—
particularly strategic and metacognitive regulation—as a central
mechanism underlying academic success rather than a peripheral learner attribute (Zimmerman 2002; Kizilcec et al. 2017).

6.2   |   Cultural Scripts and Systemic Constraints
on SRL
Interpreting cross-­system differences in SRL requires careful
attention to cultural and institutional contexts. Prior research
has often portrayed East Asian learners as uniformly diligent
and disciplined; however, the present findings point to substantial heterogeneity in how regulatory behaviours are manifested across systems. In Korea and Singapore, stronger SRL
profiles may reflect not only cultural values associated with
effort and perseverance, but also sustained policy initiatives
that promote metacognitive awareness, formative feedback, and
reflective learning practices within formal curricula (Ng 2023;
Ha et al. 2023; Wu 2019). Such environments may support both
external accountability and internalised regulation, consistent
with self-­determination theory (Ryan and Deci 2000).
By contrast, the comparatively weaker SRL profiles observed
in Hong Kong and Macao, despite high achievement levels,
may be indicative of learning environments characterised
by high-stakes assessment and strong conformity pressures.
These conditions can prioritise performance optimization over
learner autonomy and reflective engagement (Dweck 2006; Li
et al. 2023). Chinese Taipei appears to occupy an intermediate position, combining strong academic outcomes with more
limited evidence of motivational autonomy or strategic self-regulation. Existing scholarship suggests that exam-oriented
pedagogies and didactic instructional traditions may constrain
opportunities for SRL development in such contexts (Hallinger
and Lu 2013; Rappleye and Komatsu 2018; Zhao 2021).
It is important to acknowledge the interpretive boundaries
of these conclusions. Although the present study considers
students' economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS) as a
European Journal of Education, 2026

background variable, cross-­system differences in specific ESCS
components were not explicitly modelled in relation to SRL
profiles. Consequently, socioeconomic factors may partially account for the observed variation across systems. Future research
that more fully exploits PISA's background questionnaire data
could clarify the extent to which family resources, parental education, or school-­level composition interact with institutional
practices to shape SRL–achievement patterns. In this sense, the
present findings should be viewed as theory-­informed interpretations grounded in observed associations rather than definitive
explanations of causal mechanisms.

6.3   |   Policy and Pedagogical Implications
Despite these limitations, the findings offer several implications
for educational policy and practice. First, SRL should be conceptualised not as an innate learner disposition but as a set of
competencies that can be systematically cultivated through instruction. Targeted scaffolding in goal setting, monitoring, and
self-­evaluation is particularly important for students operating
in highly structured or performance-­oriented systems (Schunk
and Zimmerman 2007).
Second, even within high-­achieving systems, policymakers and
educators should critically examine the conditions under which
SRL is fostered. In Korea and Singapore, continued attention
is needed to ensure that efforts to promote discipline and accountability do not inadvertently suppress autonomy, creativity,
or adaptive expertise (Jonassen 2000). In Chinese Taipei, Hong
Kong, and Macao, recalibrating assessment practices and classroom pedagogy toward formative feedback and student agency
may help support deeper engagement with learning processes.
Finally, education systems must reflect on the implicit messages
they convey about learning and success. Whether students are
encouraged to reflect on failure, take intellectual risks, and
assume ownership of their learning trajectories has important
implications not only for achievement outcomes but also for
learners' long-term motivation and well-being (Duncan and
Murnane 2011; Gorski 2013).

6.4   |   Limitations and Future Research
When interpreting the findings, it is imperative to consider the
limitations of the study. The cross-­sectional nature of the dataset
imposes limitations on the inferences that can be drawn about
developmental processes. To this end, longitudinal research is
necessary to trace the evolution of SRL behaviours across educational transitions and assess how they interact with academic
achievement over time (Mullis et al. 2020; Zimmerman 2013).
An additional promising avenue for exploration pertains to the
examination of the affordances and constraints inherent within
digital learning environments. While online platforms offer
opportunities for autonomy and personalised feedback, they
may also exacerbate inequities in SRL readiness and digital
self-­efficacy (Kizilcec et al. 2017; Wong et al. 2019). Subsequent
studies should examine how technological tools mediate SRL
differently across cultural and socio-­economic strata.

academic success in some contexts may be sustained through
instructional structures and assessment regimes that place
stronger emphasis on external regulation and procedural compliance. Importantly, this interpretation should be understood
as explanatory rather than causal, as the present analyses do not
directly model the mechanisms through which such patterns
emerge.
