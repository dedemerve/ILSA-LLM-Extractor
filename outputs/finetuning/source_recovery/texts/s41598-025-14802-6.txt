Discovering action insights from
large-scale assessment log data
using machine learning
Minyoung Yun1,4, Minjeong Jeon2 & Heyoung Yang3
This study introduces a novel machine learning algorithm that combines natural language processing
techniques, such as Word2Vec and Doc2Vec, with neural networks to identify and validate significant
actions within human action sequences. Using the 2012 Program for the International Assessment of
Adult Competencies dataset, the algorithm visualizes and analyzes action sequences in a 2D vector
space to uncover high-impact behaviors that influence performance. The methodology, validated
across two problem sets (“Party Invitation” and “Club Membership”), successfully distinguishes
performance groups by focusing on critical actions, leading to enhanced classification accuracy (up to
94.6%) and clustering coherence (silhouette score of 0.491). This approach demonstrates potential
applications in personalized education, healthcare diagnostics, and consumer behavior prediction,
advancing the understanding of human behavior through digital footprints.

Keywords Human action sequence, Meaningful actions, Machine learning, Natural language processing,
PIAAC log data

Understanding human behavior through digital interactions has become increasingly critical across various
fields, including education, healthcare, and marketing. As human interactions continue to migrate to digital
platforms, the ability to analyze and interpret behavioral patterns has transitioned from theoretical exploration to
practical application, enabled by large-scale datasets. Action sequences, representing series of user interactions,
provide a unique lens through which decision-making and performance can be examined.
The availability of vast digital interaction data allows researchers to uncover nuanced patterns and
relationships that were previously inaccessible. For instance, analyzing sequences of actions performed during
problem-solving tasks can reveal significant insights into cognitive strategies, decision-making processes, and
behavioral tendencies. This information has profound implications for improving outcomes in domains such as
personalized education1–5, early medical diagnostics6–12, and customer behavior prediction13–22.
Traditional analytical methods, such as adjacent action analysis or n-grams23–25, often fall short of capturing
the complexity of non-linear and non-consecutive relationships within sequences. These limitations hinder the
ability to uncover deeper behavioral insights necessary for accurate predictions and meaningful interventions.
Additionally, conventional methods struggle to integrate diverse and dynamic datasets, further limiting their
applicability in real-world scenarios.
Recent research has applied natural language processing (NLP) and vectorization techniques to improve
the representation and interpretation of log data. For example, Xiao et al. (2024) proposed a vectorizationbased framework for system log parsing that enables deeper pattern discovery35. Similarly, Kampik et al. (2023)
demonstrated the utility of NLP for extracting semantic insights from business process logs, while RamosGutiérrez et al. (2021) emphasized improving log quality through linguistic analysis for more robust downstream
inference37. These works highlight the expanding role of NLP in understanding behavioral patterns from event
logs.
This study addresses these gaps by introducing a novel algorithm that leverages advanced natural language
processing (NLP) techniques, specifically Word2Vec and Doc2Vec, combined with neural networks26,27
and random forest28. Neural network is a recently popular machine learning algorithm, and random forest
is a traditional algorithm. Neural networks are powerful machine learning tools designed to classify data
by identifying complex patterns. Modeled after the structure of the human brain, they consist of layers of
interconnected nodes that learn from data through training. In this study, neural networks were employed for
classification, utilizing their capacity to model non-linear relationships and adapt to various input features.

Using the 2012 Program for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) dataset31, this

Methods

Data collection

The study used the 2012 PIAAC dataset, focusing on two problem sets: “Party Invitation” and "Club Membership."
The “Party Invitation” dataset involved 1394 participants who performed 35 distinct actions, while the “Club
Membership” dataset included 1345 participants carrying out 25 distinct actions. In the “Party Invitation”,
participants performed actions sequentially to solve the problem, selecting from the 35 available actions.
Similarly, in the “Club Membership” task, participants performed actions in sequence from the 25 actions. The
total number of actions ranged from 10 to 348 (with a median of 31) for the “Party Invitation” task, and from
10 to 253 (with a median of 48) for the “Club Membership” task. Figure 1 Histogram of the number of actions
of `Party Invitation’ and `Club Membership’ problem sets across participants shows the distribution of the total
number of actions across participates for both tasks. The participant age and gender details are provided in
Table 1. The age groups in the 10 s and 60 s and above are slightly smaller, but the remaining age groups are
fairly evenly distributed. The dataset is also balanced in terms of gender. Participant scores are categorized into
performance levels in Tables 2 and 3, which form the basis for identifying high-impact actions. Tables 4 and 5
provide examples of action sequences for the score0 and score3 groups, as well as for the correct and incorrect
groups.
To ensure consistency and reliability, the dataset was preprocessed to remove incomplete logs or sequences
with missing actions. The preprocessed data were then stratified by performance levels to facilitate comparisons
between high- and low-performing groups. These problem sets provided diverse contexts for evaluating the
methodology’s robustness and generalizability across different types of tasks.

Feature verification

To represent entire sequences holistically, Doc2Vec was employed, specifically the Distributed Bag-of-Words
(PV-DBOW) architecture26,30. This approach facilitated sequence-level comparisons by embedding sequences
based on their constituent actions, enabling deeper insights into group-level differences. Hyperparameters,
including vector sizes and training epochs, were optimized to enhance embedding quality, like a vector size of
100, a window size of 5, training epochs of 100, ensuring robust feature selection across diverse contexts27.
To validate the significance of identified actions, a structured verification process was conducted using three
distinct scenarios. In the first scenario, original sequences containing all actions were analyzed as a baseline.
The second scenario focused exclusively on sequences comprising only the identified meaningful actions, which
were determined through clustering and visualization30,33. In the third scenario, sequences were modified to
exclude these meaningful actions, allowing for an assessment of their absence on clustering coherence and
classification accuracy.
The validation process involved calculating silhouette scores to measure the quality of clustering within
the embedded vector space32. Higher silhouette scores indicated better-defined clusters, signifying clearer
distinctions between high- and low-performing groups27. Additionally, sequence embeddings were visualized
to qualitatively assess spatial separation between clusters, further corroborating the quantitative findings33.
We also applied Neural Network and Random Forest for group classification, and verified how well the group
classification was performed depending on the presence or absence of the features suggested in feature selection.

Feature verification—Doc2Vec, NN and RF result
Consequently, we used the mail-moving actions to create three distinct case studies to assess the significance of
mail-moving actions in distinguishing between the score 0 and 3 groups: original sequences (case1), subsequences
consisting only of mail-moving actions (case2), and subsequences consisting of actions without mail-moving
actions (case3). Each case, except for case 1, involves subsequences. Doc2Vec is then used to generate vectors
from these subsequences. Silhouette score for each case study is presented in Table 6. Visualization of sequences
of actions by Doc2Vec is presented in Fig. 5. In Fig. 5, purple dots indicate sequences by participants in the score
0 group and yellow dots indicate sequences by participants in the score 3 group. In Case 1, which encompasses
all 35 actions, the degree of separation, as evidenced by a silhouette score of 0.36, can be visually observed
in Fig. 5a. Case 2, focusing solely on mail-moving actions, demonstrates an enhanced degree of separation,
reflected in a silhouette score of 0.491, as presented in Fig. 5b. Case 3, which excludes mail-moving actions,
exhibits a lower degree of separation between the two groups compared to Case 1 and Case 2, indicated by a
reduced silhouette score of 0.216 (see Fig. 5c).
Although the silhouette score may appear relatively low, this can be reasonably attributed to two key
factors: distortions introduced by dimensionality reduction techniques and variations in intra-cluster density.
In particular, dimensionality reduction methods such as t-SNE or PCA—commonly used for visualization—
often distort the original high-dimensional distance relationships, leading to discrepancies between visual
interpretation and quantitative clustering metrics. Additionally, intra-cluster density variation refers to the
phenomenon where some clusters are tightly packed while others are more dispersed. This heterogeneity
increases the average intra-cluster distance, thereby reducing the silhouette score, even if the clusters are visually
well-separated.
Moreover, due to the inherently noisy and overlapping nature of behavioral log data, it is challenging to
obtain ideally high silhouette scores in unsupervised clustering settings. Nevertheless, among the three
experimental cases, Case 2 clearly exhibits the most distinct cluster separation in the 2D projection, and from
a relative performance perspective, it demonstrates the most coherent and interpretable clustering structure.
These findings provide empirical evidence supporting the validity of the clustering configuration employed in
Case 2.

Discussion and conclusion

This study introduced a novel algorithm leveraging NLP techniques, specifically Word2Vec and Doc2Vec, to
identify and validate meaningful actions in action sequences using the 2012 PIAAC dataset. These NLP methods
enabled the vectorization of individual actions and sequences, facilitating visual inspections to uncover key
action sequences. The algorithm was tested on two problem sets, successfully identifying meaningful actions
through the visual examination of word vectors in a 2D space.
A significant advantage of employing Word2Vec is its ability to uncover action relationships independently of
their relative positioning, which is challenging for conventional methods like n-grams. The proposed algorithm
demonstrated the effectiveness of combining action sequence vectorization with neural networks for binary
classification, as evidenced by improved prediction accuracy and clustering coherence. Silhouette scores
confirmed the spatial differentiation between high-score and low-score groups, reinforcing the robustness of the
identified meaningful actions.
In both problem sets, our methodology successfully identified actions critical to participant performance. For
the “Party Invitation” problem, three key actions were identified among 35 behaviors, distinguishing participants
scoring 0 and 3. Similarly, in the “Club Membership” problem, four key actions were identified among 25
behaviors, effectively differentiating correct and incorrect groups. These findings suggest that individuals who
do not perform these critical actions well are significantly more likely to achieve lower scores.

Implications and applications

The findings of this study demonstrate substantial implications across various fields, showcasing the versatility of
the proposed methodology. In education, the algorithm enables the development of adaptive feedback systems
that guide individuals by identifying critical actions they fail to perform, thereby improving learning outcomes.
In healthcare, the approach provides a framework for detecting behavioral patterns linked to conditions such as
depression or dementia, offering potential for early intervention and personalized treatment plans. Similarly, in
marketing, the algorithm can analyze clickstream data to predict consumer purchasing intentions, optimizing
customer targeting and enhancing campaign effectiveness. These applications underline the broader utility of
action sequence analysis, providing a pathway for translating behavioral data into actionable insights across
disciplines.
Furthermore, our method is computationally efficient and suitable for real-time applications, even when
executed on moderately resourced hardware. In practice, each experiment was completed within half a day, and
with optimized data handling, we anticipate scalability to datasets involving tens or even hundreds of thousands
of sequences. This efficiency supports the applicability of our approach to large-scale behavioral data analysis
across various domains.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, this study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. The analysis relies on the
Belgian PIAAC dataset, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other cultural or demographic
contexts. Additionally, the sequences analyzed were relatively short and structured, which may not fully
represent the complexity of real-world behaviors. Temporal dynamics, such as transitions between actions, were
not explicitly modeled, potentially overlooking critical sequential patterns. Furthermore, the computational
cost associated with vectorization and clustering processes raises concerns about scalability when applying
the algorithm to larger datasets or real-time applications. Addressing these limitations is essential for broader
adoption and improved robustness of the methodology.
One important limitation of this study is the potential bias in action sequence patterns arising from
demographic differences among participants. While prior research has shown that factors such as age, gender,
and digital familiarity can influence interaction behaviors e.g.34, our analysis could not incorporate subgroupspecific comparisons due to insufficient demographic labeling in the available dataset. Future studies should
incorporate fairness-aware modeling approaches to assess whether the algorithm performs equitably across
diverse user populations and mitigate any unintended bias.

Future research directions

To overcome these limitations and enhance the applicability of the proposed approach, future research should
focus on several key areas. First, the algorithm should be validated using datasets from diverse cultural and
demographic backgrounds to ensure robustness and generalizability. In particular, subgroup analyses based
on age, gender, and digital literacy could help identify whether certain demographic groups exhibit distinct
behavioral patterns, thereby improving the fairness and interpretability of the model. Second, predictive modeling
using advanced temporal architectures—such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks and Transformerbased models—should be explored to capture long-range dependencies and improve outcome prediction
based on action sequences. These architectures may also provide greater expressiveness in modeling complex
behavioral transitions over time. Third, the methodology should be extended to analyze unstructured or longer
action sequences, which are more representative of real-world scenarios. Additionally, improving computational
efficiency through algorithmic optimization will address scalability concerns, enabling applications to larger
datasets and real-time settings. Finally, integrating multimodal data such as text inputs, physiological signals,
action lengths, and timestamps per action enriches analytics and expands the scope of behavioral insights,
opening the way to innovative applications in human behavior modeling and predictive models.

Conclusion

This study makes a significant contribution to the field of human behavior analysis by introducing a novel NLPdriven methodology that captures the complexity of action sequences. By uncovering meaningful actions, the
proposed algorithm enhances prediction accuracy and provides actionable insights across diverse domains.
Through continued refinement and expansion of this approach, future research can unlock further potential
in understanding and influencing human behavior, enabling innovative solutions in education, healthcare, and
beyond.
