Trust in Political Institutions (TIPI) is important for political stability as well as political change.
We integrate two theories into the conceptualisation of political socialisations of students with
and without immigration backgrounds: The theory of social capital conceptualises trust as a
generalisation of positive social relationships, whereas the theory of the critical citizen implies
that institutions need knowledgeable and scrutinising citizens for democratic improvements. This
paper investigates the role of the school setting for political socialisation concerning TIPI using
weighted German data from the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study 2016
(N = 1451 students in 59 classes; 52% were girls; mean age was 14.29; 40% of participants had
immigration backgrounds). Students with immigration backgrounds reported relatively worse
social relationships and bestowed less TIPI compared to their peers without immigration backgrounds. As predicted by the theory of social capital, all groups of students bestowed more TIPI if
they experienced positive relationships at school to a higher degree. A weaker predictive power
of civic knowledge on TIPI for students with immigration background supports notions of the
critical citizen. Results indicate that social relationships are relevant for the formation of TIPI of
all students, while the role of civic knowledge varies between social groups.

1. Introduction
The persistence of democracies is never a given and in recent years a growing number of scholars analysed and worried about the
decline of trust in democratic institutions (Foa & Mounk, 2016). Trust in political institutions (TIPI) is an important resource of
political systems and can be deﬁned as cognitive and aﬀective belief in the benevolence and capacities of political institutions (Norris,
2017). Political institutions are organisations that carry the political powers of a regime (e.g., police, courts of justice, parliaments).
Distrust in political institutions can function as correctives if political leaders ignore the interests of groups. Currently, in many
democracies, populist parties utilise scepticism against those institutions to gain traction (Norris & Inglehart, 2019, pp. 257–293).
Despite a potentially functional shakeup, they are also able to undermine the civic culture with a general scepticism towards elites
and the inclusion of immigrants or minorities (Norris & Inglehart, 2019, pp. 409–442).
The discourse about this democratic challenge highlights the question about the resources of TIPI. Following political culture
approaches (Easton, 1965), TIPI is not only based on current experiences, but also on political socialisation in a speciﬁc political
context. We understand political socialisation as “the meaning-making process of being and becoming (a) political (subject)”
(Andersson, 2019). More speciﬁcally, political socialisation refers to the process in which people negotiate their role(s) within the

and discrimination (Hahn-Laudenberg & Achour, 2019; Röder & Mühlau, 2012), their cohort (Fine, Kan, & Cauﬀman, 2019), and
political climate (Kim, 2019; Lauglo, 2013). TIPI is important for the persistence of political systems (Norris, 1999), yet little is
known about the relevance of political socialisation during adolescence for the formation and bestowal of TIPI. Researchers repeatedly found diﬀerences between people with and without immigration backgrounds (e.g., Adman & Strömblad, 2015; Röder &
Mühlau, 2012; Wilkes & Wu, 2019). Children of people who migrated further tend to bestow less TIPI than their non-immigrant peers
(Röder & Mühlau, 2011).
Students with immigration backgrounds can be seen as a vulnerable group as they have to reconcile diﬀerent cultural identities.
They depend more strongly on positive interactions at school than their peers (Göbel & Preusche, 2019), but schools are not fully
prepared to support them (Schachner, Juang, Moﬃtt, & van de Vijver, 2018). School can be a tool to reduce inequalities and integrate
all students in the political system (Deimel, Hoskins, & Abs, 2020; Fend, 2009), but there is little knowledge about school related
variables concerning the bestowal of TIPI in students. This paper goes beyond previous research by applying two theoretical frameworks to the political socialisation of students to explain the diﬀerential bestowal of TIPI by students with and without immigration backgrounds while regarding aspects of system stability, system change, and the students' role in the political system.
To answer the question, which school related variables inﬂuence the bestowal of TIPI by students with and without immigration
backgrounds, this article draws on two theories: The theory of social capital and the critical citizen theory. The ﬁrst one is useful to
explain which factors may function to ensure a baseline of trust in societies. The second is concerned with the deserved bestowal of
TIPI and the development of political systems. We use both theories to explain how the school context may inﬂuence the political
socialisation of students with and without immigration backgrounds regarding their bestowal of TIPI. In a next step we will use data
from the International Civic and Citizenship Educations Study 2016 (ICCS 2016) to empirically investigate the role of the school
context for the political socialisation.
1.1. Social integration and social capital
School is seen as a central agent for the political socialisation of adolescents, aiming to integrate them into the political system
(Fend, 2009). In democracies, political integration is the process through which adolescents are enabled and empowered to guide
their own political development and to assume their roles as emerging active citizens. Building on Esser (2001), Hahn-Laudenberg
and Achour (2019) diﬀerentiate between three forms of political integration, each interacting with each other and fostering diﬀerent
aspects of students' political development. Cognitive integration includes teaching and learning about politics and democracy to foster
competencies related to civic citizenship skills. Structural and social integration relates to the opportunities that schools can provide
for students to participate in representative structures and political discussions as well as opportunities for fostering social relationships. In doing so, structural and social integration is intended to promote external and internal political eﬃcacy. Finally, the
emotional and identity dimension of political integration involves opportunities for developing civic identities, values, and attitudes –
such as TIPI – that are relevant for the stability of political regimes.
Social interactions at school are a tool of political integration and an aspect of political socialisation. A feeling of safety and
positive social relationships in school are vital for the formation of political attitudes (Flanagan, 2013; Ziemes & GutzwillerHelfenﬁnger, 2019). The quality of the relationships between teachers and students impacts on learning, motivation, and performance (Konishi, Hymel, Zumbo, & Li, 2010). Teachers' inﬂuence shapes the school climate and the quality of students' social relationships. As important adult agents of socialisation, teachers can also act as role models. Students themselves are also important
partners for each other in their respective political development: they interact with each other at the same hierarchical level and see
each other's opinions as more authentic and less prescriptive than those of adults. All these social interactions support the development of socio-cognitive abilities such as perspective-taking (Ferreira, Azevedo, & Menezes, 2012). As will be discussed, the working
assumption underlying this paper is that experiencing multiple positive social interactions generates trust.
The theory of social capital is useful to analyse the importance of social relationships and focuses on trust as form of social capital,
which is highly valuable for societies. The concept used here is informed by the works of Coleman (1988) and Putnam (1995) who
aimed to prerequisites of cooperation between individuals in societies. Social relationships, rules, and norms inﬂuence the strength of
social links that add up to social capital. Those social links create trust among the people in the community. Trust, in general, can be
understood as “the belief that ‘others’ are fair, that they will not take advantage of us, although they could” (Flanagan, 2013, p. 169;
italics in the original) – i.e., ‘trust’ entails a general belief in non-hostility. Trust is a precondition for successful human cooperation.
People who trust each other need fewer precautions and resources can be shared and used more eﬀectively. Social capital is therefore
seen as an unequivocal desirable resource for organisations and societies (Flanagan, 2013, pp. 161–196; Putnam, 2007).
1.2. Political support and the critical citizen
Trust in political incumbents and institutions can be conceptualised as one aspect of political support (Norris, 2011). The concept
of political support encompasses attitudes and behaviours that are relevant for the persistence of political systems, such as
democracies. Here, persistence does not merely imply stability but a notion of dynamic change in political systems as they respond to
the needs and desires of their citizens (Easton, 1975). The theory of political support diﬀerentiates between diﬀuse and speciﬁc
aspects. More diﬀuse aspects, such as values and identities, tend to be more stable; and more speciﬁc aspects, such as attitudes
towards institutions and oﬃce holders (e.g., TIPI), tend to be more subject to (re-)evaluation in light of changing events. While these

aspects of political support interact with each other, they do not determine one another.
The question of political support awarded by adolescents matters both conceptually and empirically. Conceptually, adolescents
are often thought of as emerging citizens (Ziemes, Hahn-Laudenberg, Batista, & Abs, 2017) or even as young citizens (Flanagan,
2013), even though they lack many of the rights that adult citizens enjoy, such as active and passive electoral rights. Torney-Purta
(2004) ﬁnds that in adolescents, attitudes such as TIPI are often quite similar to the attitudes of adults, and she concludes that “by the
age of 14 many young people are already members of the political culture they share with adults” (p. 472). Other researchers stress
that political attitudes are more malleable in adolescents than in adults and conclude that it is therefore important to be aware of how
students form their political attitudes and identities (Flanagan, 2013; Meeus, 2011).
TIPI awarded by immigrants and their descendants depends in part on their speciﬁc expectations. Adults who just migrated from a
country with less trustworthy institutions tend to trust the institutions of their new country of residence more than people who did
not migrate (Röder & Mühlau, 2012). However, this higher level of trust seems to manifest itself only during a certain grace period.
Over time, as these individuals' expectations of institutions rise and expectations become more similar to those of people without
immigration backgrounds, the immigrants' level of TIPI decreases. A similar grace period is not observed in the children of immigrants, who are likely to have the same expectations as their peers without immigration backgrounds. Accordingly, empirical
studies suggest that children of immigrants bestow less TIPI than their peers without immigration backgrounds (Adman & Strömblad,
2015; Vera-Toscano, Blasko, & Dinis da Costa, 2018). Immigrants and their children are more likely to be confronted with negative
experiences, such as marginalisation and discrimination, than individuals without immigration backgrounds are. The resulting sense
of low security, unfairness, and discrimination can be used to explain the decrease in TIPI in adult immigrants (Röder & Mühlau,
2011; Wilkes & Wu, 2019).
Bestowing less TIPI can be sensible for individuals and productive for the political system overall. If trust is awarded undeservedly, it serves neither the individual nor the political system; after all, “[t]rust is not gullibility” (Flanagan, 2013, p. 173).
Ignoring problems within political institutions can wake support for populist parties (Norris & Inglehart, 2019). Norris (1999, 2011)
developed the theory of the critical citizen to explain when and how democratic systems beneﬁt from a dynamic of trust and distrust in
political institutions, and she supports her theory with empirical analyses. In short, TIPI is, and should be, bestowed following
scrutiny of the institutions in a political system. If these institutions are imperfect – and all institutions are imperfect to some extent –,
citizens should withhold some trust until the institutions improve. Fuchs (2002) elaborates on this notion and suggests that from a
system persistence perspective, diﬀuse types of support (values and identities) which uphold the system are generally desirable, while
more speciﬁc types of support (trust in institutions and oﬃce-holders) should be variable to ensure institutional accountability.
Adolescents can be critical citizens already. Younger students tend to bestow generalised trust more or less solely based on the
quality of relationships they experience; with increasing age and socio-cognitive abilities, the relevance of social relationships for
bestowing trust decreases, albeit without disappearing completely (Flanagan, 2013, pp. 173–174). Also, students learn to bestow
trust less generalised and distinguish between authorities (Fine et al., 2019). Lauglo (2013) used ICCS 2009 data and found that the
level of students' civic knowledge correlates more positively with TIPI in countries with better indicators of state functioning, such as
less corruption or a higher Human Development Index (HDI). Students with little civic knowledge bestow trust more indiscriminately
while those with more civic knowledge bestow their trust more adequately. Within the framework of the critical citizen theory, the
civic knowledge should be the instrument that people use to scrutinize institutions.
Students may bestow trust based on scrutiny and the social experiences they make. Both sources of trust can be regarded as
desirable and problematic, depending on the theory at hand. The tension between these points of view can be decreased if we assume
that neither inﬂuence is absolute but that each has some impact on the formation of TIPI during adolescence. In this way, the theories
complement each other. TIPI is desirable for system persistence, but only to a degree that still allows students to hold institutions
accountable and to work towards improvements or necessary changes at the system level. This is in line with the idea of system
persistence. The diﬀerential relevance of these aspects deserves further empirical attention.
1.3. Further predictors for TIPI
Gender and socioeconomic status (SES) are background variables that researchers often control for. While neither has been found
to have a great inﬂuence on the bestowal of social trust (Flanagan & Stout, 2010), the economic disadvantage of people with
immigration backgrounds is discussed as one important reason for the diﬀerences in the extent to which TIPI is bestowed (Röder &
Mühlau, 2012). Wilkes and Wu (2019) argue and ﬁnd that the discrimination through racializing (marking certain groups as `other´
based on their supposed background) partly mediates the trust gaps between people with and without immigration backgrounds.
ICCS 2016 employs a single item on the perception of discrimination in the country of survey. While this is not a variable which is
shaped by the school context and therefore is not included in the focus of the present research, it is an important contexts for students'
political socialisation (Röder & Mühlau, 2011).
National identity is the most diﬀuse aspect of political support and does interact with the formation of TIPI. Civic and citizenship
education distinguishes between diﬀerent forms of national identity. National identity is appraised positively only in models of civic
conceptions in which it is connected to rights and duties rather than ancestry or ethnicity (Ziemes, Hahn-Laudenberg, & Abs, 2019).
Berg and Hjerm (2010) ﬁnd that such forms of civic national identity have a positive impact on TIPI. The critical citizen theory
depends on the premise that high diﬀuse support and low speciﬁc support will lead to concrete action(s) to spark institutional
improvements with the aim of decreasing the dissonance between positive identity and low trust. Yet individuals may also decrease
cognitive dissonances by being more trusting and less punitive towards the politicians with whom they identify (Halmburger,
Rothmund, Schulte, & Baumert, 2012). These approaches are not mutually exclusive because reciprocal processes may be at work.

2. Hypotheses
The previous sections allow us to derive hypotheses to conﬁgure our analysis of the ICCS 2016 data. The research reviewed above
found that students with immigration backgrounds bestow less TIPI than their peers without immigration backgrounds. We will
investigate if this result holds true in Germany, North Rhine-Westphalia (H1.1). We will also investigate the quality of social relationships at school and assume that students with immigration backgrounds experience fewer positive social relationships at school
than students without immigration backgrounds (H1.2).
Next, we will examine the relevance for TIPI of types of social relationships at school for students with and without immigration
backgrounds. Based on the theory of social capital outlined above, we will assume that the relationships of students with each other
and with teachers will be positively related to TIPI (H2.1 & H2.2), while victimisation will negatively predict TIPI (H2.3).
Finally, we will consider the roles of perceived discrimination against immigrants within a country, of civic knowledge, and of
national identity in the formation of TIPI. We would expect that perceived discrimination will predict that less TIPI will be bestowed,
especially by students with immigration backgrounds, as the perception of being discriminated against would imply that institutions
are failing to prevent such discrimination. As the experience of discrimination by immigrants can further be regarded as a negative
indicator of state functioning in a democracy, we will assume that discrimination perceived by individuals with immigration
backgrounds will be connected to less TIPI (H3.1).
Lauglo (2013) has shown that students with more civic knowledge judge and praise institutions based on higher levels of scrutiny
than those with less civic knowledge. If we transfer this ﬁnding to diﬀerent social groups, then civic knowledge should be less
positively connected to TIPI in marginalised groups than it is in non-marginalised groups. Therefore, we would expect that the
relationship between civic knowledge and TIPI diﬀers for students with and without immigration backgrounds; speciﬁcally, we
would expect there to be a more positive relationship between civic knowledge and TIPI for students without immigration backgrounds (H3.2).
National identity will be introduced to control for diﬀuse political support. As national identity is an aspect of political support,
we will assume that it will positively predict TIPI in all groups (H3.3).
Assuming that TIPI is not bestowed based merely on group membership but based on experiences and judgements, we will explore
whether the variable ‘immigration background’ can sustain its predictive power even if the variables introduced above are controlled
for (RQ4).

3. Methods
Our analysis draws on data from ICCS 2016, which is after 1999 and 2009 the most recent international study on civic and
citizenship education conducted by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA). 24 educational systems in diﬀerent countries in Asia, Europe and South-America participated. In some countries with multiple education
systems, only one education system took part (i.e., Flanders – Belgium, North Rhine-Westphalia – Germany). ICCS focuses on the
preparation of students in democracies; therefore, all democratic educational systems can participate in the study. Reports and
datasets on all study cycles can be accessed via the IEA homepage (IEA, 2020; Schulz, Ainley, Fraillon, Losito, & Agrusti, 2016). For
the present analyses, we use the subsample from North Rhine-Westphalia (NRW). The federal state has over 17 million inhabitants
and is Germany's biggest constituent state by population.

3.1. Sample
ICCS 2016 was conﬁgured to provide representative samples of students in the grade in which students' average age was
13.5 years or over, which, in Germany, corresponds to Year Eight. In 2016, one in three students of all ages in NRW had an
immigration background (IT.NRW, 2017). Therefore, the sample is particularly suitable for investigating the eﬀects of immigration
backgrounds on TIPI. In NRW, the realised sample consisted of 1451 students in 59 secondary schools (female = 52%; mean
age = 14.29 years; immigration background = 40%). This constitutes approximately one third of the projected sample. In each
selected and participating school, one Year Eight class was randomly selected and assessed. The data was weighted by strata and it
can, with some caution, be considered to provide an approximation of a representative sample of Year Eight students in NRW
(Ziemes, Jasper, Deimel, Hahn-Laudenberg, & Abs, 2017). Written consent from a guardian was a prerequisite for each students'
participation.
Table 1 displays the descriptive statistics for the sample. The category ‘immigration background’ was created based on three items
asking the students if they themselves, or their female or male guardian, were born outside of the country of survey. Three groups
were created based on the available variables. If students reported to have been born outside of Germany, they were categorised as
‘First Generation’ (N = 69), while students who reported that they were born in Germany and that at least one guardian was born
outside of Germany were categorised as ‘Second Generation’ (N = 504). Finally, students who did not report a guardian or themselves to have been born abroad were categorised as ‘Non-Immigrant’ (N = 878). The sample size of the ‘First Generation’ group is
too small to allow for reliable estimations of population values; therefore the group was omitted from regression analytics. The
reported means for this group should not be interpreted as population values.

3.2. Procedure
Students were assessed in full classes. First, a timed test (45 min) was administered to measure the students' civic knowledge.
After a break, students ﬁlled out student questionnaires that included questions on sociodemographic background, attitudes, identities, and participation on the one hand and civic related school processes on the other.
For the analysis presented in this paper, means, correlations, and linear regressions were calculated with the IDB Analyzer (IEA,
2018), which provides macros for SPSS to estimate population values. Weights and Jackknife sampling variables were employed to
correct for the sampling error, thereby attenuating an underestimation of p-values. With Jackknife variables, the realised sample is
used to estimate the estimation error of population values by matching schools and comparing the results of repeated analyses in
which single schools are replaced. For Germany, sampling strata for selecting classes for participation were school type in accordance
with the secondary school track system, and proportion of students with migration backgrounds in school (more vs less than 30%). All
secondary school tracks except those for children with special needs are included in the stratiﬁed sample. For further information, see
the technical report on ICCS 2016 (Schulz, Carstens, Losito, & Fraillon, 2018).

3.3. Measures
For all scales, the IEA provides values based on Weighted Likelihood Estimations (WLE) of partial credit models (Schulz et al.,
2018, p. 117). The WLE scales have a decreased bias and allow for comparisons across populations and study cycles. In the
2009 cycle, all the scales are calibrated to have an international mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10. The scales which were
used in ICCS 2009 and ICCS 2016 were calibrated based on the mean and standard deviation of ICCS 2009. The speciﬁcs can be found
in the technical report (Schulz et al., 2018).
Trust in Political Institutions (TIPI) was measured using six items of the study. Students were asked to rate on a four-point Likert
scale (‘not at all’ to ‘completely’) the extent to which they trusted the national government, the local government of their town or city,
the courts of justice, the police, political parties, and the national parliament, respectively.
A national composite indicator of socioeconomic status (SES) is provided by the IEA and consists of three elements: The guardians'
occupation (according to the International Standard Classiﬁcation of Occupations 08), guardians' education, and number of books in
the home. The SES is z-standardised at the national level. Gender was assessed by one question with two options (‘boy’ and ‘girl’).
Three scales were used to investigate social interactions at school: the relationships of students with teachers, the relationships of
students with each other, and experiences of peer violence. The scale on the relationship between students and teachers was based on
ﬁve items (e.g., ‘Students get along with most teachers’). The quality of relationships of students within a school was assessed with four
items (e.g., ‘Most students treat each other with respect’). For both item batteries, students rated items on a four-point Likert scale
from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’. Higher values on the scales indicate a more positive relationship between students and
teachers or students and students respectively. The aim of the victimisation scale was to describe the amount of peer violence that
students perceive within their school. Six situations (such as ‘A student called you by an oﬀensive nickname’) were listed, and
students could indicate if the situations had happened to them ‘not at all’, ‘once’, ‘2–4 times’ or ‘5 or more times’ within the last three
months. Higher values on this scale indicate higher frequencies of experiences of victimisation.
In NRW, perception of discrimination of immigrants was assessed with a single item (‘In Germany, immigrants are more exposed to
unfair treatment than other groups’) in four steps from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’.
Civic knowledge was assessed using a timed test that included multiple choice and open-ended tasks. Five plausible values for each
student were provided based on Rasch-analyses. This scale was calibrated around the international mean of 500 and standard
deviation of 100 with reference to ICCS 2009 results.

The national identity scale consisted of ﬁve items (e.g., ‘I have great respect for Germany’) to which students could indicate their
agreement on a four-point Likert scale (‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree’). Higher values indicate a stronger identiﬁcation with
the country. This scale is in line with conceptions of national identity based on civil rights and duties rather than heredity (Ziemes,
Hahn-Laudenberg, & Abs, 2019).
Preparatory analyses for the scales on social relationships at school, TIPI, and national identity assessed the statistical invariance
for students with and without immigration backgrounds (see Appendix 1). We found strong support for the assumption of metric
invariance for all scales, and we recorded mixed ﬁndings for the assumption of scalar invariance (Rutkowski & Svetina, 2014). The
general ﬁt of the victimisation scale was rather poor, but we found strong support for the assumption of scalar and metric invariance.
We found poor support for the assumption of scalar invariance for the national identity scale and the student-student relationship
scale. Weighted means are reported but should be interpreted with care. Correlations and regressions can be reliably compared across
groups.
4. Results
4.1. Diﬀerences in the experiences and attitudes of students with and without immigration backgrounds
In order to investigate the diﬀerences in scale means between the diﬀerent groups, sample and descriptive statistics are presented
in Table 1. NRW has a tracked secondary school system, and the analysis shows that a lower percentage of students with immigration
backgrounds attend the highest track (‘Gymnasium’, the most academic track). Overall, the ‘Non-Immigrant’ group has the highest
SES, the most TIPI, experiences the best social school relationships, identiﬁes more strongly with Germany, and has more civic
knowledge than the other groups. The ‘Second Generation’ group has the lowest SES. The level of trust and the experiences of the
social relationships reported by the ‘Second Generation’ group are in between the values of the ‘Non-Immigrant’ and ‘First Generation’ groups. The ‘First Generation’ students bestow least TIPI and experience worst social relationships at school. Separate t-tests
were conducted to compare the ‘Non-Immigrant’ and ‘Second Generation’ groups. The results are presented in Table 1. As the sample
size of the ‘First Generation’ was much smaller, this group was not compared or tested against the other groups.
Table 2 displays the unweighted Cronbach's alphas of the scales, which range from good (0.866) to acceptable (0.619). The
weighted bivariate correlations are also listed. As expected, TIPI correlates positively with national identity and good student-student
and student-teacher relationships, and negatively with victimisation. The correlations are stronger in the group of students with
immigration backgrounds, especially the correlation of TIPI with national identity.
The results support H1.1, which states that students with immigration backgrounds will report less TIPI than their non-immigrant
counterparts. Further, both TIPI and national identity correlate signiﬁcantly with indicators of social relationships within the school,
supporting H1.2. The diﬀerential correlation coeﬃcients for the two groups indicate that separate regressions might reveal diﬀerential eﬀects.

4.2. The relevance of the social relationships at school for TIPI
The relevance of social relationships in school for TIPI in students with and without immigration backgrounds was examined by
applying separate regressions analyses for all students, i.e., for students with and without immigration backgrounds. Table 3 displays
the results of the regression analyses with TIPI as a dependent variable. The ﬁrst column lists the results relating to the group of nonimmigrant students. The second column shows the results for the students of the ‘Second Generation’ group. The third column lists
the results of all students combined. In this last column, immigration backgrounds are considered as a predictor.
The ﬁrst model – student background – serves as a baseline model that includes student background variables. Gender is not a
signiﬁcant predictor in any group. SES predicts TIPI only in the group of non-immigrant students (β = 0.10). Immigration backgrounds negatively predict TIPI in the group of all students (β = −0.15). Overall, the model explains only one to 4% of the respective
variances.
The second model – social relationships at school – introduces the scales regarding social relationships at school: the perception of
the relationships of students with other students and with teachers, and of victimisation. In all groups, the student-student and the
student-teacher relationships are signiﬁcant positive predictors. The predictive power is stronger in the group of students with
immigration backgrounds compared to the group of non-immigrant students (β = 0.32 vs. β = 0.23. for the student-student relationship, and β = 0.23 vs. β = 0.17 for student-teacher relationship). Victimisation is not a signiﬁcant predictor in any group. The
predictive power of immigration backgrounds in the group of all students is still signiﬁcant but reduced. The second model explains
more variance in all groups: 15% in the group of non-immigrant students, 25% in the group of students with immigration backgrounds, and 21% in the combined group.
In the third model – overall – the items relating to the perception of discrimination of immigrants, civic knowledge, and national
identity are introduced. The perceived discrimination of people with immigration backgrounds as a predictor does not reach the
7

Learning, Culture and Social Interaction 26 (2020) 100429

J.F. Ziemes, et al.

p < .05 level in any group. Civic knowledge predicts TIPI for non-immigrant students (β = 0.19) but not for students with immigration backgrounds. National identity is a weaker predictor for non-immigrant students (β = 0.14) compared to students with
immigration backgrounds (β = 0.25). The social relationship variables remain relevant predictors in all groups. In the group of all
students, immigration background was no longer found to be a signiﬁcant predictor. In the group of non-immigrant students, 19% of
the variance was explained; 30% in the group of students with immigration backgrounds; and 24% in the combined group.
The ﬁnding that the relationships among students and of students with teachers are positive predictors and the ﬁnding that the
explained variance increases meaningfully supports H2.1 and H2.2. Social relationships have a strong and robust predictive power for
TIPI bestowed by adolescents. Victimisation fails to impact the regression model meaningfully, and H2.3 therefore ﬁnds no support.
Contrary to expectation, the single item on the perceived discrimination of immigrants in the country has no predictive power on
TIPI in students with immigration backgrounds (H3.1). This could be due to the limitations of the measurement, which we will review
in the discussion. Civic knowledge as an indicator of cognitive integration has a positive regression weight for students without, and a
non-signiﬁcant regression weight for students with, immigration backgrounds. This is in line with H3.2. Lauglo (2013) found that the
correlation coeﬃcients of civic knowledge and TIPI were more positive in countries with less corruption and a higher HDI and,
thereby, found evidence that capable students can adequately judge institutions. The present analysis focused on one education
system in Germany, where the HDI is relatively high and corruption relatively low (Transparency International, 2017). The fact that
we still found a diﬀerence in the size of regression weight indicates that students do not only judge the trustworthiness of institutions
in general, but also reﬂect on the possibility that institutions may not be equally trustworthy for all groups of students. As civic
knowledge and social relationships are both shown to be relevant, we conclude that the theories of social capital and the critical citizen
are important for explaining the formation of TIPI during adolescence.
As expected, national identity predicted TIPI in both groups (H3.3). Interestingly, national identity had a stronger predictive
power among the students with immigration backgrounds. The cross-sectional nature of the data does not allow for any conclusion on
the causality of this relationship. As the ﬁnal model controls for social relationships, it seems more likely that the results indicate a
spillover from identity to TIPI. A feeling of belonging, socially and in the form of national identity, appears to be especially important
for students with immigration backgrounds.
In the ﬁnal model, which includes all students irrespective of their immigration backgrounds, the variable ‘immigration background’ holds no predictive power (RQ 4). The other variables explain large portions of the variance between students with and
without immigration backgrounds. These ﬁndings seems to suggest that students with immigration backgrounds would not bestow
less TIPI if they experienced the same quality of social interactions, wielded the same civic knowledge and identiﬁed to the same
degree with the country as students without immigration backgrounds.
5. Discussion
5.1. Limitations
While the ICCS 2016 sample and questionnaire are appropriate for gaining insights concerning the relevance of social interactions
at school for students' political socialisation, multiple limitations need to be noted. First, results regarding the mean diﬀerences
between immigrant students and other groups should not be interpreted as reliable estimators of population values. While the
standard errors and p-values are sensitive to shifts in sample size and regressions were calculated separately, analyses with small
sample sizes necessarily increase the uncertainty of estimated values and standard errors and so decrease the probability that they are
reliable estimators for the population.
Minority status is a “black box” which indicates the presence of process variables (Wilkes & Wu, 2019). One item on perceived
discrimination was introduced as an indicator of the perceived level of state functioning, but no relevant connection to TIPI could be
found. It is plausible to assume that students with immigration backgrounds (as well as students without immigration backgrounds
but who support values of equality) who perceived the country to be discriminatory against immigrants would withdraw TIPI. As a
single item holds limited variance, the results here do not allow for much interpretation beyond the ﬁnding that the expected results
could not be obtained using the operationalisation at hand. A targeted investigation of personal experiences of discrimination or more
precise measures of perceived discrimination within the country might produce diﬀerent results. While data on the perception of
discrimination in the country was available, data concerning personal experiences of discrimination was not. This variable might
oﬀer a further insight in the processes of bestowal of TIPI by students with immigration backgrounds.
Selecting a single education system has advantages as well as disadvantages. Analysis reveals cross-country variance in the means
of TIPI and the relationships between TIPI and civic knowledge (Lauglo, 2013) and educational expectations (Kim, 2019). This can
also be seen as a warning not to delve hurriedly into cross-country analyses but to explore single systems cautiously before trying to
generalise models. The speciﬁc type of political integration may diﬀer signiﬁcantly between countries. A comparison of diﬀerent
education systems might build on the ﬁndings presented here. In order to reliably identify potential process variables, experimental
and longitudinal study designs would be required. Both speciﬁc social interactions and social cohesion within schools and the
relevance of these variables for political socialisation all need further attention in research and practice.
5.2. Further research and consequences
This paper goes beyond previous research by incorporating the theories social capital and critical citizenship into the conceptualisation of political socialisation of students with and without immigration backgrounds. The theory of social capital enables an
8

Learning, Culture and Social Interaction 26 (2020) 100429

J.F. Ziemes, et al.

understanding of the development and relevance of a baseline of TIPI. We found that all students, but especially students with
immigration backgrounds bestow more TIPI if they experience positive relationships at school – and that students with immigration
backgrounds experience worse social relationships. The theory of the critical citizen states that TIPI is not only an end in itself but
must be earned by institutions and that civic knowledge is important to scrutinize them. We found civic knowledge to be diﬀerentially related to TIPI among students with and without immigration backgrounds. While both theories have an additive value to the
understanding of socialisation, their combination enables an understanding of socialisation that combines notions system needs as
well as students' agency: Students are sensitive to the social environment around them, recognise a lack of social capital, and will hold
political institutions responsible.
As predicted by the social capital theory, social relationships inﬂuence the development of political support, especially for students with immigration backgrounds. Student-student and student-teacher relationships explain diﬀerent aspects of the variance in
TIPI. While students share a hierarchical level, teachers are responsible for students' learning and wellbeing in school. Both forms of
relationships seem to be relevant for TIPI and deserve further theoretical and empirical attention. Further analyses could investigate
if a certain minimum level of quality of social relationships is needed to promote students' successful social and political integration.
Such a hypothesis would also imply that failing to provide such quality relationships at school would prompt students to distance
themselves from the political system, which would in turn undermine their constructive engagement within the system (Banks,
2015).
We applied the critical citizen theory to subgroups within one system and achieved promising results. The relationship between
civic knowledge and TIPI seems to be speciﬁc to the group and their social position. Analyses of these variables in large samples
should be accompanied by an examination of possible relevant subgroups. The diﬀerences in the relationships between TIPI and civic
knowledge between the groups support the idea that civic knowledge may be especially important for students from marginalised
groups. Civic knowledge can help students to be critical citizens that are to understand why their experiences with institutions diﬀer
from those of students who are not marginalised. If marginalised students develop a more critical stance, they can hold the institutions accountable and induce change. Therefore, in order to enable students from marginalised groups to hold discriminatory
institutions accountable, schools need to ensure that those students have adequate access to civic education.
The theories of social capital and the critical citizen complement each other in the understanding of students' political socialisation and speciﬁcally their bestowal of TIPI. Schools and the social interactions that they provide are an important environment for
students' political development. Good social relationships seem to lay the foundation for TIPI in adolescents. This conclusion might be
seen as bad news for the theory of the critical citizen, which would prefer individuals to have a purely rational approach to the
bestowal of trust or indeed distrust. One may conclude that very positive relationships may lead to uncritical high values of trust,
which may hinder the further improvement of institutions. That said, the group-speciﬁc analyses indicate that TIPI is inﬂuenced but
not fully determined by social relationships. Schools, as representative state institutions, can foster TIPI by striving for strong positive
social relationships and can simultaneously foster civic knowledge to enable students to become critical citizens of their democracies.
