Abstract
Computer-­based interactive items have become prevalent
in recent educational assessments. In such items, the entire
human-­computer interactive process is recorded in a log file
and is known as the response process. These data are noisy,
diverse, and in a nonstandard format. Several feature extraction methods have been developed to overcome the difficulties in process data analysis. However, these methods often
focus on the action sequence and ignore the time sequence
in response processes. In this paper, we introduce a new feature extraction method that incorporates the information in
both the action sequence and the response time sequence.
The method is based on the concept of path signature from
stochastic analysis. We apply the proposed method to both
simulated data and real response process data from PIAAC.
A prediction framework is used to show that taking time
information into account provides a more comprehensive
understanding of respondents' behaviors.

1 | I N T RODUC T ION
With the development of technology, computer-­based interactive items have been used to assess respondents' problem-­solving skills in large-­scale assessments. In these items, participants complete real-­life
tasks in simulated environments. Computer technology not only provides a flexible environment to evaluate their skills but also helps collect rich data for studying participants' problem-­solving behaviours. As
participants interact with the computer interface to complete the tasks, their problem-­solving processes
are recorded in computer log files as a sequence of actions as well as their specific timestamps. Such data
describe how the participants solve the tasks and are often referred to as process data.
In classical tests, the recorded responses to items are often the final outcomes. Each item response
is a univariate categorical variable. Such data have been studied both intensively and extensively (Rupp
et al., 2010; van der Linden, 2016; von Davier & Lee, 2019). Models such as item response theory

models (Lord, 1980; Rasch, 1961; Reckase, 2009; Samejima, 1997) and cognitive diagnosis models (de
la Torre, 2011; de la Torre & Douglas, 2004; Junker & Sijtsma, 2001; Templin & Henson, 2006) have
been developed for measuring respondents' latent abilities based on such data. Process data contain
respondents' entire problem-­solving processes and thus provide much more information than classic
responses. Nonetheless, process responses are often difficult to analyse for the following reasons. First,
the response process often has very detailed information on file, much of which is irrelevant to the latent
skills of interest. Therefore, it brings in both additional useful information and noise. Second, process
data are in the format of a sequence of actions (along with their timestamps) whose lengths vary a lot
among observations. Thus, the actions are not well aligned for standard parametric models. Such data
pattern also adds to the difficulty of the analysis.
A natural approach to analysing process data is to extract features from the response processes
and then apply standard analysis to the features. The diversity of problem-­solving tasks adds to
the difficulty in extracting features from process data. Response processes for each task have their
unique patterns. Many works on process data are case-­by-­case studies (Greiff et al., 2016; Kroehne &
Goldhammer, 2018), where features of response processes are defined and validated based on domain
knowledge and respondents' cognitive processes. Performing such analyses on a wide range of items
requires a considerable amount of resources because designing the features is usually item-­specific and
relies on domain knowledge.
Several generic methods for extracting features from response processes have been developed in the
literature. He and von Davier (2015, 2016) adopted the concept of n-­g rams from natural language processing to explore the association between action sequence patterns and traditional item responses. Tang
et al. (2020) proposed to extract features from action sequences via multidimensional scaling (MDS).
A neural-­network-­based method is proposed in Tang, Wang, et al. (2021). The features extracted from
these methods have been used to demonstrate the rich information in response processes and identify
behaviour patterns closely related to the final response outcome and respondents' demographics (He &
von Davier, 2015; Zhang et al., 2021). The extracted features have also been used to improve assessment
precision (Zhang et al., 2023). These methods focus solely on the action sequences in response processes. The time sequences are ignored when extracting features. Intuitively, time sequences record respondents' reaction time and the pace of problem-­solving processes, which could be useful for assessing
respondents' latent traits or understanding their problem-­solving behaviours. Therefore, it is desirable
to incorporate the timestamp sequences in feature extraction.
Time information related to response processes has been studied in the literature. Timestamps have
been used in statistical modelling of response processes (Chen, 2020; Chen et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020).
In terms of feature extraction, the time spent on solving an item (hereafter total response time) is
the most commonly used time-­related feature (Goldhammer et al., 2014; Liao et al., 2019; van der
Linden, 2007; Wang & Xu, 2015; Zhan et al., 2018). Features containing more detailed time information
have been considered in a few recent studies (Albert & Steinberg, 2011; Eichmann et al., 2019; Qiao &
Jiao, 2018; Ulitzsch et al., 2021; Zhang & Andersson, 2023). Although these studies show that simple
summary statistics of timestamp sequences such as time to the first action and variation of time intervals are useful for improving test design and assessment, there is no systematic approach to extract the
information in the timestamp sequence, inhibiting a thorough investigation of additional useful information in the timestamp sequences.
In this article, we propose a feature extraction method that systematically incorporates the information in both action and timestamp sequences. It can also be used to extract features from the action
sequences or the timestamp sequences alone. When applied to the timestamp sequences, the proposed
method produces more informative features than the simple summary statistics that have been used in
the literature. Similar to other generic feature extraction methods, our method is applicable to response
processes generated from a wide range of human–computer interfaces.
The proposed method is based on path signature. It is a set of infinite elements describing the characteristics of a path. The concept originates from the study of piecewise regular paths (Chen, 1958)
and is then extended to characterize the interactions between highly oscillatory systems (Lyons

The features extracted from the proposed method can be treated as surrogates of response processes in a standard matrix format. Incorporating both action and time information, these features can
be used for revealing latent structures of response processes, exploring heterogeneity in respondents'
problem-­solving behaviours and improving existing psychometric practice. We demonstrate the utility
of these extracted features through simulations and empirical analysis. In particular, in the empirical
analysis, the proposed method is applied to process data from the Problem Solving in Technology
Rich Environments (PSTRE) items in the 2012 Programme for the International Assessment of Adult
Competencies (PIAAC). Using a prediction procedure similar to Tang et al. (2020), the extracted features are used to explore whether problem-­solving behaviours vary among respondents with different
latent characteristics or demographics. Furthermore, we demonstrate the potential of the extracted features for improving assessment precision by constructing process-­based scores of respondents' PSTRE
skills using the approach proposed by Zhang et al. (2023). Our analyses also show that the timestamp
sequences in response processes contain useful information that the action sequences are not able to
characterize. The features capturing the information are often more complex than simple summary
statistics of the timestamp sequences such as the total response time and time to the first action. One
limitation of the method is that path signatures are designed to capture interaction between actions and
their ability to capture higher-­level problem-­solving strategies is relatively weak.
The rest of this article is organized as follows. We begin with describing the path representation
of response processes in Section 2. In Section 3, we introduce a method for extracting features
from process data using the concept of path signature and log signature. A simulation study is presented in Section 4 to demonstrate the method and how the latent structure of response processes
is revealed in extracted features. In Section 5, we present the case study of response processes from
PIAAC PSTRE to demonstrate that the extracted features can be used for examining respondents'
heterogeneous problem-­solving behaviours and improving assessment precision. Concluding remarks are made in Section 6.

A response process for an item is a time-­stamped action sequence describing how a respondent
solved a task. A generic response process o is often represented as a time-­stamped action sequence
o = ((s1 , t1 ), …, (sL , tL )), where, for l = 1, …, L , sl is the action taken at step l and tl is the corresponding timestamp recording the time elapsed since the start of the response process until taking sl . The
first action s1 is often a special action, denoted by ‘Start’ in this article, indicating the start of the response process. The first timestamp t1 is 0. For l = 2, …, L , sl takes the value from the item's action
set 𝒜 = {a1 , …, aM }, where M is the number of possible actions of the item. We call s = (s1 , …, sL )
and t = (t1 , …, tL ) the action and timestamp sequence of o , respectively. Note that t is an increasing
sequence 0 = t1 < t2 < … < tL with tL being the total response time for the item.

as an expanded version of the action sequence s . Each of the M sequences corresponds to one action
in the action set 𝒜, recording how many times the action has been used up to a step. More specifically,

progress. It is used to construct features in Section 3. In the path representation W , the first M components are related to the action sequence and WM+1 is related to the timestamp sequence. We distinguish
the two types of components in the discussions. As the information extracted from response time is
limited if there is only one timestamp component, we will further expand the component in Section 3
to enrich the information in the extracted features.
The path representation of response processes described earlier is closely related to the marked
point process framework for modelling process data. Chen (2020) proposed a continuous time dynamic
measurement model for process data, where the action and timestamp sequence pair (s, t ) of a response
process is treated as a realization of a marked point process where timestamps represent the event occurrence times and actions serve as the marks indicating the type of an occurred event. In this framework, each event type (action) is associated with a counting process that tracks the number of events
of that type up to a given time t . In our path representation, for j = 1, …, M , each count sequence w j ,
along with w M+1, can be seen as a realization of the counting process associated with event type (action)
j . Despite this connection, the two approaches have distinct goals. The marked point process framework focuses on statistical modelling and thus imposes distributional assumptions on the action and
timestamp sequences, whereas the path representation in this article is used for mathematical feature
extraction and does not need such assumptions.

3 | SIGNAT U R E S OF PAT HS
In this section, we introduce signature features and log signature features of response paths. The
two types of features are related to the concept of path signature and log signature (Chevyrev &
Kormilitzin, 2016) in stochastic analysis. Each feature is extracted using one or more components of
the response path. A feature based on k components is a level-­k feature. We first define lower-­level
(level-­one and level-­t wo) signature features and log signature features in Sections 3.1 and 3.2. The interpretation of level-­t wo features is provided in Sections 3.3–3.5. Higher-­level features are discussed in
Section 3.6.

3.6 | Higher-­level features
With the leading and lagging time processes, we augment the response path W described in Section 2
by replacing the single time process WM+1 with Wlead and Wlag:
W P = (Wlead , Wlag , W1 , …, WM ).

(12)

Each of the features discussed in the previous sections is constructed from one or two components of
W P. In general, for k indices i1 , …, ik from ℐ P = {e, a, 1, …, M}, a level-­k signature feature xi1 ,…,ik is con-

structed using Wi1 , …, Wik through path integrals. Each feature describes certain geometric characteristic of
the trajectory formed by the components in a k-­dimensional space. The mathematical expression of xi1 ,…,ik

on PS features up to a level. We will show in Section 5 that PS features up to level two often already contain
a good amount of information of response processes.

3.7 | Comparison between AS features and N-­gram features
N-­g ram features have been used for analysing action sequences of response processes (He & von
Davier, 2015, 2016; Qiao & Jiao, 2018; Stadler et al., 2019). Originating from natural language processing, these features record the number of appearances of n-­g rams (short sequences of consecutive
actions) in an action sequence. Similar to the AS features proposed in this article, each n-­g ram feature
involves one or more actions and these features can be grouped based on the number of actions involved. When a single action is involved, the AS feature and n-­g ram feature are equivalent, as both
measure the count of that action. However, when multiple actions are involved, the two types of features characterize different aspects of action sequences. AS features describe the relative positions of
actions within an action sequence, while n-­g ram features capture the frequency of action patterns. A
resulting key distinction is that n-­g ram features only focus on consecutive actions, whereas AS features
have no such restriction. For example, for the sequence (A, B, C , A, B, C ), the n-­g ram feature associated
with the action pair (A, C ) has a value of 0, as actions A and C never occur consecutively. In contrast,
the corresponding AS feature has a value of 3, as explained in Section 3.1.

4.1 | Experiment settings
We consider response processes with 12 possible actions denoted by A, …, L in the simulation experiments. Among them, A and L denote the start and the end of a response process, respectively, meaning
that each action sequence always starts with A and ends with L. As a result, only 10 of the 12 actions, B,
…, K, are related to problem solving and thus used for constructing process paths.
Two experiments are conducted to investigate whether the features extracted from the proposed
method can reveal latent group structures in response processes. In both experiments, 100 datasets of
500 response processes are generated. In Experiment I, we focus on action sequences and omit timestamp sequences. Following Tang et al. (2020), we use Markov models to generate action sequences
with a two-­g roup structure. Markov models have been used for describing response processes in the
literature (Han et al., 2021). For each dataset, the 500 action sequences are generated from two different
Markov models with 250 sequences from each. The probability transition matrices, P (1) and P (2), of
the two models are randomly generated in the same way as in Tang et al. (2020). Once generated, they
are fixed and used to simulate all 100 datasets. Given a probability transition matrix, we generate an
action sequence by starting from A and sampling the subsequence actions according to the matrix until
L appears. After data generation, we extract ALS features up to level two (55 features) for each action
sequence. We would like to examine how well these extracted features can reveal the action sequences'
group identity defined according to the model from which they are generated. To achieve this, each
dataset is randomly split into a training set with 400 action sequences and a test set with the remaining
100 action sequences. A logistic regression of the group identity on the extracted features is fitted on the
training set, and its performance on predicting the group identity is evaluated on the test set. Prediction
accuracy, that is, the proportion of correct predictions, is used as the evaluation criterion. To compare
the ALS features with features extracted using other methods, we also extract 55 MDS features and use
them for predicting the group identity for each dataset.
In Experiment II, both action sequences and timestamp sequences of response processes are considered. Each dataset in this experiment also has a two-­g roup structure, but the structure is reflected in
the timestamp sequences not in the action sequences. For the 250 response processes in the first group,
the action sequences are generated from the Markov model with P (1) obtained in Experiment I as the
probability transition matrix. The action sequences in the second group are set as the same as those in
the first group. For both groups, given a generated action sequence (s1 , …, sL ), the corresponding timestamp sequence (t1 , …, tL ) is generated by first setting t1 to zero and then generating the time elapsed

In this section, we present a case study of PIAAC 2012 data to demonstrate how path signature features
help investigate response processes.

5.1 | Data
The dataset we use is obtained from PIAAC 2012 study, the first round of Cycle I PIAAC. Participants
from 17 countries1 are included in the dataset. We consider the response processes of 12,108 participants
who completed all 14 PSTRE items in the PIAAC 2012 study. Due to an incomplete codebook for item
U21, response processes from 13 out of 14 items are used in our analysis. Some elements in response processes are system events automatically generated by the computer for summarizing the item interface's state
change caused by participants' actions. These elements are removed in our analysis as they are redundant in
reflecting respondents' problem-­solving behaviours. Besides the response process, a polytomous score is
also recorded for each respondent–PSTRE item pair. The number of score categories ranges from two to
four across items. Table 2 gives a brief description and several summary statistics of the response processes
by item. The items vary in the item interface, the complexity of the required task and difficulty level. The
diversity of the items poses a great challenge to extract informative features from response processes. As
we show later in this section, our proposed feature extraction method can be applied to all of these items.
Our dataset also contains a wide range of background information about participants' demographics, education level and employment status. The extracted features, along with the background information, are
used in the case study to explore the variations in the problem-­solving behaviours in both action and timestamp sequences.

As shown in Table 3, the dimension of the signature and log signature features can be quite high,
particularly in cases with large d . To reduce the computational burden and possible overfitting brought
by high dimensional covariates, we use L1 regularized regression (Tibshirani, 1996) to select the most
relevant features for a given Y from each set of features for each item before fitting the prediction models involving signature and log signature features. The penalty parameter is determined through five-fold cross-­validation. For each item and target variable pair, the number of selected features is typically
fewer than 200. Only these selected features are used in Analyses I and II.
In each round of cross-­validation for evaluating the prediction performance of various models, we
further apply L2 regularization to 𝜷 in each model to mitigate the potential multicollinearity among the
covariates. Based on our experience, this additional L2 regularization often helps to improve models'
prediction performance. The penalty parameter for L2 regularization is determined by evaluating a
model's prediction performance on a validation set, which is created by randomly sampling 10% of the
cross-­validation training set (consisting of four folds of data). The validation set is not used when fitting
the model.

5.6 | Analysis III
In this section, we demonstrate the features extracted from the proposed method are helpful for
improving the precision of latent trait assessment. Recently, Zhang et al. (2023) proposed a generic
Rao-­Blackwellization procedure for improving the precision of latent trait assessment by using the information in response processes. In the procedure, process information comes from features extracted
from response processes. More specifically, the procedure first obtains sufficient statistics of the latent
trait 𝜃 using the extracted features and item-­response-­theory-­based (IRT-­based) estimates of 𝜃. Then
a process-­based estimate of 𝜃 is constructed from the sufficient statistics. It is shown theoretically and
empirically that the process-­based estimate obtained from this procedure is more accurate than the IRT-based estimates. In this analysis, we adopt this procedure with process information provided through
various log signature features to construct and compare process-­based estimates.

6 | DIS C US SION
In this article, we proposed a method for extracting features from response processes recorded in interactive problem-­solving items. The method incorporates the information in both action and timestamp
sequences into the extracted features, which are the signature or log signature of a path constructed
from a response process.
Besides the ability to account for timestamp sequences in feature extractions, another potential advantage of the proposed method over the MDS and the autoencoder feature extraction methods is that
it is a model-­free method. Both MDS and autoencoder methods require model fitting using observed response processes. If a new set of response processes becomes available, one may consider to recalibrate
the model used for extracting features. In this case, the features of the old response processes are likely
to change as well, which may lead to substantial revision in the subsequent analysis. In contrast, the
signature and log signature features of a response process do not depend on other response processes.
Also, as no model fitting is needed, extracting signature or log signature features is computationally fast.
It takes only a few seconds to extract all the signature and log signature features used in the case study.
The PIAAC case study shows that, for some variables of interest, TS and TLS features can provide
more information than the total response time and time to the first action. The results suggest that

further investigation of the more granular time data in response processes can help us obtain a better
understanding of respondents' problem-­solving behaviours. Developing statistical models for time sequences is a potential future direction.
Dependence between action sequences and time sequences is often observed in response processes. For
example, certain actions are usually associated with longer times until the next action is taken, and repeated
actions tend to have shorter time intervals in between. The proposed method does not explicitly account for
such dependencies when extracting signature or log signature features. However, comparing the prediction
performance of the resulting features can reveal the relationships to some extent. For instance, Figure 14
shows that both action features and time features contain substantial information about ICTHOME as the
prediction performance improves significantly when using ALS(20,2) or TLS(5) compared to the baseline
model, but combining ALS(20,2) and TLS(5) provides only a small amount of additional improvement,
suggesting that action features and time features overlap in the information they capture.
In Analyses I and II of the PIAAC case study, we use GLMs to predict the target variables and use
an L1 regularization followed by L2 regularization procedure to enhance the stability of the models'
prediction performance. These choices are made for convenience and to achieve relatively low computational cost. Alternative approaches can be considered. For example, machine learning models such as
random forests (Breiman, 2001), XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) and neural networks (Hastie, 2009,
Chapter 11) could be used in place of GLMs. Similarly, statistical methods such as SURE screening (Fan
& Lv, 2008) and elastic net regularization (Zou & Hastie, 2005) can be used for feature selection and to
mitigate overfitting. We explored these alternative methods and found that they produced results and
conclusions consistent with those obtained in Sections 5.4 and 5.5.
The analyses in Section 5.5 focus on demonstrating that there exist differences in respondents'
problem-­solving behaviours by comparing the prediction performance of the extracted features without
identifying specific behaviour patterns that distinguish respondents. Despite the challenges brought by
the high dimensionality of the extracted features, there are existing methods which can help interpret
the results. For instance, sparse principal component analysis (Zou et al., 2006) can be used to obtain
principal components that load on only a few of the original features, producing lower dimensional
features associated with more meaningful behaviour patterns and improving the interpretability of the
prediction models. Additionally, when black-­box machine learning models such as boosting or neural
networks are used for prediction, interpretable machine learning tools (Molnar, 2022) such as feature
importance rankings and partial dependence plot can be used to better understand the effects of specific features of interest. With that being said, statistical-­learning-­based features are able to capture
relatively simple patterns of the response process. Their abilities to capture the higher-­level problem-solving strategies are still limited.
Although signature and log signature features cannot be directly used to identify specific distinct
behaviour patterns among respondents, they still have practical utility. As demonstrated in Section 5.6,
these features can help improve the precision of latent trait assessment. Beyond this, features extracted
from process data can also be used in other psychometric applications such as item selection in computerized adaptive testing and mitigating the issue of DIF (Qi, 2023).
