The Spanish PISA 2018 students sample (>35,900 fifteen-year-old students) has been analysed by
calculating the Mathematics minus Reading scores of all individuals in the sample, relating this
Maths-Reading index to the personal and educational characteristics of the respondents. Results
of the first step of the analysis show that the positive Maths-Reading Index values (higher scores
in maths than in reading) are mainly related to gender, and negative values (higher scores in
reading) are mainly related to the joy of reading and to a series of factors linked to interest and
respect for other cultures, among others. Subsequently, the individual level of global performance
is taken into account with a cluster analysis generating groups of high, medium and low performers. Changes in factors affecting the Maths-Reading index when this level-based grouping is
considered are analysed, showing the importance of grade repetition for low performers and the
influence of socio-economic factors for medium performers, while high performers are affected
especially by non-cognitive aspects such as self-concept or expected occupational status. Finally,
combined effects of gender and level of performance are taken into account, showing that the
factors affect male and female students differently for the disparate performance-based groups.
This study contributes to a more precise knowledge of the main factors associated with students’
tendency to have better scores in mathematical items than in reading items or vice versa, and to
distinguish the differential role of personal and environmental factors not only globally, but also
when gender and performance levels are taken into account. Studying this personal tendency is
significant as it is related to students’ study choice, especially Science-Technology-EngineeringMathematics (STEM) degrees.

1. Introduction
The economic and social future of countries is closely related with their capacity to cope with scientific and technological progress
and developments, which are advancing at an unstoppable pace worldwide. One indicator that can be used to measure capacity to be at
the forefront of progress is the number of students in each country opting for university degrees related to science and technology and
who can join the research, technical and productive sector in the future.
A significant overall drop has been detected in young people enrolling in higher STEM studies in both Spain and abroad, especially
worrying among female students (Bacovic et al., 2022; Blackburn, 2017; Ceci et al., 2009; Anon., OECD, 2017a; Anon., UNESCO, 2017;
Verdugo-Castro et al., 2022; Wang & Degol, 2017; Wang et al., 2017; Yazilitas et al., 2013).

Various authors (for example, Correll, 2001; Maltese & Tai, 2011; Sáinz & Müller, 2018; Wang et al., 2017) point out that the
decision to pursue STEM studies is taken during secondary education. Therefore, one possible source of data to analyse factors
associated with this decision are the different PISA report waves by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
(OECD). These data can be used to analyse how 15-year-old students worldwide perform in mathematics, reading and science, and also
to establish relationships between this performance and diverse personal factors related to the socio-family environment, the school
and the education system.
Most studies based on PISA data used to focus on Mathematics, Reading or Science scores due to the structure of PISA and the main
objective of each PISA wave. These subjects (Mathematics, Reading and Science) have been analysed separately even in large, farreaching studies, with a small number of studies which aim to capture the possible relationship between them, for example trying
to find a correlation or a prediction between different PISA scores. Following this idea, Shelley and Yildirim (2013), studying the
transfer of knowledge between mathematics, science and reading with data from PISA 2009 in Turkey, conclude that science and
reading are good predictors of performance in mathematics, especially science; also mathematics and language are good predictors of
scientific performance, especially mathematics, and mathematics and science are good predictors of reading. Koyuncu and Firat (2020)
use PISA 2018 data from Turkey, China and Mexico to show the predictive relationship between reading scores and mathematics and
science scores, especially for science, independently of global performance level. On the other hand, Akbasli et al. (2016), using data
from PISA 2006, 2009 and 2012 for different countries, conclude that reading comprehension is correlated with success in mathematics and science for high performance countries, but this correlation is not seen in countries with low or average performance. Cai
and Yang (2022) recently used PISA 2018 data from 77 countries to postulate that reading strategies affect mathematical learning and
that the relationship between reading and mathematics is not linear, but that it follows an Island Ridge Curve (IRC).
However, this paper intends to show a different approach based on analysing the relationship between maths and reading scores
jointly rather than separately. This joint study was conducted by generating an ad hoc index (called the Maths-Reading Index) and
determining its relationships with the educational or personal environment of the respondents, using the Spanish sample of PISA 2018
data.
Some previous studies using a similar approach with PISA data from different waves (Breda & Napp, 2019; Keller et al., 2021; Rapp
& Borgonovi, 2019) and with conclusions that can be included in Eccles’ expectancy-value theory, according to which the relationship
between scores in mathematics and reading is directly related with students’ degree choice (Eccles, 2009; Eccles & Wang, 2016), were
used as a starting point. Breda and Napp (2019) argued that the small gender gap in maths scores does not explain the
under-representation of women in maths-related fields; but the results change when reading performance is also taken into account.
They develop a MR (maths minus reading) index using individual data for 300,000 students across 64 countries from the PISA 2012
database and show that the difference between performance in reading and maths is much larger for female than for male students,
which explains the biggest part of the gender gap in the intention of studying maths-related degrees. A similar index called DIFmathread was developed by Rapp and Borgonovi (2019) with PISA 2012 data from all countries. They showed that the difference
between performance in mathematics and reading is strongly related to gender and some personal characteristics such as
self-confidence in maths, and not so much with the socio-cultural status (which affects both subjects in a similar way) or with school
characteristics. Keller et al. (2021) generate three achievements profiles for top performers, subtracting students’ scores in mathematics
minus reading, mathematics minus science and science minus reading, studying the differences by gender and their relationship with
same gender equality indicators. The difference between individual scores in mathematics and reading is strongly related with gender,
and the underrepresentation of female students among high-performer in mathematics could be behind the low number of women
choosing degrees with a strong mathematical component.
1.1. The mathematics minus reading index (MRI)
Following this idea of jointly analysing results in mathematics and reading, and based on data from the Spanish PISA 2018 sample
(35,000 students), we have developed an individual index called the Maths-Reading Index or MRI. This index is formed by the difference between mathematics scores minus reading scores for each student based on plausible values following OECD guidelines.
Positive MRI index values are related with the student’s individual tendency to have better scores in mathematics than reading; on the
contrary, negative MRI values would be related with a tendency to have better scores in reading than mathematics; MRI values close to
zero with the tendency to obtain similar scores in both areas.
It is important to note that positive, negative or near-zero MRI values for each individual are independent of their overall performance so students with very different global performance levels can have similar values in the index. An MRI-based classification
therefore departs from the usual classification by PISA points as it does not show performance in mathematics and reading separately,
but rather the tendency to be more oriented towards “mathematics” or “reading” for each individual, assuming that higher scores are
obtained in disciplines in which they have greater abilities. It is thus an individual “polarisation” measure for each 15-year-old Spanish
student who sat the PISA tests.
This “polarisation” of each individual between mathematics and reading may be used as an indicator of tendency to choose science
degrees following the expectancy-value theory by Eccles (Eccles, 2009; Eccles & Wang, 2016). In a study by Wang et al. (2017), relative
skill in the different areas (mathematics-science-reading) guides degree choice rather than performance: more polarised (“asymmetric”) individuals will tend to choose degrees in line with their strengths. In Wang et al. (2013), high-performing individuals in both
mathematics and reading are more likely to opt for a STEM-type career than individuals with high performance in mathematics and
lower verbal performance. On the other hand, relative independence of individual preferences with regard to performance data is
consistent with the study by Maltese and Tai (2011), according to which individual interest in science and not scores in science subjects

1.2. Mathematics, reading and gender on PISA scores
Looking for personal factors associated with PISA scores, a known result linked to maths or reading scores is a significant influence
of gender, with gender differences in reading being larger and more consistent than differences in mathematics (Baye & Monseur,
2016; Gevrek et al., 2020; Guisoet al., 2008; Hermann & Kopasz, 2021; Keller et al., 2021; Ma, 2008; Marks, 2008; Meiseberg, 2016;
Munir & Winter-Ebmer, 2018; Salvi del Pero & Bytchkova, 2013; Stoet & Geary, 2013).
The mathematics gender gap favouring male students has been reported as small but consistent in developed countries although
there are some differences across countries (Breda & Napp, 2019; Liu & Wilson, 2009; Ma, 2008; Marks, 2008; Stoet & Geary, 2013).
González de San Román and De la Rica (2016), using PISA 2009 data across 63 countries, show that this mathematics gender gap is
closely related to cultural and social factors by means of the intergenerational transmission of gender roles and how the mother’s
occupation influences the performance of female students, especially in countries where the incorporation of women into the workplace is not very frequent. Else-Quest et al. (2010) analyse, among other aspects, the mathematical performance data of PISA 2003 and
show that, although the gender gap is not very high on average, there is great variability between countries and this variability is
associated with different indices of gender equity and equality development, as does Guisoet al. (2008) using PISA 2003 data, Munir
and Winter-Ebmer (2018) using PISA 2012 data, or Keller et al. (2021) using PISA data from 2000 to 2015. Gevrek et al. (2020) find a
significant relationship between gender gap in mathematics across countries for PISA 2012 with some particular gender equity
measures, such as gender equity in access to tertiary education and gender wage gap. Some studies contradict the abovementioned
relationship between PISA gender-differenced outcomes and gender equality measures (Ireson, 2017; Marks, 2008; Meisenberg, 2016;
Stoet & Geary, 2013, 2015; Tao & Michalopoulos, 2018).
The reading gender gap favouring female students has been found to be more extended and consistent than mathematics almost
universally and has been studied to relate it with some personal or contextual characteristics. For example, regarding personal

linguistic competences of Italian students are related with their mathematics performance using mathematics items from PISA 2012

reading demand and male students performing better in maths items with low reading demand, independently of the level of reading
literacy in all the students. They concluded that reading ability plays a decisive role in mathematical learning. In the Wang et al. (2013)
study, female students are more represented in the group that performs highly in both mathematics and verbal, while male students
usually have better verbal performance than mathematical which, according to the authors, may explain that the former choose studies
outside the STEM field.

1.4. Objective of the study and research questions
The study consisted of two phases. Firstly, we generated the Maths-Read Index (MRI) by calculating the individual Maths score
minus the individual Language score for all participants in each of the 10 plausible levels of achievement given by PISA data. Subsequently, we carried out a correlation analysis to capture the relationship between this MRI and some characteristics of the respondents. This part of the study tries to connect these student characteristics with them being more ‘mathematical’ or more ‘verbal’,
namely, having positive or negative values in the MRI. Therefore, the first Research Question is:
RQ1. What factors are related with the MRI for the whole Spanish sample of PISA 2018?
Secondly, results were refined with a cluster analysis, grouping students by performance, using all available maths, reading and
science scores. This grouping takes into account OECD guidelines on managing plausible values and student sample weights (Anon.,
OECD, 2017b). Using this approach, it is possible to distinguish between students who have good scores in all subjects (high performers), students who have low scores in all subjects (low performers) and other students (medium performers). This grouping was
used to analyse the relationship between the MRI and personal and contextual factors separately for each group of performers. This
yields two Research Questions:

2. Method
Under a quantitative approach, a secondary data analysis of PISA 2018 databases was conducted. We implemented a nonexperimental cross-sectional design in order to conduct an exploratory and correlational analysis. From the population of 15year-old Spanish students, we analysed the full Spanish sample included in the PISA 2018 students database (n = 35,943 students).
The criterion variable was the Maths-Reading Index (MRI), obtained by calculating the student’s performance in mathematics
minus his or her reading performance. Both groups of factors provided in the PISA 2018 student database (Anon., OECD, 2019) were
included as explanatory variables: Simple Composite Indices and Scale Composite Indices. Thus, given the exploratory nature of this
paper, bivariate correlations between all compound contextual and educational factors included in PISA 2018 and the MRI index were
tested. The results only report relevant correlations.
To complete the study, two main procedures were implemented.
First, we calculated the MRI. This index was calculated 10 times, once for each plausible value of performance in mathematics and
reading.
Second, we obtained a classification of 3 performance-based groups: To avoid human intervention in this classification, a k-means
clustering algorithm was used. Specifically, models with 3 clusters were calculated including the Maths and Reading Performance
variables. Again, 10 independent clustering models were necessary, one for each pair of plausible values. All models were significant.
After obtaining the 10 different groupings of students according to their 10 plausible performances in mathematics and reading, given
that in all models the groupings were similar (CLUSTER 1: low performance in both subjects; CLUSTER 2: moderate overall performance; CLUSTER 3: high performance in both subjects), the final student grouping was as follows:
LOW PERFORMERS: Students grouped in CLUSTER 1 in all 10 models. A total of 4118 students were part of this group.
HIGH PERFORMERS: Students grouped in CLUSTER 3 in all 10 models. A total of 6238 students were included in this group.
○ MEDIUM PERFORMERS: All students who did not meet either of the two above conditions. The remaining 25,587 students were
included in this group.

3. Results
3.1. Results for the total sample of students
Table 1a and 1b show non-trivial correlation coefficients between the MRI and the Composite Indices that show significant or
marginally significant p-values. Table 1a corresponds to positive values (maths-related) and Table 1b to negative values (readingrelated), all sorted in descending absolute r-value.
The influence of gender in maths and reading scores considered separately is well known, so it is not surprising to find a significant
relationship between Gender and the MRI. The correlation is positive and the dummy variable is 0=female, 1=male, so being a male
has a strong positive relationship with having positive MRI values (better results in mathematics than in reading PISA scores).
Another positive influence is the Discriminating school climate factor, with positive values (more discriminating school climate)
related with better mathematical scores. Also related with good mathematical scores are the factors ICT as a topic in social interaction,
Use of ICT outside of school (for schoolwork activities) and Use of ICT at school in general.
Negative correlations indicate the factors related with better performance in reading than in mathematics. The first two, unsurprisingly, are Joy/Like Reading factor, and Self-concept of reading: Perception of competence. The rest of the factors are Respect for people
from other cultures, Student’s interest in learning about other cultures, and Student’s attitudes towards immigrants. Some other factors related
with reading-oriented students seem to have a non-cognitive/psychological origin, such as Perspective-taking, Global-mindedness, Work
mastery or How much effort did you put into this test?
3.2. The effect of performance-based grouping
To continue with this analysis, we analysed positive correlations (factors related to maths-oriented students) and negative correlations (factors related to reading-oriented students) separately. The goal is to see the possible change in non-trivial and significant or
marginally significant factors when taken into account the performance-based grouping, and also the emergence of new factors
affecting students belonging to one or various of groups which was not the case in the total sample analysis.
First, we must analyse the positive factors shown in Table 1, comparing these results for the total sample with the correlations when
dividing the sample in performance-based groups in Table 2. When the p-value exceeds 0.1 (non-significant p-value), the cell is left
blank.
As can be seen, the effect of gender is not significant for high-performance students, and the only factor affecting all groups is
Discriminating school climate. The factors ICT as a topic in social interaction and Body Image are non-significant for students in the low
performers group. Use of ICT outside of school, Learning time (minutes per week) - <science> and Use of ICT at school in general are only
significant for medium performers, and Family wealth disappears when considering this performance-based grouping.
Additionally, some factors emerge as significant which were not in the total sample, as can be seen in Table 3.
Table 3 shows the importance of Grade compared to modal grade in country for low performers and the emergence of a group of socioeconomic factors affecting mainly medium and high performers, but not low performers. These socio-economic factors include Index of
economic, social and cultural status, Index of highest parental education in years of schooling, Highest parental occupational status, Father’s
Education, Highest Education of parents and ISEI of mother. Another group of socio-economic factors only affects medium performers: ICT
resources, Home possessions and Mother’s Education.
Another two subjective factors are: Self-concept of reading: Perception of difficulty, which affects medium and high performers, and
Subjective well-being: Positive effect, which is non-trivial only for medium performers.
The same analysis can be carried out for negative factors, i.e., factors related to reading-oriented performance. Factors affecting
reading for the total sample when taking into account the performance-based grouping are analysed in Table 4.
Joy/Like Reading is still most important factor related with reading-oriented students, also when considering level of performance,
and it is especially significant (p=<0.001) for medium and high performers. Other factors affecting all groups are cultural-oriented
factors such as Respect for people from other cultures, Student’s interest in learning about other cultures and Student’s attitudes towards
immigrants, which are more significant for medium performers.
The rest of the factors vary when considering level of performance. For example, Work mastery becomes non-significant for high
performers. Two factors, Self-concept of reading: perception of competence and Global mindedness become non-significant for low performers; self-concept as a competent reader is an especially significant factor for high performers. The medium performers group is the
most similar to the total sample as the only total factor that disappears for this group is How much effort did you put into this test?, which
is also non-significant for high performers.
New factors related with reading-orientation which emerge when taking into account level of performance are shown in Table 5.
Meta-cognition: summarising emerges as a relevant factor as it is related with the three levels of performance. Awareness of intercultural communication is related with medium and high performance, but not low. Meta cognition: understanding and remembering only
affects low performers. Medium performers are affected by two factors related with socio-cultural context: Immigration status and
Number of school changes. Finally, Student’s expected occupational status (SEI) and Meta-cognition: assess credibility only affects high
performers.

4. Discussion
A Maths-Reading Index (MRI) has been developed by subtracting individual maths scores from reading scores for the Spanish
sample of PISA 2018, taking into account PISA methods of measuring mathematics and reading achievement. The values of that index
would show the individual trend of having better scores in mathematics (positive values) or in reading (negative values) despite the
individual values of both subjects and have been used for different purposes in some previous studies (Breda & Napp, 2019; Keller
et al., 2021; Rapp & Borgonovi, 2019). In this case, we have studied the relationship between the MRI and contextual factors of the
individuals by means of a correlation analysis, but adding a new point of view with different levels of grouping: the total sample, an
achievement-level based grouping and the interaction between achievement and gender.
4.1. Maths-related factors
Factors showing a positive correlation with the MRI are associated with achieving better maths scores than reading scores. Not
surprisingly, due to the relationship between mathematical scores and reading scores with gender highlighted by many studies (for
example Else-Quest et al., 2010; Guiso et al., 2008; González de San Román & De la Rica, 2016; Hermann & Kopasz, 2021; Marks,
2008; among others), the MRI is strongly positive when related with Gender: being a male student is associated with having better
maths scores than reading scores, as indicated by the prior research mentioned.
Nevertheless, when performance level is taken into account, the MRI is related to Gender for low and medium performers, but not
for high performers. In previous studies which analyse mathematical or reading scores separately and take into account gender and
some performance levels, the situation clearly differs. For example, Salvi del Pero and Bytchkova (2013), analysing the gender gaps in
mathematics, reading and science for the whole PISA 2009 data, find larger gender differences for low and high performers than for the
medium performers in the three domains. Also Gilleece et al. (2010), using PISA 2006 mathematics and science data in Ireland, find a
significant effect of gender when comparing high and low performers. In the same vein, Zhou et al. (2017) find consistent male
outperformance in PISA 2003 and PISA 2012 mathematics scores for high performers in ten representative countries. In our opinion,
the explanation for this apparent discrepancy lies in the influence of reading skills over mathematical —and eventually science—
achievement (Ajello et al., 2018; Akbasli et al., 2016; Ding & Homer, 2020; Koyuncu & Firat, 2020; Shelley & Yildirim, 2013) and the
correlation between both scores (Stoet & Geary, 2015; Rapp & Borgonovi, 2019).
Apart from gender, some other factors are positively correlated with the MRI. The set of factors can differ if the total sample or the
performance- based grouping is taken into account, except for Discriminating school climate, which is positively correlated for the total
sample and also when considering achievement level. If both gender and performance are combined, this factor is significantly
correlated with both genders for medium performers, but only with males for high performers; it disappears for low performers. This

result for the total sample could be related with some non-cognitive/psychological factors, probably the salience of discriminating
messages from teachers.
No more global maths-oriented factors are statistically significant for all groups when the level-based grouping is taken into account, especially for low performers. It is interesting to note a group of ICT-related factors for the whole group, which corresponds to
medium performers (ICT as a topic in social interaction, Use of ICT outside the school -for schoolwork activities- and Use of ICT at school in
general) and high performers (ICT as a topic in social interaction). Medium or high performers who have better scores in maths than in
reading probably feel comfortable with the use of technical devices inside or outside school, as they are frequently oriented in their
future to science or technical careers and use this preference as a theme for social interaction with their peers. When gender is added to
this analysis, these factors appear in the medium performers group, affecting both genders (Use of ICT outside the school -for schoolwork
activities-), men (Use of ICT at school in general) or women (ICT as a topic in social interaction).
When the total sample is split using performance levels some new factors emerge. They include some socio-economic factors
usually established in previous studies as related with mathematical scores (Gorostiaga & Rojo-Alvarez, 2016; Zhou et al. 2017),
reading scores (Kılıç & Depren, 2022) or science scores (Alivernini & Maganelli, 2015; Chen et al., 2021), but they affect only medium
and high performers (Index of economic, social and cultural status, Index of highest parental education in years of schooling, Highest parental
occupational status, Father’s Education, Highest Education of parents and ISEI of mother) or medium performers (ICT resources, Home
possessions and Mother’s Education), and they do not affect low performers. When the combined effects of gender and performance are
analysed, socio-economic factors still do not affect the low performers group and affect only female students from the high performers
group (Index of economic, social and cultural status). For medium performers, these factors affect separately only male students (Index of
economic, social and cultural status, Index of highest parental education in years of schooling, Highest Education of parents (ISCED), Mother’s
Education (ISCED), ISEI of mother, ISEI of father, Highest parental occupational status) or only female students (Home educational resources). These results point to the need for a more detailed analysis of how socio-economic factors influence achievement.
Grade compared to modal grade in country is also a very important new factor in the level grouping analysis, affecting solely low
performers; and when the combined effects of gender and achievement are analyzed, it is statistically significant just for females in the
low performance group, and just for males in the medium performance group. A similar factor named Grade repetition is found to be
significant, affecting male students at medium performance level, but having a negative effect, i.e., related with better reading than
mathematical scores for the male members of this group. These factors can also be related with the Number of school changes which is
relevant for medium performers of both genders. In this vein, Hermann and Kopasz (2021), studying the relationship between the
educational policies of countries and the gender gap in mathematics, reading and science, find that Grade retention has the most
consistent correlation with the gender gap: male students perform better than females in countries when there is a high number of
grade repeaters, but they find no statistically significant differences between high, medium or low performers. Gorostiaga and
Rojo-Alvarez (2016) analyse factors associated with top performance in mathematics for the Spanish sample of PISA 2009 and found
Grade repetition to have a negative effect. Grade level, which is a measure of grade repetition and the school starting age, is also a
relevant predictive factor for high and low achievement in mathematics and science according to Gilleece et al. (2010). As Spain is
listed among the countries with a higher percentage of grade repeaters, this question should be researched carefully in order to
reinforce maths achievement among low performers.
Finally, we will review factors related to students’ self-concept. Some subjective factors such as Self-efficacy have been established
as affecting achievement (Alivernini & Manganelli, 2015; Chen et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2017). When the level of performance
grouping is considered, two subjective factors emerge: Self-concept of reading: Perception of difficulty for medium and high performers,
and Subjective well-being: Positive effect for medium performers. In other words, students’ self-perception as having difficulties reading is
related with better scores in mathematics, at least for medium and high performers. A similar result is found in Koyuncu and Firat
(2021) for reading literacy. Remarkably, when gender is taken into account Self-concept of reading: Perception of difficulty only affects
female students for medium performers.
4.2. Reading-related factors
The strongest negative correlation for the MRI for the total sample is Joy/like reading. This factor is also related to the MRI
regardless of whether performance level or gender are taken into account. These results show the important association between
enjoying reading and the tendency to have better scores for PISA reading items than for mathematical items, and they are in
concordance with some previous studies: for example, when analysing the reading gap for PISA 2009 data Brozo et al. (2014) also use a
‘enjoyment of reading’ factor as a part of ‘reading engagement’, which is strongly associated with PISA reading scores. Enjoying
reading also plays an important role in the verbal motivation of top performers in the study by Keller et al. (2021) using six waves of
PISA data (2000 to 2015) for 82 countries. Kılıç and Depren (2022) find that Enjoy/like reading is associated with high performance in
reading scores for the Chinese PISA 2018 sample, while Koyuncu and Firat (2020) find this factor is relevant for the same PISA 2018
reading data in China, Turkey and Mexico. Gorostiaga and Rojo-Alvarez (2016) analyse factors associated with top performance in
mathematics for the Spanish sample of PISA 2009 and also that this factor has a positive effect.
Apart from Enjoy/like reading or Grade repetition, which have been analysed previously, two sets of factors related with negative
values in the MRI can be highlighted from the results of this study.
The first set of factors is found initially for the total sample, and correspond to Respect for people from other cultures, Student’s interest
in learning about other cultures, and Student’s attitudes towards immigrants, Perspective-taking or Global-mindedness, which can be clearly
related with a better cross-cultural vision obtained by reading. Reading also provides experiences that increase young students’
perspective taking, providing them with a more open-mindedness about the world and eventually a greater degree of maturity. The
10

‘other cultures-immigration’ factors affect all level-based groups and mainly male students when gender is also taken into account.
The second set of factors correspond to subjective factors or meta-cognitive factors. Self-concept of reading: Perception of competence
affects the total sample, medium and high performers when the level of performance is considered, and gender has different effects
depending on level: for low and high performers it only affects female students, but for medium performers it affects both. Metacognition is not a significant factor for the total sample, but Meta-cognition: summarising is significant for all levels, Meta-cognition:
understanding and remembering for low performers, and Meta-cognition: assess credibility for high performers, especially men. In this vein,
Koyuncu and Firat (2021) established Meta-cognition: assess credibility and Meta-cognition: summarising as the most significant factors
affecting students’ reading literacy in Turkey, China and Mexico using PISA 2018 data, with a set of ten factors also found to be
relevant, including Meta-cognition: understanding and remembering. Furthermore, Kılıç and Depren (2022) find Metacognition: assess
credibility to be a factor which maximises high performance in reading scores for Turkey and China.
Another interesting subjective factor is General fear of failure. It is as a negative factor for females in the medium performance group
and seems to be inversely related to subjective positive factors regarding self-competence, also affecting female students. These results
indicate the importance of providing successful experiences in science and mathematics for female students at this age, in order to
decrease fear and feelings of failure and increase the presence of women in STEM-related fields.
5. Conclusions
The rise in young people, especially female students, choosing STEM-related studies requires more detailed knowledge of the
reasons and personal and contextual characteristics that can affect this decision. Following Eccles’ expectancy-value theory (Eccles,
2009; Eccles & Wang, 2016), individual polarisation relative to the tendency to obtain better scores in mathematics or reading may be
behind this decision, as evidenced in various previous studies (Breda & Napp, 2019; Keller et al., 2021; Maltese & Tai, 2011; Wang &
Degol, 2013; Wang et al., 2013, 2017).
Developing a new construct such as the MRI can therefore provide a better understanding of factors associated with this polarisation, based not only on gender but also performance levels and how these two factors interact. This paper addresses this analysis,
understood as an exploratory and correlational study applied to a specific sample, but it can also yield some recommendations for
future actions. Overall, we can conclude that increased effectiveness requires the design of actions that are not homogeneous, but
rather that take into account students’ gender and performance levels.
Gender is one of the factors linked to the MRI, demonstrating that male subjects in the sample have positive index values, i.e., a
tendency to have better results in mathematics than reading, than females in the sample; the same cannot be said for all performance
levels and it does not affect students who perform well in both areas. So, one possible conclusion from this study is that female students
have good scores in reading independently of their results in mathematics; so female students who are good in mathematics are also
good in reading (and consequently belong to the high performance group) and the relative mathematical ‘advantage’ of male students
found in other levels disappears. As long as this situation can be associated with the intention of studying maths-related degrees, a
consequence for us would be the importance of reinforcing mathematical success for female student and to highlight their capacities in
mathematics or science —especially for high performers— in order to increase the number of women in STEM fields.
5.1. Limitations and future studies
This study has only taken into account the Spanish sample of PISA 2018 data, which may affect the generalisation of results for
other countries or regions. Future short- and medium-term studies should therefore replicate these results in other educational contexts
and in other countries in order to detect possible patterns associated with the MRI.
On the other hand, the correlation analysis highlights the presence of positive or negative association between the MRI and some
factors but does not imply causality. Including some more complex statistical techniques could be considered to extend the analysis of
relationship between maths-reading achievement and some personal and non-personal factors, for example at school level.
