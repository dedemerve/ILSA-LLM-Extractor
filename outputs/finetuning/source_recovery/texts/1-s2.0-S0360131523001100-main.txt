A B S T R A C T  
The interactive, constructive, active, and passive technology scale (ICAP-TS) is a recently 
developed measure of qualitatively different modes of educational use of technology. Therefore, 
this study had a two-fold purpose. On the one hand, it aimed to examine the construct validity of 
the ICAP-TS in the Serbian school context. Additionally, we used multilevel modeling to analyze 
to what extent the two characteristics of school context, namely principal support and innovative 
school climate, could predict how teachers use technology measured by the ICAP-TS. The par­
ticipants were 364 primary and secondary school teachers in Serbia. Confirmatory factor analysis 
demonstrated that the 12-item ICAP-TS has an expected internal structure in a sample of Serbian 
teachers. Furthermore, the results revealed positive within-school effects of the innovative school 
climate on all four types of educational use of technology. Contrary to our expectations, the 
principal support was not related to the integration of educational technology at the individual or 
school level. Implications for future research and educational practice were discussed.   
1. Introduction 
One of the fundamental challenges of school systems around the world is the successful integration of technology. The role of digital 
technology and learning has come to the fore especially in the period of the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the fact that we are 
surrounded by technology does not guarantee that educational institutions will use it productively and harness all its benefits (Sailer, 
Murb¨ock, & Fischer, 2021). On the one hand, it is important to continuously examine the factors that enhance the successful 
implementation of technology in teaching and learning. On the other hand, it is not enough to examine only the quantity (frequency) of 
technology use, but also the quality of technology use in the classroom. 
In this study, we seek to examine the quality of technology integration in the Serbian school context. For this purpose, this study 
aims to validate the Interactive, Constructive, Active, and Passive Technology Scale (ICAP-TS; Antonietti et al., 2023) in a sample of 
Serbian teachers. In this way, we will expand the existing literature on the construct validity and reliability of the ICAP-TS. Another 
goal of this study is to examine the relationships between principal support and innovative school climate, on the one hand, and 
different types of technology use in learning activities, on the other hand. The current empirical research can provide valuable 
knowledge about the role of the school organizational environment in encouraging the application of technology in teaching and 
learning. Such knowledge is needed for a finer understanding of the determinants of teachers’ fostering cognitive engagement of

2. Conceptual framework 
2.1. Technology integration – the ICAP framework 
The educational use of technology is a complex concept that includes different dimensions that have been measured in various ways 
(Consoli, D´esiron, & Cattaneo, 2023). Scholars have pointed out that digital technology implementation is not a binary act but a 
multidimensional process (Liu, Geertshuis, & Grainger, 2020). Consoli et al. (2023) define technology integration as ‘the use of 
technology in educational contexts to support educational goals, or as the process leading to that’ (Consoli et al., 2023, p. 16). In recent 
years, researchers have paid more and more attention to the quality of technology integration in learning environments. In pursuit of 
this objective, it is important to use theoretically well-founded instruments that assess the use of technology in enhancing high-quality 
instruction. 
The ICAP model (Chi et al., 2018; Chi & Wylie, 2014) provides a comprenhensive explanation of how outcomes of student learning 
are related to their cognitive engagement. In this framework, cognitive engagement is conceptualized as ‘the process of interacting 
with instruction or instructional materials’ (Chi et al., 2018, p. 1791). The basic premise of this model is that student achievement 
depends on the degree of their cognitive engagement. Moreover, students’ learning activities are observable manifestations of four 
different types of cognitive engagement of students, which are arranged hierarchically from the highest to the lowest: interactive, 
constructive, active, and passive. A passive type of engagement (e.g., watching a video) is characterized by paying attention to the 
information contained in the teaching material (Chi et al., 2018). Active learning (e.g., note-taking) is based on students’ hands-on 
work and leads to connecting new information with existing knowledge (Sailer et al., 2021). The constructive mode of engagement 
(e.g., creating a concept map) involves cognitive activation of students in such a way that they independently create new knowledge. 
Finally, interactive learning (e.g., group problem solving) is the highest mode of cognitive engagement that is based on dialogue and is 
characterized by information sharing between students, discussion, and exchange of ideas (Chi et al., 2018). It should be noted, 
however, that not all group activities result in interactive student learning. Authentic interactive learning implies that at least two 
students exchange constructive statements and respect each other. In this way, students can co-construct new knowledge together, 
which otherwise would not be attainable through individual activities (Chi & Wylie, 2014). 
Passive, active, constructive, and interactive learning activities can be supported by technology, which may lead to different 
outcomes (Wekerle, Daumiller, & Kollar, 2022). For example, a recent study of distance learning (Fütterer, Hoch, Lachner, Scheiter, & 
Stürmer, 2023) indicated that the use of technology in learning activities at higher levels (according to the ICAP framework) is a 
predictor of students’ cognitive activation. Wekerle et al. (2022) reported that active, constructive, and interactive learning activities 
with technology are positively related to higher education students’ learning outcomes. Therefore, a solid body of research indicates 
the ICAP framework is relevant for understanding different types of technology integration in teaching and learning (Antonietti et al., 
2023; Fütterer et al., 2023; Sailer, Murb¨ock, & Fischer, 2021). 
Starting from the ICAP theory, Antonietti et al. (2023) created an instrument, the ICAP-TS, which measures different types of 
technology use in teaching and learning: passive, active, constructive and interactive (Antonietti et al., 2023). These types of tech­
nology use in learning activities are arranged on a continuum of student cognitive engagement, and each subsequent type requires a 
higher level of student engagement. It is important to understand that different modes of using technology can be effective depending 
on the desired learning outcomes (Sailer, Schultz-Pernice, & Fischer, 2021). Although technology-enhanced interactive activities are 
associated with the best learning outcomes, it is unlikely that students participate only in higher-level learning activities. Lower-level 
learning activities, such as, for example, watching instructional videos, can serve as a preparation for activities at higher levels of the 
ICAP continuum (Sailer, Schultz-Pernice, & Fischer, 2021). The significant advantages of the ICAP-TS are briefness and a good 
theoretical foundation. Unlike technocratic-based measurement instruments that assess how often different technological means are 
used in the classroom, the ICAP-TS assesses to what degree technology is used to engage students in different types of learning ac­
tivities. The authors reported that a scale model with four first-order factors and a model with 4 group factors and a higher-order factor 
can be validly used. However, since it is a new scale, it is important to examine its metric characteristics on heterogeneous samples of 
teachers. 
2.2. Relating principal support to technology integration in teaching and learning 
The productive use of technology in learning environments depends not only on the students’ and teachers’ personal beliefs and 
skills but also on the institutional and organizational context (Rauseo et al., 2023; Sailer, Schultz-Pernice, & Fischer, 2021). In fact, 
school organizational factors can influence the quantity and quality of implementation of technology in learning activities (Kon­
stantinidou & Scherer, 2022; Vanderlinde & van Braak, 2010). Until now, scholars have mostly examined teachers’ motivational 
beliefs and skills as determinants of technology integration. For example, more recent evidence (Sailer, Murb¨ock, & Fischer, 2021) 
shows that teachers’ basic digital competencies are a predictor of passive, active, and interactive technology use in teaching and 
learning. In addition to that, knowledge about the application of technology in teaching was significantly related only to constructive 
learning activities with technology (Sailer, Murb¨ock, & Fischer, 2021). However, less is known about the effects of characteristics of 
the school context on the use of technology in teaching (Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022). In the present study, the focus is on the role

of support of the principal and an innovative school climate. 
Principal support represents one of the crucial elements of school leadership (Park, Lee, & Cooc, 2019) that has an important role in 
improving all aspects of teaching. Principal support focused on the application of technology in teaching can be understood as part of 
the principal instructional support to teachers. The leadership functions of school principals related to technology include commu­
nicating and clarifying the goals of implementing technology in the school, empowering teachers in the process of integrating tech­
nology, and creating conditions for the development of teachers’ digital competencies (Ruloff & Petko, 2021). 
However, previous studies have not reported conclusive findings on the role of principal in facilitating the use of technology in 
teaching and learning. For example, recent evidence suggests that principal digital instructional leadership, defined as ‘school leaders’ 
information technology supported strategies that promote and create coherence for teaching and learning in hybrid or remote 
schooling model’ (Berkovich, 2023, p. 2), is positively related to teachers’ internal motivation to implement digital teaching (Ber­
kovich & Hassan, 2022). However, it seems important how teachers perceive the support of the principal in the technology imple­
mentation process. Berkovich and Hassan (2022) argue that principals’ digital leadership will not have positive effects if teachers 
perceive it as controlling behavior. One study (Karaca, Can, & Yildirim, 2013) did not find that the support of the principal was directly 
related to technology integration. Petko, Prasse, and Cantieni (2018) measured the support of the principal as one of the dimensions of 
a higher-order construct called school readiness for information and communication technology (ICT) implementation. In this study, it 
was found that the readiness of the school to implement ICT has a significant positive effect on the readiness of teachers, while the 
latter variable, in turn, has a strong influence on the use of ICT. Using data from the International Computer and Information Literacy 
Study (ICILS) 2018, Konstantinidou and Scherer (2022) reported that in some Western European countries, the expectations of 
principals are positively related to the frequency of using ICT in teaching and the willingness of teachers to develop computer and 
information literacy of students. Based on the presented evidence, we hypothesize that principal support is positively related to the 
different types of technology integration measured with the ICAP-TS. 
2.3. Relating innovative school climate to technology integration in teaching and learning 
Moolenaar, Daly, and Sleegers (2010) define an innovative climate as ‘as the shared perceptions of organizational members 
concerning the practices, procedures, and behaviors that promote the generation of new knowledge and practices’ (Moolenaar et al., 
2010, p. 627). In recent years, researchers have paid considerable attention to the role of school innovativeness in the improvement of 
teaching. For example, analyzing data collected in 48 countries as part of the Teaching and Learning International Survey 2018, 
Bl¨omeke, Nilsen, & Scherer, 2021 found that an innovative climate is positively related to students’ cognitive activation. This means 
that an innovative climate is a determinant of one of the important dimensions of teaching quality. Furthermore, the positive effects of 
school innovativeness have been documented at the individual and school level (Bl¨omeke, Nilsen, & Scherer, 2021). 
Technology integration scholarship has also recognized the role of organizational culture and climate. It has been demonstrated 
that organizational culture is a significant predictor of university professors’ intention to use technology (Huang & Teo, 2020). Chou, 
Shen, Hsiao, and Shen (2019) showed that an innovative school climate is positively related to teachers’ innovative behavior with ICT. 
Moreover, it was found that an innovative climate mediates the relationship between the acceptance of technological innovations by 
teachers and the innovative use of ICT. 
Despite the significant contributions of previous studies, several significant gaps in the existing literature can be observed. For 
example, we did not find any empirical research on the relation between an innovative climate and the different modes of technology 
use in learning activities. We believe that the positive effects of an innovative climate are expected because more complex types of the 
educational use of technology can be considered as specific forms of innovative behavior of teachers (Thurlings, Evers, & Vermeulen, 
2015). Moreover, an innovative climate represents a context that can enable teachers to learn how to better implement technology in 
their instructional practice. 
The use of technology for the purpose of cognitive engagement of students can be understood as a job demand that, on the one 
hand, presents a challenge to teachers, and on the other hand, offers opportunities to improve teaching. From the perspective of the Job 
Demands-Resources theory (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017), an innovative climate is one of the factors of job crafting, which includes 
behaviors aimed at searching for new job resources and changing job demands (M¨akikangas, Bakker, & Schaufeli, 2017). According to 
this theory, an innovative climate represents a work environment that encourages teachers to engage in demanding work tasks 
(Bl¨omeke, Nilsen, & Scherer, 2021). This assertion is supported by empirical evidence accumulated in a non-school context 
(M¨akikangas et al., 2017) which indicates that an innovative climate positively predicts job crafting. Moreover, teacher behavior can 
be influenced by social norms that characterize an innovative climate (Chou et al., 2019). Thus, it is possible that an innovative climate 
positively predicts the self-innovativeness of teachers, which is significantly related to teacher technology-supported instructional 
practice (Vidergor, 2023). 
It should be noted that until now, studies that were carried out at the individual level, based solely on the individual perceptions of 
teachers, have dominated. Innovativeness in school is contextually determined, and what in one school is a new practice in another 
school may be part of the daily routine (Moolenaar et al., 2010). More precisely, innovation can be conceptualized as individual 
teacher behavior or as a characteristic of the school (Bl¨omeke, Nilsen, & Scherer, 2021). Therefore, it is necessary to use analytical 
techniques that enable simultaneous modeling of the effects of this construct both at the individual (teacher) and school level.

3. What are the relationships among school-level principal support, an innovative climate, and different types of implementing 
technology in learning activities at the school-level? 
3. Methods 
3.1. Sample and procedure 
In total, 364 teachers from 17 primary and secondary schools across Serbia participated in this study. According to the teaching 
level, four types of teachers work in the Serbian school system: primary school class teachers (grades 1–4; students’ age 7–10), primary 
school subject teachers (grades 5–8; students’ age 11–14), vocational school teachers (grades 9–12; students’ age 15–19), and grammar 
school teachers (grades 9–12; students’ age 15–19). The average age of the teachers was 46.36 (SD = 9.60) years. Demographic 
characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. 
Data were collected online and in paper-and-pencil form. Schools with which collaboration had been established earlier were 
contacted by email with a request to participate in the research. Schools that agreed to participate in the study were allowed to choose 
the form of administering the survey to teachers. Teachers from nine schools that opted for an electronic questionnaire received a link 
to Google Forms. In the remaining schools, one of the authors of the paper collected the completed questionnaires in agreement with 
the contact person. All teachers were informed about the purpose of the research and that participation was voluntary and anonymous. 
Data collection was carried out in the period October–December 2022. 
3.2. Measures 
The support of the principal. The support of the principal was measured by items from the instrument that was previously used to 
assess the readiness of the school for the implementation of ICT in the study by Petko et al. (2018). This construct was measured with 
three items that read ‘The principal clearly supports the integration of digital technology in our school’, ‘Colleagues with new ideas 
regarding educational technology are actively supported by the principle’, and ‘The principal is well informed about how teachers use 
digital technology’. Teachers gave their answers using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Given that 
the scale contains three items, the tested one-factor model was a just-identified model with no degrees of freedom. That means that fit

Innovative climate. Innovative climate was measured with an instrument that was developed by Moolenaar et al. (2010). The scale 
contains six items that measure the school’s innovative climate as a unidimensional construct. An example item is: ‘Teachers are 
continuously learning and developing new ideas’. Participants answered to what extent they agreed with the offered items using a 
Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Since the RMSEA value of the original one-factor model of the scale was 
not satisfactory, we allowed one pair of residuals to correlate. More specifically, we correlated residuals for two similarly worded 
items: ‘Teachers are generally willing to try new ideas’ and ‘Teachers are continuously learning and developing new ideas.’ The fit of 
this modified model was acceptable: χ2(8) = 18.205, p < .05, CFI = 0.991, TLI = 0.983, RMSEA = 0.072, 90% confidence interval (CI) 
[0.027, 0.116], and SRMR = 0.018. The composite reliability of the scale was high (ω = 0.920). 
Technology use in learning activities. Antonietti et al. (Antonietti et al., 2023) developed the ICAP-TS, a 12-item scale to measure the 
frequency of different technology-supported learning activities implemented by teachers and students in classes. The items are equally 
distributed in four subscales, each of which reflects passive, active, constructive, and interactive learning activities supported by ICT, 
as defined by the ICAP taxonomy. Therefore, the items measure how often teachers use technology within different learning activities. 
The passive learning subscale includes three items that describe activities in which teachers use technology to present and explain 
content (e.g., ‘To inform about learning objectives and content’). The active learning subscale is made up of three items that describe 
the active use of technology by teachers and students in order for students to apply previously acquired knowledge (e.g., ‘So that they 
write down and record the knowledge imparted’). The constructive learning subscale includes three items that describe learning ac­
tivities in which students acquire new knowledge independently (e.g., ‘So that they can acquire new knowledge individually.‘). Finally, 
the subscale for interactive learning consists of three items that measures cooperative learning activities that represent the context of 
acquiring new knowledge through dialogue (e.g., ‘So that they develop new knowledge together with others.‘). For each item, we asked 
teachers to indicate how often they and their students use ICT to perform the activities presented in the items. Participants were 
informed that ICT include computers, desktop and portable, tablets, smart phones, digital cameras, as well as various programs, 
applications, and networks. Answers were provided on a five-point Likert scale (0 = almost never, 4 = almost every lesson). The results 
of the analysis of the metric properties of this scale are presented in the results section. 
We translated the ICAP-TS using the back-translation procedure. In the first phase, one of the authors of the paper translated the 
items from English to Serbian. Additionally, the professor of German language translated the original items from German into Serbian. 
These two versions of the translation were compared and it was concluded that there are no essential differences. In the next step, 
another independent translator back-translated the Serbian version into English. It turned out that the back-translated version did not 
differ from the one presented in the article by Antonietti et al. (2023). All scale items used in this study are provided in Table A1 in 
Appendix. 
3.3. Data analysis 
In examining the internal structure of the ICAP-TS, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used. CFA allows examination of data 
structure and assumptions about the number of latent factors. CFA was also used in evaluating the construct validity of the measures of 
principal support and an innovative school climate. When evaluating the fit of the tested models, we used indices such as comparative 
fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square 
residual (SRMR). In this study, we followed the traditionally proposed cutoff values (Hu & Bentler, 1999) for the aforementioned fit 
indices. For CFI and TLI, values ≥0.90 were interpreted as satisfactory. For RMSEA and SRMR, values ≤0.08 were considered to 
indicate an acceptable fit of the model to the data. Also, due to the non-normal distribution of data on certain variables, we used the 
maximum likelihood robust estimation method with Huber-White standard errors (Savalei & Rosseel, 2022). 
Multilevel modeling was used to examine the relationships between support of the school principal, an innovative climate and 
teacher scores on the ICAP-TS subscales. Multilevel modeling is a statistical analysis technique that is suitable when the collected data 
are of a hierarchical nature and when participants are grouped into clusters (Hox, Moerbeek, & Van de Schoot, 2017). We tested 
several successive models. First, the random intercept (null) model, which does not include predictors, was tested. This model shows

Given the modest number of schools included in the sample (17 schools), when estimating the model, we used restricted maximum

establish whether the model with more estimated parameters is significantly better compared to the more restricted model. 
CFA was implemented in the R package lavaan (version 0.6.12; Rosseel, 2012), while multilevel modeling was implemented in the 
R package lmer4 (version 1.131; Bates, M¨achler, Bolker, & Walker, 2015). The lmerTest package (version 3.1.3; Kuznetsova,

was used to manipulate and arrange the data, and the MuMIn package (version 1.47.1; Barto´n, 2022) was used to calculate the 
percentage of variance explained by the predictor variables. Variable centering was performed using the misty package (version 0.4.6; 
Yanagida, 2023) in the R environment. The reliability of the scores on the scales was examined using the semTools package (version 
0.5–6; Jorgensen, Pornprasertmanit, Schoemann, & Rosseel, 2022) in R, while correlations and descriptive statistics were calculated in 
the psych package (Revelle, 2022). 
4. Results 
4.1. Construct validity of the ICAP-TS 
In this study, two models of the ICAP-TS were tested, which were also examined in the original validation study (Antonietti et al., 
2023). The first-order four-dimension model had satisfactory fit indices: χ2(48) = 132.846, p < .001, CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.958, RMSEA 
= 0.084 with 90% (CI) [0.068, 0.102], and SRMR = 0.050. Although the RMSEA was slightly above the recommended cutoff value, it 
should be noted that this index is sensitive to the number of indicators and sample size (Maydeu-Olivares, Shi, & Rosseel, 2018). Thus, 
we interpreted the holistic fit of this model as adequate. Furthermore, we tested a model with four first-order factors and one 
higher-order factor. Model fit indices were very similar: χ2(50) = 144.946, p < .001, CFI = 0.966, TLI = 0.955, RMSEA = 0.087 with 
90% (CI) [0.071, 0.104], and SRMR = 0.057. Based on the CFA results, we decided to retain the first-order four-factor model. All factor 
loadings were high and in range from 0.603 to 0.939 (Table 2). 
All ICAP-TS dimensions were positively correlated with each other (Table 3). A particularly high latent correlation was recorded 
between the scores on the constructive and interactive (r = .890, p < .001) learning subscales.

5. Discussion 
This study had a twofold purpose. Firstly, we sought to evaluate the construct validity of the ICAP-TS in the Serbian school context. 
The second goal was to examine the within-and between-school effects of principal support and an innovative climate on different 
dimensions of educational use of technology. In the rest of this section, we will look at the most important results of the present study. 
5.1. Construct validity and reliability of the ICAP-TS 
The obtained findings showed that the ICAP-TS can be validly and reliably used in the educational environment in Serbia. The two 
models with acceptable fit indices were the first-order four-factor model and the higher-order factor model. These findings fully 
support the initial study (Antonietti et al., 2023) conducted in Switzerland. The obtained descriptive statistics showed that teachers in 
the Serbian context most often use technology in teaching so that students play a passive role. This result corroborates the findings 
obtained in the German context (Sailer, Murb¨ock, & Fischer, 2021). The remaining three ways of using technology that require higher 
levels of cognitive engagement of students had lower mean values. Nevertheless, the means of the dimensions of integrating tech­
nology in teaching are similar to the results obtained by researchers in other educational contexts (Antonietti et al., 2023). 
Factor loadings of the indicators were high and ranged from .603 to .939. Although the first-order four-factor model and the 
second-order model showed similar fit indicators, high correlations were recorded between the constructive and interactive use of 
technology in lessons. This finding indicates that these two dimensions are not clearly separated, which may compromise the 
discriminant validity of the scale. Therefore, we encourage researchers in Serbia to consider using a parsimonious scale model with a 
higher order factor.

5.2. Multilevel relations between principal support, an innovative school climate, and technology integration in learning activities 
The obtained results showed that an innovative school climate is significantly related to all four ways of using technology in 
learning activities. More specifically, within a typical school, teachers who perceive a greater presence of an innovative climate are 
more likely to use technology in different ways. This implies that it is important to work on improving the innovative climate in schools 
in order to better integrate technology within teaching and learning. This finding is consistent with the results of previous studies 
(Chou et al., 2019) that showed that the organizational innovative climate is positively related to the innovative use of ICT by teachers. 
A plausible interpretation of these findings is that an innovative climate encourages teachers’ self-innovativeness, which is a positive 
predictor of the successful use of technology in different ways (Vidergor, 2023). On the other hand, between-school effects of inno­
vative climate were not significant. According to our results, it appears that it is important how teachers within a school perceive the 
presence of an innovative climate compared to their colleagues. This finding is partially consistent with earlier research (Bl¨omeke, 
Nilsen, & Scherer, 2021) in which the positive effects of an innovative climate both at the teacher and at the school level were 
documented. It should be noted that the Serbian educational system has its own specificities that should be taken into account when 
interpreting these findings. Relevant factors that were not examined in our study but which can influence the quality integration of 
technology in the school are, for example, the competencies of the principals and teachers, and the goals of the national curriculum. 
Contrary to our expectations, it was found that there is no significant relationship between principal support and different types of 
technology implementation by teachers. This result can be explained in several ways. Firstly, it is possible that the effects of the support 
of the principal on technology use are indirect and mediated by teachers’ motivational beliefs and other professional competences.

practice are predominantly indirect. In the literature (Petko et al., 2018), there is evidence that school readiness to use ICT is positively

schools. From the perspective of the Serbian school context, the question is open to what extent school principals are directly involved 
in improving teaching. Most schools in Serbia have an assistant principal and a school counselor who are involved in improving the 
quality of teaching. 
When comparing the results of our study with the findings available in the literature, it should be borne in mind that previous 
research on the role of principals generally did not separate within-school and between-school effects. Konstantinidou and Scherer 
(2022) conducted one of the few studies in which the relationship between principals’ behavior and teachers’ use of ICT at the school 
level was examined. Using data from teachers and principals from eight countries, this study revealed that the principal’s prioritizing 
of ICT use facilitation was not significantly related to teachers’ ICT practices at the school level. This finding is consistent with our 
research. On the other hand, the principal’s expectations regarding the implementation of ICT had a significant positive effect on some 
aspects of teachers’ implementation of technology (Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022). It should be borne in mind, however, that 
different predictor and outcome variables were examined in this study, as well as that the data were collected in educational contexts 
that differ from the Serbian one. 
It is possible that the perception of support for the application of technology in teaching depends on the role of protagonists in the 
school organization. A Swiss study (Rauseo et al., 2023) revealed that members of school management are more satisfied with the 
pedagogical support of ICT implementation than teachers. Our study showed that the surveyed Serbian teachers perceive the prin­
cipal’s support as present, but that it is not instrumental for different ways of using technology in teaching. It appears that teachers 
need a school principal to set a clear strategy that will convey guidelines on how to better use the potential of digital technologies in 
teaching and learning (Rauseo et al., 2023). 
5.3. Implications and limitations 
The significant strength of this study is the further validation of a new instrument, which has not often been used until now, and 
which is very well theoretically grounded. We believe that this study provides enough evidence for researchers in Serbia to use the 
ICAP-TS to carefully assess how teachers in Serbia use educational technologies. Another strength of this study is the separation of the 
variance of technology integration in teacher and student activities. To date, scholars have focused primarily on the role of teachers’ 
characteristics and their effects on the use of educational technology. 
When it comes to practical implications, the findings indicate that the ICAP-TS is a measuring instrument that can be used to gain 
valid insights into which technology-supported learning activities are dominant in Serbian schools. This is both practical and valuable 
information from the perspective of creating a professional development program for teachers. Furthermore, this study indicates that 
in order to encourage the utilization of technology in various learning activities, it is necessary to work on developing an innovative 
climate in schools. An innovative climate is characterized by norms that encourage risk-taking, new approaches to professional tasks, 
and continuously changing practices (Liou, Canrinus, & Daly, 2019). Considering the characteristics of an innovative climate, it is clear 
that its cultivation in schools is not simple. However, available empirical evidence indicates that principal transformational leadership, 
which includes creating a vision for school development, recognizing teachers’ needs and encouraging teachers’ professional learning, 
contributes to the development of an innovative climate (Moolenaar et al., 2010). 
This study has several limitations that need to be explicated. It is important to keep in mind that the ICAP-TS measures the degree to 
which teachers use technology to increase the possibility of students’ cognitive engagement. However, this scale does not assess other 
dimensions of the quality of technology-supported instruction, such as teacher support and classroom management (Runge, Lazarides, 
Rubach, Richter, & Scheiter, 2023). Likewise, the ICAP-TS is not designed to measure the extent to which teachers and students use 
technology responsibly and safely in teaching and learning activities. Therefore, it is necessary for researchers to create and apply 
measures that are based on a broad understanding of the quality of technology integration in learning environments (Consoli et al., 
2023). Since we did not control individual variables such as teachers’ digital competence, further studies should consider both in­
dividual and school level factors in achieving high-quality teaching with digital technology. A relatively small number of schools were 
included in this study, which probably reduced the statistical power of the tests used. More studies are needed that would test the 
replicability of the obtained results in different settings, using heterogeneous samples of teachers. Subsequent studies can be based on 
longitudinal designs to examine the relationships between those constructs across time. Additionally, in order to better control the 
measurement error, it would be beneficial to apply multilevel structural equation modeling in analyzing the collected data. Finally, 
since the principal support was not identified as a direct predictor of the use of technology in learning activities, it would be good for 
future studies to examine potential mediating mechanisms through which the principals’ behavior creates conditions for the quality 
implementation of technology in teaching and learning. 
6. Conclusion 
The main purpose of this study was to contribute to a better understanding of the factors associated with different ways of using 
technology in the activities of teachers and students. We sought to overcome the limitation of previous research that did not separate 
within-school and between-school effects of school environment characteristics on the qualitatively different ways of applying 
technology in teaching and learning. The obtained results highlighted the role of an innovative school climate. Although our results did
