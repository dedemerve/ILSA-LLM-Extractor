Recent studies have revealed a close link between students' social background and their acquisition of digital competencies. This study investigates i) whether social disparities in digital
competencies are increasing or decreasing over time, and ii) the primary mechanisms driving
these changes. Using data from the German sample of the large-scale ‘International Computer and
Information Literacy Study’ (ICILS), we analyse the digital competencies of secondary school
students and their home and school learning environments in 2013, 2018 and 2023. Theoretically, we draw on the Theory of Effectively Maintained Inequality and hypothesise how social
differences in digital competencies have evolved in the digital age. Through linear regression and
decomposition techniques, our findings indicate that social differences in students' digital competencies have increased significantly over the past decade. Over the entire observation period,
the cultural home environment and school type are the primary factors driving social differences
in digital competencies. Their relevance increases over time in proportion to the overall rise in
social differences in digital competencies. The mechanisms of inequality in digital competencies
closely mirror those in other areas of social inequality. However, it is also apparent that an
increasing proportion of the competence differences by social background remains unexplained
over time, suggesting the need for an in-depth analysis.

1. Introduction
Technological change has introduced digital processes into various aspects of life, and digital competencies have emerged as one of
the most essential skills of the 21st century (European Commission, 2020; OECD, 2015). Various education policy initiatives have been
launched to align the education system with the digital age, and there is a growing demand for digital competencies to be incorporated
into the curriculum as a distinct competence dimension. According to Fraillon (2024), digital competencies primarily involve enabling
students to use information and communication technology (ICT) in a self-determined and effective manner, so they can actively
participate in social life. Acquiring digital competencies is therefore not only about preparing for the labour market but also about
gaining the necessary skills to navigate everyday life in the digital era. However, current research indicates that students from less

privileged families face greater challenges in the digital learning environment than their peers from more privileged families and are
less likely to reach a sufficient level of digital competencies (Bonal & González, 2020; Drossel et al., 2020; Karpiński et al., 2023;
Kennedy et al., 2024). A child's social background is not only a significant factor in educational attainment and success, but also one of
the strongest predictors of their digital competencies (Scherer & Siddiq, 2019).
In recent years, the COVID-19 pandemic has raised public awareness of the importance of digital competencies in the education
sector and has once again drawn attention to the persistent social inequalities in education. In response to these changes, research on
social stratification and social inequality has increasingly focused whether differences in digital competencies (digital inequality) may
represent a new form of social inequality. A central argument of this research is, that parents with high socio-economic status (SES)
tend to secure advantages for their offspring ‘wherever advantages are commonly possible’ (Lucas, 2001, p. 1652). Given the
expansion of education and the growing importance of digital skills, it is reasonable to assume that privileged groups, in particular,
support their children in acquiring digital competencies. The recently published results from the large-scale International Computer
and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) reveal significant differences in digital competencies by social background - with a tendency to
increase over time (Casamassima et al., 2024). However, it remains unclear to what extent this change is statistically significant and
what factors are driving these shifting social disparities.
This study explores this phenomenon and examines both theoretically and empirically how digital inequalities by social background change over time. Analysing changes in educational inequality over time is challenging, as the data and instruments used must
be comparable over time. To identify the changed mechanisms in such time series analyses, it is often necessary to limit oneself to
comparable variables and to use a sufficiently long observation period. In this article, we analyse the mechanisms underlying digital
inequalities on the basis of a representative trend study in Germany covering a period of ten years (ICILS 2013–2023). When we refer to
digital inequality, we mean differences in digital competencies by social background (parental SES). In Europe, countries differ not
only in terms of their degree of digitalisation, but also in terms of their education systems and the extent of social inequalities. For our
study, we have chosen a ‘most-likely’ case design (Levy, 2008), i.e. a case in which an increase in digital inequality over time seems
particularly likely: Germany. The reason for this is a combination of two factors. Firstly, an increase in digital inequality is particularly
likely when the general level of (educational) ICT use is still relatively low at the outset and rises thereafter, because this uptake of the
new opportunities is possibly socially selective. In Germany, educational ICT use was at a low to medium level in the early 2010s
(OECD, 2015), which leaves much room for an increase thereafter. This may especially be a strategy employed by families with high
SES. Secondly, current research increasingly focuses on the role of the school context in the acquisition of digital competencies (Loh
et al., 2025; Lörz et al., 2024). Thus, a higher digital inequality can be expected in countries with higher levels of school segregation.
Germany has a highly stratified education system and pronounced levels of educational inequality by social background (Skopek &
Passaretta, 2021; van de Werfhorst, 2018). A stratified education system with early tracking may contribute to increasing inequalities—including those related to digital competencies—if schools differ systematically in their learning environment (Maaz et al.,
2008). Thus, we selected Germany as a most likely case for observing increasing digital inequality. However, the mechanisms driving
digital inequality remain unclear. This study aims to contribute to addressing this question.
2. Inequalities in digital competencies: State of the art and theoretical background
2.1. State of research and research questions
Research on social inequalities in digital competencies frequently refers to the concept of ‘digital divide’ (Hargittai, 2002; van Dijk,
2020). While unequal access to digital resources was the primary focus in the 1990s (referred to as the ‘first digital divide’), there has
been a subsequent shift since the 2000s towards inequalities in digital usage and skills (known as the ‘second digital divide’) (Attewell,
2001; DiMaggio et al., 2004; van Dijk, 2006). Several studies have demonstrated a correlation between individuals' SES and their
digital skills (e.g. Lybeck et al., 2024; van Deursen & van Dijk, 2011, 2015). This finding also applies to students who have grown up in
a digital world: students from high-SES families tend to possess stronger digital competencies than their less privileged peers (Fraillon
et al., 2019; Hargittai, 2010; Kennedy et al., 2024; Scherer & Siddiq, 2019), a pattern also observed in Germany (Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023; Senkbeil et al., 2019). The literature discusses several reasons for these social disparities in students' digital competencies. In the following, we will first examine the key factors that explain digital inequality based on international research
literature. We will then turn our attention to Germany and outline how conditions have changed in recent years. Finally, we identify
the open research questions that we address in this article.
2.1.1. Three main mechanisms explaining digital inequality based on international research
First, several studies focus on the digital home environment of students, including not only access to digital resources, but also
patterns of digital usage within the family. While there is now near-universal access to the Internet across OECD countries
(Organisation for Economic Co-operation & Development, 2024), significant social disparities persist in the quality of access—for
instance, in terms of Internet speed (Silva et al., 2018) or the number and diversity of digital devices available at home (van Deursen &
van Dijk, 2019). Beyond access, parental digital involvement and support also varies according to SES. Parents with higher SES tend to
be more likely to provide guidance that emphasize educational use and critical digital practice, whereas lower-SES parents usually
offer comparatively less targeted support (Hou et al., 2024; Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023; Ren et al., 2022). Regarding students'
usage of digital media, the total time spend online appears to show minimal variation across social backgrounds (OECD, 2015).
However, students from high-SES families tend to use the Internet more frequently for informational and educational purposes
(Becker, 2022; Micheli, 2015; OECD, 2015; Vekiri, 2010)—in other words, they are more likely to engage in ‘capital enhancing

activities’ (Micheli, 2015, p. 56), which are generally found to be positively associated with digital competencies (Gil-Flores et al.,
2012; Mehrvarz et al., 2021). In sum, the digital home environment of high-SES students often appears to be structured in ways that
reinforce the acquisition of digital competencies, whereas the digital home environments of lower-SES students may provide fewer
opportunities for skill development. Overall, the available evidence suggests broadly similar findings across different age groups and
national contexts (USA, China, Europe, international PISA data); however, these findings should be interpreted with caution given
differences in data sources, operationalisations, and research designs. Most of the reported results stem from quantitative research but
are also supported by qualitative studies (Calderón Gómez, 2020; Hou et al., 2024). Despite this finding of SES differences in the digital
home environment, it is unclear how these disparities develop over time and whether any change has also brought about a change in
the SES gap in digital competencies.
Second, another strand of literature focuses on the (offline) learning conditions at home—that is, the cultural home environment. This
body of work is often inspired by Bourdieu's (1977, 1986) theory of cultural and social reproduction, which emphasises the role of
parental cultural capital. Cultural capital in this sense does not only include material resources such as books and cultural assets, but
also knowledge, skills, and orientations such as orientation towards learning. According to this theory, the transmission of cultural
capital from parents to children is assumed to be a key mechanism through which social inequality is reproduced. Several scholars have
argued that these ‘classical’ forms of cultural capital may also be relevant for students' acquisition of digital competencies (Hou et al.,
2024; Notten & Becker, 2017; Yuen et al., 2018). In fact, several empirical studies have demonstrated an association between families'
cultural capital—often measured by the number of books at home—and students' digital competencies (Hatlevik et al., 2015; Kennedy
et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2022). This correlation may indicate that cultural resources traditionally associated with academic success can
also offer advantages in the digital domain as well, possibly by fostering critical literacy and learning orientations that facilitate the
development of digital competencies. The evidence here is mainly based on (international) large-scale studies such as PISA and ICILS
that regularly measure the number of books at home and similar items, but qualitative research from different countries yields similar
results as well (Calderón Gómez, 2020; Hou et al., 2024; Yuen et al., 2018). However, we still lack evidence on the question in how far
this association between the cultural home environment and students' digital competencies remained stable over the last years.
A third strand of literature attributes social inequalities in digital competencies to differences in the school environment. Educational
and social stratification research reveals that both the allocation of students to school types and the choice of specific schools are
socially selective, with children from high-SES backgrounds disproportionally concentrated in more prestigious tracks and institutions
(Alegre & Benito, 2012; Barg, 2013; Dumont et al., 2019; Saporito, 2003). If these schools differ in their provision of digital resources
and learning opportunities, this may contribute to social inequalities in students' digital competencies. In fact, Loh et al. (2025)
demonstrate that students with highly educated parents attend schools with superior ICT resources compared to their less privileged
peers. Although the overall evidence regarding the role of school characteristics in fostering students' digital competencies remains
limited (Fraillon et al., 2019; Loh et al., 2025), some findings suggest a possibly meaningful effect. For example, Loh et al. (2025)
estimate that 11% of the association between parental education and students’ digital skills is attributed to differences in school ICT
resources. This indicates that school environments may play a role in shaping social disparities in digital competences. However, the
overall evidence on the role of school characteristics are rather weak and partly inconsistent. This could be the case because the results
may depend on the respective country context and the respective education system. In addition, digitalisation has increased in many
schools since the COVID-19 pandemic. However, it is not clear whether this has led to a reduction in digital inequality or, on the
contrary, students from privileged backgrounds have benefited more strongly from this process.
To summarise the state of research so far, the finding of social inequalities in students’ digital competencies is widely reported in
the literature. Social differences in the digital home environment, cultural home environment, and school environment, represent
likely contributing factors. The results of studies that consider several of these factors simultaneously have often pointed to a core role
of parental cultural capital (Kennedy et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2022). However, previous research has rarely examined the changes in
digital inequalities over time and considered multiple sets of factors simultaneously when analysing the underlying causes. Our study
addresses this research gap. Since we focus on Germany as a case, we will next report on how digital inequalities have changed in
Germany and which context-specific factors regarding the school environment need to be taken into account.
2.1.2. Changing digital inequality and context-specific factors in Germany
In Germany, studies that address changes in digital inequalities are still relatively scarce and often tend to be descriptive in nature.
For instance, Casamassima et al. (2024), examined cultural conditions at home and revealed that the mean differences in digital
competencies are more or less stable, while the differences in competence levels are increasing. Using a similar approach, Senkbeil
et al. (2019) also reported a tendency towards an increase in social inequalities in digital competencies; however, the changes are
relatively small and insignificant. At the same time, both studies focus on cultural conditions rather than socio-economic conditions,
and the reasons for the changed associations remain unclear.
In addition to the factors discussed in the previous section, it is often assumed that the role of school type is particularly pronounced
in highly stratified education systems such as the German education system, where access to the most prestigious and demanding track
(‘Gymnasium’) is closely associated with family background (see section 3.2 for more details on the German education system). In line
with this assumption, recent studies focusing on Germany report that students attending the upper secondary school track (‘Gymnasium’) possess higher digital competencies than students in other school types (Eickelmann et al., 2024; Passaretta & Gil-Hernández,
2023). Moreover, Passaretta and Gil-Hernández (2023) indicated that the school track accounts for more than half of the SES gap in
students' digital skills in grade nine in Germany. When analysing changing digital inequalities in Germany, it is therefore necessary to
take into account the type of school and the conditions at home.

2.1.3. Research questions of this study
To our knowledge, there is no study that investigate whether and how the explanatory contribution of the various explanatory
mechanisms changes over time. In our study, we address these research gaps by analysing the following research questions (RQ):
RQ1 Has social inequality in the digital competencies of students in Germany changed between 2013 and 2023?
RQ2 If digital inequalities change over time, which mechanisms are primarily responsible for this change?
2.2. Theoretical mechanisms and hypotheses
Various resource-oriented theoretical approaches can be used to analyse digital inequalities, ranging from rational choice approaches, which focuses on differences in parental investment behaviour (Boudon, 1974), to cultural reproduction approaches, which
tend to focus more on the cultural learning conditions in the parental home (Bourdieu, 1977). Since we are interested in changing
societal conditions and digital inequalities over time, we draw on the Theory of Effectively Maintained Inequality (EMI) that explicitly
focuses on the development of inequalities over time (Lucas, 2001). The EMI theory has already proven its validity in explaining
changes in social inequalities over time across various outcomes (Ayalon & Shavit, 2004; Lörz et al., 2026; Lucas, 2024) and has the
advantage of integrating the arguments of rational choice approaches and cultural reproduction theory regarding changes in inequality
over time. According to rational choice theory, investment in education is the result of a cost-benefit analysis; for example, as costs
decline (e.g., by lowering financial barriers), a reduction in educational inequality is predicted (Boudon, 1974). This mechanism is
integrated into EMI theory. However, it also incorporates Bourdieu's argument regarding the persistence of inequality. As Lucas (2001,
p. 1652) states, ‘Effectively maintained inequality posits that socioeconomically advantaged actors secure for themselves and their
children some degree of advantage wherever advantages are commonly possible’. Lucas (2001) developed this theory in the context of
educational expansion and persistent educational inequalities. He argues that as long as a certain level of education is not universally
attained, high-SES families will aspire to reach that level of education and will utilise their resources to achieve it. However, once that
level becomes nearly universal, they will ‘seek out whatever qualitative differences there are at that level and use their advantages to
secure quantitatively similar but qualitatively better education’ (Lucas, 2001, p. 1652). Families may even combine both strategies—pursuing both quantitatively and qualitatively superior educational outcomes. This reasoning aligns with Bourdieu's (1977, p.
497) theory of cultural and social reproduction, which posits that ‘the most culturally privileged find their way into institutions
capable of reinforcing their advantage’. High-SES parents understand the ‘rules of the game’ and develop strategies to maintain or
enhance their children's advantages (Lareau et al., 2016, p. 279).
In sum, the EMI theory integrates key approaches and mechanisms from inequality research. This allows to combine different
trends over time regarding the development of educational inequalities at different levels or dimensions, such as a decline in inequality
in secondary education and the persistence or even increase of inequality in tertiary education (Lucas, 2001). In the digital sphere, this
aligns with the shift from the first to the second digital divide, i.e. decreasing inequalities in access but increasing inequalities in usage
patterns und competencies (see above).
We apply the EMI theory to the context of digital competencies. It can be argued that digital competencies are an increasingly
significant asset in today's societies (Calderón Gómez, 2020; Hou et al., 2024), and parents with high SES will increasingly seek to
ensure their children are well-equipped in this domain. The acquisition of digital competencies can be regarded as a new strategy for
securing an advantage in future participation in society (Calderón Gómez, 2020). With an increasing level of digitalisation, we expect
this strategy to become more important over time and, therefore, hypothesise a rise in social inequality in students' digital competencies in Germany.
H1. The association between parental SES and students' digital competencies has increased between 2013 and 2023 in Germany.
However, it remains unclear how precisely this occurs. According to Bronfenbrenner's (1977, 1994) ecological approach, individual development is largely influenced by the learning context in which individuals are embedded. Following this perspective, we
assume that the core mechanism underlying social differences is the selection of a stimulating learning environment that fosters
children's digital competencies. We identify three distinct types of learning environments in this regard, derived from our literature
review: (1) the digital home environment, (2) the cultural home environment, (3) the school learning environment.
(1) The digital home environment
High-SES parents are more likely to invest in their children's digital learning, both because they can afford a superior digital
infrastructure (e.g., multiple devices, high-speed Internet) and because they often possess greater ‘digital skills and digital-pedagogical
literacy’ (Hou et al., 2024, p. 10). According to Lucas's (2001) Theory of Effective Maintained Inequality, such parental investments
can take two forms: quantitative, for example through increased availability of digital resources and qualitative, through guided,
goal-oriented, or ‘capital-enhancing’ usage of ICT. By investing in these ways, high-SES families can maintain or even increase advantages, even as overall access to digital resources becomes more widespread.
H2. The increased association between parental SES and students' digital competencies in Germany (H1) is due to changes in the
distribution or relevance of the digital home environment.

(2) The cultural home environment
Since the ‘classical’ cultural capital is regarded as the foundation for the accumulation of ‘digital capital’ (Calderón Gómez, 2020;
Hou et al., 2024), parents may also seek to invest more in the traditional cultural home environment (e.g., spending more time on
cultural activities). Such cultural activities can indirectly foster their children's digital competencies by affecting their learning
orientation, reading skills, abstract thinking and problem-solving competencies (Mikus et al., 2020; Sullivan, 2001, 2008). However,
parents might also attempt to adapt the cultural home environment so that their children derive greater benefit from it in terms of
developing digital competencies, i.e. shifting cultural activities to the digital sphere (e.g. digital reading, online museum visits). In
culturally rich environments, education is generally highly valued, creating favourable learning conditions that support the development of school-relevant skills. As digital competencies have gained greater priority in schools, it follows that they will be
increasingly promoted in the milieus that possess greater cultural capital. In this sense, the distinction between the ‘cultural’ and the
‘digital’ home environment is heavily blurred and should therefore be regarded as an analytical heuristic.
H3. The increase in the association between parental SES and students' digital competencies in Germany (H1) is due to changes in the
distribution or relevance of the cultural home environment.
(3) The school environment
The choice of a higher track at secondary school (in Germany: the ‘Gymnasium’) is a well-known strategy employed by parents with
high SES to secure intergenerational status maintenance (Boudon, 1974; Maaz et al., 2008). The promotion of digital competencies
may be a result of this choice, as students in the upper secondary school track tend to demonstrate better digital competencies than
students in lower tracks (Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023). However, it appears unlikely that the association between parental SES
and school track choice has increased over the past decade, as empirical studies have indicated declining inequalities at the transition
to upper secondary education in Germany (Blossfeld et al., 2015; Schindler, 2017). Nonetheless, inequalities in students' digital
competencies still increase if the upper secondary school track increasingly provides better opportunities to learn digital competencies
compared to other school types (Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023). Several mechanisms may contribute to this trend. Upper secondary schools typically possess greater resources overall and are therefore better equipped to enhance their digital infrastructure
compared to other school types. Parents may also increasingly advocate for the use of digital media at school—a tendency that may be
particularly pronounced among parents with high SES whose children attend the upper secondary school track. Finally, students in the
higher track may derive increasing benefits from peer composition, as they are more likely to be surrounded by digitally competent
classmates (the results of Lörz et al., 2024 support these considerations, but see Hübner et al., 2023 for some contrary evidence). Taken
together, these factors illustrate how the school environment can amplify the advantages of students from high-SES backgrounds in the
acquisition of digital competencies.
H4. The increase in the association between parental SES and students' digital competencies in Germany (H1) is due to changes in the
relevance of the school environment.
In summary, we expect that all three learning environments are likely to play a role in explaining social disparities in digital
competencies. Thus, an increase in the SES gap in students’ digital competencies could be explained by a growing significance of one or
more of these three environments.

In Fig. 1, we summarise our theoretical expectations in more detail graphically. Firstly, we expect a direct association between SES

explainable by the explanatory variables included (H2, H3, H4). With regard to the presumed explanatory mechanisms, we distinguish
between two aspects that could lead to a change in the association between social background and digital competencies. On the one
hand, social inequalities in digital competencies could increase or decrease if the association between social background and the
learning environments change over time (changed distribution: H2A; H3A; H4A). On the other hand, digital inequality may also change if
the importance of the various learning environments for the acquisition of digital skills changes over time (changed correlation: H2B;
H3B; H4B). This distinction between differences in distribution and differences in correlation is important for better understanding why
digital inequality change over time. For example, digital inequalities may increase even if the social distribution across different types
of schools remains stable over time, but the importance of school type for the acquisition of digital skills has changed.
3. Methodology: Sampling, instruments, and analytic procedures
3.1. Sampling in ICILS
To test our theoretical considerations, we use the German sample from the ICILS data from 2013, 2018, and 2023 (Fraillon, 2024;
Fraillon et al., 2019), conducted by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA). The dataset spans a
ten-year period during which digital competencies have gained increasing significance in the education sector. Drawing on a
computer-based assessment of students' computer and information literacy (CIL), alongside various background information about the
students’ conditions at home and school, the data are well-suited for analysing changing patterns of digital inequality over time. The
sampling strategy employed by ICILS utilised a multi-stage clustered sampling approach to gather a representative cross-section of
eight-grade students. In the first step, a random sample of schools was selected, followed by a random sample of eighth-grade students and
teachers at these schools in the second step. The ICILS data is widely regarded as representative for the countries that participated in this
study (Fraillon et al., 2020). The full German sample dataset, covering all three time points, includes 10,945 students. However, for our
analysis, we focus on students from regular schools who were aged between 14 and 16, resulting in an analytical sample of 8548 cases.
However, the sampling approach employed in ICILS requires further clarification, as it was combined with the plausible value
approach of the achievement test. First, digital competencies are considered as a latent construct, and five plausible values were
calculated for each student. The plausible value approach requires that researchers conducting analyses involving CIL achievement
data perform calculations with each of the plausible values and then average the statistics (Braun & von Davier, 2017; Fraillon et al.,
2020; Rubin, 1987). Second, these analyses must be weighted to account for unit non-response, using the student-level weighting
factors provided in the dataset. Third, to obtain unbiased significance testing, we imputed missing values (item non-response) as far as
possible using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). Due to missing values and uncertainty in parameter estimation (FMI
= 0.28) the multivariate analyses were based on 30 imputed datasets that were integrated according to Rubin's rules (1987). Fourth, in
order to reduce sampling bias and adequately account for the hierarchical data structure in the analyses, we calculate cluster-robust
standard errors at school level (Wooldridge, 2002) using the vce(cluster) analysis option implemented in STATA 19. Due to the
clustered data structure, a multilevel analysis was also conducted as a robustness check. As can be seen from a comparison of the classic
OLS regression (Table A4a–c) and the multilevel analysis (Table A3a–c), the key findings differ only marginally (see Appendix).

3.2. Instruments
The central dependent variable in this analysis is digital competencies, measured via a standardised test of computer and information
literacy (CIL). CIL is defined as a person's individual ability to use digital media to research, create, and communicate information to
participate in social life. CIL was measured by a computer-based test covering various areas of digital life (Fraillon, 2024; Fraillon et al.,
2019). The scale ranges from 210 to 708 points (95 percent of cases are between 377 and 621 points): the higher the value, the better
the students' digital competencies.
Parental socio-economic status (SES) is the key independent variable in our analysis. It is a multidimensional construct comprising
various aspects such as income, occupation, and education. In this study, we operationalise it via the ‘International Socio-Economic
Index of Occupational Status’ (ISEI) (Ganzeboom et al., 1992), which is derived from information on parents' occupation provided
by students. The ISEI classification was developed to maximise the role of occupation as an intervening variable between education
and income (Ganzeboom et al., 1992), and therefore encompasses all core aspects of SES. The scale ranges from 10 to 89 points, with
higher value indicating a higher parental SES. To ensure the comparability of results across different years, we z-standardised the ISEI
by year. Furthermore, in the descriptive analysis we distinguish between four quartiles of the ISEI-distribution (25%) and analyse how
the differences between the first (low SES) and fourth (high SES) quartiles have evolved over time.
We test Hypothesis 1 by examining how the relationship between social background and digital competencies has changed over
time (total effect). To provide further insight into the causes of social disparities in digital competencies (indirect effects), we use
various explanatory variables in the analysis (see Table 1).
We employ the number of digital devices (access), the experience with computers and the type of media use (usage) as indicators of
the digital home environment (H2). In the questionnaire, students were asked to indicate how many digital devices (desktop computers, laptops, tablets) were available in their parents' homes. The number of digital devices in households rose markedly between
2013 and 2023 (see Table 1). Based on these figures, it can be assumed that the opportunity to engage with digital devices at home has
increased substantially over time (2.13 in 2013 vs. 2.40 in 2023; p < 0.001). As an indicator of digital media usage, we use students'
experience with computers, measured in years. We distinguish between students who have more than five years of computer experience (X = 1) and students with less experience (X = 0). In contrast to the increased availability of computers, experience in using
computers seems to diminish over time (0.50 in 2013 to 0.41 in 2023; p < 0.001). Furthermore, students were asked about the
purposes for which they use digital media outside of school. In 2013 and 2018, students were questioned about specific activities. We
use the items ‘to prepare for presentations’ and ‘to prepare for tests’ to identify the use of digital devices for school-related purposes. In
contrast, the 2023 questionnaire asked only about ‘use of digital devices for school tasks’. In addition to the wording of the questions,
the response scales also differ between the surveys. Nonetheless, we considered these variables to provide at least a rough proxy for
school-related media use, allowing us to distinguish between students who use digital media for school-related purposes less frequently
(X = 0) or more than once a month (X = 1). Despite these methodological challenges, Table 1 demonstrates that digital media are
increasingly being used for school purposes over time (45% in 2013 vs. 90% in 2023; p < 0.001).
We utilised the number of books at home as an indicator of the cultural home environment (H3). Unfortunately, the data does not
contain in-depth information – such as cultural practices – that could provide a more comprehensive picture of the cultural learning
environment. However, previous research has shown that the number of books in the parental home is still a key variable in explaining
educational differences based on social background (Heppt et al., 2022; Weber & Becker, 2019). The students were asked to estimate
the number of books in their parents' home using a four-point scale, ranging from ‘less than 25 books to ‘more than 200 books’. In the
descriptive analysis, we differentiate between students with more than 100 books at home (X = 1) and those with fewer books (X = 0).
In the context of increasing digitalisation, the distribution of books in households appears to be declining gradually (0.46 in 2013 vs.
0.41 in 2023; p < 0.001).
The school environment is measured by school type in secondary education (H4). Germany has a tracked and hierarchically
structured education system in which all students complete primary school and are then divided into separate secondary school tracks
based on their prior school performance (see Fig. 2). The central allocation of students takes place after primary school in the 4th or 6th
grade (depending on the federal state), whereby the central distinction in secondary education is between the Gymnasium (X = 1,
shaded in grey in Fig. 2) on the one hand and the lower secondary tracks (X = 0) on the other. In addition, there are different types of
comprehensive schools that encompass multiple tracks (X = 0). Overall, these types of school differ in terms of the stimulating learning
environment, the vocational orientation (academic vs. vocational) and access to higher education. In particular, the upper secondary
school track (Gymnasium) is associated with more stimulating learning environments (Maaz et al., 2008) and offers a direct route to
university after successful completion. Many studies have reported that the transition to the Gymnasium is socially selective with
strong differences in transition rates depending on parental SES (for an overview see Maaz et al., 2008). However, it is important to
note that the distribution across school types remained largely stable (Gymnasium~36-37%) over the period of our analysis (see
Table 1). We focus on the school type as the most relevant indicator for the school environment in the German context, as Passaretta
and Gil-Hernández (2023) demonstrated that the school type accounts for more than half of the SES gap in students' digital skills in
grade nine in Germany while additional measures on schools’ ICT infrastructures did not play any role.
To reduce bias in the results, gender (1 = female; 0 = male) and migration background (1 = yes; 0 = no) were controlled for in the
analysis (control variables). These factors must be considered to avoid misinterpretation, particularly in view of the changing distribution of migration background over time (19% in 2013 vs. 29% in 2023; p < 0.001).

3.3. Analytic procedures
To demonstrate how digital inequalities are changing and identify their underlying causes, we proceed through four separate
analysis steps. This step-by-step analysis is structured in the same way as the theoretical considerations (see Fig. 1).
In the first step, we present a descriptive overview of how social differences in digital competencies have changed between 2013
and 2023 (section 4.1). This allows us to assess how the total effect of SES on digital competencies has changed over time (test of
hypothesis H1). In a second step, we describe the changed associations between parental SES and the selection of a stimulating learning
environment (section 4.2). This offers insights into whether the learning environments of the different social groups increasingly differ
over time (test of changed distribution in hypothesis H2A, H3A and H4A). In a third step, we use ordinary least square (OLS) regression
models to analyse how the correlations between the different learning environments and digital competencies changed across the three
time points (section 4.3). This step assesses whether particular learning environments have become more influential in the acquisition
of digital competencies (test of changed correlation in hypothesis H2B, H3B and H4B). Analysis steps 2 and 3 are designed to obtain
more detailed information on how learning environments (distribution) and their impact on the acquisition of digital competencies
(correlation) change over time. Finally, in a fourth analytical step, a KHB effect decomposition is calculated for each year (Karlson
et al., 2012) to illustrate how the overall inequality mechanisms (indirect effects) have changed over time (test of hypothesis H2, H3
and H4 in section 4.4). The KHB effect decomposition indicates the extent to which observed SES differences in digital competencies
are related to the different learning environments (for an example how this method is applied to a similar research question see
Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023).1 By comparing the results across years, we can determine which learning environments are gaining
or losing importance in the development of digital inequality.
4. Results
4.1. How have social differences in digital competencies changed over time?
The first question addressed in this study—how social differences in digital skills develop—can be answered by the descriptive
findings presented in Fig. 3. Fig. 3 demonstrates the average levels of digital competencies across various SES groups. Throughout the
observation period, students from privileged families (4th quartile of the SES distribution) consistently exhibit higher digital competencies than those from less privileged families (1st quartile), with the gap widening over time. Although the average level of digital
competencies has decreased across all SES groups, the social differences in digital competencies significantly increased, rising from 43
competence points in the year 2013 to 66 competence points in the year 2023 (p < 0.001). The gap widens particularly between 2018
and 2023, which may be related to the changes in (digital) learning conditions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. This trend is
primarily attributable to the sharp decline in digital competencies among students from low-SES families. When examining the differences in terms of standard deviations of the ISEI metric rather than SES quartiles, social differences in digital competencies also
increase significantly—from 15.9 competence points in 2013 to 25.0 points in 2023 (see results of linear regression in Table A1a–A1c
in the appendix). These descriptive findings support the theoretical expectations outlined in Hypothesis 1.
4.2. Do learning environments increasingly differ according to social background?

4.3. Are learning environments becoming more important for the acquisition of digital competencies?
The third step of the analysis poses the question of whether the significance of the various learning environments for the acquisition
of digital competencies has changed over time. To this end, OLS regressions were calculated and the correlations between learning
environments and the acquisition of digital competencies were compared between years. If a learning environment becomes more
important for the acquisition of digital competencies, this should be reflected in a stronger correlation (regression coefficient). For
clarity and focus, we only consider the coefficients for 2013 and 2023 in Fig. 4 (the coefficients of the year 2018 can be found in Fig. A1
in the appendix). If the confidence intervals intersect the red zero line on the left-hand side, the correlations are not statistically
significant. These intervals can also be used to assess whether a change in the correlation between 2013 and 2023 is statistically
meaningful or attributable to random variation. Significant differences can be identified by the extent to which the confidence intervals overlap. In addition, a chi2 test (χ2) with Bonferroni-adjusted p-values was calculated to determine whether any change in a
regression coefficient between 2013 and 2023 is statistically significant—such changes are marked with the corresponding number of
stars in Fig. 4.
Firstly, digital, cultural, and school environments were already linked to students’ digital competencies in 2013. This is evident
from Fig. 4, as most of the confidence intervals in 2013 (blue lines) do not intersect the zero line.
With regard to the digital home environment, the correlation between the digital resources and the acquisition of digital competencies remains low over time. Compared to the other factors, the coefficients of the digital conditions are closer to the zero line. In
addition, the overlapping confidence intervals indicate that this relationship has not increased over time significantly in most cases.
Only computer equipment has become more important for acquiring digital skills between 2013 and 2023 (χ2 = 4.8; p < 0.05). Overall,
however, the digital home environment is probably not the main driver for the increase in digital inequality: neither in terms of the
distribution (Table 2) nor with regard to the changing correlation (Fig. 4) do we find strong evidence in favour of Hypothesis 2.

the acquisition of digital competencies is much more pronounced in 2013 (blue dots) and has even strengthened over time (red dots),

4.4. Why have social differences in digital competencies increased?
Finally, the question remains as to what extent the changed distributions and correlations described in the previous sections (4.2
and 4.3) are related to the evolving social inequalities in digital competencies (section 4.1). Fig. 5 illustrates the findings of the KHB
effect decomposition for the years 2013, 2018, and 2023. The graph on the left-hand side depicts the absolute contribution of the
various variable sets (learning environments) to the SES gap in digital competencies (in competence points), while the graph on the
right-hand side displays the relative explanatory share of these variables (in percent). Beginning with the graph on the left-hand side,
the progressive increase in bar length (i.e. the total SES effect) demonstrates the widening SES gap in digital competencies (15.9
competence points in 2013; 25.0 competence points in 2023 per standard deviation of the ISEI variable), supporting our Hypothesis
H1.
The objective of the decomposition of competence differences over time (2013, 2018, 2023) is to reveal the key drivers for the rise
in social inequality. To evaluate the changing mechanisms, we examine whether the explanatory power of the digital environment
(H2), the cultural environment (H3) or the school environment (H4) has increased over time. This should be reflected in an increasing
proportion of these variables explaining the social differences in digital competencies over time. The colored areas in Fig. 5 illustrate
the extent to which the SES gap is attributable to each explanatory dimension. Graph A on the left-hand side depicts the absolute
contribution of these variables in competence points, while graph B on the right-hand side displays the relative contribution (in
percent).
However, the results do not yield a definitive conclusion; the interpretation varies depending on whether one focuses on absolute or
relative contributions. However, the findings indicate which explanations might be relevant and which are not. Most notably,
consistent with the previous analysis steps, there is no evidence to suggest that the digital home learning environment has played a
significant role in exacerbating social inequalities in digital competencies. Its explanatory contribution remains both small and stable
over time (1 competence point (absolute contribution); around 5-6 per cent (relative contribution); yellow bars). We therefore do not
find empirical support for Hypothesis 2 (rejection).
The increasing SES gap in students' digital competencies appears to be (at least slightly) related to a changing relationship between
cultural resources at home and the acquisition of digital competencies. In 2013, the cultural home environment accounted for 4.4
competence points of the SES-gap in students’ digital competencies, which increased to 5.5 points in 2023 (blue bars, graph A in Fig. 5).
This slight gain in absolute terms (+1.1 competence points) somewhat supports the considerations of Hypothesis 3. The different
cultural conditions of SES groups are therefore reflected in digital inequalities, and the increasing digital inequalities are linked to a
certain extent to the growing importance of cultural learning conditions for educational attainment. However, if we consider the
relative contribution (graph B in Fig. 5), the relative share of the cultural home environment as an explanatory factor has remained
more or less stable over time (around 26 percent).
Similarly, the increase of social disparities in digital competencies appears to be related to the differences in the school environment
between high- and low-SES students when we examine the absolute differences (graph A). The explanatory contribution of the
different school learning environments increases from 9.9 competence points in 2013 to 12.4 competence points in 2023 (grey bars,
graph A). Compared to the explanatory contribution of the other variables, this increase (+2.5 competence points) is quite substantial
and could be interpreted as supporting Hypothesis 4. This shows once again that the choice of educational track is central to the
acquisition of skills and, with the increasing importance of digital processes in schools, also explains part of the growing digital inequalities. However, regarding the relative contribution, the relative share of the school environment as an explanatory factor remains
rather stable or even slightly decreases over time (60 to 58 percent, graph B).
The increase in social differences in digital competencies is clearly related to the cultural conditions of the students at home and
their differing educational pathways. However, the explanatory contributions of both the cultural and school learning environment
have grown in proportion to the widening SES gap in digital competencies. This is why we refer to these variables as becoming
increasingly significant in relation to the rise in inequality in absolute terms, while they continue to exhibit stable explanatory patterns
in relative terms. Moreover, and perhaps more crucially, we must acknowledge that the increase in inequalities cannot be fully
explained by the variables considered. Over time, we have observed an increasing unexplained proportion of social differences in
digital competencies (white bars, graph A): while the SES gap in digital competencies could be fully explained by our variables in 2013,

5. Discussion
The aim of this study was to investigate whether social inequalities in digital competencies are increasing or decreasing, and to
identify the reasons for the trends observed. While previous studies by Casamassima et al. (2024) or Senkbeil et al. (2019) found only
minimal change over time based on cultural conditions, our analysis demonstrates a significant increase in socioeconomic disparities in
digital competencies between 2013 and 2023. We therefore answer our first research question, ‘has social inequality in students' digital
competencies in Germany changed between 2013 and 2023?’, with ‘yes, it has increased’ (support of hypothesis 1).
Our findings refer to Germany, a country with a highly stratified and socially selective education system, whose level of digitisation
was still less pronounced at the beginning of the 2010s. However, given the comparatively high level of educational inequality in many
European countries (Kennedy et al., 2024) and the growing importance of digital processes worldwide, it would be reasonable to
expect similar trends of growing digital inequality in other European countries as well — in particular in countries with an early
tracking education system similar to that in Germany. This assumption is based on our finding of the high and increased relevance of
the school type in our results and requires further research in other countries.
When analysing the second research question on the ‘underlying mechanisms of this change,’ we considered various explanatory
factors simultaneously and employed separate decomposition analyses to examine how the explanatory contributions evolved over
time. In contrast to earlier studies, we conducted a more differentiated analysis and examined, first, how the origin-specific composition of the various learning environments has changed over time (distribution effect) and, second, how the relationship between the
learning environment and the acquisition of digital competencies has changed (correlation effect). The results highlight the relevance
of the cultural learning environment and the school type as key drivers of digital inequality. This result is consistent with studies that
highlight the importance of cultural resources for the acquisition of digital skills (Casamassima et al., 2024; Kennedy et al., 2024; Ren
et al., 2022), as well as studies that view differences in educational pathways as a key cause of digital inequality (Lörz et al., 2024;
Passaretta & Gil-Hernández, 2023). Our differentiated analysis shows that although differences in learning environments have
remained relatively stable over time (stable distribution effect), cultural and school learning environments in particular have become
increasingly important for the acquisition of digital competencies (rising correlation). However, as the results of the decomposition
analysis show, this contribution does not provide a conclusive explanation for the full extent of why social inequalities in digital
competencies are growing. Our findings indicate that the increase is not attributable to the digital conditions in the parental home
(rejection of hypothesis 2) but may be related to some extent to differences in the cultural and school environment (some limited
support for hypothesis 3 and hypothesis 4). Nevertheless, these two elements alone do not completely explain the rise in social inequalities, as there is an increasing unexplained variance that cannot be accounted for by the considered variables. This could possibly
be related to processes during the COVID-19 pandemic which we were unable to consider in our study. Competent use of digital
applications has suddenly become very urgent during the pandemic, and it can be assumed that possible reactions to this challenge
were dependent on parental SES (e.g. parents' social capital).
However, the increasing unexplained variance could also stem from several further limitations of this study. Firstly, the measurement of learning environments, particularly the cultural learning environment (measured solely by the number of books at home),
is rather simple. In general, many large-scale surveys do not measure cultural conditions in a differentiated and in-depth manner, so
that the significance of cultural conditions is likely to be underestimated. Furthermore, the number of books is becoming increasingly
less representative of the cultural conditions within the household in a digital age. Secondly, the measurement of digital infrastructure
also lacks a qualitative dimension. This could become particularly relevant if access to digital devices becomes universal and other
aspects such as the use of digital media and the provision of a stimulating digital learning environment become more important.
Thirdly, the consideration of the school environment is limited to the school type, overlooking the actual practices and resources
within schools. Fourthly, the operationalisation of the various theoretical considerations has already shown that their comparability
over time was not always perfectly guaranteed. In future surveys and large-scale assessments, care must be taken to ensure that such
instruments are collected in a comparable manner.
These limitations underscore the need for further research in this field and a better operationalisation of the various explanatory
concepts. Future studies should focus on refining the measurement of the cultural learning environment, critically assessing the
relevance of ‘books at home’ as a proxy in the digital era, especially given its persistent relevance to the SES gap despite the declining
number of books in households. Beyond improving the measurement of the traditional understanding of cultural capital, research will
probably also have to find new indicators to better understand cultural capital in a digital world. In addition, it may be worthwhile to
consider the quality and variety of digital devices in future studies. In this context, it will be important to consider the extent and
quality of digital support available in parents' homes. Furthermore, the substantial effect of school type warrants deeper investigation
to understand the underlying mechanisms. While existing research on ICT resources in schools shows limited evidence of their direct
relevance, exploring compositional effects within schools with multilevel modelling may be a promising avenue. Finally, research
should aim to identify additional relevant factors contributing to the increasing unexplained variance, potentially considering the
impact of events such as the COVID-19 pandemic or other aspects on the system level. For this purpose, it would be advisable to take
various country-specific characteristics into account in further international comparative studies and to examine the conditions under
which digital inequalities can be reduced. Identifying such conditions and possible interactions can also contribute to a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of the emergence and change of digital inequalities, which should also be addressed more
intensively in future research.

Despite these limitations and the need for further research, our findings offer valuable insights. The observed rise in social
inequality in digital competencies aligns with the Effective Maintained Inequality Theory. With the increasing importance of digital
competencies for educational success and the labour market, digital inequalities based on social background are growing. It therefore
appears that a new field of social distinction is emerging in the realm of digitality. However, this increasing inequality is not attributed
to the digital learning environment, suggesting it is more a consequence of ‘classic’ inequalities. In particular, the choice of educational
pathway is closely linked to the acquisition of digital competencies and social inequalities. It would therefore be advisable either to
reduce social differences in the choice of educational pathways or to improve the conditions for acquiring digital competencies in the
lower tracks, as these tracks are attended in particular by children from low SES families. It is also crucial to consider the overall
decline in average digital competence during the study period. This implies that the increasing inequality is less about certain groups
improving more, but rather about which groups experienced less loss in digital competencies, an observation mirrored in other academic performance areas (e.g., PISA) and significantly exacerbated by COVID-19, although it was already present beforehand.
Therefore, future research might benefit from focusing on the Compensatory Advantage Effect (Bernardi, 2014), investigating which
groups are better able to mitigate unfavorable developments. In this context, the results of our study show that cultural learning
conditions in the parental home are a key factor influencing the acquisition of digital competencies, which means that schools will have
to do much more in future to compensate for the less favourable learning conditions of children from low SES families. Given the
growing importance of digital competencies in various areas of daily life, this trend towards increasing digital inequality should be
viewed critically, as it can be seen as an increasingly relevant aspect of social exclusion.
