Does socioeconomic sorting of teacher qualifications exacerbate
mathematics achievement inequity? Panel data estimates from 20 years
of TIMSS
Leah Natasha Glassow *, 1, Kajsa Yang Hansen 2, Jan-Eric Gustafsson 3
Department of Education and Special Education, University of Gothenburg, Gothenburg, Sweden

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
TIMSS
Teacher sorting
SES achievement gap
Country fixed effects

Recent and older studies have reported either a persistence or a widening of the socioeconomic achievement
gap—the difference in performance between students in top and bottom socioeconomic groups. Using a panel
data technique with country fixed effects for 32 education systems and six waves of data from the Trends in
International Mathematics and Science Study, we examine whether the sorting of teachers by specialization level
in mathematics education and novice status across students of different socioeconomic backgrounds exacerbates
mathematics achievement inequity despite the presence of a time-varying control for socioeconomic school
segregation. We find modest evidence that sorting by mathematics education is associated with achievement
inequity, but no evidence supporting the importance of sorting based on teacher experience. Socioeconomic
school segregation, on the other hand, clearly and persistently exacerbates achievement inequity. The results
have policy implications regarding the effective distribution of educational resources.
Availability of data and materials: The data are freely available via the TIMSS data repository.

Educational inequity—the tendency for students of higher socioeconomic status (SES) to academically outperform their more disadvantaged peers—has been the focus of policy debate for over half a
century (Coleman et al., 1966; Betts & Roemer, 2005; Organization for
Economic Cooperation and Development, OECD, 2012; Kyriakides et al.,
2020). The proliferation of international large-scale assessments (ILSAs)
such as the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study
(TIMSS) has allowed this phenomenon to be evidenced in virtually every
country sampled, albeit to varying degrees (van de Werfhorst & Mijs,
2010; OECD, 2019; Strello et al., 2021). Despite decades of interventions, policymakers have struggled to close the SES achievement
gap, and recent work demonstrates that the segmentation between the
rich and the poor is growing across the world (Marcinczak et al., 2016;
Chmielewski, 2019). Increasingly, both national and international
publications have set forth differential access to teacher quality as an
underexplored determinant of inequitable student outcomes (OECD,

Isolating the effects of teachers continues to be a challenge in
educational effectiveness research (Hattie, 2003; Wayne & Youngs,
2003; Rivkin et al., 2005; Goe, 2007; Nilsen & Gustafsson, 2016; Burroughs et al., 2019). As a result, there is still disagreement regarding
what specifically makes an effective teacher (Rockoff et al., 2011). The
importance of assessing individual teacher characteristics such as their
formal qualifications has been rejected altogether by some, in favor of
value-added models (Hanushek & Rivkin, 2012; Goldhaber et al., 2015;
Jackson et al., 2014; Isenberg et al., 2021). Other approaches include
path analytic techniques (Nilsen & Gustafsson, 2016) focusing on the
association between student performance and formal qualifications,
characteristics such as self-efficacy and preparedness, and teaching
processes such as classroom management and cognitive activation.
Overall, evidence supporting the role of qualifications has been mixed
(Goe, 2007; Hanushek & Rivkin, 2012; Burroughs et al., 2019). The
findings, however, may be compromised by the differential allocation of
teachers to students based on ability and background as well as unobserved heterogeneity in the study participants (Bitler et al., 2014; Sancassani, 2021). In many cases, estimates of teacher effectiveness are
difficult to generalize across countries (Blömeke and Delaney, 2012).
Taking a cross-national approach to the investigation of educational
performance and inequity has allowed researchers to tackle old questions using novel methods (Hanushek & Woessmann, 2006; Hanushek &
Woessmann, 2011; Strietholt et al., 2014; Gustafsson, 2013; Cordero
et al., 2017; Teltemann & Schunk, 2019; Strello et al., 2021; Sancassani,
2021). This is due in large part to the fact that ILSAs have a
cross-sectional design at the student-level, but a longitudinal design at
the country-level (Gustafsson, 2006; 2013). We take advantage of this
feature and construct an unbalanced panel dataset from six waves of
TIMSS data from 1999 to 2019. The aim is to investigate the impact of
socioeconomic teacher sorting on educational inequity or the association between student SES and student mathematics achievement (Kyriakides et al., 2020). Rather than measuring the association between
teacher characteristics and student outcomes at the classroom or teacher
level, we consider how educational systems allocate teacher qualifications across students of varying socioeconomic backgrounds. To control
for unobserved heterogeneity at the institutional level, we include
country and year fixed effects. We also include several time-varying
controls related to the overall proportion of students with novice or
unspecialized teachers and social segregation across schools. Focusing
on system-level features allows for the comparison of educational features which often do not vary sufficiently within countries and accounts
for selection effects at lower levels, such as if higher-achieving students
are seeking out schools in which they know the teachers are more
qualified (Hanushek & Woessmann, 2014). Additionally, restricting the
variation in the explanatory variable to within-countries over time
controls for time-invariant sources of bias at the country-level, such as
cultural attitudes towards education (Hanushek and Woessman, 2014).
Recent work has criticized the exaggeration of substantive effect sizes in
fixed effects panel regressions (Mummolo & Peterson, 2018). The effects
of such designs are often overestimated due to the reduction in variation
when the analysis is restricted to within subjects (in this case, countries
or education systems). We therefore compute the average within-unit
treatment effect based on the residualized standard deviation of the
explanatory variables after considering the country and year fixed effects. Our analysis therefore not only contributes a novel approach to the
study of teacher sorting, but also provides a more substantively valid
estimate for this and the social segregation control.

1.1. Teacher qualifications as a proxy for teacher competence
There are several reasons for narrowing our analysis to the sorting of
teachers based on their qualifications. Many studies looking at the role
of teacher quality fail to account for the relationship between the
teaching context and the teaching process and that teaching in some
contexts is more challenging than others (Opdenakker & Van Damme,
2006; Jackson, 2009; Thrupp, 1999; Lazear, 2001). This source of
endogeneity presents a barrier to isolating the allocation of teachers
based on student socioeconomic background alone. Teachers may enter
the classroom with high levels of competency but teaching practices may
be constrained by the classroom ability or socioeconomic composition
(Lazear, 2001). Because this study is primarily concerned with whether
or not students are taught by teachers with lower qualifications and
competency levels as a result of their socioeconomic background, we
disregard characteristics of teacher competence which are amenable to
the classroom environment, including self-efficacy and instructional
practices, although some of these characteristics are clearly important
(see for example Baumert et al., 2010; Jackson, 2018).
Revisiting the relationship between teacher qualifications and student outcomes has relevance for school leaders, as hiring and allocation
decisions are in large part based on formal qualifications (Engel and
Finch, 2014). Due to this and the aforementioned empirical challenges,
we limit the focus of this study to the allocation of teachers based on
their experience and educational training in mathematics across socioeconomic student groups within education systems.4 Although a select
few recent studies call into question the experience variable altogether
(Isenberg et al., 2021; Sancassani, 2021), it is assumed that teachers are
subject to a ‘learning-by-doing’ effect, where teaching competence improves dramatically during the first few years on the job (Berliner,
2004). This has been shown empirically repeatedly across a variety of
studies (Rice, 2003; Rockoff, 2004; Rivkin et al., 2005; Goe, 2007;
Croninger et al., 2007), although some argue that the returns to
achievement occur further into a teacher’s career (Papay & Kraft, 2013).
Additionally, a larger share of experienced teachers not only contributes
to student performance, but also likely to a school’s academic climate
(OECD, 2018c).
It is also assumed that teachers with no educational training in
mathematics will have lower teaching quality. In 1986, Schulman
established pedagogical content knowledge and content knowledge as
distinguishing characteristics of an effective teacher, and a host of
empirical studies have since confirmed this theory (Wayne & Youngs,
2003; Hill et al., 2005; Baumert et al., 2010; Burroughs et al., 2019;
Sancassani, 2021). The theory posits that high quality instruction,
especially in mathematics, can be attributed to the level of subject
matter knowledge in general as well as pedagogical expression of this
knowledge. However, subject matter knowledge is also not without
mixed evidence. In a large review of empirical literature, Darling-Hammond and Youngs (2002) note that while subject matter knowledge
is indeed strongly related to student test scores, student-teacher experience and pedagogical coursework are at least as important. Others
have also failed to replicate this connection (Rockoff et al., 2011).
Nevertheless, a host of empirical work has confirmed the importance of
content knowledge (along other cognitive credentials), and we therefore
assume its relevance for instructional quality and student test scores.

educational inequity

many. Higher-income students are afforded more of the well-known

forms of capital (Bourdieu, 1986)—economic, social, and cultural—through investments and stimuli provided by their parents (Haveman &
Wolfe, 1995; Sirin, 2005; Bukodi & Goldethorpe, 2013). In many
countries, however, the same forms of capital afford higher-SES students
access to better schools, in better neighborhoods, with better learning
opportunities (OECD, 2012; OECD, 2019; European Commission, 2020).
Rather than ‘the great equalizer,’ schooling may end up compounding
the effects of social origin on lifetime outcomes through the inequitable
allocation of educational resources (Bourdieu, 1977; Downey & Condron, 2016; Kratz et al., 2022).
One frequently hypothesized mechanism through which this occurs
is the socioeconomic sorting of teacher qualifications, training, and
competency, where low-SES students are exposed to teachers of lower
average quality (Akiba et al., 2007; Jackson, 2009; Luschei & Jeong,
2019; OECD, 2018; Glassow & Jerrim, 2022). This has been evidenced
even in the most egalitarian societies in the world, such as Sweden and
Norway (Hansson & Gustafsson, 2016; Bonesrønning et al., 2005), but
has been found in many countries (Allen et al., 2017; Sims & Allen, 2018
for England; Lankford et al., 2002; Adamson & Darling-Hammond,
2012; Goldhaber et al., 2015; 2018; Clotfelter et al., 2005; Jackson,
2009; Rice, 2003; for USA; Meckes & Bascope, 2012 for Chile; Özoğlu,
2015 for Turkey, Luschei et al., 2013 for Mexico). This has generally
been attributed to the most competent teachers self-selecting into preferable school and classrooms, labor market conditions, and opportunistic teacher allocation on the part of school leaders (Bacolod, 2007;
Jackson, 2009; Engel et al., 2014; Vagi & Pivovarova, 2017). Teachers
new to the profession may also be motivated to teach in lower-SES
contexts and are more likely to find openings in such settings (Sims &
Allen, 2018). Schools serving low-income students often have higher
teacher turnover rates and trouble with recruitment (Vagi and Pivarova,
2017; Craig, 2017), which accrues additional costs and administrative
burdens, as well as impedes academic and behavioral outcomes. In
educational systems with more heterogenous school compositions, the
sorting may happen across classrooms within schools (Luschei & Jeong,
2019; Hanushek & Rivkin, 2012). Teachers with better credentials may
leverage their positions to secure higher ‘ability’ classrooms and school
leaders, in turn, may assign better teachers to such classrooms to
maximize grades and therefore the market position of a school (Grissom
et al., 2015; Kalogrides et al., 2013). In educational systems with
centralized allocation, the mechanisms are less straightforward, but
sorting inequities have been documented here too and may be due to
growing inequality in society more generally (Luschei & Jeong, 2019).
The primary way to measure teacher sorting is to take the difference in
exposure rates (at district or national levels) to various teacher characteristics (most commonly qualifications or teacher value-added scores)
between socioeconomically diverse groups of students (Goldhaber et al.,
2018; Sims & Allen, 2018).
Only a handful of studies have investigated inequity in teacher
allocation and student inequity from a cross-national perspective.
Perhaps the most well-known is a study by Akiba et al. (2007), who used
TIMSS 2003 grade 8 data from 46 countries and OLS regressions and
found that teacher qualification ‘opportunity gaps’ were largely unrelated to gaps in student achievement, save for educational training in
mathematics. Using PISA 2009 data from 65 countries, Chiu (2015)
finds that in schools with teachers with a higher share of university
degrees, SES-gaps were larger, arguing that students with more cultural
capital benefit disproportionately from teachers with more university
training. Last, Luschei and Chudgar (2011) use TIMSS 2003 data from 25
countries and find no evidence that teacher qualification distribution
matters with regards to student achievement or equity. Each of the
aforementioned studies look only at cross-sectional data and are therefore limited in their capacities to make conclusions about the nature and
direction of this relationship. As such, the relationship between socioeconomic teacher sorting and achievement inequity is still largely
unclear.

1.3. Distinguishing teacher and peer effects
The effect of the unequal allocation of teacher quality on the SESachievement gap should be differentiated from effects related to other
forms of unequal educational inputs which may be correlated with the
allocation of teacher competence. It is now well-evidenced that students
in schools or classrooms with a higher share of socioeconomically
disadvantaged students underperform (Cumberworth & Cumberworth,
2018; Nilsen et al., 2018; European Commission, 2020; Sacerdote,
2011). These ‘compositional’ or ‘peer’ effects dictate that a student’s
academic profile will be related to the socioeconomic profile of their
school or classroom via several mechanisms, including teacher and
student educational expectations, homework, course difficulty level,
and students’ feelings about safety (Rumberger & Palardy, 2005). Often
touted by school leaders as based on ability levels, cross-school and
cross-classroom segregation may be becoming more prevalent due to
rising income inequality, growing inequality in parental investment, as
well as changing demographics and migration patterns (Baker et al.,
2002; Andon et al., 2014; Marcinczak et al., 2016; Chmielewski, 2019;
Gutiérrez et al., 2019). It may also result as a consequence of selective (i.
e., tracked) education systems, ability grouping, or other reasons, such
as neighborhood segregation or increases in school choice and school
marketization (Burger, 2019; Strello et al., 2021; Strietholt et al., 2019).
Because policies which change the composition of students are also
likely to change the composition of teachers (Jackson, 2009), controlling for socioeconomic student segregation between schools should
capture unobserved policy changes which are correlated with teacher
sorting.
Distinguishing conceptually peer effects from effects related to
teacher quality is more challenging (Sacerdote, 2011). For instance, if a
classroom or school has one or more disruptive or low-performing students which impede teachers’ attention and ability to provide
high-quality instruction, this may be considered both a peer and a
teacher effect. Indeed, schools or classrooms with more disruptive students or students with a negative attitude towards education will modify
the teacher’s ability to provide effective instruction (Lazear, 2001).
However, we posit that more effective teachers will be better equipped
to handle this challenge. As peer effects are now well-established, the
question remains how large are remaining teacher effects (Sacerdote,
2011). With these considerations in mind, the present study aims to
answer the following research questions:
1) Do socioeconomic gaps in the sorting of teacher qualifications
significantly predict inequity in student performance in
mathematics?
2) Does the presence of a time-varying social segregation control alter
the above results?

2.1.2. Variables at the individual- and school-levels
The main student-level variable is student socioeconomic status. We
create a composite socioeconomic measure (a factor score) for each
country-year comprised of student reported number of books in the
home, mother’s level of education and father’s level of education.10 The
number of books in the home is an ordered categorical variable from (1)
0–10, (2) 11–25, (3) 26–100, (4) 101–200, (5) more than 200 books.
Although number of books in the home is a widely used measure of
cultural capital, it has come under scrutiny in recent work (Engzell,
2019). Despite this, student-level associations between books in the
home and other associations of SES (parental occupation and income)
are growing stronger over time (Chmielewski, 2019). Parental education
is an ordered categorical variable, with (1) some primary or lower secondary, (2) lower secondary, (3) upper secondary, (4) post-secondary,
non-tertiary, (5) short-cycle tertiary, (6) bachelor’s or equivalent, (7)
postgraduate degree.11 There are also some concerns regarding student
reports of parental education, though in grade 8 the reports are more
accurate (Jerrim & Micklewright, 2014). To estimate the socioeconomic
status scores for each student and for each imputed dataset (country-by-year) we conduct a factor analysis with polychoric correlations to
estimate factor loadings and extract the single factor scores. This has
been done using the function ‘fa’ in the RStudio ‘psych’ package. The
factor scores are estimated within each country-by-year unit of analysis
because we conceptualize socioeconomic status as a positional rather
than absolute good with relative rather than absolute significance.
At the individual student level, the analysis controls for gender,
foreign born status, language spoken at home and age. This information
comes from the student questionnaires and is present in each TIMSS
cycle from 1999 to 2019. At the school level, we take information from
the questionnaires filled out by school principals. The analysis controls
for remote or rural school location (with 1 indicating that the school is in
an area with fewer than 15,000 people). We also control for whether the

To determine the system-level teacher sorting measures for each
imputed dataset (each country-year), we estimate the proportion of
students in high- and low-socioeconomic contexts with novice- and outof-subject teachers in each year and each country. We apply the mathematics teacher weights provided by TIMSS.13 We use the same
approach as Goldhaber et al. (2018), calculating the difference in
exposure rates to teachers of lower average quality for students in the
top and bottom SES categories, resulting in an estimate between − 1 and
1. A qualification gap of − 1 would signify a perfectly equitable distribution of teachers (where no low-SES students have teachers with lower
qualification levels), whereas a qualification gap of 1 would signify a
perfectly inequitable distribution of teachers. A qualification gap of
0 implies no difference between exposure rates. For a visual display of

2.1.3. System-level variables
2.1.3.1. Teacher qualification gaps. To capture teacher sorting (the
‘treatment’ and main independent variable), we compute the teacher
qualification gaps through a series of steps. We create the category of
having a ‘novice teacher’ at the student level based on the years a
teacher has been teaching altogether from the teacher questionnaires in
each cycle. Teachers with 5 or less years of experience are coded as
‘novice’ in line with past research12 (Rice, 2003; Rockoff, 2004; Rivkin
et al., 2005; Goe, 2007; Papay & Kraft, 2013). The category of ‘out-of-subject’ teacher is created based on mathematics education-related
information collected from the teacher questionnaires in each cycle.
Teachers reported on which subject they studied in their post-secondary
educations, such as ‘mathematics’ or ‘mathematics education’, as well as
other subject areas such as science or history. Teachers citing ‘math’ or
‘mathematics education’ or both may have some level of formal
specialization in mathematics. We therefore designate a teacher as an
‘out-of-subject’ mathematics teacher if they choose neither. Next, we
group students into top and bottom thirds (33rd and 66th percentiles) on
the SES spectrum, within each country-year on this scale to determine
their relative SES position, applying the TIMSS student weights. This
allows us to ‘compare students at the top and bottom relative position
within a socioeconomic distribution, even as the absolute meanings of
these positions change’ (Chmielewski, 2019, p. 525). We use thirds
instead of quartiles or deciles to ensure maximum statistical power and
minimum sampling error for each country-year.

2.1.3.2. Time-varying controls. We control for the exposure rates to
novice or out-of-subject mathematics teachers for all students, regardless of socioeconomic status. These values are not included in the
imputation procedure and are used as a proxy for the general level of
teacher quality in an education system. When computing this measure,
the TIMSS mathematics teacher weights are also applied.
We also include a time-varying control for socioeconomic school
segregation. Country-by-year estimates can be found in Table B in the
Appendix. We compute the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) of
student SES, using a multi-level modelling approach well-established in
previous work (i.e. Burger, 2019; Goldstein & Noden, 2003; Raudenbush
& Bryk, 2002). The ICC denotes the proportion of variance at the student
level which can be attributed to the clustering of schools (or the ratio of
school-level SES variance to total SES variance in a country). Values
closer to 1 indicate that students are more similar within-schools than
between. The ICC model is as follows:

4. Discussion
Unequal access to the most qualified and competent teachers is
increasingly cited as one of the key determinants of achievement inequity by international authorities such as the OECD and educational researchers alike (OECD, 2018; Strietholt et al., 2019). While there has
been a plethora of national studies (Clotfelter et al., 2005; 2007; 2008;
Allen et al., 2017; Goldhaber et al., 2018; Jackson, 2009; Sims & Allen,
2018), very few cross-national studies have attempted to address this
question. This study set out to address this gap in the literature by taking
advantage of the country-level longitudinal nature of international
large-scale assessment data using a panel data approach with country
and year fixed effects. By taking advantage of the fact that teacher
sorting is an inherently system-level feature, we investigated empirically
the oft cited claim that the socioeconomic sorting of teacher qualifications exacerbates mathematics achievement inequity.
4.1. Teacher sorting based on educational specialization in mathematics
exacerbates mathematics achievement inequity
Based on the array of literature documenting the importance of
teachers, we would expect the relationship between SES and achievement to be stronger as a result of pronounced socioeconomic teacher
sorting as it would mean less equal educational opportunities for lowerSES students and further advantages to more affluent students over and
above their family backgrounds. We find a consistently null pattern of
results regarding sorting by experience. In both models (before and after
controlling for social segregation), novice teacher sorting has no effect
on the dependent variable. This was true regardless of how we defined a
‘novice’ teacher (by 1, 3 or 5 years) and regardless of how we grouped
the students socioeconomically in the teacher qualification gaps (by
thirds or quartiles). It was also unchanged when we investigated relative
student achievement and subset the data by SES. This finding overall is
contrary to the long-standing view that experience matters for student
achievement during the first few years on the job (see Rice, 2003;
Rockoff, 2004; Goe, 2007; Rivkin et al., 2005). However, other
cross-national evidence also finds no relationship between academic
outcomes and any level of teacher experience using fixed effects approaches controlling for student ability (Sancassani, 2021). More recent
studies from the US are also beginning to call into question this
long-standing finding (Isenberg et al., 2021).
We also find that a teacher labor force characterized by a higher
share of unspecialized mathematics teachers is more detrimental to
student outcomes than one with recently qualified teachers. This is not
so surprising, as the novice teacher designation says little about teaching
practices, whereas an ‘out-of-subject’ designation necessarily requires a
lack of content knowledge in mathematics. However, this interpretation
requires a degree of caution. It may be the case that teacher experience
matters in a few countries but not others (Blömeke and Delaney, 2012).
It is also perhaps the case that unspecialized teachers lack other
important qualifications such as pedagogical certification. In addition,
school systems with high degrees of unspecialized teachers point to a

4.2. The effect of social segregation is larger than teacher sorting
The effect of teacher sorting should be interpreted relative to the
effect of socioeconomic school segregation. The analysis finds consistent
evidence that social segregation is more detrimental for mathematics
achievement inequity than sorting across both dimensions. This is in line
with other research on social segregation and student outcomes
(Hanushek & Woessmann, 2006; Burger, 2019; Strello et al., 2021;
Teltemann & Schunk, 2019; Sacerdote, 2011). Education systems have
thus far largely failed to adequately address social segregation (Gutiérrez et al., 2019), and our analysis provides yet more evidence that it is
harming the most vulnerable students. When the results are broken
down by SES subgroup, there is again clear evidence that increases in
social segregation result in lower test scores for students in the bottom
SES group and higher test scores for students in the top SES group. These
findings concern most of the education systems in our sample, as many
of them display upwards trends in socioeconomic school segregation.
As mentioned, the potential sources of social segregation in our
sample are multiple. Primarily, however, they may be due to more selective educational systems or residential segregation or school choice
decisions (Burger, 2019). Because we use educational systems as their
own controls and only consider the within-unit variation, the social
segregation measure is robust to cross-country degrees of randomness in
the distribution of students. It is also immune to time-invariant academic entry requirements of certain schools. In line with other studies,
our findings suggest that socioeconomic school segregation amplifies
inequitable educational outcomes (Burger, 2019; Strello et al., 2021;
Strietholt et al., 2019). The average within-unit effect for high-SES
students was about 3.5% of a standard deviation and about − 1.4% of
a standard deviation for low-SES students. As the model controls for a

and ability grouping, as well as the unequal distribution of teacher
competence, the socioeconomic school segregation measure likely captures peer effects to a large degree.

4.4. Conclusions
While access to teacher competence and the sorting of teachers has
been of interest in national studies for some time, such studies tell us
little about the prevalence and impact of this phenomenon across
educational contexts. This study provides the first international evidence with panel data on the topic of the sorting of teacher qualifications (primarily mathematics educational specialization) as a
determinant of achievement inequity. However, the results also
demonstrate that socioeconomic teacher sorting exacerbates achievement inequity through primarily increases in the test scores of higherSES students.
Brighouse et al. (2016) provide a framework for decision makers in
educational policy and stress that policymakers operate within boundaries and that strategic decisions within educational governance are
contingent upon this fact. If policymakers could change residential
segregation in the United States, for example, their decisions about
certain policies might change (Brighouse et al., 2016, p. 5). Without the
capacity to reorganize student socioeconomic segregation, the sorting of
teacher qualifications may be one of the few important educational inputs capable of closing the SES achievement gap. However, this may
require taking away the opportunities of higher-SES students. If the goal
is to reduce performance inequity via improvements in the test scores of
low-SES students, focus on the redistribution of teacher qualifications
may not be the most effective path. As the results show, the success of
educational inputs related to teachers are likely to pale in comparison to
the degree of socioeconomic segregation in a school system. When
possible, policymakers should therefore continue to look beyond
educational inputs to ensure more equitable educational outcomes.

4.3. Limitations
As with all empirical research, it is important to point out the limitations which constrain our findings. First and perhaps most importantly, we only have access to a somewhat narrow dimension of teacher
competence via their qualifications; in this case, limited to the dimensions of mathematics education and experience as proxy variables.
Had we been able to include a dimension of teachers which could
differentiate pedagogical content knowledge and content knowledge
which was comparable across countries, we presume the specialization
sorting effect would have been larger. The present study has investigated
the effect of systems which sort teachers with no relevant education in
mathematics, but future studies could examine whether education in
more proximal (such as physics) or distal subjects is relates to the
findings. TIMSS may improve the usage of teacher qualification measures by including country specific items (as in the case of the country
specific home educational resources) relating to the quality of teacher
educational credentials and training. As noted, it would have been
useful to have consistent data regarding pedagogical certification, but
this has been inconsistent in both wording and presence across the
TIMSS cycles since 1999. We also conceptualize novice teacher sorting
based on 5 years of experience; as noted, subsequent examinations of
different measurements (1 or 3 years of experience) yielded no difference in the country rankings of sorting inequity. Nevertheless, our estimates and conclusions may vary from nationally focused findings
which use other measures of teacher competence or only focus on one
region within a country (as is common in US studies, for example). Our
findings do not rule out the importance of teacher qualifications altogether as they are likely to matter when combined, as evidenced by
Rockoff et al. (2011). They do however, point out the fact that socioeconomic sorting varies cross-nationally depending on the qualification
and that the underlying mechanisms are likely heterogeneous.
TIMSS data are also not teacher representative and therefore inferences can only be made at the student-level. We limit the analyses to
differences between socioeconomic tertiles as opposed to more extreme
ends of the socioeconomic spectrum, such as deciles. This is largely due
to the risk of sampling error in international large-scale assessment data,
which is also important to stress as a limitation (see for example Anders
et al., 2021; Micklewright et al., 2012). Other patterns of teacher sorting, such as urban-rural divides have also been put aside in this paper
but may certainly be the foci of future research. A final and equally
important limitation relates to the specifications of the panel models: the
assumption of time-invariant confounders, as well as the number of
countries included. While we have attempted to control for such confounders via the social segregation measure, we cannot rule out every
possible confounder. We also cannot examine in which countries the
effects are strongest, and this effect heterogeneity may be explored in
future research. While computing the average within-unit effect size
using the within-unit standard deviation gives some additional substantive context to the findings, this method does not correct for uncertainty in the estimates due to the reduction in variation imposed by
the fixed effects. A final but important limitation of the model relates to
the measurement errors associated with the independent variables. The
categorical classification involved in constructing the teacher sorting
measure necessitate a higher degree of measurement error as compared
to a continuous variable. While each step in our analysis is empirically
and conceptually well-founded, it is important to exercise a degree of
caution with regards to policy implications in light of these limitations.
