ABSTRACT

Assessing achievement is a complex task due to its dependence on multiple factors and the hierarchical structure of educational
data, yet surveys like TIMSS offer valuable insights into its determining factors like students' mathematics anxiety. However,
disregarding the nested structure of data and ignoring the assumptions of models causes poor performance such as inaccurate
predictions and biased estimates. Our research utilises linear mixed models (LMMs) and machine learning (ML) techniques
(e.g., REEM-­tree and GP boosting) especially chosen for their abilities to model nested data and capture non-­linear relationships. This study is a pioneer in the literature as these ML algorithms are implemented for the first time in TIMSS. Accordingly,
mathematical tendency and emotional factors are the two primary predictors of mathematics achievement across all methods,
acknowledging the possibility of potential bias due to reliance on self-­report responses. However, there are variations in the effect size of the students' origins among the methods. This indicates different algorithms yield distinct results according to their
inner processes and priorities, such as revealing statistical significance of predictors or contributing to predictive performance.
Moreover, gender has a negligible impact across all models in our analysis, caused by cultural differences in the sample. Overall,
while LMMs are widely accepted, ML methods remain competitive alternatives in prediction and flexibility. All three methods
yield similar benchmarks, yet ML methods offer slightly better performance in RMSE, MAE, and MAPE while exhibiting high
predictive power and capturing nonlinearity and interaction. Although they take more computation time, parallel processing
mitigates this in larger datasets. Consequently, ML methods and LMMs concurrently provide broader and more precise insights
in terms of predictive and inferential gains.

1   |   Introduction
In the field of mathematics education, achievement is often
measured by the examination scores of students. Although
mathematics scores of students may appear like just a number
at first sight, they reflect several insights into students' competence regarding cognitive abilities, intellectual capacities,
and knowledge of underlying structures. The reason behind
mathematics playing a pivotal role in cognitive development

and academic achievement is that it develops problem-­solving
skills, procedural knowledge, and conceptual understanding
in students. Intrinsically, it can be concluded that students
mostly having high scores are likely to have well-­developed
cognitive skills and be successful in mathematical concepts
under relatively convenient conditions such as optimum
testing environment, fair evaluation metrics, and personal
bias-­free assessment. However, even when the proper conditions are satisfied, analyzing, deducing, and monitoring the

TIMSS evaluates mathematics and science abilities of 4th-­and
8th-­grade students from around the world (Fishbein et al. 2021).
It is conducted by the International Association for the
Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) approximately
every 4 years, starting in 1995, and has been conducted in about

However, the assessment is still being processed. Therefore,

teacher, and school environments, in addition to their achievement scores. TIMSS offers a comparative analysis of students'
mathematics and science abilities, but not all data is provided for
every participating country.
The TIMSS committee implements changes and establishes
guidelines to establish standards for the data collection process
(Martin et al. 2020). This allows for a more consistent sample
and allows for similar subgroups in different countries to be
compared. For example, the mean age of students should be
at least 9.5 at the time of the test for 4th-­grade students in all
countries. Moreover, some countries may have different curricula for 4th grades. In such cases, fifth-­or sixth-­grade students
from countries with non-­standard programmes may participate
in the 4th-­grade test. These countries are separated from other
countries in the comparisons of success, which is also the case
in this study.
One common and traditional strategy to handle large-­scale educational data is Hierarchical Linear Model (HLM). However,
considering the magnitude of large-­scale data sets in education
and their complexities, various alternatives that could be more
effective in terms of statistical performance have been utilized
in this study. For example, machine learning (ML) algorithms
can handle the non-­linearity (Hilbert et al. 2021). Since the
relation between dependent and independent variables in educational data such as TIMSS is likely to have a non-­linear structure, the linearity assumption of HLM can yield poor and biased
predictive performance compared to ML algorithms. Moreover,
the linearity assumption of the traditional models attempts to
establish analytically meaningful ground for the model, but it
decreases the reliability of an analysis due to inconsistency with
data's underlying nature. In addition to that, the classical models
may require selection and combination of predictors to be able to
convert data into a single model because of the multicollinearity

and overfitting issues (Yarkoni and Westfall 2017). However,
ML models can handle this difficulty in a more practical manner (Ozen et al. 2025). Besides the flexibility in application, they
can cope with a moderate correlation between predictors and
the dependent variable (Nguyen et al. 2023).
This study stands out as a pioneer in several dimensions such
as the first application and comparison of Random Effects/EM
tree (REEM-­tree) and Gaussian Process Boosting (GP boosting)
in TIMSS data. It does not only investigate the factors affecting students' achievement scores from three countries from different levels of development in TIMSS 2019 data by utilising a
comprehensive method, but also introduces, for the first time,
the applicability of new ML techniques such as REEM-­tree and
GP boosting to TIMSS data and also considers schools nested
within the country effect as a random term in the models. In
fact, GP boosting was first introduced for nested educational
data, and we obtained non-­negligible results on significance and
effect sizes of the variables. It is crucial to note here that having
country as a random effect allows us to generalise the finding to
any country by including its own characteristics in the model.
Comparisons between the models, strategies, and algorithms are
presented to guide practitioners in the analysis of educational
data. Moreover, the results can show us the most crucial factors
that need improvement in order for students to get better math
scores. Also, the gender effect on achievement is still a black box.
There are many studies criticising whether being a boy or girl
affects math scores (e.g., Thomas 2024; Reynolds et al. 2022).
This study also makes a statistically significant contribution via
a country-­specific view. In addition, governments can take into
account the advice given about immigrant students in this research since the critical results are presented. Consequently, this
study underscores several key points that are relevant to diverse
groups, including policy-­makers, educators, and others.

2   |   Literature Review
The TIMSS dataset has been used to investigate various
factors that contribute to student achievement from different perspectives. In recent years, thanks to the advantages
of ML algorithms, their applications on TIMSS data have
been increasingly adopted in the field of education (Hilbert
et al. 2021). Even though there exists a rising trend in the literature, studies that utilised traditional methods such as exploratory data analysis (EDA) and HLM are still predominant. For
example, the studies of Wiberg (2019), Ramirez (2006), and
Carnoy et al. (2015), which employ traditional methods on
TIMSS data, focus on analysing relationships between different concepts and variables, such as the relationship between
students' TIMSS mathematics scores and their grades or national test scores. In their study, Wiberg (2019) used descriptive
statistics, linear regression, and multilevel analysis to examine the relationship between these variables obtained from
students in Sweden. Similarly, Ramirez (2006) used hierarchical modelling to investigate the mathematical achievement of
Chilean students, and both studies reveal that the educational
status of the family is a significant factor in student success.
The study by Carnoy et al. (2015) uses descriptive statistics to
compare the TIMSS and PISA performances of Russian students concerning different levels of family academic resources

found that logistic regression and SVMs are best suited to classify the successes of students. Moreover, in the study of Wang

in their analysis and implement a single-­level (or fixed-­effect)
RF algorithm to examine the TIMSS data. Although the results
appear consistent with the literature, ignoring the nature of the
hierarchical structure may restrict the reliability of the findings
and prevent the revelation of hidden aspects of the data.

It is well-­k nown that hierarchical data sets like TIMSS or
PISA include information depending on their levels. While
student-­dependent factors provide individual characteristics
of students, school-­dependent factors give information on the
resources and structural aspects of schools. Therefore, the personal elements of students are expected to be different, but the
characteristics depending on facilities and resources must be
the same for all the students within the same school. Similarly,
students in the same country reflect common items regarding
the country since the levels of development, economic status,
and educational policies are identical. Without incorporating
these levels of information, all observations are treated as independent, which causes a loss of information. Additionally,
it leads to biased estimators, and consequently, biased conclusions (Moerbeek 2004). On the other hand, it causes under-­or
overestimated standard errors and thereby inflated type I or
type II errors, respectively. This inflation is serious particularly when the dependency of observations within the same
unit is strong (Wampold and Serlin 2000). Thus, it is necessary
to analyse such data using appropriate ML algorithms such as
REEM-­tree or GP boosting ensuring model-­data compatibility, as we did in our study.

2.1   |   Research Questions
Based on the gaps defined in the literature review, this study
aims to address the following research questions:
1. Which factors are the significant elements considering the
mathematical achievement of the students according to
TIMSS 2019 data?
2. What type of similarities and differences do the algorithms
REEM-­tree, GP boosting and LMM capture about the variables regarding the significance and strength, and how do
these variables differ across the algorithms implemented in
this study?
3. Which algorithm yields the best predictive performance
metrics in terms of Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE),
Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Mean Absolute
Percentage Error (MAPE)?
The rest of the study is organised as follows. Initially, we outline the contribution of this research. Following this, we provide an overview of the TIMSS dataset, followed by a detailed
examination of its characteristics. Next, we summarise both
traditional and modern ML methods employed in our analysis.
Subsequently, we present all outputs and results derived from
our study. In the last section, the discussion and conclusion are
presented.

4   |   Instruments
The modelling design begins by identifying dependent and independent variables, examining assumptions, and specifying
random levels. Mathematics score is used as a dependent variable (see all variables used in Appendix A, Table A1). However,
TIMSS does not provide the actual scores; instead, it provides
five plausible values for each area, which are drawn from the
resulting posterior distribution for each observation (Fishbein
et al. 2021). Martin et al. (2020) highlight that these plausible
values are not individual test scores and are randomly selected
from the conditional normal distribution for each individual. To
ensure reliable inference, Martin and Mullis (2012) recommend
using an average of the five plausible values. Therefore, the
mean score of these values is performed as a dependent variable
of the analysis.
The data includes 18 highly related variables representing
students' opinions (or self-­criticism) about math. We prefer to
utilize factor analysis (FA) to decrease the effect of multicollinearity between highly related predictors. To reduce the data
dimension and increase conceptual integrity, it is applied in
the preprocessing stage. After implementing FA, we gathered
two factors, and they were identified as mathematical tendency and emotional factors. Factor loadings and 18 variables
used can be seen in Appendices A and B, Tables A1 and B1.
Utilizing these two factors instead of the original 18 Likert-scale variables enhances model interpretability. Our modeling approach encompasses both statistical and ML methods,
culminating in the identification of the most effective model
while prioritizing parsimony.
Furthermore, the variable SES is also constructed in the preprocessing step. The original data includes variables representing
the socio-economic status of students. Including these variables as a compact structure is more preferable for increasing
the interpretability of variables. We aggregate all relevant variables into a single SES variable while summing them. It can be
found in Appendix A, Table A1 for the details of each individual
variable.

6.2   |   ML Methods
6.2.1   |   REEM-­T ree
Beside LMM, some ML methods have been developed for nested
structured data sets. REEM-­Tree (Sela and Simonoff 2012) and
GP Boosting (Sigrist 2022) are two of them, and we focus on
these two methods since they enable us to consider the nested
structure of the data with fewer parametric assumptions and
provide flexible tree-­based applications (Salditt et al. 2023).
REEM-­tree models have various advantages in addition to
the strengths of classical random-­effect models, making them
effective and flexible tools in statistical modelling that can
exceed the boundaries of traditional approaches. One of the
key strengths of the classical method lies in its capability to
capture the magnitude of relationships between dependent
and independent variables. However, this characteristic does
not hold true for REMM-­t ree and GP boosting. Nevertheless,
these methods provide additional perspectives that LMM
has limited ability in terms of flexible implementation. For
instance, REEM-­tree does not assume a linear relationship
between dependent and independent variables, whereas
LMM does.
REEM-­tree includes two iterative parts. At the first step, the algorithm fits a regression tree without random effects which are
initialized as zero at the beginning. Afterwards, the tree is used
to create indicator variables. At the second step, LMM using
maximum likelihood (MLE) or restricted maximum likelihood
estimation (RMLE) is fitted to obtain current random effects
European Journal of Education, 2025

from the estimated model. The process lasts until convergence
occurs (Sela and Simonoff 2012).
Although REEM-­tree is an extended version utilising LMMs,
it is not possible to write the output obtained from REEM-­tree
in equation-­based closed form. On the other hand, it provides
several metrics to assess the power of models and strategies. In
REEM-­tree, the same model comprising all the predictors and
schools nested within countries as a random effect is employed
for the purpose of comparing the performance of LMM and
REEM-­tree.

6.3   |   Interpretative Comparison of the Algorithms
Besides investigating the factors affecting mathematics achievement, another perspective of this study highlights the competitive edge of extended ML algorithms such as REEM-­tree and
GP boosting when compared to the traditional method of LMM.
The algorithm of LMM is relatively straightforward and based
on parameter estimation equations. It does not only provide predictions but also allows for inference. For example, in addition
to ML models where we can predict and describe relationships
between variables for countries, schools, or students based on
the measurements, LMM also constructs how these relationships occur. It shows the magnitude of the relationship between
independent variables and the dependent variable.
On the other hand, modern algorithms, REEM-­tree and GP
boosting, have several benefits such as predictive power. It is
important to note that ML algorithms generally aim to achieve
high predictive power as their first and main goal. However, although it may seem like an inadequate and controversial feature
of ML algorithms at first glance, this raises different perspectives for researchers to consider. For example, high predictive
performance enhances the reliability of the model and, accordingly, increases its real-­world application in relevant areas such
as finance, healthcare, or education, thereby supporting system
automation. Similarly, insufficient predictive power may indicate that the structural suitability of the model needs to be investigated, or that the model may be ignoring important variables
(Salditt et al. 2023). Moreover, aiming to obtain high prediction
power helps in selecting the best-­performing model and tuning
the parameters. Therefore, while focusing on predictive power is
a central aspect of ML, it is not inherently problematic.
The REEM-­tree offers more effective performance compared
to standard trees and allows for modelling data without considering linearity. Sela and Simonoff (2012) also indicate that
14 of 23

REEM-trees outperform when the true relationship follows
a tree structure and demonstrate comparable performance to
linear models incorporating random effects, even when a tree
structure is not assumed. Furthermore, they demonstrate that
REEM-­trees are more skilled at estimating the underlying functional form and random effects compared to LMMs, especially
in situations where there are limited observations per object
(Sela and Simonoff 2012). Additionally, despite the absence of
a mathematical equation as an output, visualisations serve as
supplementary materials to enhance the comprehensibility of
the impact. Among the methods examined, GP boosting is best
suited to obtain factors that influence math scores, as it can
easily produce a variable importance plot as an output of the
algorithm.
It is seen that LMM has dominated statistical analyses in the
educational field and become the prior option to analyse nested-structured data over the years, even though relevant assumptions have not been satisfied in empirical data. If practitioners
would like to follow a fully parametric route, methods like nonlinear mixed models (NLMMs) are appropriate if one can posit
a reasonably nonlinear functional form of the underlying process. However, on the other hand, we have emphasised that the
state-­of-­the-­art techniques, such as REEM-­tree and GP boosting, can enable practitioners to analyse nested-­structured data
with enhanced performance in a more flexible way. For example, REEM-­trees can capture nonlinearities, are less vulnerable
to certain parametric requirements, and provide better predictive power than LMM (Sela and Simonoff 2012). Moreover, GP
boosting seems more advantageous than the others, LMM and
REEM-­tree, in terms of the flexible nature of the GP boosting
algorithm for grouped random effects, certain parametric assumptions such as zero and linearity assumptions for the prior
mean function, and independence assumption unlike other
boosting algorithms (Sigrist 2022). REEM-­tree and GP boosting
are highly competitive with LMM and promising to enhance
predictive power. Therefore, these algorithms have the potential
to significantly outperform classical methods.
Consequently, ML methods have the ability to handle large
amounts of data without considering non-­linearity, convergency,
and complex data structures with high predictive performance
(Hilbert et al. 2021). Moreover, with data that is continuously
growing, ML algorithms will become more visible and crucial in
the coming years (Romero and Ventura 2010; Hilbert et al. 2021).

and modeling path. An important issue concerns the original
dataset, which contains a significant amount of missing data.
Due to TIMSS 2019 sample design, achievement gaps between
schools in Turkey and South Africa cannot be distinguished
from achievement differences between classes within the same
schools.
The situation is similar in England, where one class per school is
included in the sample, except for 20 schools. Therefore, we omit
the class level and build the models with a two-­level hierarchical structure: schools nested within countries. Since the data are

7   |   Discussion
Capitalising on the advantages of traditional and modern approaches, this study implements various statistical techniques
to investigate the factors that significantly impact students'
mathematics achievement as determined by TIMSS 2019 data
with the secondary aim of providing guidance to policymakers
and researchers on student success. However, in this study, our
attention is directed not only towards the elements impacting
mathematical achievement but also towards the comparison of
the algorithms implemented. Besides the classical random effect
model (i.e., LMM), the algorithms REEM-­tree and GP boosting
are also employed to criticise the applicability and performance
of ML algorithms. To the best of our knowledge, this article presents a fresh perspective in this regard.
The analysis of mathematics achievements in both developed
and developing countries is conducted utilising random effect
modelling and modern ML techniques to obtain solid, comparable, and meaningful results. Because of the data structure,
it is compulsory to account for both school-­specific effects and
schools within-­country effects, thereby increasing the statistical
power of the analysis thanks to incorporating subject-­specific

European Journal of Education, 2025

random effects. It is important to acknowledge and account for
these variations in order to effectively implement new education
policies and take appropriate precautions. Failure to do so may
result in a significant loss of information and a lack of precision
in the analysis of educational data.
The analyses create several critical outputs that enable us to
make logical inferences directly and differ in critical inferences.
The mathematical tendency is the most important element in
students' math success because of its high level of positive contribution in all three approaches. Lee and Stankov (2018) identified variables such as confidence in mathematics and students'
educational aspirations, respectively, as important predictors of
mathematics achievement, which are similar to the mathematical tendency and emotional factors in our study. They suggest
that confidence in mathematics is the most important factor
influencing math achievement, which coincides with our findings here.
While algorithms may yield similar results concerning mathematical tendencies and emotional factors, they often diverge
in other variables. For example, origin appears as the second
most important variable according to the random effect model.
However, both modern approaches indicate that it is not as significant. It does not even feature in the pruned REEM-­tree regression output (see Figure 7). Moreover, it takes place as the
second least important element in the GP boosting variable importance plot.
Gender serves as another predictor where all algorithms converge on a similar conclusion. Based on the findings, it is not
feasible to generalise that gender difference contributes a valuable gap between girls and boys in terms of math scores. The literature also shows similar results (Doz et al. 2023). For example,
Kim (2021) found that the gender effect has almost no contribution to the student's achievement in South Korea. However, despite the small effect on math scores, it is worth mentioning that
the traditional way argues that girls tend to achieve higher math
scores than boys with a statistically significant contribution.
SES is the most used attribute to examine factors affecting mathematical achievement of students in the literature. This is due to
the crucial need to notice the confounding impact of economic
contributions on students' successes since it can create inequity
and a huge gap in achievements. In our research, we found that
students with higher SES tend to achieve higher math scores
than those without. However, the contribution of this variable
found with LMM is limited. On the other hand, for GP boosting
and REEM-­tree, the effect is much more considerable among all
predictors since it is the third and fourth most important factor,
respectively. In the literature, consistent findings emerge across
numerous studies, indicating the SES in relation to mathematics
achievement (e.g., Takashiro 2017; Ersan and Rodriguez 2020;
Singh 2015).
Practitioners and researchers may consider using these modern
statistical techniques, which are currently of limited use in the
educational field but are continuously growing in relevance, to
offer better actions for stakeholders and beneficiaries involved
in education.

the gender effect does not appear among the significant predictors affecting the mathematics achievement of students. The

nested structure in the hierarchical data. These algorithms have
better performance metrics in terms of RMSE, MAE, and MAPE
compared to the LMM. Thus, they can be considered strong alternatives to the traditional methods for the hierarchical data in
the educational field.

9   |   Recommendations and Implications
This study offers that the innovations made only on the curricular reforms are not adequate for students' mathematics
achievement. Since the most crucial element of success is found
as the mathematical tendency factor across all the methods implemented in the current study, educational policies should be
included and prioritized to improve the mathematics-­related
motivation of students. Policymakers and educators are advised to consider students' attitudes towards mathematics and
administer tests to track progress. This study suggests that educators should focus on making math more appealing to students. Similarly, educators should focus on decreasing the stress
and anxiety level of students caused by mathematics while encouraging collaborative learning since the emotional factor is
among the top elements influencing success. On the other hand,
our study indicates SES is the significant variable in students'
mathematics achievement. Therefore, our study highlights the
importance of socio-­economic equality and recommends that
policymakers should strengthen reforms in supporting students
from lower socio-­economic backgrounds to prevent a significant
disparity between students' mathematics achievement.
Moreover, researchers can utilise modern ML algorithms thanks
to their high predictive accuracies and adaptabilities. Besides
the competitive edge and certain advantages of them, researchers intending to employ the ML algorithms should be careful
about the data type compatibility. Still, this study encourages
the usage of these algorithms in the field of education, not individually, but in combination with the traditional methods to
recognise key points better.

10   |   Future Study
In future studies, we intend to extend the study to an econometrics perspective by including additional variables such as GDP
and education expenditure at the country level as external predictors. Moreover, plans for a follow-­up study using TIMSS 2023
