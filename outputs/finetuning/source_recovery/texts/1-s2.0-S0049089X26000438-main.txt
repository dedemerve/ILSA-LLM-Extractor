Adolescents' expected social standing shapes their educational choices, persistence, and wellbeing, yet little is known about the role of social and emotional skills in forming these expectations. Drawing on the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 data from
205,231 15-year-olds in 27 countries, we examine how perseverance, assertiveness, curiosity,
cooperation, empathy, stress resistance, and emotional control relate to expected relative social
status at age 30. We find that adolescents are broadly optimistic: on average, they expect to attain
a higher social position than their current standing, with expectations varying only modestly by
socioeconomic background. While several social and emotional skills are positively associated
with expectations, perseverance and assertiveness show the strongest associations. These skills
are themselves positively associated with socioeconomic background and account for a substantial share of the association between current and expected social status. In contrast, academic
achievement shows a nonlinear association with expectations once background is considered.
Many low-achieving students − often from disadvantaged backgrounds − report highly optimistic
expectations. This results in a misalignment between expectations and likely mobility pathways
and a weak alignment between academic achievement and expected social status. These findings
suggest that adolescents’ mobility expectations are associated not only with socioeconomic
background and achievement, but also with social and emotional skills. Optimism among
disadvantaged youth may both motivate effort but also carry risks of future frustration if expectations remain unmet.

1. Introduction
Adolescence is a key period for the formation of self-regulated learning habits and future-oriented beliefs (Wigfield and Koenka,
2020). During adolescence, young people set goals and develop expectations about their place in the world. Expectations about one's
future position in the social hierarchy, i.e. status expectations, influence motivational processes such as effort allocation, persistence,
and course enrolment decisions. Optimistic but realistic expectations can buffer against stress and support psychological well-being
(Karvonen and Rahkonen, 2011; Rivenbark et al., 2019). However, unrealistic expectations, particularly in the face of adversity or

limited support (Browman and Destin, 2015), may lead to frustration and disengagement from education (Cox, 2004).
While status expectations are increasingly recognized as important predictors of educational and psychological outcomes (Day and
Fiske, 2019; Kelley and Kelley, 2009), little is known about how they are formed in adolescence or how individual differences
contribute to their development. Although prior research has highlighted the role of academic achievement and socioeconomic
background in shaping adolescents' aspirations, expectations about future relative social position – i.e. how one anticipates standing in
society compared to others − have received limited attention in psychological science. In this study, we fill this gap by examining how
individual differences in social and emotional skills are associated with adolescents’ expectations for their future social standing.
Although few empirical studies have considered subjective status mobility and its determinants (see Kelley and Kelley, 2009;
Bar-Haim, 2018, for important exceptions) and no study to date has examined expectations of status mobility among young people,
many studies have examined young people's educational and career expectations. These studies indicate that young people's educational and career expectations are strongly associated with parental educational and occupational attainment levels (Sikora and Saha,
2007) and academic achievement (Bozick et al., 2010; Han and Borgonovi, 2020; Karlson, 2015; Marks, 2010; Parker et al., 2016).
However, a growing body of evidence suggests that noncognitive skills, such as perseverance, emotional control, empathy, and curiosity, also play a critical role in shaping young people's futures. These skills, often referred to as ‘noncognitive capital’ or ‘social and
emotional skills,’ can be transmitted intergenerationally (Attanasio et al., 2024) and may contribute to the reinforcement of social
stratification (Duckworth et al., 2019; Heckman et al., 2006; Wilmot et al., 2019; Farkas, 2003; Gruijters et al., 2024) and learning (Liu
et al., 2025). Despite this, limited research has explored how these social and emotional skills interact with other factors, such as
academic achievement, to shape perceptions of social mobility among young people.
We exploit new unique data from a large representative sample of 205,231 15-year-old students who participated in the 2022
edition of the Programme for International Student Assessment Study (PISA) in 27 countries to consider the role of social and
emotional skills in shaping social stratification processes. We consider the following social and emotional skills: perseverance,
emotional control, stress resistance, empathy, cooperation, curiosity, and assertiveness. Our contribution is fourfold. First, we provide
cross-country comparable evidence on young people's social status expectations and how these relate to current social status. We
distinguish between baseline status measured objectively and adolescents' perceived current social standing, which we use as a
robustness check. Second, we identify the associations between social and emotional skills and the social status young people expect to
achieve by age 30. Third, we compare associations between social and emotional skills and social status expectations with the association between academic achievement and social status expectations. Fourth, we assess the extent to which socioeconomically
advantaged young people hold higher expectations and if social and emotional skills mediate the social reproduction of advantage.
Our work extends the sociology of education literature on adolescent identity formation and goal setting by examining the social
cognitive and motivational antecedents of future status expectations, offering new insights into how individual differences interact
with structural constraints to shape young people's visions of their future.
2. Literature review
2.1. Social and emotional skills
The literature uses various terms to describe the skills we refer to as social and emotional skills. Terms such as cultural resources,
noncognitive skills, 21st century skills, personality traits, character skills, and soft skills have all been used in the same conceptual space as
social and emotional skills (Duckworth and Yeager, 2015; Jones and Doolittle, 2017). However, the specific number and nature of the
skills vary considerably (Abrahams et al., 2019).
Theoretical and empirical research indicates that the majority of conceptual frameworks of social and emotional skills, noncognitive skills, 21st century skills, personality traits, character skills, and soft skills, align with the five domains of the Big Five personality
framework (Abrahams et al., 2019; Soto et al., 2022). In fact, similar social, emotional, and behavioral factors are commonly used to
define and assess these constructs (Soto et al., 2023). The key distinction lies in how the constructs are conceptualized: social and
emotional skills are typically considered as functional capabilities, whereas personality traits are conceived as behavioral tendencies
(Steponavičius et al., 2023). In terms of the specific overlaps between skills and personality traits, perseverance maps onto the Big Five
trait of conscientiousness; emotional control and stress resistance map onto neuroticism; empathy and cooperation onto agreeableness;
curiosity onto openness to experience; and assertiveness onto extraversion (see John et al., 2008 for a review of the Big Five personality
framework and OECD (2021) for the overlap between the OECD social and emotional skills assessment and the Big Five framework).
Although the Big Five model was originally developed for research on adults, studies indicate that the Big Five personality model, and
by extension the PISA social and emotional skills measures, can be validly applied to populations of school-aged children in
comparative contexts (De Fruyt and De Clercq, 2014; Kankaraš and Suarez-Alvarez, 2019; Steponavičius et al., 2023; Tackett et al.,
2012).
2.2. Expectations of relative social status attainment
Social status expectations describe young people's beliefs about their future position within the socioeconomic hierarchy (Kelley
and Kelley, 2009). Such expectations are conceptually different from more commonly studied educational or occupational expectations, which focus on discrete achievements such as the expectation of completing a university degree or entering a managerial or
professional role. While these achievements are important markers of upward mobility, they do not necessarily reflect whether individuals believe that they will climb the social ladder relative to others. This is because their marker of relative status depends on a

society's distribution of certain characteristics in the population. For example, if young people expect that both they and their peers
will achieve a tertiary-level qualification, relative status expectations would be lower than if they expected to achieve a tertiary-level
qualification but did not expect their peers to do the same (Empey, 1956; Bukodi and Goldthorpe, 2016; Bukodi et al., 2020).
Expectations play a crucial role in adolescence, a developmental stage when young people make key educational choices, such as
whether to continue into upper-secondary education, enroll in particular courses or tracks, or apply for certain preparatory programs
(Wiesner et al., 2003; Hirschi, 2011; Verhoeven et al., 2018; Palacios-Abad, 2021). These decisions often occur as adolescents begin to
exercise autonomy from their parents or guardians, making personal judgments about the paths most likely to yield desirable outcomes, with their beliefs about future opportunities becoming especially salient (Breen and Goldthorpe, 1997; Bodovski, 2014). As
adolescents navigate this period, their beliefs about future social standing can act as powerful motivational drivers, shaping day-to-day
behaviors (Shane and Heckhausen, 2013; Mugabe et al., 2016). Students with ambitious expectations may invest more effort in
academically challenging courses or engage in extracurricular activities that develop skills they believe will confer a competitive
advantage (Palacios-Abad, 2021).
Expected relative social status captures a higher-level comparative goal state, rather than discrete attainment dimensions (such as
expectations of educational qualifications or occupation). Focusing on relative status expectations is important in light of ongoing
educational expansions and economic transformations. As service-oriented and knowledge-based sectors continue expanding, more
jobs require advanced qualifications, and more young people are encouraged to pursue tertiary degrees (Bukodi and Goldthorpe,
2016). Moreover, adolescents' specific occupational aspirations are not fixed and often change over time, as shown in longitudinal
research on the dynamics of career aspirations (e.g., Jacobs et al., 1991). Anchoring expectations to a social ladder rather than discrete
events allows us to consider how adolescents situate their aspirations within a stratified system, where reference-group comparisons
shape young people's perceived opportunity sets.
Expected relative social status integrates multiple status-relevant dimensions (education, occupation, income, and prestige) and
can shape young people's choices and effort as they pursue social status (Anderson et al., 2015). It can be understood as a broad,
identity-consistent goal that guides more specific, actionable goals, such as course selection, study effort, and later career planning,
consistent with expectancy–value and identity-based motivation theories (Eccles and Wigfield, 2002; Oyserman, 2015; Destin and
Oyserman, 2009). Although expected relative social status may appear less under individuals' control than factors traditionally
examined such as educational or career expectations, for many, relative social status is the underlying motivational goal that guides
adolescents to pursue concrete strategies that can help them achieve a desired future rank. As such, subjective status beliefs are not
only descriptive but guide young people's actions (Rivenbark et al., 2019).
This shift could lead to an overall rise in the proportion of individuals occupying traditionally high-status educational or occupational positions, thereby altering the reference group against which adolescents assess their prospects.
2.3. The role of social and emotional skills in shaping social status expectations
Research has highlighted the critical role social and emotional skills play in shaping educational and occupational success
(Duckworth et al., 2019; Heckman et al., 2006; Wilmot and Ones, 2019). These skills facilitate goal-directed behavior and adaptive
responses to social and academic challenges, influencing whether young people perceive themselves as capable of securing an
advantaged position in adulthood (Gruijters et al., 2024). For example, higher levels of curiosity and perseverance have been linked to
increased school engagement (Huttunen et al., 2024), suggesting that these skills may shape not only academic outcomes but also
broader expectations about one's future social standing. Those who perceive themselves as socially and emotionally capable are more
likely to anticipate upward mobility.
At the same time, previous research indicates that not all social and emotional skills observed in childhood predict adult outcomes
to the same extent. In particular, a large body of literature documents that perseverance (and related constructs) has a strong positive
association with both academic achievement and labor market success. By contrast, there is weaker evidence of positive associations
between cooperation, curiosity, empathy and young people's future educational and labor market outcomes (Steponavičius et al.,
2023; Del Bono et al., 2024). Moreover, some skills linked to negative outcomes in childhood are associated with more favorable
outcomes in adulthood. For example, aggression and impulsivity, which often contribute to conduct problems in childhood, have been
linked to better labor market outcomes later in life (Del Bono et al., 2024). While we use the term ‘social and emotional’ skills, a key
aim of our study is to examine how specific skills within this category shape young people's expectations of their future status.
Our selection of seven skills was both data-driven and theory-driven. We aimed to cover Big Five-aligned facets that were strongly
implicated in status-relevant motivational drive and intentionally excluded conceptually adjacent self-beliefs, such as growth mindset
and locus of control. Furthermore, because constructs measured in PISA such as having a growth mindset or locus of control have a
substantial overlap with included constructs that are part of the Big Five framework, they offer limited incremental validity (Credé
et al., 2017; Yeager and Dweck, 2012). By focusing on Big Five-aligned constructs, we are able to develop a parsimonious model that
limits construct redundancy and aligns with the comparative PISA framework.
2.4. Social and emotional skills, future expectations, and variation by achievement
In addition to social and emotional skills, academic achievement plays a central role in shaping young people's social status expectations. Higher academic achievement often expands opportunities for educational advancement, such as admission into selective
programs (Parker et al., 2016). As a result, strong achievement can increase the likelihood of obtaining educational qualifications
necessary for higher-status occupations.

Recent evidence, however, suggests that the influence of academic achievement on educational aspirations varies by socioeconomic status (SES). Among advantaged youths, high expectations are often maintained regardless of academic performance, whereas
for disadvantaged youths, strong grades and test scores play a more critical role in shaping optimism about upward mobility (Bernardi
and Valdés, 2021). Additionally, students interpret signals about their likelihood of success in class-specific ways (Larsen, 2023; Holm
et al., 2019). Low-SES students, lacking a strong family push toward higher education, are more sensitive to these signals and may
adjust their pathways accordingly. While advantaged background students tend to persist despite a negative signal, disadvantaged
students are more likely to opt for lower-risk tracks that still offer future opportunities.
Building on this, the relationship between academic achievement and future success may not be linear. While academic
achievement is generally associated with educational and labor market success, some lower achieving students may overestimate their
ability (Miller and Geraci, 2011) and, as a result, may hold inflated expectations about their prospects (Jerrim, 2014). This miscalibration can arise from multiple mechanisms, including classroom and peer reference effects (Marsh and Parker, 1984; Wouters
et al., 2012), informational constraints, and adaptive identity processes that sustain engagement under adversity (Oyserman, 2015;
Rivenbark et al., 2019; Snyder, 2002).
Because academic achievement and beliefs in one's achievement are closely tied to subsequent educational outcomes (Honicke and
Broadbent, 2016; Borgonovi et al., 2021), they may mediate part of the relationship between current social status and expectations of
future relative status. Adolescents from higher socioeconomic backgrounds tend to perform better in standardized tests and overall
school assessments because of concerted cultivation (Bernardi, 2014; Lareau, 2011), attendance in higher-quality schools, and greater
access to personalized support to remedy gaps and build upon their strengths. This academic advantage, in turn, can reinforce young
people's confidence in their ability to attain or maintain a privileged position in the social hierarchy, partially explaining why current
social status predicts expectations of future relative status.
Moreover, academic achievement can confound the relationship between social and emotional skills and social status expectations.
Adolescents with strong social and emotional competencies (e.g., assertiveness and emotional regulation) may be more adept at
navigating competitive academic environments, but the extent to which these skills translate into high-status aspirations can differ
depending on actual academic performance (Jonkmann et al., 2012). For instance, students with higher narcissism‒a trait related to
heightened self-focus and overconfidence‒may maintain elevated aspirations despite lower academic performance, whereas those
with higher neuroticism, a trait that is related to anxiety and self-doubt, might experience greater discouragement from even modest
academic setbacks. In this sense, academic achievement can amplify or weaken the influence of social and emotional skills on adolescents’ beliefs about their future positions.
Given the potential association between academic achievement, social and emotional competencies, and status expectations, we
included measures of academic achievement when examining the association between social and emotional skills and young people's
expectations. Without accounting for academic achievement, the observed relationship between social and emotional skills and expected status could be overestimated or misattributed. Controlling for academic achievement helps to disentangle the distinct role
played by social and emotional skills in shaping young people's expectations.
2.5. The influence of current social status on expectations of future relative social status
Studies show a strong and positive association between current SES and expectations of future status, with advantaged youth more
likely to expect to work as managers and professionals and to complete tertiary education (Bourdieu, 1986; Bukodi et al., 2020; Schoon
and Polek, 2011; Anders, 2017). According to status attainment theory (Blau and Duncan, 1967; Sewell et al., 1969), young people
from higher socioeconomic backgrounds internalize the expectations and opportunities around them, shaping both their aspirations
and beliefs in what is achievable. Parents with higher SES communicate the value of educational success and offer the resources
necessary for children to turn their aspirations into attainable goals (Bodovski, 2014).
By contrast, cultural reproduction theory (Bourdieu and Passeron, 1990) emphasizes the role of cultural capital and habitus in
shaping adolescents’ perceptions of future status. According to this theory, students from socioeconomically advantaged backgrounds
acquire cultural resources and dispositions that schools and institutions tend to reward. This implicit alignment between family and
institutional expectations fosters a sense of entitlement and confidence in pursuing higher-status positions (Lareau, 2011). Parents pass
on cultural values tied to success, such as perseverance and emotional regulation (Breen and Goldthorpe, 1997; Bodovski, 2014).
However, it is also possible that disadvantaged young people might develop ambitious expectations despite structural barriers and
expect to achieve educational and occupational trajectories similar to their more advantaged peers. Some youth may maintain high
aspirations as a way of asserting a virtuous identity (Frye, 2012).
Social and emotional skills may serve as key mediators in the association between current status and status expectations, given that
adolescents from higher socioeconomic backgrounds tend to report stronger social and emotional skills than their peers from less
advantaged backgrounds, particularly in domains like innovation and self-management (Lechner and Urban, 2025). Research on
educational expectations indicates that adolescents' aspirations are strongly influenced by social and psychological factors, including
parental socialization and peer influence, which shape their self-efficacy beliefs, sense of agency, and perceived opportunities (Andrew
and Flashman, 2017; Cheng and Starks, 2002; Salikutluk, 2016). Socioeconomically advantaged families promote not only academic
but also social-emotional development (Attanasio et al., 2024; Bodovski and Farkas, 2008; Yeung et al., 2002). In line with the notion
of concerted cultivation (Lareau, 2011), higher-status parents are more likely to engage in parenting practices – such as positive
reinforcement, guided problem-solving, and enrichment activities – that hone children's abilities to regulate their emotions, interact
confidently with adults, and navigate complex social dynamics (Weininger et al., 2015). These practices instill a sense of self-efficacy
that can elevate future aspirations. By contrast, young people from disadvantaged households tend to have fewer opportunities to

develop these skills due to more punitive or less supportive environments (Lareau, 2011), potentially leading them to form more
modest expectations of future relative standing.
However, opportunities to develop these skills remain unequally distributed. Adolescents from advantaged backgrounds – who
have more opportunities to develop these competencies – are likely to internalize both the tangible resources (e.g., institutional
support, enriched learning environments) and intangible resources (e.g., confidence, self-regulation) conferred by high-status environments. These advantages may lead them to expect not only to maintain but even surpass their current social position. Adolescents
from less advantaged backgrounds often face greater challenges in developing these crucial skills. Limited access to skill-building
opportunities such as high-quality schooling, extracurricular enrichment, and supportive socialization may hinder their development of social and emotional competencies (Yeung et al., 2002). As a result, even when these adolescents aspire to upward mobility,
the absence of robust social and emotional competencies may diminish their confidence in sustaining a higher relative position
(Heckman et al., 2006).
If social and emotional skills function as a form of noncognitive capital (Farkas, 2003), then disparities in their development across
socioeconomic groups may help perpetuate existing social inequalities. By mediating the relationship between current and expected
future status, these skills could become a key mechanism of stratification: adolescents from already-advantaged families are primed not
only to achieve high-status outcomes but also to expect them, thereby perpetuating a self-fulfilling cycle of advantage (Bodovski and
Farkas, 2008; Lareau, 2011).
3. Data and methods
3.1. Programme for International Student Assessment
PISA is a triennial international large-scale assessment that has been administered to nationally representative samples of 15-yearold students since 2000. Because of the pandemic the 8th edition of the study, which we used in our work, was administered in 2022
instead of 2021. All cases used in our analyses were extracted from the public-use files for PISA 2022 available at https://www.oecd.
org/pisa/data/2022database/. Although 80 education systems participated in PISA 2022, our study was based on the subset of
countries that administered questions on expected social status by age 30, and indicators of social and emotional skills. Our analytic
sample included 205,231 students in 27 countries. A list of 27 countries is available in the Supplementary Online Annex and can be
seen in Fig. 1. Participants were first administered a timed 2-h test and then a questionnaire that never exceeded 1 h.

3.3. Key independent variables
Our key independent variables are indicators of present objective relative social status and the seven indicators of social and
emotional skills.
3.3.1. Social and emotional skills
The social and emotional skill scales used in this study were the official PISA 2022 scales provided by the OECD. Each scale was
constructed using students' responses to a small set of 5-point Likert items ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. Students
reported on seven domains: perseverance, emotional control, stress resistance, empathy, cooperation, curiosity, and assertiveness.
Examples of items used to construct the perseverance scale included statements such as “I keep working on a task until it is finished” and
(reverse-coded) “I give up easily.” Curiosity items included “I am curious about many different things” and “I like to know how things
work.” Cooperation items included “I enjoy cooperating with my classmates” and (reverse-coded) “I avoid working together with other
students.” Empathy items captured sensitivity to others' feelings (e.g., “I can sense how others feel”) as well as reverse-coded items such
as “I ignore the feelings of others.” Assertiveness items included “I enjoy leading others” and (reverse-coded) “I keep my opinion to myself in
group discussions.” Stress-resistance items included “I remain calm under stress” and (reverse-coded) “I get nervous easily.” Finally,
emotional control items included “I keep my emotions under control” and (reverse-coded) “I get mad easily.” All response scales ranged
from strongly disagree to strongly agree, and reverse-coded items were re-scaled before constructing each indicator.
Table S1 in the Supplementary Online Annex lists all the items used to construct each scale and Table S2 reports Cronbach's alpha
coefficients by country. The majority of alpha values are equal or greater than .70, while a small share falls between .60 and .70. These
values are above the reporting threshold used for PISA questionnaire-derived scales (OECD, 2024). In large-scale cross-national assessments that rely on short scales administered across multiple languages and cultural contexts, alpha coefficients in the .60–.70 range
are not uncommon and are often considered acceptable for comparative research, given the well-known sensitivity of alpha to scale
length and item heterogeneity (Taber, 2018). In PISA, administration constraints limit most social and emotional scales to a small
number of items (typically 3–5), which can attenuate internal-consistency estimates. Because our study relies on the OECD-provided
indices, we follow the OECD's established psychometric standards and use the scales as constructed in the international database, while
interpreting the results with appropriate caution.
Different social and emotional skills are only moderately or weakly correlated. The Pearson's pairwise correlation coefficient is
highest between stress resistance and emotional control (r=.505), perseverance and curiosity (r = .384), and empathy and cooperation
(r = .377). All other correlations are positive and below .3, except for the correlation between empathy and stress resistance, which is
very small but negative (r=− .076).

PISA ESCS indicator is derived using students' responses to questions about parental educational attainment, parental occupation, and
the availability of a range of resources at home. The ESCS index can be conceived as a measure of students' access to family resources
(financial, social, cultural, and human capitals) which determine the social position of the student's family/household (Avvisati, 2020).
The ESCS measure allows for comparisons of students across all countries participating in PISA on a common metric. However, because
in this work our interest is in relative social status positions within their national context, we construct a relative status measure by
assigning respondents to weighted deciles in the national distribution of the ESCS index within each country, using PISA sampling
weights to ensure nationally representative cut-points.
3.3.3. Subjective relative social status
In addition to parental education, occupation and household resources which were used to construct the ESCS objective status
indicator, PISA 2022 asked students to report their perceptions of their family's current social standing (SRSS) using the same instrument used to gauge expected social status, i.e. the youth version of the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status (Goodman et al.,
2001; Karvonen and Rahkonen, 2011). Students were asked where their family “stands at this time” in their country, with anchors
referring to income, education, and job respect and value 1 indicating being worst off and 10 best off. This item captures perceived
relative rank in the national status distribution. In our pooled sample, SRSS was moderately correlated with expected status at age 30 (r
= .457). We used SRSS in robustness analyses that replicate our main models, replacing objective ESCS deciles with perceived current
family status as the baseline status measure.
3.4. Control variables
We controlled for gender differences by including an indicator for being a girl. As an indicator of academic achievement, we used
quintiles of PISA mathematics scores, calculated separately within each country. While PISA measures achievement in mathematics,
reading, and science, these domains are highly correlated; we selected mathematics because it received the most testing time in 2022.
PISA performance scores are based on Item Response Theory (IRT) and reported through ten plausible values, which represent multiple
draws from a student's posterior ability distribution. To construct achievement quintiles, we first computed weighted national quintile
cut-points separately for each plausible value using PISA sampling weights. Each plausible value was then incorporated into each
imputed dataset so that quintile assignment reflects both measurement uncertainty and imputation uncertainty. Final estimates
combined information across all plausible values and imputations using Rubin's rule. We used achievement quintiles to capture nonlinearities in the association between achievement and status expectations. The low-stakes nature of PISA means that students do not
receive information on their test performance. As a result, some students' performance on the test may differ from their self-perceived
academic ability and how they perform relative to their classmates in teacher-graded tests. Finally, we controlled for whether the
student has an immigrant background using a dichotomous indicator. It should be noted that we did not take school-level variables into
account, as the variance decomposition analysis showed that the vast majority of the variation in expected social status at age 30 was at
the individual level, with only a small portion attributed to differences between schools and across countries.
3.5. Modelling strategy
Table S3 in the Supplementary Annex details the percentage of item non-response across key variables. To further assess missing
data patterns, we examined the distribution of missing variables across individuals and countries (Table S5). These diagnostics indicate
that missingness is moderately clustered across social and emotional variables, and in a small number of countries (notably the United
Kingdom, Germany, and Chile), reflects a subset of respondents with high levels of item non-response. However, the majority of respondents have either complete data or only partial missingness, suggesting that sufficient information remains for imputation.
To address missing data, we implemented multiple imputations using chained equation (ICE) in Stata (Royston, 2004), generating
10 imputed datasets. The imputation model included all variables used in the analysis, including social and emotional skills scales,
ESCS, mathematics plausible values, immigrant background, and gender. We additionally included students' reading and science
achievement scores as auxiliary variables, which are observed for all respondents and are strongly predictive of both outcomes and
missingness. Country fixed effects were also included to account for potential country-specific differences in missing data patterns.
Continuous variables were imputed using linear regression, and binary variables using logistic regression. All estimates were subsequently combined using Rubin's rule, ensuring that both imputation and sampling uncertainty were incorporated into the standard
errors. In all estimations student final weights and balanced replication weights (BRR) were used to take into account the PISA complex
sampling strategy and the clustered nature of PISA data to obtain unbiased estimates for standard errors in line with OECD's
recommendations.
While the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption cannot be directly tested, as a robustness check, we re-estimated the models
excluding countries with the highest levels of missingness. Results remain substantively similar, with the main conclusions unchanged
(see Supplementary Online Annex Table S6).
We first conducted descriptive analyses to identify, for each country in our sample, young people's expectations for the future and
how these compare with their objective and subjective relative social status. We complemented these with a three-level multilevel
model – considering students as level 1, schools as level 2, and countries as level 3 – to identify the extent to which variation in our
outcome of interest lies at the individual, school, or country level. We found that virtually all the variation in students' social status
expectations lay at the individual level. This finding justified the focus of our work on individual determinants – such as social and
emotional skills – rather than on between-country differences and structural conditions. It also justified our decision to present results

from models estimated on the pooled sample with country fixed effects in subsequent analyses.
In a second step, we operationalized our theoretical considerations into a set of empirical models to examine associations between
social origin and social status expectations, social and emotional skills and social status expectations, and mathematics achievement
and social status expectations. We conceptualized social and emotional skills as a developmental factor through which socioeconomic
origin was associated with expected relative social status: family resources and socialization were linked to distinct skill profiles
(Attanasio et al., 2024; Bodovski and Farkas, 2008; Lareau, 2011), and these skills, in turn, were associated with beliefs about future
standing. Accordingly, we treated mediation as a statistical decomposition of associations consistent with this
socialization-to-expectations pathway. We fitted mediation models to identify the indirect channel, through which social origin was
associated with mathematics achievement and social and emotional skills, which were in turn associated with individuals’ expected
relative social status in adulthood and the direct channel, capturing the independent association between social origin and status
expectations net of skills and achievement. Fig. 1 summarizes the conceptual structure of our mediation model, showing the pathways
from socioeconomic background (ESCS) to expected relative social status (ERSS) via mathematics achievement and social and
emotional skills; gender, immigrant background, and country fixed effects are included in the estimates as controls but are omitted
from the diagram for clarity.
In a final step, we assessed the robustness of our findings to how current social status was operationalized. Our main analyses used
objective relative SES based on ESCS deciles. However, comparisons between objective current status and the expected future ladder
placement could partly reflect adolescents' imperfect knowledge of their family's location in the national distribution of social status, as
well as the tendency for ladder responses to cluster around upper-middle points. To address this, we reestimated the main models using
students' subjective perceived current family status which was measured on the same 10-point ladder. This specification allowed us to
evaluate whether differences in expected future status by background primarily reflected adolescents' perceptions of their current
standing (i.e., perceived mobility) rather than expectations of mobility relative to objective socioeconomic rank. Full results are reported in the Supplementary Online Annex Table S7. Table 2 indicates that subjective relative social status is only weakly correlated
with objective relative social status, an indication that adolescents' perceived family standing captures a related but distinct construct
from ESCS-based socioeconomic rank.
The dependent variable was kept in natural units (i.e. the expected relative decile in the national distribution of social status).
Continuous covariates at the student level (i.e. social and emotional skills), except for math achievement scores and PISA ESCS index,
were standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1 across the analytic sample before running analyses and
country fixed effects were added in all models.
Because the analytic sample is very large, we expect many coefficients to be statistically significant even when they are quantitatively small. Therefore, we focused on the magnitude of estimated associations and their interpretation in meaningful units (expected
point on the 1–10 ladder), rather than on statistical significance. In addition to reporting coefficients in ladder units, we translated key
estimates into SD units of the outcome. Funder and Ozer (2019) note that when reliably estimated (a condition that our analysis
satisfies), what is typically considered to be a very small effect using Cohen's d benchmarks (Cohen, 1988) can still have potentially
consequential effects and that effect sizes need to be evaluated and classified within a frame of reference.

4. Results
4.1. Cross-country comparisons of expectations of relative social status
Fig. 2 indicates that on average 15-year-old students expect to have a relative social status of 7.65 on a 10-point scale by the time
they are 30 and report having a subjective relative current social status equal to 7.14. Objective relative social status was constructed as
a decile within each country, meaning that average objective social status is approximately 5. This suggests that, on average, students
hold optimistic expectations about their future social standing, anticipating being above the median national status distribution and
that they already perceive to have a higher social status than is the case.
Results from the variance decomposition analysis indicate that 93.8% of the variance in expected social status at age 30 lies within
individuals, 1.74% lies between schools and 4.46% lies between countries.
4.2. Differences in expectations of relative social status by social and emotional skills, academic achievement and objective socioeconomic
status
Table 3 presents results from a series of models that examine the extent to which adolescents’ expectations of future status are
shaped by social and emotional skills, academic achievement and objective social status. Model 1 in Table 3 indicates that young
people with a more advantaged SES hold more ambitious expectations of relative social status by age 30 than their more disadvantaged
peers. A one-decile difference in the national distribution of objective social status is associated with a difference of 0.052 points on the
1-10 scale of expected social status. This difference is only half as large when we control for social and emotional skills in Model 2: a
one-decile difference in the national distribution of objective social status is associated with a difference of 0.025 points when controlling for social and emotional skills. By contrast, the association is unaffected by the introduction of mathematics achievement
controls in Model 3.
Model 2 in Table 3 reports results for the pooled model examining associations between the seven social and emotional skills
indicators and young people's expectations of social status at age 30. Across the 27 countries in our sample, perseverance and
assertiveness are the two social and emotional skills that are most strongly associated with expected relative social status at age 30: a
one SD difference in perseverance is associated with a difference of 0.210 points in the expected social status at age 30 and a one SD
difference in assertiveness is associated with a difference of 0.221 points in expected social status at age 30. The difference in expected
social status at age 30 associated with a one SD difference in stress resistance is 0.129, for cooperation this is 0.051, for curiosity 0.066,
for empathy 0.034, whereas for emotional control, the association is 0.022.
Model 3 in Table 3 suggests that mathematics achievement is not strongly associated with social status expectations when controlling for objective SES and that the association is not linear. Estimates are quantitatively small and not statistically significant with
the exception of the second achievement quintile. Compared to students in the top quintile of mathematics achievement, students in
the second lowest quintile have 0.063 points higher expected social status on the 1-10 points scale.
4.3. Social and emotional skills and mathematics achievement as mediators of expected social status
Model 2 in Table 3 indicates that current objective social status is positively associated with expectations of relative social status,
and that the strength of this association decreases when social and emotional skills are introduced. This finding suggests that the
relationship between current socioeconomic background and expectations of future social status is not solely direct, but also operates
indirectly, and is mediated by the association between social status and social and emotional skills.
To quantify the extent to which social and emotional skills mediate the association between current SES and expected future social
status, we calculated the product of the path coefficients linking ESCS to each social and emotional skill and those linking each skill to
expected status. Findings presented in Table 3 suggest that, collectively, the indirect association between current SES and expected
social status that is mediated by the seven social and emotional skills represents around 44% of the total association between present
social status and expected social status when not controlling for gender and immigrant background in Model 4 and 43% when controlling for these factors in Model 5.
In Model 4, we consider the extent to which specific social and emotional skills mediate the association between current and
expected social status before controlling for gender and immigrant status, whereas in Model 5 we control for these. In both models, we
fit indirect paths between current status and social and emotional skills. Findings indicate that all social and emotional skills mediate
the association between current and expected social status. In particular, young people from more advantaged households have higher
levels of assertiveness and curiosity than their less advantaged peers. For example, as indicated in Model 5, a one-decile difference in
current status is associated with a 0.046 SD difference in assertiveness, a 0.042 SD difference in curiosity, a 0.034 SD difference in
empathy, and a 0.029 SD difference in perseverance.
Among these skills, perseverance and assertiveness exhibit the strongest positive associations with expected social status, making
perseverance and assertiveness important mediators of the association between current and expected social status. A one-SD difference
in perseverance is associated with a 0.213-point difference in expected social status and a one-SD difference in assertiveness is
associated with a 0.219-point difference in expected social status, suggesting that students with higher levels of these skills are more
likely to expect higher future social standing. By contrast, curiosity, cooperation, and empathy, despite being positively associated
with current social status, show only weak associations with expectations of relative social status. Consequently, they do not appear to
be important mediators of the relationship between present and expected status.

4.4. Robustness: Subjective relative social status
In order to assess the sensitivity of our findings to response effects clustering toward upper-middle points in the social status ladder,
we replicated our main models using students’ perceived current family status on the same 10-point ladder as the expected social status
measure (Supplementary Online Annex Table S7).
Results reveal a stronger association between current status and expected status when baseline status is measured subjectively
(Model 5: β = 0.425), which is consistent with both items capturing perceived rank on a shared response frame. However, substantive
results are aligned: assertiveness and perseverance remain the strongest skill correlates of expected future status (β = 0.192 and 0.168,
respectively), and the association between mathematics achievement and expected status remains modest once current status is
accounted for. If anything, the earlier inverted achievement pattern is attenuated under the subjective-status specification, suggesting
that part of that pattern reflects differences in perceived standing and reference frames rather than a direct achievement–expectations
link.
5. Discussion
Given the central role of beliefs and perceptions in shaping individual behavior, policy choices, and political action, this study
investigates how young people in 27 countries that participated in PISA 2022 form their social mobility expectations. Over the past
decades, the world has witnessed significant improvements in absolute standards of living and well-being. For example, today's young
people are the most well-educated in history, infant mortality rates have reached record lows, and the number of people living in
absolute poverty has declined more than ever before (UNDP, 2024). However, these advancements coincide with rising inequality in
many countries (Hoffmann et al., 2020) and declining social mobility (Bukodi et al., 2020). Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic has
substantially influenced young people's attitudes and experiences (Kennedy and Strietholt, 2023; Residori et al., 2023), as well as their
educational experiences (Blaskó et al., 2022).
We situate high expectations within institutional and sociocultural contexts. Students in underresourced schools may nonetheless
experience favorable relative standing within their local peer ecology (Marsh and Parker, 1984), and some adolescents maintain
ambitious expectations as a resilience/identity-affirming response to structural barriers (Oyserman, 2015; Snyder, 2002). This
perspective complements accounts emphasizing overconfidence (Miller and Geraci, 2011) by highlighting unequal environments and
the protective function of hope and social identity.
The literature indicates that holding optimistic expectations is linked to positive outcomes for young people. For example, young
people with ambitious educational and career expectations are more likely than their less optimistic peers to invest effort in their
studies to achieve their goals (Palacios-Abad, 2021; Shane and Heckhausen, 2013). Furthermore, optimism in adolescence is associated
with positive mental health, and it has been shown to buffer against stress, and to protect young people from pathological symptoms
and risky behaviors (Rincón Uribe et al., 2022). At the same time, although holding ambitious expectations can help young people
improve their odds of being upwardly mobile, unmet expectations can lead to dissatisfaction and negative affect, potentially
13

Social Science Research 136 (2026) 103350

F. Borgonovi and S.W. Han

influencing young people's political engagement, attitudes, and behaviors (Cox, 2024).
Our work suggests that in 2022, young people generally expected to improve their relative social standing by age 30. We find that
some, but not all, social and emotional skills are positively associated with young people's expectations of relative social status. In
particular, perseverance and assertiveness are positively associated with expected social status at age 30, even after controlling for
individual characteristics, including academic achievement. Although several coefficients are small in absolute terms, this is expected
in observational research on complex social outcomes, where multiple influences operate simultaneously. Small effects may still
accumulate over time and across populations, but they should be interpreted in context rather than through statistical significance
alone (Funder and Ozer, 2019). For example, the practical differences associated with perseverance and assertiveness are modest but
nontrivial on a 1–10 point ladder, whereas associations in the case of emotional control and empathy are unlikely to have practical
significance.
Perseverance and assertiveness also mediate part of the relationship between current social status and future status expectations,
suggesting that they can amplify advantages linked to socioeconomic background. By contrast, academic achievement (proxied here
by PISA mathematics proficiency) shows a nonlinear association with status expectations: many low-achieving students, especially
those in the bottom two quintiles, report highly optimistic future status expectations. Because achievement is strongly associated with
existing social status, the highly ambitious expectations of low-achieving students in the bottom two quintiles help narrow socioeconomic disparities in status expectations. This suggests that these students remain optimistic about mobility despite current
disadvantages.
Digital environments may help explain why academically low-performing adolescents hold highly ambitious status expectations.
Social media platforms curate success stories of influencers, gamers, and entrepreneurs whose achievements are detached from success
in formal education. Although these success stories represent outliers, algorithms designed to promote engagement among young
people magnify such narratives, conveying the message that success is common and independent of academic achievement. This
narrative may be especially salient among low achievers whose sense of self is enhanced by these narratives. Furthermore, low
achievers may overestimate their competence (Dunning, 2011). Holding grand expectations may also serve a protective motivational
function: affirming a hopeful future identity buffers self-worth amid present failures (Rivenbark et al., 2019).
In addition to social media exposure, the weak alignment between mathematics achievement and expected future status may reflect
heterogeneity in what adolescents understand as a pathway to achieve high social status. Young people's status expectations are likely
to be grounded in the types of routes to economic success and social respect they observe in their local communities, which can vary by
socioeconomic background. For example, adolescents from less advantaged communities may be more familiar with individuals who
obtained high earnings and social status through self-employment or small businesses in skilled trades (such as construction, transport
or repair services), positions which do not necessarily require strong academic achievement. By contrast, adolescents from more
advantaged communities may often observe pathways to high earnings and social status through professions such as medicine or law,
which require high levels of academic achievement for entry.
Research on subjective expectations shows that students’ beliefs about the economic returns to education vary across individuals
and groups and that such variation can shape educational intentions and investments (Jensen, 2010; Boneva et al., 2022). Career
development theories further emphasize that occupational aspirations and perceived status are formed through processes of
circumscription and compromise that depend on social context, perceived accessibility, and available role models (Lee and Rojewski,
2009). The relatively stronger associations we observe between perseverance and assertiveness and expected status are also consistent
with an entrepreneurial/agentic route to social mobility (Zhao et al., 2010; Brandstätter, 2011), and prior research shows that
exposure to self-employed role models increases the likelihood of entering self-employment (Chlosta et al., 2012). We view these
mechanisms as potentially complementary rather than competing explanations for optimistic status expectations among lower
achievers.
5.1. Limitations and future directions
Before we discuss the implications of our findings, it is worth noting that evidence presented in this work is correlational. The
observed contemporaneous associations between social and emotional skills and social mobility expectations could reflect different
causal mechanisms or unobserved differences between groups of young people with specific social and emotional skills and social
mobility expectations. Future research could establish causality by examining, for example, interventions that enhance social and
emotional skills, first measuring changes in these skills, then tracing any effect on social mobility expectations.
Because our data are cross-sectional, we also refrain from making causal claims about mediation and interpret indirect paths as
descriptive decompositions. Alternative structures (such as moderation as discussed by Liu, 2019) could also generate the observed
patterns. Future work leveraging longitudinal and/or experimental designs could further investigate how these social and emotional
skills not only mediate but also moderate the relationship between socioeconomic background and expectations of social status and
mobility.
Key measures in our study, namely expected relative social status and social and emotional skills, are self-reports, raising the
possibility of common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Including a test-based indicator of mathematics achievement, such as PISA
scores, helps attenuate single-source concerns for key covariates, since not all covariates in our study rely on self-reports. However, we
cannot rule out inflation/deflation of associations. Future work could use behavioral measures of social and emotional skills
(Borgonovi et al., 2023). Finally, the MacArthur-type ladder provides a deliberately broad representation of social status, which is a
strength for comparability but may also invite respondents to anchor on different occupational prototypes (for example, professions
requiring high levels of educational qualifications rather than high-earning self-employment which often does not require specific

educational qualifications). These often vary across social groups and local labor markets which may explain the weak and nonlinear
association between social status expectations and academic achievement.
Data used in this work were collected in 2022 when the COVID-19 pandemic was receding but was still ongoing. The pandemic had
a profound effect on the educational and personal development of young people, and may have influenced their beliefs, expectations,
and perceptions. Reported associations do not vary greatly across countries that pursued different policy decisions and faced different
health and economic consequences during the pandemic. Nonetheless, the reported findings may, at least in part, reflect the unique
historical circumstances of the pandemic and the effects the pandemic had on educational outcomes, status perceptions, and expectations about the future. Should similar data be collected in PISA 2025 or future rounds, it will be possible to identify the stability of
identified associations.
Although the present study represents the largest analysis of the social mobility expectations of adolescents worldwide, data reflect
the composition of countries that participated in the PISA assessment in 2022 and decided to administer questions on social and
emotional skills in the background questionnaire. Although participating countries vary in terms of educational policies, academic and
social emotional skills, levels of social mobility, and distribution of economic resources, it is possible that associations would differ in
other countries and world regions. Moreover, the study reflects perceptions and expectations of adolescents who were part of the PISA
target population. This comprises 15-year-old children enrolled in lower secondary or upper secondary schools. It is possible that
results would differ had different age groups and out of school populations been considered.
5.2. Conclusion
This is the first study mapping expectations of relative social mobility in large, representative samples of adolescent students in 27
countries worldwide. Social mobility expectations reflect the extent to which individuals imagine their future selves to occupy a
position in the social hierarchy that is different from the position they currently occupy. Our study focuses on 15-year-old students and,
as such, complements work conducted among adults on social mobility perceptions and work conducted among teenagers on
educational and career ambitions. Our work suggests that young people in general are optimistic about their expected relative social
position and opportunities for upward mobility.
The key question examined in this work is whether social and emotional skills help perpetuate or disrupt the reproduction of social
advantage by shaping expectations of relative social status. Even though countries examined in the study are all high- or middle-upper
income, our work has a relatively high degree of generalizability: estimates are in fact based on large and representative samples from
countries that differ with respect to several economic, educational, and cultural factors. The study reveals that adolescents from
advantaged backgrounds exhibit higher levels of perseverance, curiosity, assertiveness, empathy, stress resistance, and emotional
control. Perseverance and assertiveness, in particular, predict higher expectations of future status and mediate the relationship between current SES and anticipated social position, thereby contributing to social stratification (Gruijters et al., 2024).
Our finding that expected future status is only weakly correlated with current socioeconomic position diverges from research on
educational and occupational aspirations, which tends to show a strong reproduction of social status. One explanation is that the
MacArthur “ladder” invites adolescents to locate their future selves on a broad societal scale rather than select a specific credential or
job. This open frame may foster aspirational egalitarianism, especially in societies that espouse meritocratic ideals. In addition,
globalization and mass media expose youth across classes to similar representations, potentially narrowing socioeconomic disparities
in declared ambition.
At the same time, our finding that adolescents rate both their current and future status well above the midpoint suggests that
mobility expectations in these data partly reflect perceived rank and scale use. This matters for interpretation: if many students
perceive their current position as already above average, then upward mobility inferred from objective deciles can overstate the extent
to which adolescents anticipate changing their rank, and it may instead capture a broader orientation toward optimism, selfenhancement, and locally grounded comparisons (Goodman et al., 2015; Zell et al., 2020). At the same time, subjective beliefs are
substantively important because they shape motivation and investment, including educational choices, and evidence shows that
perceived returns to education vary across groups and influence schooling decisions (Boneva et al., 2022; Jensen, 2010).
If future work using more rigorous methods confirms a causal link, these findings highlight the potential of interventions aimed at
developing social and emotional skills (Heckman et al., 2006; Steponavičius et al., 2023). Our results also underscore the importance of
narrowing socioeconomic disparities in academic achievement. Many young people from disadvantaged backgrounds have low math
proficiency but expect to obtain high-status positions. Ambitious educational and career expectations help young people develop a
positive sense of their identity, seeing themselves as socially valued and morally praiseworthy (Baker, 2017; Frye, 2012). At the same
time, because they lack both the academic standing and the social networks that are needed to turn ambitious aspirations into a reality,
they may face frustration in the future.
