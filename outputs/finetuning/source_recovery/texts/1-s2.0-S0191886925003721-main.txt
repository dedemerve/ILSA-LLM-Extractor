A B S T R A C T
A meta-analytical approach was used to test the greater male variability hypothesis using the mathematics and 
reading performance of more than 26 million Italian children across second, fifth, and eighth-grade. In keeping 
with the hypothesis, boys were more variable than girls in mathematics performance especially in more 
developed geographic regions with larger sex differences. However, there was no clear pattern for sex differences 
in variability in reading performance, although there was a trend for boys to be more variable in regions with 
larger sex differences, favoring girls. These results provide a robust test of the greater male variability hypothesis 
and suggest that it is not a ubiquitous phenomenon but rather varies across traits.
1. Introduction
Ellis (1894) was the first to provide empirical evidence on the so- 
called greater male variability hypothesis (GMVH). He noted a higher 
number of boys than girls among people at the lower end of the normal 
distribution (e.g., people with intellectual disabilities), and at the higher 
end (e.g., talented or gifted). Sex differences in variability have been 
studied for over a century, with no clear consensus. The standard 
approach to testing the GMVH involves using Jensen's (1971) Variance 
Ratio (VR), where greater male variation has been observed across 
various cognitive domains (Hedges & Nowell, 1995). In an early appli­
cation, Maccoby and Jacklin (1974) found that boys showed greater 
variability in mathematics and certain spatial abilities, but the VR for 
verbal abilities was close to 1. Later, Feingold (1992) showed larger 
variability for boys in numerical and spelling ability using national 
norms from various standardized tests, but more mixed findings for 
other verbal abilities, such as verbal reasoning and language. However, 
more recent meta-analyses indicate that greater male variability extends 
beyond academic domains, including creativity (Taylor et al., 2024) and 
cooperation (Th¨oni et al., 2021), although these effects are generally 
small and moderated by sociocultural factors such as stereotyped ex­
pectations and educational access (Feingold, 1994; Makel et al., 2016; 
Taylor et al., 2024). Biological mechanisms (Arden & Plomin, 2006; 
Deary & Der, 2005; Johnson et al., 2008; Lynn, 2017), such as variations 
in brain development, have also been proposed to account for sex dif­
ferences in variability.
Sex differences in variability are particularly relevant for academic 
achievement. For instance, mean sex differences in mathematics are 
typically small and can vary across topics and nations (Else-Quest et al., 
2010; Hyde et al., 1990). For general samples there are about two boys 
for every girl at the high end (top 5 %) of math performance (Stoet and 
Geary, 2018), and this increases to four boys for every girl for high- 
achieving samples (> top 1 %) (Wai et al., 2010). These differences 
help explain the higher male participation and success in mathematics- 
intensive STEM fields (Madison & Sundell, 2024; Wai et al., 2018). 
However, the consistency of these findings has been questioned (Hyde & 
Mertz, 2009). Accordingly, we examined sex differences in variability in 
mathematics and reading performance for >26 million second, fifth, or 
eighth grade students, and explored whether these varied across grade, 
regional economic development, and mean sex differences.

However, Hyde and Mertz (2009) found substantial cross-national dif­

World Economic Forum, 2024), was associated with a lower proportion 
of boys among top-performing students. Gray et al.'s (2019) meta- 
analytic extension also examined the VRs across international assess­
ments (e.g., PISA), with similar results. Specifically, countries with 
higher gender equality tended to show smaller VRs (i.e., more equal 
variance), particularly in mathematics.
Investigations using international assessments such as Programme 
for International Student Assessment (PISA) and Progress in Interna­
tional Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) revealed that across a wide range 
of countries, boys showed more variability in reading performance than 
girls (Lynn & Irwing, 2004; Lynn & Mikk, 2009), which has been 
confirmed in more recent assessments (Baye & Monseur, 2016; Gray 
et al., 2019). Intriguingly, as girls' mean reading performance increases 
so does variability among boys on reading measures. In contrast, Hedges 
and Nowell (1995) found no significant difference in variability in 
reading comprehension and vocabulary in the NLSY and NELS datasets. 
A meta-analysis of NAEP (1988 to 2015) also showed greater variability 
in boys' than girls' reading performance (Reilly et al., 2019). To add to 
these mixed findings, another meta-analytic investigation analyzing 
PISA and PIRLS data showed that boys' performance was more variable 
than girls' performance across various content areas, including reading 
(Gray et al., 2019). For reading, countries with higher mean perfor­
mance in girls also showed more variation in boys' reading performance.
1.2. Aims and hypotheses
This study assesses sex differences in math and reading VRs using a 
large sample of Italian second, fifth, and eighth graders and to evaluate 
whether the magnitude of these differences varies across grade, regional 
economic development and gender equality, and mean academic sex 
differences. The following hypotheses guided the analytic approach: 
First, we hypothesized that boys would show greater variability (VR > 1) 
in mathematics and in reading. Second, we hypothesized that VRs would 
vary with standardized mean differences (SMD) in mathematics and 
reading, but with no directional prediction, as previous findings were 
inconsistent. VRs are generally consistent across grades but this has not 
been systematically explored and so we did so but without a specific 
hypothesis. Finally, we investigated the relation between VRs and a 
proxy indicator of gender equality using a geographical gradient vari­
able that ordered Italian provinces along a north-south axis. This 
gradient captures regional differences that have been previously linked 
to national indicators of wealth and development that are closely asso­
ciated with gender equality in Italy (Giofr`e et al., 2020). As with grade, 
findings were inconsistent, so we had no directional hypothesis.
2. Methods
2.1. Materials
The study used population-level data from Italian students in second, 
fifth, and eighth graders who took the INVALSI national standardized 
test (50.68 % males). We used 2022/23 as an example; descriptive 
statistics were similar across years. Mean age of our sample was: 7.29 
(SDage = 0.60), 10.29(SDage = 0.62), and 13.33(SDage = 0.68) for sec­
ond, fifth, and eighth grades, respectively. Italy's mostly public educa­
tion system captures broad socioeconomic backgrounds. Mathematics 
and reading achievement data were from the INVALSI (Istituto Nazio­
nale per la VALutazione del Sistema di Istruzione [National Institute for 
the Assessment of the Instruction System]) assessments of Italian 
students from 2009 to 2023. A total of 26,645,542 students were

2.2. Data analysis
A meta-analytic approach was used to investigate VR using the 
“metafor” package (Viechtbauer, 2010) within R (R Core Team, 2024). 
For each year and grade level in every Italian province, the VR between 
boys and girls and the SMD in performance were computed. Therefore, 
the statistical unit is represented by the effect size for a given province in 
a given survey year. Since multiple survey years are examined, multiple 
effect sizes were coded for each year and each province. Moderators 
were SMD and grade (second, fifth, and eight grade; see Supplementary).
3. Results
3.1. Preliminary analysis
A preliminary analysis showed that mathematics and reading scores 
were correlated across grades: second-grade, r = 0.704, 95 %CI [0.676, 
0.730]; fifth-grade, r = 0.713, 95 %CI [0.685, 0.738]; and eighth-grade, 
r = 0.348, 95 %CI [0.300, 0.393]. Results from the first series of meta- 
analyses showed that boys' variability in mathematics was higher than 
girls' variability, VR = 1.101, (log)VR = 0.097, increasing with grade: 
second VR = 1.082, (log)VR = 0.079; fifth, VR = 1.109, (log)VR = 0.104; 
and eighth, VR = 1.113, (log)VR = 0.107. A mixed trend was observed in 
reading, in which boys' variability was higher only in the fifth-grade: 
second grade, VR = 0.986, (log)VR = −0.014; fifth-grade, VR =
1.023, (log)VR = 0.023; and eighth-grade, VR = 0.991, (log)VR =
−0.009. For reading, these effects were trivial with the overall (log)VR 
close to zero.
The odds of being in the top 5 % of mathematics achievers were 
slightly higher for boys than for girls, second-grade, OR = 1.060; fifth- 
grade, OR = 1.460; and eighth-grade, OR = 1.764. Conversely, the 
odds of being in the bottom 5 % were lower for boys than for girls, 
second grade, OR = 0.490; fifth-grade, OR = 0.285; and eighth grade, 
OR = 0.204. As for reading, results showed that the odds of being in the 
top 5 % were consistently lower for boys compared to girls, but this 
decreased across grades: second-grade, OR = 0.561; fifth-grade, OR =
0.166; and eighth-grade, OR = 0.052. In contrast, boys were more likely 
than girls to be in the bottom 5 % of reading performance, which 
increased across grades: second grade, OR = 2.331; fifth grade, OR =
4.199; and eighth grade, OR = 8.201.
3.2. Analysis of moderators
In-depth analyses investigated the second and the third hypothesis. 
Firstly, a null model (Model 0) with only random effects (i.e., provinces 
and survey years) was performed for both mathematics and reading. 
Secondly, due to high heterogeneity, moderated meta-analyses were 
performed in math, τ = 0.069, and reading, τ = 0.027. For math, the VR 
increased with SMD (Model 1), b = 0.271, p < .001 (Fig. 1a), and 
explained 33.034 % of the heterogeneity (Table 1). The change in VR 
across grades (Model 2) was statistically significant but explained little 
variance (b = 0.006, p < .001). The geographical gradient (Model 3) 
explained 27.598 % of the heterogeneity in VR, though effect size was 
small (b = −0.001, p < .001), the wide geographic range indicates 
meaningful north–south differences and indicated the variances of boys 
and girls were more similar in southern than northern provinces 
(Fig. 2a).
The moderators were significant for reading, but the explained het­
erogeneity was small (Models 1–2; Table 1). It is worth noting, however, 
that the effect of SMD was the opposite of that found in mathematics, 
suggesting that as girls' advantage increased, boys' variability in reading 
increased (Fig. 1b). Geographical gradient was not a significant

4. Discussion
The current study assessed the male variability hypothesis for 
mathematics and reading based on one of the largest samples ever used 
to investigate this issue. Our results show more variation in boys' than 
girls' mathematics performance, especially in more economically and 
gender equal regions of Italy and as boys' overall mean performance 
increases. Although there were more girls at the high end of reading and 
more boys at the low end, there was not a consistent sex difference in 
variation.

2016; Feingold, 1992; Gray et al., 2019; Hedges & Nowell, 1995). The 
associated (log) VR ranged from 0.079 to 0.107, indicating that boys' 
variability was about 8–11 % greater than girls' variability. These results 
are consistent with most prior large-scale assessments (e.g., PISA,

TIMSS), where boys' variability was roughly 12 % greater than girls' 
variability (Baye & Monseur, 2016). Our results are also consistent with 
previous findings suggesting that differences in variability are related to 
sex differences in mean performance (Gray et al., 2019; Nowell & 
Hedges, 1998), that is, as boys' mean advantage in mathematics 
increased, their variability became greater than girls' variability. 
Although these sex differences in variability might look small, they 
could have a strong impact at the extreme tails of the distribution. In 
other words, even a small increase in VR might result in a more boys or 
girls among low and high achievers.
This result is consistent with other findings of a relation between 
mean performance and variability in mathematics (Gray et al., 2019; 
Nowell & Hedges, 1998). Our results suggest that the distribution of 
boys' performance is shifted to the right relative to girls' performance. 
This is sometimes reflected in an overrepresentation of boys at the high 
end of mathematics performance, especially in older children (OR =
1.06–1.76) and at the same time, an underrepresentation at the low end 
(OR = 0.490–0.204). This means that the odds ratio is relatively small in 
magnitude in earlier grades, but becomes larger in later grades. These 
findings are consistent with prior results indicating that boys are more 
likely than girls to be among high-achievers, and less likely to be among 
low-achievers in mathematics (Giofr`e et al., 2020; Nowell & Hedges, 
1998; Oakley et al., 2024; Reilly et al., 2015). According to Lynn's 
(2017), sex differences in intelligence become more pronounced during 
adolescence. This emerging advantage may partly explain grade-related 
effects. However, Lynn also noted that greater male variability in IQ 
scores is evident even before puberty, suggesting that developmental 
changes in mean IQ alone cannot fully account for the observed sex 
differences. Indeed, mathematics and reading achievement are 
evolutionarily-novel and thus dependent on instructional practices, but 
how these practices interact with more basic sex differences, such as 
spatial abilities, remains to be determined (Geary, 2024; Geary et al., 
2023).
Though statistically significant, the grade effect was not practically 
meaningful. The finding could be due to the meta-analytic method itself 
and because our large sample could produce significant effects that are 
relatively trivial. In the broader literature, the grade or educational level 
effects are mixed (Gray et al., 2019; Lakin, 2013; Reilly et al., 2015), and 
our findings suggest that any such effects are not substantial at least 
before secondary school. Gray et al.'s (2019) PISA study found an even 
larger VR for Italy (1.26) for adolescents, suggesting the variation might 
increase in secondary school. Hedges and Nowell (1995) found that four 
of the five VRs for secondary and older students were larger than found 
in our sample (1.16–1.20) but one of them was in the same range (1.05), 
and so the issue remains to be resolved.
We found that northern Italian regions presented with higher VRs for 
boys than southern regions. This result mirrors the pattern observed in 
mean performance, with differences in mathematics in northern than 
southern regions (see also Giofr`e et al., 2020). At first glance, this 
finding appears to be inconsistent with previous findings suggesting that 
greater gender equality is associated with a reduction in the sex differ­
ence in variability in mathematics (Gray et al., 2019; Hyde & Mertz, 
2009). However, a closer examination of Gray et al. (2019) meta- 
regression results reveal a more nuanced picture. Specifically, while 
their overall findings supported the GMVH, they also observed that some 
sub-components of the GGGI, such as increased educational attainment 
for women, were paradoxically associated with higher VRs favoring boys 
in mathematics and science. This suggests that gains in women's 
educational access might be related to boys' overrepresentation at the 
higher end of performance or might be a proxy for broader improve­
ments in education that affect both sexes. Their results may also be 
influenced by outliers (e.g., Saudi Arabia, VR = 1.43), which can distort 
global trends and strongly affect the regression line.
Finally, according to the gender equality paradox, sex differences in 
STEM-related abilities are more pronounced in countries with higher 
gender equality. Our findings are consistent with this pattern, i.e., we 
found more variability among boys than girls in mathematics, particu­
larly in more developed and gender-equal northern Italian regions. In 
contexts in which boys achieve higher levels of mathematics perfor­
mance, they also show increased variability. This could result from more 
boys excelling in these contexts, which would increase mean perfor­
mance and variation among boys. If these contextual effects dispro­
portionately influence the performance of higher achieving boys, then 
contexts that do not support high achievement should result in lower 
mean scores for boys and less variation among them. These socio- 
cultural and instructional factors could contribute to the sex difference 
in mathematics VRs (Dykiert et al., 2009).
4.2. Reading
Interpreting the findings for reading is less straightforward than for 
mathematics. We found a (log)VR between −0.014 and 0.023 (VR =
0.99–1.02), indicating that girls' variability was about 1 % greater than 
boys', except in fifth grade (VR = 1.02), though trivially so. While prior 
studies reported boys' variability to be 15–16 % higher (Baye & Mon­
seur, 2016; Gray et al., 2019), our findings suggest roughly equal vari­
ability between sexes in Italy. Indeed, the overall pattern across prior 
studies is mixed, with boys' variability being higher in 15-year-olds 
(approximately ninth and tenth grade), and similar variability across 
boys and girls among 10-year-olds (Lynn & Mikk, 2009). Differences 
across reading assessments might be contributing to these mixed results. 
The PIRLS assessment seems to be similar to the INVALSI as both suggest 
small sex differences in reading variability for Italian students (Lynn & 
Mikk, 2009). Beyond Italy, sex differences in VR for reading are small for 
the PIRLS across countries (Schwabe et al., 2015; Tse et al., 2006, 2013). 
The contrasting finding of more variation among boys for the PISA might 
be due to differences in the reading measures relative to PIRLS and 
INVALSI or the older sample for PISA (Brunner et al., 2013; Lynn & 
Mikk, 2009; Machin & Pekkarinen, 2008), but this remains to be 
definitively determined.
Consistent with previous findings, differences in means were asso­
ciated with differences in variabilities (Gray et al., 2019; Nowell & 
Hedges, 1998). Unlike mathematics, an increase in the mean sex dif­
ferences in reading favoring girls was not associated with regional 
development and gender equality, but was associated with an increase in 
boys' variability. However, this moderator, albeit statistically signifi­
cant, accounted for very little of the heterogeneity in VRs. The results for 
grade were consistent across reading and mathematics, that is, grade 
accounted for a very small portion of heterogeneity in VRs.
We found that boys were under-represented at the high end of 
reading performance, indicating that they are less likely than girls to be 
among the top achievers in reading (Giofr`e et al., 2020; Nowell & 
Hedges, 1998; Oakley et al., 2024; Reilly et al., 2015). The magnitude of 
this effect was larger than the over-representation of boys in mathe­
matics, suggesting a particularly pronounced gap in favor of girls at the 
upper end of the reading distribution. At the same time, boys were 
markedly over-represented at the low end of the reading distribution, in 
keeping with findings from the U.S. (Reilly et al., 2019). These findings 
are also consistent with previous research showing that more boys than 
girls are diagnosed with reading disabilities or dyslexia (Moll et al., 
2014; Quinn & Wagner, 2015). It is also worth noting that, while much 
of the literature has emphasized the under-representation of women and 
girls in mathematics, far less attention has been paid to boys' reading 
performance (see also Giofr`e et al., 2020). This is an area where boys 
consistently lag behind girls, particularly at both the highest and lowest 
ends of the achievement distribution. Also, this pattern could represent 
earlier developmental gaps, as well as gender-related motivational and 
behavioral characteristics that disadvantage males in literacy tasks 
beginning in primary school (Logan & Johnston, 2009; Retelsdorf et al., 
2015).
Unlike mathematics, there was no relation between geographical 
gradient and reading VRs. This result indicates that regional differences

that some components of the GGGI, such as political empowerment, 
were associated with increased girls' variability in literacy, further 
suggesting that patterns in reading variability are less uniform across 
countries and might be more dependent on the specific assessment than 
for mathematics.
Finally, the reading results show a different pattern in relation to the 
gender equality paradox. Specifically, we did not find heterogeneity 
among Italy's territories in differences in variability between boys and 
girls, unlike mathematics achievement. These results are consistent with 
previous research showing that sex differences in variability could 
depend on contextual factors. For example, Feingold (1994) reported 
that VRs did not consistently favor boys across nations, and sometimes 
girls' variability was even greater. Similarly, Makel et al. (2016) found 
greater girls' representation in the upper tail of verbal ability distribu­
tions in both Asian and Western countries.
4.3. Limits and future research
Despite its contributions, this study has some limitations. INVALSI 
assessments are carried out every year in Italy, but does not include all 
grades. Also, the INVALSI assessment varies each year. Even though 
scores are computed via robust statistical methods it is possible that even 
small differences in items (for example, items advantaging boys over 
girls) could distort the results from one year to the next. Finally, the 
INVALSI assessment only includes data from a single country, but even 
so the patterns are generally consistent with and add to the broader 
literature.
Future research may consider investigating other subjects, such as 
science, and specific skills, such as writing, as prior studies suggest boys 
are more variable in these areas (Baye & Monseur, 2016; Gray et al., 
2019; Reilly et al., 2015, 2019). Also, it would be interesting to study sex 
differences in variability in second language learning, which is English 
in most of the Italian schools.
Socio-cultural and economic factors, such as the GDP and the GGGI, 
which are related to sex differences in economic and political partici­
pation, have been associated with sex differences in mathematics and 
reading (Giofr`e et al., 2020; Gray et al., 2019; Stoet & Geary, 2018). 
Comparable data were not available for any given Italian province, 
although our geographical proxy for them suggests they might be 
important for understanding VRs for mathematics but possibly not for 
reading. Prior results from previous INVALSI reading data did not sug­
gest an association between socio-cultural factors (i.e., the geographical 
gradient) and mean sex differences in reading, as differences in means 
are substantial across Italian regions (Giofr`e et al., 2020).
5. Conclusions
The extremely large sample across grades and geographical regions 
provided a robust test of the GMVH. The results suggest that greater 
male variability is not found in all domains. Here, boys' variability was 
consistently greater than girls' variability for mathematics, especially for 
more developed and higher performing regions of Italy, but this pattern 
did not extend to reading. The results for mathematics are in keeping 
with a recent study of VRs for subtests of the WISC (Giofr`e et al., 2024), 
whereby the sex with the highest mean subtest scores was also the more 
variable sex for that subtest. It is not clear why this same pattern did not 
emerge for reading, but these findings and those of Giofr`e et al. (2024)
indicate that greater male variability is not a ubiquitous phenomenon 
nor is it consistently linked to economic development or gender 
equality.
