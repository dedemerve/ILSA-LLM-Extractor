feedback in digital reading performance: Evidence from 32 OECD countries

Background: A growth mindset is one’s belief that intelligence is malleable. Extensive research has shown that a
growth mindset can enhance students’ academic performance. However, its mechanism on students’ digital
reading performance has been little explored.
Aims: This study examined the correlation between perceptions of teachers’ and parents’ process feedback and
adolescents’ growth mindsets and the relationships among feedback, a growth mindset, and digital reading
performance.
Sample: Data on 250,912 fifteen-year-old students from 32 Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD) countries who participated in the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2018 were drawn.
Methods: The data were analyzed through the use of multilevel mediation.
Results: The results showed that in most countries, perceptions of teachers’ and parents’ process feedback used
positively predict adolescents’ growth mindsets, which in turn positively predict digital reading performance and
mediate the relationship between perceptions of feedback and reading performance.
Conclusions: Educators and parents should intentionally integrate process-oriented factors into their feedback to
help students realize their academic potential. Cultural heterogeneity should also be considered concerning the
role of a growth mindset.

1. Introduction
The concept of mindset has gained much attention from scholars due
to its positive association with academic achievement and learning in
school and beyond (e.g., Aronson et al., 2002; Blackwell et al., 2007;
Sisk et al., 2018). Recent meta-analyses have made this approach even
more popular due to the variability in and question of its evident
effectiveness (e.g., Burnette et al., 2023; Macnamara and Burgoyne,
2023). Previous studies have shown that adult feedback that attributes
children’s failures to effort, strategies, or other process-oriented factors,
known as process feedback, contributes to the development of a growth
mindset (e.g., Gunderson et al., 2013). Nevertheless, the impact of subtle
forms of process feedback and their connection to academic achievement requires further research. Additionally, research on the role of a
growth mindset in the field of reading is limited by inconsistent findings
(e.g., Cho et al., 2021; McCutchen et al., 2015). Considering the

increasing importance of digital reading, understanding the correlation
between a growth mindset and digital reading is crucial. Based on these
research gaps, this study sought to examine the relationship between
specific types of process feedback from teachers and parents and adolescents’ mindsets. The study also aimed to determine whether a growth
mindset leads to greater digital reading achievement and mediates the
relationship between adults’ process feedback and adolescents’ digital
reading achievement.

(1988) to explain the underlying personality and motivational variables
that drive people to respond differently to setbacks. It proposes that

According to Dweck and colleagues, process feedback tends to shape
children’s mindsets through its motivational function (e.g., Cimpian
et al., 2007; Dweck and Leggett, 1988). Specifically, it provides task
strategies to change erroneous beliefs, which facilitates the attribution
to unstable abilities in case of failure and the belief that performance can
be improved through effort. It also provides elaborate messages about
one’s strengths, weaknesses, and strategies toward learning goals, which
support students’ feelings of competence and maintain their motivation
amid failure.
Haimovitz and Dweck’s (2017) model also implies the importance of
understanding the disconnection between the mindsets that adults
believe they hold and the mindsets that children perceive adults to hold.
Some related research has referred to the disconnection as a “false
growth mindset”, which means that educators who claim to have a
growth mindset might exhibit behaviors that instead communicate a
fixed mindset (Dweck and Yeager, 2019). As the “false growth mindset”
phenomenon can cause misleading statistical results in studies in which
adults’ reported mindsets are used as predictors of students’ mindsets
and fail to reveal the most direct predictors of students’ mindsets, it is
essential to investigate this phenomenon from students’ perspectives,
especially the common features of process feedback in their eyes, such as
positive comments on effort and promoting persistence (e.g., Murphy
and Dweck, 2010; Rissanen et al., 2019).
Despite rich findings about process feedback, previous empirical
research has investigated the association between limited types of process feedback, such as praise (e.g., Mueller and Dweck, 1998) and criticism (e.g., Kamins and Dweck, 1999), and mindset. While these are
typical forms of feedback that stress the learning process, several subtle
forms that might better reflect the fundamental nature of process feedback, such as perceived teachers’ willingness to help and interest in the
process, and parental encouragement of confidence amid frustration,
have rarely been studied. More research is needed to determine how
adults can effectively communicate growth mindset beliefs.

individuals differ in their beliefs about the malleability of intelligence:
people with a growth mindset believe that their intelligence is flexible
and can be enhanced through effort and problem-solving strategies,
whereas those with a fixed mindset feel that their intelligence is mostly
unchangeable (Dweck and Leggett, 1988). Different beliefs about the
nature of intelligence can lead to different learning patterns, which can
affect academic achievement in school (e.g., Costa and Faria, 2018).
2.2. Teachers’ and parents’ process feedback and adolescents’ growth
mindset
By definition, feedback is the information provided by an agent, such
as a teacher, parent, or peer, about the discrepancy between current and
desired understanding or performance (Hattie and Timperley, 2007).
Different psychological perspectives and approaches have been used
(Winstone and Nash, 2023) to explore the taxonomies of feedback, with
the important goal of exploring what makes feedback effective (Panadero and Lipnevich, 2022). Process feedback, as opposed to
grade-oriented or outcome feedback, has been found to have the most
beneficial effects on helping individuals reduce discrepancies and
positively impact the learning process (e.g., Butler and Winne, 1995;
Earley et al., 1990; Harks et al., 2014; Lim et al., 2021). Specifically,
process feedback focuses on specific learning and performance processes; refers to an individual standard; attributes performance to unstable abilities; and provides elaborate cues about one’s strengths,
weaknesses, and supportive task strategies to change incorrect beliefs
and move toward learning goals (Harks et al., 2014).
On the other hand, a considerable amount of literature has delved
into how mindset is fostered in home and school settings, focusing on
parent- and teacher-related factors. While some research reveals a direct
positive association between adults’ mindsets and adolescents’ mindsets
(e.g., Mesler et al., 2021), other researchers have found that adults’
mindsets do not always shape children’s mindsets (e.g., Haimovitz and
Dweck, 2016). Instead, extensive findings suggest that it is adults’ behaviors that help children understand outcomes in terms of behavioral
or trait factors and predict children’s mindsets (e.g., Cimpian et al.,
2007; Gunderson et al., 2013).
In a recent study, Haimovitz and Dweck (2017) provided a more
refined picture of where children’s mindsets originate. They proposed
that in response to children’s performance, adults’ beliefs about whether

improves their digital reading performance. Specifically, three hypotheses were formulated:
Hypothesis 1. Students who frequently perceive process feedback
from teachers and parents are more likely to hold a growth mindset.

Numerous studies have shown that mindset can impact children’s
judgments and reactions to academic events (Dweck et al., 1995),
thereby influencing children’s performance at school (e.g., Costa and
Faria, 2018). Initially, scholars found those who view their intelligence
as malleable tend to have a mastery-oriented reaction to setbacks,
striving to improve and persisting despite failure, whereas those with a
fixed mindset often exhibit a helplessness pattern characterized by
avoiding challenges and reducing remedial effort following difficulties
(Dweck and Leggett, 1988). A study conducted by Blackwell et al.
(2007) showed that response strategies and other motivational factors
mediate the relationship between 7th graders’ mindset beliefs and their
mathematics grades. Similarly, Karlen et al. (2019) discovered that a
growth mindset is weakly yet positively related to academic achievement through adaptive motivational patterns characterized by high
mastery orientation and low work-avoidance goals. Moreover, Filippello
et al. (2020) found that perceived teacher psychological control can
predict students’ school learned helplessness, which negatively predicts
academic achievement, whereas perceived teacher autonomy support
can predict students’ school mastery orientation, which positively predicts academic achievement. This finding confirms that teaching practices affect students’ achievement through their response patterns,
suggesting a possible mechanism through which mindset beliefs act as
mediators. As research expands, findings on learning-related outcomes
boosted by a growth mindset, such as self-regulated learning (e.g., Bai
et al., 2021), resilience (e.g., Calvete et al., 2022), and wellbeing (e.g.,
Schroder et al., 2017), become more comprehensive. These
mindset-resulting coping strategies might help explain the connection
between mindset and academic achievement (e.g., Blackwell et al.,
2007). Nevertheless, limited and varied effects of mindset on students’
learning have also been suggested. A recent review and meta-analysis
study by Macnamara and Burgoyne (2023) challenged the favorable
findings of a series of previous studies, attributing them to poorly
designed interventions, reporting flaws, and bias. Another meta-analysis
(Burnette et al., 2023) revealed a significant variation in effectiveness,
indicating that positive, null, and negative effects of mindset intervention are to be expected. The literature suggests that it is crucial to
interpret the estimated relationship with caution due to the limitations
of study designs and to recognize what heterogeneity can add to the
theory.
In addition, research on the correlation between a growth mindset
and reading achievement has been limited, and there is little consensus.
Some studies have shown no or inconsistently significant correlations (e.
g., McCutchen et al., 2015; Vernucci et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021), while
others have shown positive correlations (e.g., Cho et al., 2021; Khajavy
et al., 2021; Petscher et al., 2017). The inconsistent findings on the association between a growth mindset and general and reading-specific
achievement suggest two possible explanations. First, heterogeneity
exists in the mindset’s association with outcomes across cultures (Yeager
and Dweck, 2020), necessitating further research with larger and more
diverse samples. Second, the association could be stronger in subjects
such as math and science, for which improvement is more easily
attributed to effort, than for reading, where improvement might be
cognitively related and more difficult to achieve. Since digital reading
requires both navigation effort and cognitive skills (Naumann and
Goldhammer, 2017), it could be correlated differently.

Hypothesis 2. Students who hold a growth mindset are more likely to
have better digital reading performance.
Hypothesis 3. A growth mindset mediates the relationship between
students’ perceptions of teachers’ and parents’ process feedback and
digital reading performance.
3. Methods
3.1. Data
This study used data from the latest round of the PISA (URL: https
://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/2018database/), a triennial large-scale
assessment that tests secondary school students’ performance in
reading, mathematics, and science worldwide. A total of 250,912 students from 9,923 schools in 32 OECD countries whose data were
available for the study’s variables of interest were selected as the sample, given that similar economic status of OECD members helps enhance
the comparability of the results (e.g., Chen and Hu, 2020; Hu and Wang,
2022).
The reading assessment of the PISA 2018 employed a scenario-based
approach that covered scenarios in which students read a series of
thematically related texts and completed single- and multiple-text tasks.
These tasks ranged from traditional comprehension items to more
complex tasks such as searching for and selecting relevant text, synthesizing and integrating texts, and corroborating multiple-text information (OECD, 2019a). The texts were selected from various sources,
such as textbooks, literature, policy documents, e-mails, and blogs. The
sample students in this study all participated in a computer-based
assessment that included a 1-h reading test. To maintain the richness
of the test content despite time constraints, the students were randomly
assigned different electronic test forms. Each form covered a portion of
the full set of tasks, with overlapping content. To directly compare the
scores across groups of students who completed different question
forms, the PISA employed a scale to position students’ proficiency reflected by their scores based on item-response-theory models. Student
scores were scaled to fit a normal distribution, with a mean of approximately 500 score points. This allows effective comparisons between
scores across domains, countries, and years (OECD, 2019b).
The PISA data feature a three-level hierarchy, in which students are
nested within schools and schools within countries. To understand the
proportion of variance in the dependent variable at the school level, an
interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was estimated for each country
using the lavaan package in R (Rosseel, 2012) and is provided in the
supplementary materials (Table S1). The ICCs for 31 of the 32 countries
are above.10, ranging from.14 to.58 (except for Finland), indicating the
appropriateness of applying a multilevel model according to the general
principle (Snijders and Bosker, 2012). The multilevel mediation model
was run for each country separately.
To investigate the mediating role of a growth mindset, a multilevel
mediation analysis was adopted. Structural equation modeling (SEM)
was selected as the modeling approach for this study because it can
handle hierarchical data and provides goodness-of-fit indices that help
identify the best model. The analysis was performed using the sem
function in the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) in R (R Core Team,
2019).

This study selected six variables from the PISA 2018 reading

This study used two independent variables, perceptions of teachers’
process feedback and perceptions of parents’ process feedback, coded as
TEACHSUP and EMOSUPS, respectively. Both variables were derived
using item response theory (IRT) (OECD, 2022) from the PISA. The
TEACHSUP was derived from four items measuring teachers’ process
feedback, (e.g., “The teacher helps students with their learning”). The
items were rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (every
lesson) to 4 (never or hardly ever). The values were reverse-coded so that
higher composite values denote a greater frequency. The EMOSUPS was
derived from three items (e.g., “My parents support me when I am facing
difficulties at school”). The items were rated on a four-point Likert scale
ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The scale reliabilities of the indices are provided by the PISA (OECD, 2022) and are
presented in Table S3 in the supplementary materials. As the table
shows, all the values are above.80, suggesting good internal consistency
for each scale within the 32 countries. The PISA implements a quality
assurance mechanism to ensure the reliability (OECD, 2022) and validity (Artelt and Schneider, 2015; Stadler et al., 2020) of the data.
Notably, these two latent constructs were measured using indices
that were constructed by a range of measures rather than using their
latent scores, for two reasons. First, the indices were meaningful synthetic high-level descriptive indicators (OECD, 2022) that provide a
convenient summary of different dimensions of process feedback. This
allows a quick comparison of the initial level of general perceived
feedback across countries and informs policy decisions. Second, the PISA
used psychometric techniques to assess the internal consistency and
cross-country invariance of item parameters to ensure the comparability
of latent constructs across countries (OECD, 2022) in the construction of
these derived variables, which helps enhance the validity of the results.
The dependent variable was students’ digital reading literacy, which
was defined as their ability to understand, use, evaluate, reflect on, and
engage with digital texts to achieve their goals, develop their knowledge
and potential, and participate in society (OECD, 2019a). All sample
countries participated in the computer-based assessment. Students’
digital reading literacy was not measured by actual test scores but by
random draws from a probability distribution for students’ reading literacy, known as plausible values (PVs) (OECD, 2009). The database
includes 10 PVs. Using one PV instead of ten PVs can produce similar
(OECD, 2009) and valid results (e.g., Bernardo, 2021; Hu and Yu, 2023;
Spiezia, 2010; Trinidad, 2020; Yu and Hu, 2023) for large samples;
therefore, this study used the first PV (PV1READ) as the dependent
variable.
The mediating variable was growth mindset (GM). The PISA 2018
used a single item to assess growth mindset, which aligns with the
traditional assumption that two mindsets are mutually exclusive; however, this assumption is currently disputed. GM was measured by one
item asking whether intelligence is something that one cannot change

very much. The items were rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging
from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The values were reversecoded so that higher scores indicate stronger growth mindsets. The
validity of single-item measures has been confirmed (e.g., Rammstedt
and Rammsayer, 2002) despite their limitations (OECD, 2021).
Furthermore, the single measure of growth mindset employed by the
PISA 2018 has been found to have acceptable psychometric properties
concerning reliability, comparability, and validity and can serve as an
ultrashort validated measure of growth mindset for use in large-scale
settings with severe time constraints (Rammstedt et al., 2022). The
present study treated GM as a continuous variable following previous
studies (e.g., Bernardo et al., 2021; Hwang et al., 2016).
To eliminate the influence of extraneous variables, this study
controlled for both student gender (Student Gender) and the index
measuring economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS). Previous
research has consistently shown gender differences in digital reading
literacy (e.g., Cheung et al., 2013; Rasmusson and Åberg-Bengtsson,
2015), and some studies have suggested that gender can affect students’
perceptions of feedback (e.g., Carvalho et al., 2014; Nicaise et al., 2006;
Sortkær, 2018). Therefore, “Student Gender” was transformed into a
dummy variable to prevent multicollinearity (Hardy, 1993). Likewise,
ESCS can also impact the amount of perceived feedback via differential
treatment by teachers and/or between-student differences in the
perception of feedback, as found in Sortkaer’s (2019) research.
Furthermore, ESCS has been found to be significantly associated with
reading literacy in multiple studies (e.g., Koğar, 2021; Morales Silva
et al., 2011). In this study, ESCS is derived from parents’ educational
levels, parents’ highest occupational status, and possessions at home
(OECD, 2022).
3.3. Data processing
First, missing data were imputed using the expectation maximization
(EM) algorithm because a small proportion of the data were randomly
missing. The EM algorithm is a popular iterative approach that estimates
parameters by repeating the expectation (E) step and the maximization
(M) step until convergence (Dempster, Laird, and Rubin, 1977). It is easy
to perform and can produce unbiased and efficient estimates when the
missing mechanism is missing at random (MAR) or missing completely
at random (MCAR) (Dong and Peng, 2013). Due to the rotated booklet
design and the random assignment of students to the booklets (e.g.,
Scherer and Siddiq, 2015), the values obtained from the PISA reading
test were assumed to be randomly missing. Student gender did not have
missing values and was not included in the imputation procedure. The
other five continuous variables were imputed using the software IBM
SPSS Statistics 26.0 and were transformed into Z scores in SPSS, with a
mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Correlation matrices between
variables were computed to quickly show the bivariate correlations
between pairs of variables and to diagnose multicollinearity. All correlation coefficients were less than.80, and the correlations between the
independent variables and the dependent variable were mostly significant, indicating that they could be used for further analysis (Field,
2009). Correlations with nonsignificant coefficients were further
examined in a later regression analysis in which other variables were
controlled for. Details about the correlation matrices for the 32 countries
can be found in the supplementary materials (Tables S4–S35).

Y through M is a2b. The total effects of X1 and X2 on Y are the sum of

4. Results
The study constructed a multilevel structural equation model using
maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to calculate the parameters. The
goodness-of-fit indices of the models for the 32 countries showed that all
the models were saturated, with zero chi-square and zero degrees of
freedom (see Table S36 provided in the supplementary materials),
which means that the model perfectly fit the data and that the fit indices
could no longer be used to evaluate the model. Therefore, one should
focus on the path coefficients among variables, as suggested by previous
studies (e.g., Steeger and Gondoli, 2013). Regarding the testing of
mediation, Baron and Kenny’s (1986) causal steps approach is a classic
method widely applied, yet it has been argued that the total effect
should not be used as a “gatekeeper” for the testing of mediation (e.g.,
Hayes, 2009; Shrout and Bolger, 2002). The study followed the new
method proposed by Zhao et al. (2010) and used a significant coefficient
of path ab as evidence of mediation.

5.1. Hypothesis 1: The correlation between perceptions of teachers’ and
parents’ process feedback and growth mindset
Our findings showed that in over 56% of the 32 countries, teachers’
process feedback perceived by students significantly promoted the formation of students’ growth mindsets. This positive correlation is
consistent with Haimovitz and Dweck’s (2017) model and aligns with
the findings of Park et al. (2016) and Yu et al. (2022), who found
performance-focused instructional practices and guided inquiry,
respectively, to be related to students’ growth mindset. Therefore, professional development that influences teachers’ beliefs (e.g., Stephens,
Rubie-Davies, & Peterson, 2022) should pay more attention to teachers’
understanding of mindset in addition to other beliefs, such as
self-efficacy (e.g., Jerrim et al., 2023).
This result also proves the positive correlation between implicit
forms of teachers’ process feedback and mindset development, consistent with previous research on growth mindset pedagogies (e.g., Rissanen et al., 2019; Sun, 2018). The variable TEACHSUP comprises four
items: teachers showing interest in every student’s learning, providing
extra help, assisting with learning, and continuing teaching until students understand. These practices convey teachers’ willingness to help
students and teachers’ beliefs that failure and effort are integral to the
learning process, which might help students view setbacks as opportunities for improvement and develop a growth mindset environment.

This means that parents can positively interfere by reacting constructively to children’s learning. This approach might help compensate for

mindset. Therefore, educators should consider not only the potential
heterogeneity in the effects of a growth mindset across cultural contexts
(Yeager and Dweck, 2020) but also the contextual predictors of a growth
mindset.
The differences in mediation effects across countries could be
explained by two reasons. First, cultural variations might limit the
impact of a growth mindset, especially in cultures that already emphasize hard work and leave little room for a growth mindset to facilitate
study time (Yeager and Dweck, 2020). This calls for additional research
into the interplay between cultural factors and mindset effects. Second,
countries with non-significant mediating effects of teachers’ or parents’
process feedback are found to have higher ICCs than countries with
significant mediating effects. However, the need for larger sample sizes
in high-ICC situations to achieve the same statistical power (Pan et al.,
2018) might lead to different research findings among countries.
Considering the relatively small correlation coefficients found in the
present study, several PISA-related studies, which used large samples to
examine predictors of academic achievement, have also reported modest
correlation coefficients that align closely with our findings. This consistency across studies strengthens the robustness of our observed correlations and suggests a persistent pattern in such research (e.g.,
Gimenez and Barrado, 2020; Jerrim et al., 2022; Kotte, Lietz, and Lopez,
2005; Lounkaew, 2013).

6. Conclusion
This study examined how contextual factors are correlated with
adolescent students’ growth mindsets and, in turn, with digital reading
performance. The findings showed that perceptions of teachers’ and
parents’ process feedback positively promote students’ growth mindsets
9

Learning and Instruction 90 (2024) 101874

J. Hu and Y. Zhang

in most countries and that growth mindsets mediate the relationship
between perceptions of adults’ process feedback and digital reading
performance.
This study confirmed Haimovitz and Dweck’s (2017) model and
extended our knowledge of the forms of process-focused feedback that
stimulate a growth mindset. The study’s large cross-country sample
contributes solid support for the positive correlation between a growth
mindset and digital reading performance. The findings suggest that
teachers should pay attention to adolescents’ learning processes and
support their students, and parents should focus more on adolescents’
efforts than on their competence. Furthermore, parents might have a
more consistent influence on students’ academic achievement across
countries. To narrow the digital reading achievement gap, traditional
classroom mindset interventions can be combined with ICT-based
teaching for optimal learning outcomes (Bhutoria and Aljabri, 2022).
This study has a few limitations. First, although significant correlations were found in this study, these correlations were mild and could be
due to the influence of the large sample size. Therefore, readers should
understand the limited explanatory power of the statistical results and
carefully interpret their implications for pedagogical practices. Second,

links implied by our model, follow-up longitudinal studies are needed
