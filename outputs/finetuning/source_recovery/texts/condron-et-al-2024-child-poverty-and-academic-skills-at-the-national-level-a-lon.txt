Abstract
Much research has examined school-related sources of cross-national variation in academic skills, but we
know far less about the role that broader socioeconomic conditions play. In this study, we examine how
nations’ child poverty rates associate with their overall scores on international assessments of academic
skills over time. We first establish theoretically why and how child poverty might undermine overall
academic skills at the national level. We then use pooled time-series data on 40 nations that participated
in four recent waves of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) to analyze the impact
of changes in child poverty rates on changes in nations’ overall academic skills (N = 160 nation-years). Twoway fixed-effects models find support for two hypotheses and, to a lesser extent, a third: As child poverty
increases within nations over time, (1) mean academic skills decrease, (2) the percentage of low-skilled
students increases, and (3) the percentage of high-skilled students decreases (for math only). We conclude
by discussing the theoretical and political implications of the findings.

Levels of economic inequality and standards of living vary drastically across societies, even
among the more affluent nations on the planet. Child poverty, for example, has ranged from less
than 5 percent to greater than 20 percent among Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD) countries in the past few years (OECD, 2024). This variation could hold
important implications for societal outcomes, including overall academic skills among students. Results from the 2018 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) reveal
that over 20 percent of students scored in the top two proficiency levels in some OECD countries while less than 10 percent of students demonstrated such high skill levels in others (OECD,
2019a). Accordingly, our research question is: How might child poverty drive overall academic
skills at the societal level?
In this article, child poverty refers to economic deprivation experienced by people under the age
of 18. At the societal level, child poverty is indicated by the percentage of children living in conditions of low income. Children are dependent on adults, of course, so child poverty reflects larger
issues of adult poverty, income inequality, and related policies. We therefore often discuss these
related issues, but we propose that societies’ overall educational outcomes are driven, to some
extent, directly by the degree of economic deprivation that their children experience. We use two
general terms interchangeably to refer to our dependent variable(s); educational outcomes and
academic skills both refer to possessing reading, math, and science knowledge and skills and being
able to apply them to new situations in school and beyond. Both terms also reflect the fact that we
examine multiple measures of our outcome, or dependent variable (skills that education systems/
schools teach and develop).
Since an abundance of research shows that economic deprivation undermines the development of children’s academic skills at the individual level, it is tempting to assume that nations
with higher child poverty rates will have worse overall educational outcomes. Assuming that the
link between economic deprivation and academic skills at the individual level aggregates to the
national level could be problematic, however, if societies vary in how they respond to child poverty and in the extent to which they provide supports to help economically disadvantaged children succeed educationally. Moreover, we lack empirical evidence on this matter; as recently as
2016, Hannum and Xie stated that they “did not find research that addressed explicitly the impact
of national poverty on educational expansion or achievement” (p. 466). Some research has
examined related issues, such as cross-national variations in the effect of socioeconomic status
(SES) on skills (OECD, 2019a), factors that explain why some countries have larger skill gaps
between high- and low-income children compared to others (Chmielewski, 2019; Chmielewski
and Reardon, 2016), and effects of income and socioeconomic inequalities on academic skills
cross-nationally (Condron, 2011, 2013). Still, we lack strong evidence demonstrating that societies’ child poverty rates drive their overall educational outcomes. The best evidence of this link
would come from a longitudinal study showing that societies’ child poverty rates and academic
skills change in concert with one another inversely. Such evidence would hold a clear and important policy implication, too: By reducing child poverty, societies potentially can improve their
overall educational outcomes.
In this study, we explain why and how child poverty might undermine overall academic skills
at the national level and examine this link empirically. Using data from four recent waves of the
PISA, as well as the OECD and the United Nations (UN), we analyze how changes in nations’
child poverty rates associate with changes in overall scores on international assessments of academic skills. Our longitudinal analyses estimate the impact of child poverty rates on societies’
overall levels of reading, math, and science skills and calculate predicted improvements in educational outcomes stemming from various degrees of child poverty reduction.

Background
In this section, we present the theoretical backdrop for our study, explain how we build upon existing work, and contextualize our hypotheses. Establishing why/how nations’ child poverty rates
might shape their overall educational outcomes involves consideration of the individual and societal levels, school-related and out-of-school mechanisms, and impacts of economic deprivation on
the poor and the nonpoor.

Theoretical perspective
Broadly speaking, a conflict theoretical perspective guides our study. Poverty, or economic deprivation, is a manifestation of inequality generated by group competition for access to economic,
political, honorific, and other socially valued assets/resources (Collins, 1971). Bourdieu (1986)
viewed these assets/resources as forms of “capital” and highlighted economic capital as being “at
the root of all the other types of capital” (p. 252) such as cultural and social capital. Central to the
current study are academic skills, which often are viewed as a type of cultural or human capital. To
be clear, an economics-based human capital perspective views educational attainment as important
for an individual’s eventual occupational and economic attainment. In contrast, our study views the
acquisition of academic skills during childhood as partially a product of students’ economic capital
during childhood. In this sense, it is guided by a strand of conflict sociology known as a social
reproduction perspective.
Social reproduction theorists often point to education as an institution that facilitates the perpetuation of inequalities across generations (Bourdieu and Passeron, 1990), but we build on work
highlighting how out-of-school inequalities play a key role in generating the academic skill disparities that help reproduce stratification. This line of inquiry traces back to the Coleman Report in the
United States (Coleman et al., 1966) and continues to the present with studies from various countries pointing to out-of-school environments (i.e. students’ economic backgrounds) and time periods (i.e. before K-12 schooling begins, during summer breaks) as important sources of inequalities
in academic skill development (Downey, 2020; Passaretta et al., 2022; Skopek and Passaretta,
2021). Bradbury et al. (2015) find that skill gaps present at kindergarten entry in Canada, Australia,
the United Kingdom, and the United States parallel family resource gaps and remain fairly consistent as children progress through school. Other studies document the early formation and stubborn
persistence of socioeconomic learning inequalities in Norway (Ribeiro et al., 2023), the Netherlands,
the United Kingdom, and Germany (Passaretta et al., 2022).
Mechanisms. At the individual level, broadly speaking, a social causation perspective views poverty and its related disadvantages as an important source of variation in children’s academic and
other skills. One strand of this work emphasizes poor parents’ inability to make investments that
foster children’s development (e.g. educational materials in the home); another highlights how
poverty produces family stress that undermines children’s well-being (e.g. poor mental health and
marital conflict among parents) (see McLoyd et al. (2016) for an overview). In short, economic
deprivation makes it difficult for parents to provide material and nonmaterial supports that foster
the development of children’s academic skills (Passaretta et al., 2022).
Some scholars argue that the connection between SES and children’s development actually
stems from social selection. From this perspective, parents pass positive qualities such as diligence, skills, and reliability on to their children; since parents who possess those skills earn higher
incomes as a result, it appears as though high SES produces high-achieving children when in reality individual qualities/characteristics do (Mayer, 1997). Other scholars assume that “processes of

both social causation and social selection will operate in producing an association between family
dynamics and SES” (Conger et al., 2010: 14); this is known as an interactionist perspective (Conger
and Donnellan, 2007).
At a more macro level, structural or ecological perspectives highlight the role of larger social
forces. These perspectives emphasize conditions in children’s broader social and physical environments and recognize that poor children experience numerous disadvantages that accumulate in
undermining their development. For example, Evans (2004) laments work that focuses too narrowly on psychosocial processes occurring within families (e.g. negative parenting practices) and
argues that scholars “need to come to grips with the ecological reality of poverty” (p. 88). Compared
to higher-income children, Evans argues, the ecological reality of poor children is more likely to
include air and water pollution, low-quality housing, dangerous neighborhoods, and low-quality
day cares and schools.
Tumin’s (1953) classic article provides an additional conflict lens on inequality—and, by extension, child poverty, the manifestation of inequality on which we focus here. Tumin argues that
when inequality is high, a society is less able to discover, develop, and ultimately benefit from
people who have the potential to make valuable contributions. Extending Tumin’s argument, if a
large share of a society’s population lives in conditions of economic deprivation, more people are
going to face barriers to occupational and economic attainment and lack opportunities to realize
their potential and nurture the potential of their children—stress, food insecurity, poorer physical
and mental health, disadvantaged neighborhoods, fewer educational resources, and so on. A highpoverty society underinvests in a large segment of its population, including its school-aged students, whereas a low-poverty society provides more/better opportunities for its younger people to
develop (Esping-Andersen, 2007). Andreas Schleicher (2023), OECD Director for Education and
Skills, echoes this sentiment in writing,
In sum, all countries have excellent students, but too few countries have enabled all of their students to
fulfil their potential. Achieving greater equity in education is not only a social justice imperative, it is also
a way to use resources more efficiently, increase the supply of skills that fuel economic growth, and
promote social cohesion. (p. 21)

It is important to note that higher-income societies have a greater capacity to invest in the lower
rungs of their stratification systems compared to lower-income societies. Still, not all affluent
nations do so. Some opt to maintain relatively low levels of income inequality and poverty via
significant government intervention in economic matters (i.e. higher taxation and spending on
things like education and unemployment), an approach that Kenworthy (2004) calls “egalitarian
capitalism.” Others opt for less taxation and a more limited welfare state, which enables higher
levels of income inequality and poverty. Indeed, societies vary considerably in the degree of poverty reduction that their systems of taxes and transfers achieve (Sakamoto, 2023; Zuberi, 2004).
Post-tax/transfer poverty rates vary from as low as 7 percent in Denmark and Finland to 18 percent
in the U.S. and to far higher levels in lower-income nations (OECD, 2024). The U.S. achieves the
least poverty reduction among affluent nations (Bradbury et al., 2015; Moller et al., 2003).
Disadvantaged social environments at the bottom of a stratification system thus may reflect an
inability or an unwillingness for a society to keep poverty low.
In sum, more economic deprivation among children in a society is likely to contribute to lower
overall academic skills through mechanisms operating outside of schools. In societies that are
either unable or unwilling to maintain low levels of economic deprivation generally, more children
will experience poverty’s negative impacts via adults’ struggles to provide supports within family
contexts and broader, ecological disadvantages that also undermine children’s development.

The preceding discussion helps make apparent why we might observe a negative influence of
child poverty on overall academic skills at the national level, but we suspect that additional mechanisms also are at play. These mechanisms highlight the importance of examining the link between
child poverty and skills at the national level as something more than an aggregate reflection of
what occurs at the individual level. In other words, the connection between child poverty and skills
at the individual level is not the only reason why nations with higher poverty rates might have
lower overall skill levels.
First, the degree of economic deprivation in a society could affect how its education system
manages the heterogeneity in skills that schools inherit when young children begin formal education and when students return from summer breaks. When child poverty is higher in a society,
its schools face greater nonacademic and academic challenges that in turn undermine their ability to educate students. We know from research on multiple countries that, in general, children
learn more when they are in school compared to when they are not in school (see Passaretta and
Skopek, 2021 for evidence on Germany; see von Hippel et al., 2018 on the United States). Under
conditions of greater economic deprivation, however, schools more often must assist children
who not only are stressed, food insecure, unhealthy, and/or afraid in their surroundings, but also
need remedial instruction to bring their cognitive skills up to the levels of their more economically advantaged peers. In contrast, when minimal economic deprivation is present in a society,
its education system could have a greater ability to boost students’ already-higher skills to even
higher levels.
Second, and related to the previous point, child poverty’s negative impact on educational outcomes extends beyond the individuals experiencing economic deprivation directly. Higher levels
of poverty in a society can affect not only the poor but also the nonpoor. Compared to a society
with low poverty, in a society with high poverty, more nonpoor students are exposed to the unfavorable social conditions accompanying poverty both in school and outside of school. In school,
the remedial instruction stemming from lower skill levels mentioned above will be of greater
necessity in higher-poverty schools/classrooms. This can have a spillover effect on nonpoor students, as teachers are going to be less able to help boost those students’ skills since they need to
dedicate time to remediating poor students’ skills. In their study of school economic segregation
and educational outcomes in Hungary, Hermann and Kisfalusi (2023) suggest several mechanisms
through which attending high-poverty schools might undermine educational outcomes, including
a lack of instructional resources such as highly qualified teachers, lower levels of instruction due
to lower educational expectations of poor students, and more frequent disruptive behavior in the
classroom.
Outside of school, economic deprivation is known to give rise to numerous social problems, such as social disorganization and crime, exposure to which undermines the well-being
of not only the poor but also the nonpoor. Although economic segregation is common, and
increasingly common in many contexts (Musterd et al., 2016), this does not mean that the poor
and nonpoor live completely separate from one another. The social problems that accompany
conditions of economic deprivation still exist to varying degrees within generally higherincome areas and certainly in close proximity to them. As an example, in the United States,
compared to middle-class White people, middle-class Black people more often live in neighborhoods near poor neighborhoods. As a result, their children become exposed to poverty and
are affected by its negative social conditions even though they are not poor themselves
(Pattillo, 2013). More broadly, poverty can generate costs to societies overall. For instance,
the health problems produced by economic deprivation can increase costs of medical care for
the entire society and the lower levels of skills stemming from poverty can undermine economic productivity (Rank, 2005).

A note on relative poverty. The preceding discussion assumes that poverty is absolute—that economic deprivation occurs when a person is living on less income than what is needed to acquire a
set of basic necessities. In cross-national research, however, poverty is a relative concept, the idea
being that people are economically deprived in comparison to their society’s overall income level
or standard of living. Relative poverty thresholds are typically set at half a nation’s median income;
relative poverty is higher when the incomes of larger percentages of people lie below half of
median income. This allows for comparisons of economic deprivation levels between nations and
within nations over time; it does not necessitate establishing absolute poverty thresholds in each
nation.
Given that cross-national comparisons measure relative poverty, it is important to note that relative poverty can shape educational outcomes in and of itself. With relative poverty, the purchasing
power of concern is not basic necessities in an absolute sense but rather goods and services in relation to what others in the society can afford. A family can have its basic needs met but still be
economically deprived and lacking in educational resources compared to others of greater financial
means. This is an especially important consideration given increasing income inequality that has
been driven largely by gains at the tops of income distributions (Piketty, 2014). The rich have risen
higher on stratification ladders, placing greater distance between themselves and everyone else.
Even if they are not poor in an absolute sense, lower-income families are going to be relatively
disadvantaged when it comes to the kinds of things that foster educational advantages—stimulating early childhood environments, strong nutrition, quality housing, safe neighborhoods, extracurricular programs, shadow education, and so on.
Importantly for our analysis, relative poverty varies not only across societies but also within
societies over time. Within-nation fluctuations in poverty can occur as a result of changes in policy
approaches (more vs less generous safety nets), economic changes (periods of economic growth vs
recession), or both. OECD (2024) data illustrate: Canada reduced child poverty from 17 percent in
2015 to under 10 percent in 2021. Similarly, Poland’s child poverty rate was almost 18 percent in
2005 but just 8 percent in 2021. Between 2004 and 2021, Greece saw its child poverty rate first
increase from 13 to 21 percent before declining to 14 percent. Britain reduced its child poverty rate
by 50 percent in the early 2000s (Waldfogel, 2013). During the COVID-19 pandemic, US policymakers passed temporary cash benefits to families with children that reduced child poverty from
21 percent in 2019 to 14 percent in 2021. Upon the expiration of the benefits, child poverty immediately rose to 20 percent (OECD, 2024). These kinds of fluctuations are not present in every
society—Finland, for example, has maintained low child poverty rates ranging from just 3 to 5
percent over the past couple of decades—but in general child poverty rates change considerably
over time within nations.

Extensions and hypotheses
In recent decades, international assessments of academic skills have proliferated, enabling scholars
and policymakers to seek to identify factors that affect overall skill levels and variation in skills.
Much of this work has focused on differences in nations’ education systems (e.g. Chmielewski,
2014), but a growing body of literature highlights the potential role that broader social stratification
plays too (Bradbury et al., 2015; Passaretta et al., 2022; Schleicher, 2023).
Some studies examine individual-level test scores as a function of country-level (and other)
predictors such as gross domestic product (GDP) and income inequality (Chiu, 2010; Chiu and
Khoo, 2005; Chudgar and Luschei, 2009). These studies are concerned with individual-level outcomes. Others treat nations as the unit of analysis and examine links between income inequality/
poverty and overall academic skills (Condron, 2011, 2013; Merry et al., 2020). These studies find

not only that more unequal societies and those with higher poverty rates have lower overall achievement but also that higher degrees of income inequality and poverty are associated with having
higher proportions of low-skilled students and lower proportions of high-skilled students. Although
these studies are more relevant to the current one in that they use nations as the unit of analysis,
they are limited by their reliance on cross-sectional data (they use snapshots of data on a handful
of countries).
We expand this line of inquiry and establish greater empirical leverage on the question of how
child poverty affects overall academic skills at the national level by conducting longitudinal analyses. More specifically, we compile data from four recent waves of PISA, enabling us to link the
within-nation changes in child poverty discussed above to within-nation changes in overall educational outcomes between 2009 and 2018. We test three related hypotheses.
H1: As a nation’s child poverty rate increases, its average level of academic skills decreases (the
child poverty rate has a negative effect on average skills).
First, we expect to find that, in a general sense, a higher prevalence of economic deprivation among
children in a society will reduce its mean scores on the PISA assessments.
H2: As a nation’s child poverty rate increases, its percentage of low-skilled students increases
(the child poverty rate has a positive effect on the percentage of low-skilled students).
Second, we suspect that increases in economic deprivation among children will produce not only
lower mean scores but also higher percentages of students scoring near the bottoms of the testscore distributions. Support for this hypothesis would suggest that economic deprivation affects
lower-skilled students in particular and that lower scores at the bottom drive the declines in average
skills predicted in Hypothesis #1.
H3: As a nation’s child poverty rate increases, its percentage of high-skilled students decreases
(the child poverty rate has a negative effect on the percentage of high-skilled students).
Third, some evidence suggests that child poverty places downward pressure on not only the bottoms of skill distributions but also the tops (Merry et al., 2020). This could occur if, as discussed
above, economic deprivation also affects the nonpoor. It also could result from economic deprivation creating barriers that undermine the ability of students with strong potential to develop academic skills. Support for Hypothesis #3, together with support for Hypothesis #2, would suggest
that economic deprivation also affects higher-skilled students and that lower scores at the top also
drive the decline in average skills predicted in Hypothesis #1.

Data and methods
We analyze data from three sources. The national-level estimates of academic skills come from
PISA; the measures of child poverty and the controls come from the OECD and the UN.
Fifty-six nations participated in each of four recent waves of PISA: 2018, 2015, 2012, and 2009.
Although earlier waves of PISA exist going back to 2000, fewer countries participated in the earliest waves and child poverty data for those years are sparse. Even for 2009–2018, we are unable to
obtain child poverty data for several participating nations. We explored other potential poverty data

sources to no avail (i.e. UNESCO, UNICEF, the World Bank). As a result, we have data on 40
nations for each of the four years for a total N of 160 nation-years. Our N drops to 159 for the reading analyses because we exclude Spain due to concerns with implausible responses on the reading
assessments (but not math or science) reported by PISA (OECD, 2019c). A more recent PISA
assessment did occur in 2022, but we do not include it since it was administered in the immediate
aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic’s many shocks to economic, social, and educational systems
across the globe (which arguably were considerably more far-reaching than the effects of the 2008
financial crisis that preceded our time series).

Dependent variables
Every 3 years since 2000, PISA assesses 15-year-old students’ reading, math, and science skills in
OECD countries and a number of partner countries/economies. PISA assesses students’ abilities to
apply their skills; as the OECD puts it, they assess “what students know and what they can do with
what they know” (OECD, 2019a: 32). PISA places greater emphasis on a specific academic subject
in each wave (e.g. reading in 2018, science in 2015, math in 2012), but it still assesses students in
all three subjects and produces national estimates for all three subjects in each wave. It is worth
noting that although 15-year-olds across the globe have experienced more/less curricular differentiation through tracking systems to varying degrees, evidence suggests that nonschool environments shape skill development regardless of the structure of educational systems. In their
comparative study of Germany, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom, for example, Passaretta
et al. (2022) find that the majority of social-origin gaps observed later in schooling can be explained
by mechanisms in place during the early childhood years. In short, we are confident that our outcome measures can reflect poverty’s influence despite their timing.
Within nations, PISA samples schools and then students within schools toward the explicit goal
of generating national estimates of student educational outcomes. Most of the assessment questions are multiple choice; some are open ended. In addition to overall average scores, PISA reports
percentages of students scoring at several proficiency levels. PISA generates all of these national
estimates of skills using Item Response Theory (IRT) and plausible value methodologies. We
obtain our national-level measures of skills from PISA reports provided on the OECD website
(OECD, 2022a, 2022b, 2022c, 2022d). To be clear, the OECD uses the plausible value methodology and appropriate weights when producing these estimates; we do not analyze or even handle the
individual-level data.
We predict nine dependent variables. For each of the three subjects—reading, math, and science—we examine the mean score, the percentage of low-scoring students, and the percentage of
high-scoring students. The mean scores are relatively straightforward; they are scaled to have an
overall mean of roughly 500 with a standard deviation of roughly 100. The percentages of low- and
high-scoring students are based on PISA’s proficiency levels. For each subject, PISA provides the
percentages of students whose skills reach each of several proficiency levels. Math and science
both have seven proficiency levels (below Level 1, Levels 1–6); reading is the same, but the lowest
level has additional subcategories for more detailed measurement at the bottom of the skill distribution. For all three subjects, the percentage of low-skilled students is the percentage in the lowest
two levels (i.e. at Level 1 or below Level 1) and the percentage of high-skilled students is the
percentage in the highest two levels (Level 5 or Level 6).

math, and science means are all around 490, with low-scoring nations around 400 and high-scoring
nations around 550. Nations vary widely in their percentages of low- and high-skilled students.

Independent variable
We obtain data for our independent variable from the OECD Data Explorer (OECD, 2024). We
measure nations’ child poverty rates as the percentage of children age 0–17 living below 50 percent
of median income after taxes and transfers. We thus capture societies’ overall levels of post-tax/
transfer economic deprivation among children under 18. Age-specific poverty rates are unavailable, so we cannot gauge poverty for 15-year-olds specifically. Still, since we measure the degree of
economic deprivation among children rather than that among all age groups combined, our operationalization of child poverty matches our hypotheses—it measures the extent to which the children of a society experience economic deprivation.
We lag our independent variable by 1 year. Given the years of the PISA outcomes (2018, 2015,
2012, 2009), we measure the child poverty rate for each of the preceding years (2017, 2014, 2011,
2008). This one-year lag ensures that the independent variable is measured temporally prior to the
dependent variables and, in more substantive terms, that we allow time for child poverty’s effects
on educational outcomes to manifest. As seen in Table 1, across all nation-years, the child poverty
rate ranges from 2.9 to 34.1 percent with a mean of 14.5 percent. Not surprisingly, decomposing
the standard deviation into between- and within-nation components reveals that far more of this
variation lies between nations (SD = 6.398) compared to within nations (SD = 1.498). At the same
time, and consistent with our earlier discussion of over-time changes in child poverty within societies, examining trends within each nation reveals a mixture of increasing, decreasing, and relatively stable child poverty rates.

It is difficult to obtain data on factors such as the child poverty rate for numerous nations over
a long period of time. Such data are available quite readily for some nations/years but only sporadically or not at all for others. Therefore, even though the child poverty rate is one of the more common economic indicators reported at the national level, it still is unavailable for many of the nations
that administered PISA between 2009 and 2018. As a result, we are able to analyze 40 of the 56
nations that participated in all four waves of PISA. Even then, we still have to contend with some
missing child poverty data on those 40 nations. We do so by compiling the available annual data
and interpolating the missing numbers for the focal years when necessary. For a handful of nationyears, we calculate the child poverty rate as the average of the nearest prior and subsequent rate.
For example, if a nation is missing a child poverty rate for 2011 but has data for 2010 and 2012, we
take the average of the 2010 and 2012 rates. Table 6 lists the 56 nations that participated in all four
waves of PISA, identifies which are included and excluded from this study, and notes when child
poverty rates were interpolated.

Controls
In addition to our time variables (explained below), we control for changes in gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and total population. Broadly speaking, across the globe, higher standards of
living promote better educational outcomes as more families are more highly educated and better
able to provide resources and stimulating environments outside of school and as school systems
themselves are better resourced (OECD, 2019b). Controlling for GDP per capita accounts for the
influence of any changes occurring in this broad sense. We obtain from the OECD Data Explorer
GDP per capita in constant US dollars using constant purchasing power parities with 2015 as the
reference year. We divide by 1000 to facilitate interpretations of results. The mean of 38.772 in
Table 1 thus indicates that the average GDP per capita among nations/years included in this study
is $38,772.
Our second control is population size. Including this variable allows us to account for the impact
of either increasing or declining populations within nations. Such fluctuations have implications
for the makeup of student bodies within nations, which could become more or less economically
diverse and more or less wide-ranging in terms of skill levels. Population changes also could
reflect migration patterns; for example, if immigrant students with lower socioeconomic statuses
and skill levels make up a greater share of a nation’s student body, this could impact its educational
outcomes (Chmielewski, 2019). The OECD refers users to the UN for population data. We divide
by one million to facilitate interpretation of results; the mean of 40.793 in Table 1 represents an
average population across nations/years of 40,793,000 (driven by a few particularly populous

measured temporally prior to the outcomes.

Analytic strategy
We estimate our models of educational outcomes using pooled time-series models with two-way
fixed effects. The models focus on variation within nations over time; this approach enables us to
examine how changes in the child poverty associate with changes in academic skills within nations.
The fixed-effects models compare nations to themselves, over time, rather than to other nations,
significantly reducing the potential impact of omitted variable bias. The models hold constant
time-invariant attributes of nations by essentially including a dummy variable for each nation. We
also include dummy variables for 2012, 2015, and 2018 (vs 2009, the referent). Thus, our models

Yit = α i + β X it −1 + δ t + ui + eit
The dependent variables, Yit, are the mean, the percentage of low-skilled students, and the percentage of high-skilled students in reading, math, and science for nation i (i = 1–40) at time t (t = 1–
4, for 2009, 2012, 2015, and 2018). The term αi represents the 40 nations’ intercepts. In the next
term, representing the coefficients times the predictors (the child poverty rate, GDP per capita, and
population), t –1 signifies that the independent variables are lagged 1 year. The next term, δt represents the time effects on the outcomes—as noted above, we include dummies for 2012, 2015, and
2018, with 2009 omitted as the referent. Finally, ui is the within-nation error term and eit is the
overall error term. To handle the heteroscedasticity and serial correlation present in our models, we
add to Stata’s “xtreg” command the cluster option (clustered by nation) (see Hoechle, 2007).
We choose fixed effects over random effects for three reasons. First, fixed-effects models are
more appropriate given our goal of examining how nations’ child poverty rates associate with
their overall educational outcomes over time. This question calls for within-nation comparisons
(random effects models would analyze the variation between nations). Second, fixed-effects models provide more stringent hypothesis tests because they rule out the impact of time-invariant
attributes of nations (random effects models would be much more vulnerable to omitted variable
bias). Third, Hausman tests confirmed that fixed effects are preferred over random effects. To
supplement our main findings, we present in Table 7 the results of the random effects models in
order to demonstrate how child poverty associates with educational outcomes between nations.
Tables 2 to 4 present the results of the two-way fixed-effects models of reading, math, and science
skills, respectively.

the percentage of low-skilled students increases by .266 percentage points (p < .10) net of the controls. The child poverty rate has no impact on the percentage of students with high reading skills.

within nations, increases in child poverty undermine average reading skills and contribute to higher
percentages of students with low reading skills.

Considering all of the estimates in Table 5 together helps paint a picture of how changes in child
poverty associate with nations’ overall distributions of academic skills. Looking first at the means,
it is clear that reducing poverty boosts average skill levels. For example, nations with average GDP
per capita, population, and child poverty can expect a math mean of about 486. Moving to the right,
reducing the child poverty rate by one standard deviation (i.e. going from 14.5% child poverty to
8.5%) pushes the math mean up to around 492. As child poverty declines to two standard deviations below the mean, to a very low rate of about 2.5 percent, the math mean increases to 499.
These estimates suggest that a 13-percentage point reduction in child poverty would produce a
13-point increase in the PISA math mean. On the flipside, of course, increasing poverty by 13
percentage points decreases the PISA math mean by 13 points. Compared to math, the predicted
changes in the science means corresponding to two-standard deviation changes in child poverty are
identical (13-point changes) while for reading the effects are slightly less pronounced (11-point
changes).
The increases in the means just discussed appear to stem primarily from reductions in the
percentages of low-skilled students. Table 5 makes it evident that reducing child poverty produces steady declines in the percentages of low-skilled students in all three subjects; the same
aforementioned 13-percentage point reduction in child poverty produces roughly three- to fivepercentage point declines in low-skilled students (depending on the subject). Only for math,
however, do reductions in child poverty boost the percentage of high-skilled students. As noted

As a supplement to our main analyses, we present in Table 7 the results of models estimated
with random effects rather than fixed effects. Instead of focusing on the over-time changes
within nations as our main analyses do, these analyses also model the variation between nations.
As noted earlier, and as would be expected, variation in child poverty rates is greater between
nations than it is within nations over time (the same is true of the variation in the outcomes).
Modeling the between-nation variance is less rigorous, of course, as it opens the door to omitted
variable bias more widely. Still, if analytic approaches examining both between- and with-nation
variance produce similar results, it gives us greater confidence in our overall conclusions.
Indeed, Table 7 shows that nations with higher child poverty rates have lower mean levels of
academic skills, higher percentages of low-skilled students, and lower percentages of highskilled students compared to nations with lower child poverty rates.

Discussion and conclusion
The two-way fixed-effects models presented in Tables 2 to 4 account for within-nation changes in
GDP per capita and population size, time-invariant characteristics of nations, and general overtime trends in PISA scores within nations to examine how changes in child poverty rates associate
with changes in nations’ overall academic skills net of those controlled factors. Our analyses suggest that economic deprivation among children undermines nations’ abilities to cultivate strong
overall educational outcomes.
We find that as child poverty increases within nations over time, mean scores decrease, the
percentage of low-skilled students increases, and, for math only, the percentage of high-skilled
students decreases. These results suggest that child poverty undermines educational outcomes in
general and is especially salient at the lower end of skill distributions. This aligns with other
recent cross-national work finding stronger relationships between income and skills at the bottom of the distribution (i.e. the 50/10 skill gap) compared to the top of the distribution (the 90/50
gap) (Chmielewski, 2019; Chmielewski and Reardon, 2016). Evidence that economic deprivation among children undermines societies’ overall educational outcomes is mounting.
Our within-nation analyses hold important policy implications for cross-national variations in child poverty rates and overall academic skills. The fixed-effects models presented
in Tables 2 to 4 compare nations to themselves over time as a longitudinal analytic approach
that reduces omitted variable bias. Ultimately, however, the results identify child poverty as
a factor that helps explain variation in overall academic skills between nations. In that vein,
the predictions in Table 5, combined with observed child poverty rates, help contextualize
this study’s implications for nations seeking to improve their overall educational outcomes.
Recall that a 13-percentage point reduction in child poverty associates with an 11-point
increase in the reading mean and 13-point increases in the math and science means. For a
number of nations, it would be impossible to reduce child poverty by 13 percentage points
because child poverty is already lower than 13 percent. This is true of Finland, Denmark,
Hungary, Slovenia, Norway, and several other nations (OECD, 2024). At the same time,
nations with higher rates potentially could reduce child poverty by 13 or more percentage
points. Romania, Mexico, Israel, and the United States, for example, all have child poverty
rates around 20 percent (OECD, 2024). For nations such as these with 20 percent child poverty rates, a 13-percentage point reduction in child poverty would give them a rate similar to
that of Norway at around 7 percent and, according to our models, would result in their PISA
means increasing by 11–13 points.

Theoretically, our study speaks to a conflict perspective that views social reproduction through
a lens in which out-of-school inequalities influence inequalities in academic skills. Many have
argued that schools help maintain or even widen broader socioeconomic inequalities across generations, but others have countered that academic skill disparities are driven largely by inequalities
outside of schools (Coleman et al., 1966; Downey, 2020; Passaretta et al., 2022; Skopek and
Passaretta, 2021). In our framework, when a society is either unable or unwilling to maintain low
levels of income inequality and poverty, a larger share of its children will experience economic
deprivation and its negative consequences. Our results showing that child poverty undermines
educational outcomes among 15-year-olds at the national level contribute new insights to this theoretical terrain and suggest avenues for future research.
We suspect that poorer educational outcomes among societies with increasing/higher levels of
child poverty reflect not only what occurs at the individual level but also more macro-level mechanisms. When school systems are populated by large shares of students who are economically
deprived and tend to have fewer academic skills compared to more advantaged students, educators
may struggle to produce strong overall educational outcomes. In line with our first and second
hypotheses, our findings support this view—increasing child poverty produces increases in the
percentages of low-skilled students and decreases in average skills. This suggests that child poverty primarily affects poor students themselves; the means are lower because more students at the
bottoms of the skill distributions struggle. It is also possible, however, that child poverty affects
nonpoor students, too, who experience greater exposure to poverty when its rate is high. If this is
the case, we also would observe declines in the percentages of high-skilled students due to downward pressure throughout the skill distributions. Only the math findings support this view; increases
in child poverty produce declines in the percentage of high-skilled students in math, but not reading or science. We only can speculate as to why the math findings differ. One possibility is that, to
the extent that children’s acquisition of math skills is particularly dependent on schooling, the extra
time that teachers spend remediating lower math skills in the midst of high child poverty is especially impactful (i.e. it inhibits the development of high skills more so for math than for other
subjects).
Of course, the preceding discussion assumes that the child poverty effects are causal, which
leads us to limitations of this study. First, even though the two-way fixed-effects models significantly reduce the potential impact of omitted variable bias, we certainly have not accounted for all
time-varying factors that also might shape nations’ educational outcomes. Of particular interest
would be a measure tapping into over-time changes in educational resources. We attempted to
incorporate such a measure, but in doing so discovered that in 2015 the OECD significantly
changed the survey questions measuring factors such as the adequacy and quality of teachers,
instructional materials, and physical infrastructure. The questions have very different wording in
2105 and 2018 compared to 2009 and 2012. Due to this inconsistency, we are unable to control for
a measure that tracks changes in education systems’ resources. If the PISA survey questions pertaining to school resources remain unchanged going forward, future studies will be able to make
better use of them in longitudinal analyses.
Second, our pool of nations is limited to those for which child poverty data are available. The
lack of child poverty data for many nations/years prevents researchers from utilizing the full potential of the PISA data. As noted above, 56 nations participated in the four waves of PISA that we
analyze, but we could obtain child poverty data for only 40 of them. These are mostly, but not
exclusively, OECD countries (a few OECD countries lacked child poverty data and a few partner
countries had child poverty data). In the end, three nations included in the analyses are non-OECD
countries (Bulgaria, Romania, and Russian Federation). As is evident in Table 6, our pool of nations
is mostly higher-income but does include a handful of middle-income countries. Our inferences

educational outcomes at the national level. We have proposed some possibilities, which we explain
above, but identifying more specific social, economic, and educational conditions that react to
changes in poverty and influence societies’ overall skill levels remains a goal for future research.
Despite these limitations, our national-level, longitudinal analysis of four waves of PISA outcomes—which measure not only what students know but how well they are able to apply what they
know—suggests that child poverty inhibits a society’s production of highly skilled students. More
child poverty is associated with more low-skilled students and lower average skill levels (and, for
math, fewer high-skilled students). These findings are especially relevant to affluent societies with
relatively high rates of child poverty such as the U.S. (Berliner, 2006). We often prioritize schoolrelated policies as the best avenues for improving children’s academic skills, but targeting broader
socioeconomic conditions like poverty is arguably even more important since children spend most
of their time outside of schools.
