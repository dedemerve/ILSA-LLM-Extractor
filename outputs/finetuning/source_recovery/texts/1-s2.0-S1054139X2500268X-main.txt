A B S T R A C T

Purpose: This study estimates multinational trends and correlates of adolescent loneliness.
Methods: Data from the Programme for International Student Assessment administered to
1,267,476 adolescents aged 15e16 across 38 Organization for Economic Cooperation and
Developmentemember countries in 2000, 2003, 2012, 2015, 2018, and 2022 were used. Surveys
included 6 questions on a four-point Likert-type scale about loneliness in the school context
(range: 1e4). Multilevel piecewise regression was used to estimate trends in loneliness scores
during 3 periods: 2000e2012, 2012e2015, and 2015e2022. We estimated associations between
loneliness and the following country-level factors: COVID-19 mortality, public health distancing
measures, internet use, unemployment rate, income inequality (Gini index), and peacefulness.
Models of COVID-19erelated factors were restricted to 2022. The remaining models included 3
levels (individuals, country-years, and countries), allowing estimation of time-invariant betweencountry and longitudinal within-country effects.
Results: We observed a 0.007-point decrease in mean loneliness scores per year from 2000 to 2012
(95% conﬁdence interval [CI]: 0.022, 0.008), although this was not statistically signiﬁcant.
Loneliness scores increased by 0.045 points per year from 2012 to 2015 (95% CI: 0.035, 0.054) and
by 0.017 points per year from 2015 to 2022 (95% CI: 0.008, 0.025). In within-country models, a onehour higher country-level weekly internet use and one percentage-point higher unemployment
rate were associated with 0.012 (95% CI: 0.021, 0.003) and 0.006 (95% CI: 0.011, 0.001) lower
scores per year, respectively. Remaining contextual measures were not associated with loneliness.
Discussion: Loneliness substantially increased from 2012 to 2015, followed by smaller annual
increases until 2022. Within countries, greater increases in unemployment rates and internet use
over time are associated with lower loneliness.

Lonelinessda subjective feeling that arises when “belonginess needs are insufﬁciently met” [1]dis particularly impactful
during adolescence [2]. Loneliness among adolescents may precede worse health behaviors [3], physiological stress responses
[4], and psychological sequelae including depression and other
mental distress [3,5]. Loneliness among adolescents may also
harm cardiovascular health, self-reported health, and risk of
depression longitudinally [2].
Researchers have documented substantial and rising levels of
loneliness among adolescents in the past 2 decades [6,7]. Twenge
et al. identiﬁed a near doubling of loneliness prevalence from
2012 to 2018 among adolescents from 36 countries that participated in the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA) [6]. Updated estimates of loneliness trends are needed,
especially given the rise of social connection as a public health
priority [8] and the scarcity of multinational studies using data
that were collected after the onset of COVID-19 [6,7,9].
Widespread shifts in loneliness may reﬂect broader societal
inﬂuences. Prior research primarily focuses on individual predictors of lonelinessdincluding personality differences, peer
victimization, and sociodemographic factors [10]. These studies
often frame social connection as resting within individual agency
or ﬁxed traits rather than societal conditions. However, grouplevel cultural values, economic conditions, and social norms can
inﬂuence key drivers of social connection, such as trust in others
and cooperation [11e14]. Therefore, considering both individual
predictors and societal factors is critical for the comprehensive
understanding of factors that contribute to loneliness.
The COVID-19 pandemic and contemporaneous economic
and sociopolitical shifts present a unique opportunity to study
recent trends and societal correlates of loneliness [15]. There is
some evidence that the pandemic impacted lonelinessdeither
through increased mortality rates [16], or through social
distancing measures that limited in-person contact [15] and
exacerbated mental health challenges such as social anxiety [17].
The digital world of adolescents has also transformed in the past
2 decades. Researchers have speculated that widespread internet
use has had mixed effects on loneliness by replacing some inperson interactions with online ones, yet offering platforms for
connection [6,18,19]. Other potential societal contributors of
loneliness include economic and sociopolitical conditions such
as income inequality, unemployment, and peacefulness. Higher
income inequality and unemployment rates may erode catalysts
of social connection such as well-being and trust by worsening
economic and health inequities [11e14,20]. Peacefulness may
correlate with loneliness by shaping social embeddedness and, in
times of conﬂict, contributing to trauma or loss [14,21].
We aimed to (1) characterize temporal trends in adolescent
loneliness from 2000 to 2022 and (2) examine country-level
correlates of loneliness by estimating associations (during
selected years) between loneliness and the following group-level
indicators: COVID-19 mortality, public health distancing laws,
internet use, unemployment, income inequality, and peace. Our
ﬁndings may clarify the need to address adolescent loneliness
and generate hypotheses regarding its societal determinants.
Methods
Data source and study population
The study population included 15- and 16-year-old students
who completed the Organization for Economic Cooperation and

Development (OECD) PISA survey. The survey uses a two-stage
stratiﬁed sampling strategy to obtain nationally representative
data on scholastic achievement and well-being. Schools within a
country were grouped into mutually exclusive strata based on
country-speciﬁc factors (e.g. region, urbanicity), and selected
from these strata according to probabilities proportional to the
number of 15-year-olds they enrolled [22]. PISA requires a
weighted school response rate of 85%, and a student response
rate of 80% across responding schools. If a school does not
respond, it is replaced with a predetermined replacement school.
Students within OECD-member countries who were administered the sense of belonging questionnaire in 2000, 2003, 2012,
2015, 2018, or 2022 were considered (N ¼ 1,319,544 students, 38
countries). We excluded students who skipped any loneliness
questions (N ¼ 52,068), leaving a ﬁnal population of 1,267,476
students. As data are publicly available and anonymized, this
study was considered non-human subjects research and did not
require institutional review board approval.
Measures
Loneliness. Each survey included 6 questions about loneliness at
school: “I feel like an outsider (or left out of things),” “I make
friends easily” (reverse-coded), “I feel like I belong” (reversecoded), “I feel awkward and out of place,” “Other students seem
to like me” (reverse-coded), and “I feel lonely.” Responses were
on a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from “strongly
disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (4), with higher scores indicating higher loneliness. In 2022, students were randomly
assigned 5 of the 6 loneliness questions. For each survey year, we
computed a mean loneliness score (range: 1e4) by averaging the
scores of all loneliness questions that were administered. The
six-item scale has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82, whereas the ﬁveitem scales have values ranging from 0.78 to 0.81, depending
on which item is excluded. The scale correlates with negative
effect (r: 0.18e0.46), positive effect (r: 0.29 to 0.47), and life
satisfaction (r: 0.20 to 0.45) [6].
Twenge et al. conducted measurement invariance analyses of
the PISA loneliness scale within countries over time, ﬁnding full
invariance in 12 countries, partial in 12 countries, and failure to
meet conﬁgural invariance in 13 countriesdﬁve of which were
included in the present analysis (France, Iceland, Japan, Mexico,
and South Korea) [6]. To account for variability in measurement
invariance, we will present loneliness trends within each country. To determine whether the scale measures similar constructs
of loneliness across cultures, we also analyzed measurement
invariance across countries.
Country-level correlates of loneliness. We included the following
country-level correlates: COVID-19 cumulative mortality rates,
public health distancing laws, frequency of internet use, unemployment rate, income inequality, and peacefulness. COVID-19
cumulative mortality was deﬁned as the country-level total
deaths per 10,000 population from the start of the pandemic to
December 31, 2022. Data were from the World Health Organization [23]. Public health distancing was deﬁned using the Oxford Stringency Index (hereafter, “stringency index”), a 1e100
scale composed of 9 domains of public health responses to
COVID-19 [24]. To account for policy differentiation by vaccination status, we used a weighted stringency index, which combines indices for vaccinated and non-vaccinated individuals
according to the proportion of the country’s population that is

S.L. Freije et al. / Journal of Adolescent Health 77 (2025) 643e650

vaccinated. We averaged each country’s daily weighted stringency index values across 2022 to produce a country-speciﬁc
annual index.
Weekly frequency of internet use was obtained from 2
questions about weekday and weekend use: “On a typical
weekday [weekend], for how long do you use the internet
outside of school?”. We converted response options from the
original question into approximate hours (“no time” ¼ 0, “1e
30 minutes” ¼ 0.25, “31e60 minutes” ¼ 0.75, “1e2 hours” ¼ 1.5,
“2e4 hours” ¼ 3, “4e6 hours” ¼ 5, and “>6 hours” ¼ 7). Each
students’ typical internet use on the weekdays and weekends
was multiplied by 5 and 2, respectively, and then summed
together. Students’ weekly internet use frequencies were then
averaged within each country-year. Because internet use questions were unavailable before 2012, and changed in 2022, only
2012 through 2018 surveys were included.
We obtained unemployment rate, income inequality (Gini
index), and per capita gross domestic product (GDP) for each
country and year from the World Bank [25]. The unemployment
rate is deﬁned as the average yearly percentage of the total labor
force of a country that is without work and is actively seeking
work. The Gini index measures the extent to which the distribution of income among individuals within an economy deviates
from a scenario of perfectly equal distribution. The Gini index
ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 100 (complete inequality) and
was available from 2000 to 2018.
Peace was measured using the Global Peace Index (GPI), a
measure of the peacefulness of countries developed by the Institute for Economics and Peace [26]. The index is based on 23
qualitative and quantitative indicators spanning 3 domains: societal safety and security, domestic and international conﬂict, and
militarization. The GPI ranges from 1 to 5, with higher scores
indicating lower levels of peacefulness. The GPI was available from
2012 to 2022.
Covariates. Sex, maternal education, and paternal education
were included as covariates. Sex was ascertained using the
question: “Are you male or female?”. PISA does not provide
additional response categories or ask about gender identity.
Maternal and paternal education was drawn from the question:
“What is the highest level of schooling completed by your
mother [father]?”. The question contained 5 response options,
ranging from less than the International Standard Classiﬁcation
of Education (ISCED) level 1 to completion of ISCED 3A. ISCED
level 1 corresponds to primary education. ISCED level 3A corresponds to upper secondary education that is designed to prepare
students for tertiary education.

included countries, weights were applied using methods
described by Luyten (2024) [27]. Brieﬂy, the student weights
provided by PISAdwhich indicate how many students a
respondent representsdwere summed within each country and
year. Standardized weights were then computed using the

Statistical analysis
Time trends of loneliness. Weighted mean loneliness scores were
described for each survey year, overall and by country and world
region. We used multilevel, piecewise regression to estimate
trends of loneliness across survey years within distinct time
periods. The multilevel model contains nested random effects to
model the variation across students (level-1), country-years
(level-2), and countries (level-3). The piecewise regression
technique recognizes that a single linear regression model may
not capture changing trends at different time points. We estimated an intercept and 3 linear slopes for calendar year, one for
each of the following periods: 2000e2012, 2012e2015, and
2015e2022. We selected these inﬂection points based on prior
work describing substantial increases in loneliness from 2012 to
2015, following and preceding smaller changes in loneliness,
using PISA data from several OECD-member countries [6].
Hosozawa et al. similarly observed “a marked escalation [in
loneliness] beginning in 2012.” [7] Our model included weights
and covariates for sex, maternal education, and paternal
education.
To account for the possibility that the school loneliness
scale combines distinct constructs of loneliness, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using alternative combinations
of items to represent the following constructs: perceived
sociability [28], perceived lack of belonging, positively-worded
items, negatively-worded items, and a single-item direct
measure of loneliness (details in supplement) [29].
Country-level correlates of loneliness. Analyses of COVID-19related variables were restricted to 2022 as this was the only
postpandemic year in which PISA surveys were administered. For
this analysis, participants (level-1) were nested within countries
(level-2).
In analyses of country-level correlates for which we had
multiple years of data available, the model used the same
structure as the trends analysis, with students (level-1),
country-years (level-2), and countries (level-3). We applied
Fairbrother extension of multilevel modeling to separately
estimate between-country and within-country associations
between each correlate and loneliness [30]. The betweencountry effects reﬂect variations in loneliness that are associated with absolute, cross-national differences in a countrylevel factor. To include between-country effects, country-level
correlates were grand-mean centered. For example, a country’s unemployment rate was included as a level-3 covariate
for the deviation of the country’s mean unemployment rate
across survey years from the mean unemployment rate in the
full sample. The within-country effects reﬂect how yearly
deviations in a country-level factor from its average are
associated with corresponding changes in loneliness. To
include within-country effects, country-level correlates were
group-mean centered. For example, a country’s unemployment rate for a given year was included as the level-2 deviation from the country’s mean unemployment rate across all
years. We also included a variable for year, centered on the

All models included weights and covariates for sex, maternal

Results
The school loneliness measure demonstrated conﬁgural and
metric but not scalar invariance across countries (Supplementary
Table 1). Descriptive statistics for country-level correlates of
loneliness are available in Supplementary Table 2.
Time trends of loneliness
School loneliness increased from 2000 to 2022, with the
largest increase from 2012 to 2015 (Table 1). This trend was
consistent across world regions except for East Asia (Figure 1). In
2022, loneliness was the lowest in Western Europe and East Asia.
In adjusted models, mean loneliness scores showed a slight and
nonstatistically signiﬁcant decrease of 0.007 points each year
from 2000 to 2012 (95% conﬁdence interval [CI]: 0.022, 0.008)
(Table 2). From 2012 to 2015, mean loneliness scores increased by
0.045 points each year (95% CI: 0.035, 0.054). Loneliness also
increased from 2015 to 2022, but to a lesser extent (B: 0.017, 95%
CI: 0.008, 0.025). These trends were similar when examining
alternative operationalizations of loneliness (Supplementary
Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 3).
Country-level correlates of loneliness
Estimates for associations of country-level COVID-19 mortality and stringency index with loneliness in 2022 were close to
zero and not statistically signiﬁcant (Table 3).
Detailed results of three-level multilevel models, including
variance components at the country, period, and individual level,
are in Supplementary Tables 4e7 Most variance in loneliness was
explained at the individual level, followed by period and country
levels (Supplementary Tables 4e7). Within countries, greater
increases in internet use over time were associated with larger
decreases in loneliness; each additional hour of weekly internet
use within a country was associated with a 0.012-point reduction
in loneliness scores per year (95% CI: 0.021, 0.003) (Table 4).

There was no association between cross-country differences in
internet use and loneliness. A higher unemployment rate was
associated with lower loneliness at within-country (B: 0.006,
95% CI: 0.011, 0.001) but not between-country (B: 0.001, 95%
CI: 0.009, 0.011) levels. The Gini index and GPI were not associated with loneliness at between-country (Gini: B: 0.006, 95%
CI: 0.004, 0.017; GPI: B: 0.080, 95% CI: 0.018, 0.177) or withincountry (Gini: B: 0.006, 95% CI: 0.021, 0.033; GPI: B: 0.132, 95%
CI: 0.080, 0.343) levels. In sensitivity analyses modeling time in
segments (pre-2012, 2012e2015, and post-2015), estimates were
slightly attenuated compared to main models (Supplementary
Table 8).
Discussion
School loneliness increased worldwide from 2000 to 2022,
with most notable increases from 2012 to 2015. When examining
within-country associations, on years when a country’s internet
use and unemployment rates were higher than its average across
the study period, loneliness scores were lower. There was no
association between income inequality or peace with loneliness
at between- or within-country levels. COVID-19erelated indicators (mortality, stringency index) were not associated with
loneliness.
Our trends analysis builds on the seminal work of Twenge
et al. and Hosozawa et al. by incorporating data through 2022
and analyzing trends within distinct time periods [6,7]. Despite
including a different set of countries than analyzed in either of
these studies, we similarly observed that loneliness increased
from 2000 to 2018. Although the increase in loneliness scores
was small relative to the scale’s range, even modest changes in
loneliness have been shown to impact health outcomes such as
depression and self-rated health [3]. Given loneliness is highly
prevalent, small shifts in social connection at the population
level may have meaningful impacts on mental health and other
loneliness-related health outcomes.
Our approach of decomposing societal correlates of loneliness
into between-country and within-country effects limits the
comparability of our ﬁndings with previous studies that used
standard multilevel models [30]. For instance, we identiﬁed a
positive and nonsigniﬁcant relationship between cross-national
differences in internet use and loneliness (i.e., the betweencountry effect), whereas within-country increases in internet
use were linked to lower loneliness. In contrast, previous
multilevel studies have identiﬁed a positive relationship between internet use and loneliness [6]. When applying these
multilevel models in sensitivity analyses, we similarly observed a
positive and signiﬁcant relationship between internet use and
loneliness (Supplementary Table 9). These differences emphasize
the need for careful methodological consideration when interpreting associations between societal factors and loneliness.
The mixed evidence on associations between internet use and
loneliness have contributed to ongoing debates about how digitalization impacts social well-being. Apart from differences across
studies related to modeling choices and exposure/outcome characterization that may have contributed to different ﬁndings,
several conﬂicting thoughts have been put forward. Some argue
that more expansive internet use hinders social connection by
impacting social skills and shifting cultural norms, such as through
the digitalization of interactions that would have otherwise been
in-person [6,18]. These cultural shifts may be particularly impactful during adolescence, a developmental period marked by

cannot prove causation. However, as experimental studies on
cultural change are impossible, this study still provides useful
observations for generating hypotheses related to the societal
determinants of loneliness. Finally, our population was limited to

or remain elevated thereafter. Within countries, lower unemployment rates and lower frequency of internet use in a given
year are associated with higher levels of loneliness. Our ﬁndings
underscore the urgency of addressing loneliness among adolescents. Identiﬁcation of societal-level factors that co-vary with
loneliness may generate hypotheses about large-scale interventions to promote social connection.
