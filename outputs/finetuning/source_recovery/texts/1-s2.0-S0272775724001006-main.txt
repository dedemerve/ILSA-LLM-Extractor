Students’ realistic aspirations about their educational attainment (expectations) are predictive of their efforts,
actions, and future outcomes. Limited evidence suggests these expectations are affected by the macroeconomy;
however, the direction is ambiguous. We combine seven waves of Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA) data to examine this relationship in 38 OECD countries. Using within-country fixed-effect
regressions, we find students have significantly lower educational expectations when GDP growth is low or
negative. Assessing the heterogeneity of these relationships, we find that the expectations of students with belowaverage reading skills or who attend non-metropolitan schools are most strongly affected by the state of the
economy. The results also suggest that when GDP growth is weak, students are significantly more likely to
complete zero homework and arrive late to school, are less likely to participate in extracurricular academic
programs, and expect lower labour market returns to completed education.

1. Introduction
Educational expectations, which are students’ realistic aspirations
about their future educational attainment (Hanson, 1994), are predictive of students’ efforts, actions, and realised educational attainment
(Fruttero et al., 2021; Graham & Pozuelo, 2023; Jacob & Wilder, 2010;
Lekfuangfu & Odermatt, 2022; Mello, 2008). While educational expectations are mostly stable throughout adolescence (Trusty, 2000), students do change their expectations in response to signals from their
environment, such as information about their academic standing (Geven
& Forster, 2021) and grade point average (Andrew & Hauser, 2011).
Therefore, raising students’ educational expectations can be an influential and cost-effective way to encourage upward social mobility
(Cunha & Heckman, 2007; Dalton et al., 2015; La Ferrara, 2019). In
addition, the significance of educational attainment for human capital
development, health, and earnings over the life course (Cheng et al.,
2021; Eide & Showalter, 2011; Heckman et al., 2018) implies that fully
understanding the drivers of educational expectations is important.
One under-studied factor that has the potential to change the
educational expectations of adolescents, and thus their economic

trajectory, is the status of the economy at the time of post-compulsory
schooling investment decisions. Fluctuations in economic conditions
can alter students’ perceptions of the expected returns to further investments in schooling, and the affordability of further education,
consequently impacting students’ future educational plans (Morgan,
1998). However, the direction of the effect of a changing economy on
educational expectations is a priori unclear. For example, students may
perceive that the returns to further education are lower and the costs of
additional schooling too great during a weak or contracting economy,
and subsequently reduce their educational expectations. This is sometimes referred to as the “discouraged student” effect (Tumino & Taylor,
2015). On the other hand, a weak economy may signify poor job prospects for young school-leavers, and so additional schooling is considered
a better option than entering the labour force, thus increasing students’
educational expectations. This has been referred to as the “discouraged
worker” effect (Meschi et al., 2019).
This study builds on recent work examining the impact of macroeconomic conditions on students’ post-compulsory educational decisions (e.g., Clark, 2011; Gaini et al., 2013; Meschi et al., 2019;
Sievertsen, 2016) and the potential scarring effects on college graduates

who enter the labour force during a recession (Huckfeldt, 2022; Kahn,
2010; Rothstein, 2023). However, the impact of economic conditions on
younger students’ educational expectations is unclear. One previous
study has examined whether cross-country variation in economic conditions (measured by GDP) impacts students’ educational expectations
(Salazar et al., 2019). Analysing self-report surveys of 13–14-year-old
students around the time of the Great Recession, Salazar et al. (2019)
provide evidence from random-effects regressions that students’ expected level of educational attainment reduces during economic
downturns. Two single-country studies find weak evidence of a negative
impact of household or local economic conditions on students’ educational aspirations and college expectations (Renzulli & Barr, 2017;
Taylor & Rampino, 2014). In the U.S., Renzulli and Barr (2017) examined the impact of family economic shocks (such as parental job-loss or a
significant reduction in household income) on student and parent
educational expectations. Student expectations of receiving a bachelor’s
degree were found to be marginally negatively affected by family economic shocks. In the U.K, Taylor and Rampino (2014) find little impact
of the local youth unemployment rate on high-school students’ educational aspirations and attitudes.
In this paper, we further examine whether economic conditions
impact students’ educational expectations, using variation in GDP
growth across middle to high income countries. We use GDP growth as it
is a consistent and robust indicator of macroeconomic fluctuations
across countries. Furthermore, given changes in GDP growth (especially
negative growth) are regularly reported in print, broadcast and digital
media, GDP is more salient to adolescents compared to other measures
of economic activity (e.g., wage price index, unemployment rate). We
extend previous work by using within-country fixed-effect regressions,
which are not confounded by unmeasured time-invariant factors, and a
large dataset of countries and students. We also extend the previous
literature by exploring whether economic conditions effect students’
academic behaviours and their expected returns to completed
education.
Our study utilises two decades of data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), which captures the expected
level of educational attainment of students aged 15–16 in 38 Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries.
The PISA data is well suited to answer our research questions as it
provides information about students’ educational and occupational expectations and includes rich background information about students,
parents, and schools. Notably, the students surveyed in the PISA are
slightly older than the students used in prior studies (i.e., Salazar et al.,
2019; Taylor & Rampino, 2014) and are approaching the end of their
compulsory secondary education. Students’ educational expectations
appear more realistic in later secondary years (Jacob & Wilder, 2010),
and it is argued that students only have the cognitive capacity to
properly consider the costs and benefits of their future aspirations after
age 14 (Gottfredson, 2002).
We utilise three different conceptualisations of GDP growth to
examine whether macroeconomic conditions effect students’ educational expectations. Using unstandardised, standardised within country,
and country-specific quintiles (based on 20 years of annual GDP growth
data), we also show the linear and non-linear effects of macroeconomic
conditions. Furthermore, we show the impact of both a recession and
prior poor growth years on students’ educational expectations.
In addition, we analyse whether background characteristics (reading
achievement, sex, school geographical location, and parental education)
differentially impact students’ expectations during changing economic
conditions. Previous research suggests that the educational expectations
of socioeconomically disadvantaged students are more negatively
affected by hard times (Renzulli & Barr, 2017; Salazar et al., 2019;
Taylor & Rampino, 2014). A possible explanation is that disadvantaged
families experience a decrease in household income and financial resources, coupled with a reduction in the availability of tertiary scholarships, during a contracting economy (Mortimer et al., 2014; Tang &

Miller, 2019).
We also test whether students’ educational behaviours (time spent
on homework, participation in extracurricular academic lessons, and
school attendance) vary with changes in GDP growth. Theoretically,
changes to students’ educational expectations should translate into
measurable behaviour change (Dalton et al., 2015). For instance, students with higher educational expectations are likely to complete more
homework (Jacob & Wilder, 2010). These are important outcomes as
educational time investments in adolescence have important implications for students’ cognitive development (Del Boca et al., 2016).
Finally, we explore whether changes in GDP growth impact students’
expected returns to education. Using certain assumptions regarding how
students choose careers, our approach involves estimating the heterogeneous relationship between students’ expected years of education and
the economic ranking of students’ expected occupation at age 30.c As
perceived or expected returns to further education strongly influence
students’ schooling decisions (Jensen, 2010), how students’ expected
returns to education vary with macroeconomic conditions is an important mechanism to explore.
Our results show that a one standard deviation decrease in GDP
growth reduces students’ educational expectations by almost one-fifth of
a year. Allowing GDP growth to be non-linear, we find that student’s
education expectations are substantially higher during periods of relatively high GDP growth compared to periods of poor growth. Past economic performance also appears important: expectations significantly
decrease after experiencing two or three years of weak GDP growth. The
GDP growth effect on students’ educational expectations varies with
students’ reading achievement and the location of their school. The
behavioural response associated with these changes in expectations is a
reduction in homework hours and extracurricular academic lessons, and
an increase in the likelihood of arriving late to school. A possible driver
of these effects is reductions in students’ expected returns to education.

further background on educational expectations and schooling decisions. Section 3 describes the data used. Section 4 provides the estimation strategy. Sections 5, 6 and 7 detail the research findings. Section
8 discusses the findings and concludes.
2. Background
During adolescence, students establish expectations and aspirations
for their future (Steinberg et al., 2009). Expectations incorporate a
probabilistic account of possible attainment, whereas aspirations refer
to the realm of possible options or something idealistically desired
(Fruttero et al., 2021; Gottfredson, 1981; Hanson, 1994; La Ferrara,
2019). Expectations can be thought of as a lower bound of aspirations
(La Ferrara, 2019). Importantly, individuals respond informatively to
questions eliciting their expectations for future events (Manski, 2004).
Previous research suggests that educational expectations are highly
predictive of realised educational attainment (Jacob & Wilder, 2010).
Furthermore, there is evidence that interventions, such as providing
students with information about the benefits of further education and
the availability of scholarships, increase students’ educational expectations and their future outcomes (Avitabile & de Hoyos, 2018; Bleemer
& Zafar, 2018; Carlana et al., 2022).
At age 15, many students are approaching the end of compulsory
schooling and are considering future schooling or labour market entry.

c
To our knowledge, there is little empirical exploration of why students’
educational expectations may change in response to signals from the economy.
Perhaps the most relevant research is that by Meschi et al. (2019). In this study,
the authors find that higher expected returns to education significantly predict
students’ actual enrolment in further education, especially for males. However,
expected returns to education are derived by the researchers and not based on
student self-report.

Based on Becker’s (1964) model of human capital investment, rational
choice theories of educational decisions propose that young people
consider the costs, the value or utility (i.e., benefits), and the likelihood
of success in further education, and choose the educational path that
carries the highest expected utility (Breen & Goldthorpe, 1997; Morgan,
1998; Trebbels, 2015).d Students form educational expectations based
on these preferences (Zimmermann, 2019). When deciding whether to
pursue post-secondary education, costs may include direct costs (i.e.,
tuition fees, textbooks, student accommodation) and opportunity costs
(i.e., forgone earnings via labour market entry). In terms of benefits, the
expected (ex-ante) returns to schooling influence students’ schooling
decisions (Jensen, 2010). For example, Schweri and Hartog (2017) show
that students who expected greater returns to studying nursing were
more likely to enrol in a nursing course. Expected lifetime earnings,
costs, job security, autonomy, enjoyment, and prestige of the chosen
career path also contribute to the heterogeneity in schooling decisions.
The impact of economic conditions on students’ educational expectations is a priori unclear. For instance, during periods of economic
contraction, when GDP growth is low or negative, there may be a
shortage of job openings, increased layoffs, and stagnant wages, which
reduce the possible returns to labour market entry. When the local unemployment rate is high, students may perceive limited work opportunities and consequently the opportunity cost of further education
decreases (Archer & Yamashita, 2003). Additionally, adolescents may
have more incentive to pursue tertiary education to attain the advanced
skills necessary to succeed in a weak labour market (Aronson, 2017;
Taylor & Rampino, 2014), or delay finishing their schooling until the
economy is growing again and there are more jobs available (Alam &
Mamun, 2016). This theory has been called the discouraged worker
effect (Meschi et al., 2019), the insurance effect (Tumino & Taylor,
2015), and the parking lot hypothesis (Ballarino et al., 2013), and posits
that students increase their educational expectations during times of
economic contraction.
Alternatively, costs of further education may rise during economic
contractions and the perceived returns on investment in higher education may decrease. Poor economic conditions may result in cuts to tertiary education funding and the number of available scholarships, which
increases the direct cost of further education (Jackson et al., 2021).
Further, family and personal income may reduce due to lost jobs or
reduced working hours during economic contractions (Tang & Miller,
2019) such that education becomes unaffordable (Christian, 2007).
Perhaps most importantly, young people’s expected returns to further
education may decline during economic contractions because they
expect obtaining a (good) job in the future will remain difficult (Meschi
et al., 2019; Taylor & Rampino, 2014). This theory is called the
discouraged student effect (Tumino & Taylor, 2015). As the costs of
education increase and the perceived benefits of additional schooling
decrease, this theory suggests that students decrease their educational
expectations in line with a contracting economy.

PISA has the advantage of measuring student achievement and expectations over a large timespan and across numerous OECD and partner
countries. Through student, school and parent surveys, the PISA gathers
rich information about student, school and family characteristics (see
Turner & Adams, 2007, for further details about the PISA procedure).
We restrict our analysis to 38 OECD countries (see Appendix
Table A1 for the complete list). The OECD countries were chosen as they
are economically similar and are most consistently surveyed in each
PISA iteration. Our main estimation sample comprises over 800,000
student observations.
The primary variable of interest is students’ educational expectations, measured in 2003, 2009, 2015 and 2018. In the PISA student
questionnaire, students are asked what education level they expect to
complete, choosing from International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) levels that range from Level 2 (lower secondary education) to Level 6 (second stage of tertiary education leading to an
advanced research qualification). The students are provided countryspecific examples of relevant education levels following the ISCED
levels. We convert students’ answers into a continuous measure of their
expected years of schooling. See Appendix Table A2 for more details.
Our estimation strategy uses within-country variation in real GDP
growth rates over time. The variation in GDP growth over 20 years
(1998 to 2018) can be seen in Fig. 1. We use GDP growth to measure
economic conditions at the country level as it is a more consistent and
robust economic indicator of macroeconomic fluctuations across countries than the unemployment rate, which can suffer from measurement
error (Feng & Hu, 2013).e In our models, we use annual GDP growth
data from the year prior to the PISA administration as PISA was
administered part way through the year (March – September). We also
utilise nominal GDP and GDP per capita in robustness analyses.

relatively poor GDP growth.

employed. Specifically, we include the annual unemployment rate (%)

5.3. Country subsamples
Table 4 presents estimates of the main regression specification
separately by subsamples of countries. Column (1) includes only European countries. We focus on this subsample because countries in a
similar location tend to have similar education systems and because
students of non-European countries may respond differently to economic shocks due to cultural differences. Column (2) includes only highincome countries, defined as having 2012 GDP above $20,000 USD per
capita (Montt, 2012). Educational expectations of students in
high-income countries may be less affected because tertiary education in
these countries is more commonly needed to gain employment (Fuller &
Raman, 2017). Third, in Column (3), we omit eight countries (Estonia,
Ireland, Latvia, Lithuania, Greece, Korea, Slovak Republic, and Turkey)
that experienced unusually high variability in GDP growth between
1998 and 2018, as indicated by values > 10 % and < − 10 % (see Fig. 1).l
Our main estimation results (shown in Table 4) are robust to these
different country restrictions. Most notably, the estimated effect of GDP
growth is about 20 % larger when countries with large volatility in GDP
growth are removed (Column 3). As an additional exercise, we exclude
one country at a time from the sample and re-estimate our main specification. The GDP growth coefficient is similar in each regression,
demonstrating that there is not one country is driving our results (see
Appendix Figure A1).

5.2. Heterogeneity analysis

5.4. Lagged GDP growth

In this section, we analyse how the relationship between standardised GDP growth and expected education differs with four characteristics: parental tertiary education, students’ standardised reading
score, sex, and the school’s location (city or town/village). This analysis
is shown in Table 3. First, we do not find a significant interaction effect
with parental education (Column 1), suggesting that the family’s ability
to afford education costs is not the primary consideration.
Second, we find a significant negative interaction with reading
achievement (Column 2). This indicates that high ability students are
less affected by GDP growth than low ability students. A possible
explanation is that high ability students are confident of their success in
tertiary education and the subsequent labour market, while low ability
students perceive tertiary education as a riskier investment, with the
perceived risk (and rewards) varying with the macroeconomy (a good
bet during a booming economy, a bad bet during recessions). To highlight this difference in effect sizes, consider a one standard deviation
decrease in GDP growth. A student with a reading score one standard
deviation below the mean (for their country and year) is estimated to
reduce their educational expectation by 0.262 years (0.191+0.071). In
comparison, the estimated effect for a student with a reading score one
standard deviation above the mean is about half the size (0.120 years of
education).
Third, we find a small, positive interaction effect between GDP
growth and sex, implying that the expectations of boys are more affected
by GDP growth (Column 3). However, the interaction coefficient becomes smaller and loses statistical significance once all variables and
interactions are included in the model (Column 5). This is likely due to
the negative association between male sex and reading scores (Stoet &
Geary, 2013). Finally, our results show a significant, negative interaction between GDP growth and residing in a metropolitan area (Column
4). The GDP growth effect is larger for students living in rural and
regional areas (0.216) than for students living in a city (0.147).

Repeated years of poor economic conditions could be especially
important to students’ educational outlook. To explore this possibility,
we include dummy variables for low or negative annual economic
growth (lowest quintile of GDP growth within country) at t, t-1, and t-2.
The results are presented in Table 5.
Consistent with our main estimates, we observe in Column (1) that
experiencing poor growth in the year before the survey is associated
with a reduction of 0.201 years (p < 0.001) in expected education. In
Column (2), we find that poor economic growth at t-1 (two years before
the survey) further reduces students’ educational expectations. The estimates imply that experiencing low GDP growth at t and t-1 reduces
education expectations by 0.279 (p < 0.001). Finally, we find that low
growth at t-2 (three years before the survey) also impacts educational
expectations. Three cumulative periods of poor growth reduce students’
educational expectations by 0.375 years (p < 0.001).
The estimates in Column (3) of Table 5 also indicate a ‘scarring’ effect from poor economic conditions. Even when GDP growth in the past
two years has been reasonable (not in the lowest quintile), the significant t-2 coefficient (− 0.130) suggests the memory of economic recession
from three years past or the lingering economic effects on social networks are affecting students’ expectations.

when (annual) GDP growth is negative. We find students’ educational

The previous sections show that students’ educational expectations
vary with GDP growth rates. Next, we explore whether economic conditions impact students’ academic behaviours and time investments.
Jacob and Wilder (2010) propose that if educational expectations have a
causal relationship with educational attainment, then educational expectations should predict a range of mediating factors, such as time
spent on homework. Therefore, we might expect that during good economic conditions students will invest more in positive education
behaviours.
First, we create a binary variable indicating that students spend any
time completing homework, for four waves of data. Similarly, we create
a binary variable indicating that students report spending 10 or more
hours a week on homework. Ten hours was chosen as a cut point as it is
double the average amount of homework completed by students in the
sample.m Third, we create a binary variable indicating whether students
report spending time in “out of school” academic lessons or extra
tutoring, measured over three waves. Finally, we create a binary variable indicating if students report being late to school at least once in the
last two weeks, measured over five waves. A detailed description of the
questions asked within each year and how these four variables are
constructed is provided in Appendix B.
We model Eq. (1) with these four educational behaviours as the
outcome. As seen in Table 7 (Column 1), a one standard deviation increase in GDP growth is associated with a 0.7 percentage point increase
in the probability students complete some homework or study per week.
The effect is similar in magnitude to attending a private school: students
who attend a private school are 0.6 percentage points more likely to
complete homework than students who attend a public school. In Column 2, we show that GDP growth is associated with a 0.8 percentage
point increase in the probability of students completing 10+ hours of
homework a week. This result implies that students are more likely to
complete an above-average amount of homework during good economic
conditions.
In Columns 3 and 4 of Table 7 we find further evidence that academic
time investments are associated with GDP growth. A one standard deviation increase in GDP growth is associated with a 2.7 percentage point
increase in the likelihood of completing extracurricular academic lessons and a 1.0 percentage point reduction in the likelihood of arriving
late to school. These results are robust to inclusion of labour market
indicators (see Appendix Table A11).
Taken together, this set of results might suggest that during good
economic times, students are more likely to engage in positive educational behaviours, suggesting an increase in students’ perceived value of
academic achievement. Alternatively, it is possible that students’
educational behaviours are impacted by economic conditions via
parental time investments (Stuart, 2022). During poor economic conditions, parents may be unable to invest in additional extra-curricular
classes for their children, for instance.

8. Discussion and conclusion
In this paper, we use within-country fixed-effects regressions and
data from 38 OECD countries to examine whether the educational expectations of secondary school students vary with changes in GDP
growth rates. In extensions to previous work, we also investigate
whether economic conditions are associated with changes to students’
academic behaviours, such as homework time and school attendance.
We also explore whether students’ expected returns to education are
related to the state of the economy.
Supporting the “discouraged student” hypothesis, we find that students increase their educational expectations when their country’s
economy is expanding and decrease their educational expectations
when the economy is contracting. The magnitude of our main finding is
not small. We find that a one standard deviation increase in a country’s
GDP growth rate increases students’ expected amount of education by
approximately 70 days. Further, we find that expectations decrease after
experiencing a recent recessionary period, and two or three prior years

n
We are not aware of any empirical studies that have estimated the causal
impact of educational expectations on occupational expectations or the causal
impacts of the reverse relationship.

of relatively poor economic growth. In addition, we find that the
educational expectations of students with low academic achievement
and those who attend non-metropolitan schools are more strongly
impacted by macroeconomic conditions.
A reduction in educational expectations has significant implications
for students approaching the end of their compulsory schooling. We
show that students demonstrate measurable behavioural changes during
periods of poor GDP growth. They are less likely to complete any
homework each week, less likely to participate in extracurricular academic lessons, and are more likely to arrive at school late. While the
magnitudes are relatively small, they imply that there are human capital
consequences associated with having reduced educational expectations,
which may have long-term impacts on students’ educational attainment
and lifetime earnings.
One possible explanation of the “discouraged student” effect is that
students perceive lower returns to additional schooling investments
during hard times. Our study provides new correlational evidence to
support this explanation. Our finding that students’ expected returns to
education reduce during a contracting economy is potentially worrisome for high school students as emerging evidence suggests that
returns to tertiary education are larger for students graduating high
school during hard economic times compared to better times (Bičáková
et al., 2021; Hampf et al., 2020). Moreover, entering the labour market
during an economic downturn can result in long-term, negative income
scaring effects (Borland, 2020; Kahn, 2010; Rothstein, 2023; Schwandt
& Wachter, 2019).
While our work shows that students’ educational expectations
decrease during a contracting economy, it is important to acknowledge
that previous research has often found that post-compulsory schooling
enrolments increase in times of adverse macroeconomic conditions (e.g.,
Clark, 2011; Gaini et al., 2013; Hampf et al., 2020; Meschi et al., 2019;
Pöyliö, 2020; Witteveen, 2021). It is plausible that students’ educational
expectations decline during hard times and that students (temporarily)
park themselves in further education due to limited local employment
opportunities. Some evidence suggests that while post-secondary
enrolments initially increase during poor economic conditions, students’ long-run educational attainments are negatively affected, as seen
by an increase in college drop-outs (Bradley & Migali, 2019; Long, 2014;
Witteveen, 2021). Similarly, Sievertsen (2016) shows that while students in Denmark complete more years of schooling in response to poor
labour market conditions, there is no effect on students’ long-run college
completion rates. PISA data does not allow us to follow students after the
initial survey, however, an investigation into whether students’ expectations and economic conditions at the end of post-compulsory
schooling impact long-term educational attainment would be a worthwhile future study.
Overall, this work demonstrates that students decrease their educational expectations and positive educational behaviours during hard
economic times, likely because they expect poorer returns to additional
education. Our findings have possible implications for students’
educational behaviours, educational attainment, and future outcomes.
Prior research has shown that students have a limited understanding of
actual returns to post-compulsory education (Jensen, 2010) and benefit
from being provided with accurate information about the costs and
returns to investing in further education (Peter et al., 2021). Given the
importance of educational attainment for an individual’s social mobility
and economic prosperity, initiatives that increase educational expectations of students during economic contractions are likely to be of value,
particularly if targeted at lower academic achievers and rural students.
