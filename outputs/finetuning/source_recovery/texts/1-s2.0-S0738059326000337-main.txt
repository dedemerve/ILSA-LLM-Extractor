This study addresses optimal class size in Moroccan public lower secondary schools using PISA 2022 microdata
(N = 5,962 students, 150 schools). We develop an integrated dual-methodology framework combining normative
multi-criteria optimization with simulated fuzzy regression discontinuity design (FRDD) to triangulate evidence
on class size effects. The optimization module estimates domain-specific performance functions using natural
cubic splines with socioeconomic controls, constructs an economic cost function based on Moroccan budget
parameters (teacher salaries, infrastructure), and derives optimal class sizes across eight policy scenarios varying
pedagogical-economic trade-offs. Results converge robustly on a global optimum of 23.4 students (95 % CI:
[23.35, 23.36]), requiring a 36 % reduction from the current 36.5 students. The simulated FRDD independently
corroborates these findings by identifying a "high-performance corridor" at thresholds 23–26 students, where
local average treatment effects on pedagogical, multi-criteria, and economic indices remain positive, statistically
significant, and robust to covariate adjustment (specification variation <10 %). Beyond 30 students, performance
deteriorates sharply (LATE=-0.10 to − 0.22, p < 0.001). Moving to the optimal size increases per-student costs by
49.3 % but generates substantial learning gains (+25.1 PISA points), yielding a return on investment of
29.1—classified as "Good" and exceeding typical education interventions in developing countries. Heterogeneity
analyses reveal minimal variation across socioeconomic status and performance levels (gaps <0.2 students),
supporting a uniform national policy target of 23–24 students. These findings provide evidence-based guidance
for Morocco's education reforms while demonstrating methodological transferability to other resourceconstrained systems in the MENA region.

1. Introduction
The issue of optimal class size remains one of the most debated and
long-standing topics in the economics of education. It reflects persistent
tensions between the need for cost efficiency and the goal of educational
equity. Class size does not produce direct and linear effects on student
learning. Instead, it influences teaching practices, classroom dynamics,
and the actual conditions under which instruction takes place, often in
subtle and indirect ways (Blatchford and Russell, 2020).
This debate is shaped by different methodological approaches and
evolving empirical evidence. Recent work by Hanushek and Wößmann
(2023) argues that, on average, class-size reduction is a relatively costly
policy instrument compared with investments in teacher quality.
Meta-analytical evidence by Opatrný (2023) also suggests that, once
publication bias and model uncertainty are taken into account, the

average impact of smaller classes on test scores is small and often
economically negligible, with no systematic indication that disadvantaged students gain more than others. At the same time,
quasi-experimental studies exploiting institutional rules and random
assignment – such as Fredriksson et al. (2013) for Sweden and Chetty
et al. (2011) for the United States – document sizeable long-term effects
of smaller early-grade classes on educational attainment and adult
earnings. International assessments like PISA continue to report weak
cross-country correlations between class size and achievement (OECD,
2023a), yet within-country analyses that control for student sorting and
resource heterogeneity reveal stronger and more context-specific effects
(Wößmann and West, 2006; Schleicher, 2019).
Recent studies offer a more nuanced understanding of this issue.
Angrist et al. (2011) use lottery-based admission to evaluate KIPP
Academy Lynn in Massachusetts and show that a highly structured “No

than pupil–teacher ratios in isolation. Specifically for Morocco, Bijou
et al. (2023) identify an optimal class size range between 20 and 27
students using TIMSS and PIRLS data and a semi-parametric multilevel
model, accounting for student, teacher and school diversity. Their work
strengthens the case for context-sensitive approaches in
resource-constrained settings.
Despite these advances, three important gaps remain in the literature. First, few studies have developed an explicit optimisation framework that combines both pedagogical and economic dimensions into a
workable objective function tailored to developing-country contexts
(Lazear, 2001; Rivkin et al., 2005). Most analyses either focus exclusively on achievement effects or treat costs as exogenous constraints
rather than as integral components of policy design. Second, many analyses overlook context-specific implementation constraints, particularly in lower-middle-income countries where equity concerns,
infrastructure limitations and teacher supply shortages interact in
complex ways (Banerjee et al., 2007; Muralidharan et al., 2017). Third,
without causal validation, normative recommendations from optimisation models may lack credibility for evidence-based policymaking
(Imbens and Lemieux, 2008; Lee and Lemieux, 2010). Few studies systematically compare structural optimisation results with causal estimates from quasi-experimental designs within the same dataset.
This study aims to address these gaps by developing an original
methodological framework that combines multi-criteria optimization
with causal validation using a fuzzy regression discontinuity design
(FRDD). Applied to Morocco's PISA 2022 microdata (5962students in
150 public schools), our approach includes: (i) adaptive modeling of
performance functions by subject using spline regressions with socioeconomic controls; (ii) an economic cost function based on Moroccan
budget realities (teacher salaries, infrastructure, materials); (iii) a robust
optimization algorithm exploring eight policy scenarios varying pedagogical quality vs. cost-efficiency trade-offs (α ∈ [0.4, 0.7], β ∈ [0.3,
0.6]); and (iv) a simulated FRDD that identifies critical performance
thresholds on a grid of class sizes between 20 and 32 students, using an
instrumental-variable specification where the class-size cut-off indicator
1{class size ≥ c} and its local polynomial terms instrument a simulated
treatment capturing schools’ behavioural responses to large classes.
The Moroccan context is particularly relevant for this analysis. According to our calculations using PISA 2022 microdata, the weighted
average class size in public lower secondary schools is about 36.5 students, well above typical OECD patterns, where public classes usually
include around 20–25 students and the student–teacher ratio at lower
secondary level is about 13:1 (OECD, 2023a). Public spending on

education reached about 5.9 % of GDP in 2022, a relatively high level
compared with the lower-middle-income country average of around
3.4 % of GDP (World Bank, 2024). However, Ministry of Education
statistics show that classroom conditions remain constrained: in
2022/23 there were on average 28.2 students per teacher in lower
secondary education and 17.6 in upper secondary, far above the OECD
benchmark of 13 students per teacher at lower secondary level (MEN,
2023; OECD, 2023b). At the same time, Moroccan 15-year-olds score
365 points in mathematics, 339 in reading and 365 in science on PISA
2022—roughly 110–140 points below OECD averages—underscoring
the need for more efficient and better targeted allocation of educational
resources (OECD, 2023a).
Our contributions are threefold. Methodologically, we propose the
first integrated optimization-validation framework for class size analysis
that combines: (a) domain-specific performance modeling using all ten
PISA plausible values and proper variance estimation via Rubin's (1987)
rules, ensuring that measurement uncertainty is correctly reflected; (b)
explicit multi-criteria optimization with sensitivity analysis across eight
policy scenarios; and (c) causal validation using simulated FRDD with
covariate balance checks and weak-instrument diagnostics. This
framework is fully replicable and can be adapted to other educational
contexts.
Empirically, we identify strong convergence between normative and
causal evidence. The multi-criteria optimization yields a global optimum of 23.4 students (95 % CI: [23.35, 23.36]) based on 40 % mathematics, 30 % science, and 30 % reading weights, with domain-specific
optima ranging from 22.2 (reading) to 24.3 (mathematics). The simulated FRDD corroborates this finding by detecting a "high-performance
corridor" at 23–26 students, where local average treatment effects
(LATEs) on composite pedagogical, multi-criteria, and economic indices
are positive, statistically significant, and robust to covariate adjustment
(percentage variations <10 %). Beyond 30 students, the FRDD identifies
significant negative effects on multi-criteria performance (LATE =
− 0.10 to − 0.12, p < 0.001) and economic efficiency (LATE = − 0.22,
p < 0.001), even when subject-specific scores become noisy. This defines
a "safe zone" (23–26 students) for policy implementation and a critical
deterioration threshold (30 + students) beyond which both learning and
cost-effectiveness decline sharply.
In terms of policy, we show that gradually reducing class sizes from
the current 36.5 to the optimal 23.4 students would require a 49.3 %
increase in per-student costs (from 3059 to 4568 MAD/year) but would
generate an average gain of + 25.1 PISA points across domains, yielding
a return on investment (ROI) of 29.1 (rated "Good"). This translates to
approximately 0.5 % performance improvement per 1 % spending increase. Operationally, achieving this target would necessitate recruiting
approximately 55 % more teachers over a phased implementation
period of 6–8 years, assuming gradual hiring and infrastructure expansion. Our policy scenario analysis reveals that even under budgetconstrained scenarios prioritizing cost efficiency (α=0.4, β=0.6),
optimal class sizes remain at 23.5 students, indicating robustness of the
23–24 recommendation across diverse policy priorities. However, the
36 % reduction required to reach the global optimum represents a
medium-feasibility reform given Morocco's current budget and operational constraints, suggesting that a realistic interim target of 28–30
students may be more immediately achievable while still avoiding the
critical deterioration threshold identified by the FRDD.
This research supports the growing demand for evidence-based decision-making in education policy (Kraft, 2020; Murnane and Willett,
2011). By demonstrating that normative optimization and
quasi-experimental causal inference can be systematically integrated
within a unified analytical framework, we provide a methodological
template for analyzing structural reforms in resource-constrained education systems. Our findings also contribute to the broader literature on
education production functions in developing countries (Glewwe and
Muralidharan, 2016), showing that class size remains a significant—though not dominant—determinant of learning outcomes

Lazear's Disruption Model: Lazear (2001) offers a microeconomic
theory providing micro-foundations for class size effects within the EPF
framework. He models classroom learning as a public good subject to
negative externalities: each student benefits from instruction, but
disruptive behavior by any student reduces learning for all classmates.
Larger classes amplify disruption costs because the probability that at
least one student disrupts increases with class size. If each student has
probability p of disrupting, the probability of zero disruption in a class of
size n is (1 − p)n , which declines exponentially with n. This model yields
testable predictions: class size effects should be larger in high-disruption
contexts (younger students, disadvantaged populations), non-linearities
should emerge (small increases matter more when classes are already
large), and teacher quality and class size are complements (skilled
teachers can mitigate disruption).
Non-Linear Optimization and Cost-Efficiency Models: Zhao
(2020) develops a non-linear optimization model incorporating both
pedagogical benefits and organizational costs of class size changes. His
framework posits an inverted-U relationship: very small classes suffer
from limited peer interaction while very large classes overwhelm
teachers' capacity for individualized attention. The model includes
adjustment costs (hiring, dismissal, classroom reorganization) that
create institutional inertia. Zhao (2020) shows that accounting for these
costs raises the optimal class size relative to models ignoring implementation frictions. Complementing this, Tsang (1988) presents a systemic model integrating the EPF with an education cost function (ECF),
particularly relevant for resource-constrained developing countries. His
framework distinguishes between internal efficiency (learning per unit
cost) and external efficiency (economic returns to education). Tsang
(1988) argues that optimal class size in developing countries may differ
systematically from high-income contexts due to budget constraints,
teacher supply constraints, infrastructure costs, and opportunity costs.
This perspective is particularly relevant for Morocco, where budget
constraints and infrastructure limitations significantly shape feasible
policy options.

(2020) propose a social pedagogy framework emphasizing classroom
processes rather than simple input-output relationships. Their conceptual framework invites us to move beyond the simplistic opposition
"small classes equal good learning" versus "large classes equal inefficiency." The rationale is that treating class size as a standalone variable with universal effects ignores complex mediating processes
through which class size influences learning. They identify three critical
mechanisms: teacher-student interaction quality (smaller classes allow
more individualized feedback but only if teachers have pedagogical
skills to leverage these opportunities), classroom climate and behavior
management (larger classes increase time spent on discipline but skilled
teachers can partially mitigate these effects), and teacher workload and
pedagogical adaptation (teachers in large classes report higher stress but
supportive leadership can buffer these challenges). To illustrate this

2. Literature review
The debate over optimal class size spans both theoretical models and
empirical findings, reflecting longstanding tensions between pedagogical effectiveness, cost efficiency, and equity in education. This section
reviews the main conceptual frameworks and recent empirical contributions, with particular focus on non-linear effects, optimization strategies, and contextual adaptations in developing countries. We
distinguish between theoretical frameworks providing conceptual
foundations for understanding class size effects and empirical evaluations using causal identification methods to inform practical policy
design.
2.1. Theoretical frameworks
The issue of optimal class size lies at the heart of a complex theoretical debate involving pedagogical considerations, budget constraints,
and goals of public efficiency. This subsection presents conceptual
models structuring our understanding of how class size operates as an
input in the educational production process.
The Education Production Function Framework: The education
production function (EPF) provides the foundational theoretical lens for
analyzing how school inputs, including class size, interact with family,
institutional, and contextual factors to produce learning outcomes
(Hanushek, 1992; Coleman et al., 1966). Formally, the EPF can be
expressed as Y = f(S, F, P, I), where Y represents student achievement, S
denotes school inputs (teachers, class size, materials), F captures family
background, P represents peer characteristics, and I denotes individual
student ability. This framework conceptualizes education as a production process where inputs are combined through pedagogy to generate
learning outputs. The EPF framework has several theoretical implications for class size research: conditionality (class size effects depend on
complementary input quality), substitutability (schools may compensate for large classes through improved teacher quality or materials),
and intermediate mechanisms (class size operates through teacher time
allocation, individualized feedback, and classroom management rather
than directly causing learning). Hanushek's (1992) influential

Meta-Analyses and Other Contributions. Filges et al. (2018) conducted a systematic meta-analysis of class size effects on reading
achievement, including only studies with strong internal validity (randomized controlled trials and high-quality quasi-experiments). They
find small but positive effects (d approximately 0.10–0.15), with larger
impacts for disadvantaged students and in early grades, highlighting
substantial heterogeneity across contexts. Jepsen (2015) reviews international evidence from STAR, Maimonides' Rule studies, and demographic natural experiments, concluding that effects are stronger for
disadvantaged students and in early education stages but depend
heavily on context and costs. Greenwald et al. (1996) conducted an early
meta-analysis finding consistent positive effects of school resources,
including smaller classes, on student achievement. Gary-Bobo and
Mahjoub (2013) used French administrative data with Maimonides' Rule
as an instrument, showing that reducing class size by ten students can
substantially equalize promotion probabilities between working-class
and professional children.
Evidence Gaps and Study Contributions: Despite these advances,
key gaps remain. Most studies employing normative optimization approaches develop theoretical models deriving optimal class sizes by
maximizing objective functions under budget constraints. While calibrated using empirical parameters, they do not directly validate policy
recommendations through independent causal identification strategies
such as regression discontinuity designs or randomized experiments.
Conversely, studies with strong causal identification typically estimate
local average treatment effects at specific thresholds using quasi-random
variation but do not derive globally optimal class sizes that explicitly
balance pedagogical objectives against economic constraints across the
full distribution of class sizes. Few studies propose an explicit composite
objective function integrating pedagogical and economic outcomes
within a realistic, budget-constrained framework. To our knowledge, no
applied research has developed such a model for Morocco using recent
PISA 2022 data nor empirically estimated an optimal class size threshold
through composite optimization combined with quasi-experimental
validation. To address these limitations, our study proposes an original dual-methodology approach combining normative modeling with
causal validation tailored to the Moroccan context. We first construct a
composite objective function capturing both educational performance
(via normalized PISA scores) and economic cost (via teacher salaries and
infrastructure), supported by visualizations, robustness checks, and
prospective policy simulations. Then, we complement this with a
simulated Fuzzy Regression Discontinuity Design (FRDD) allowing us to
estimate causal effects at multiple class size thresholds and empirically
validate whether optimization-identified optima correspond to discontinuities in student performance. This two-step approach helps reconcile
theoretical optima with causal evidence, contributing to more rational
and evidence-based policymaking in resource-constrained education
systems.

2.2. Empirical evidence
Over the past two decades, the empirical literature on class size effects has undergone major methodological renewal. Using robust quasiexperimental methods, structural models incorporating budget constraints, and rigorous meta-analyses, recent studies aim to identify
reliable causal effects of class size on student outcomes. Despite mixed
results, they converge on the idea that moderate class sizes, adjusted to
local pedagogical and socio-economic contexts, can improve learning,
especially in low-resource environments.
Quasi-Experimental Evidence from Developed Countries: Fredriksson et al. (2013) combine a fuzzy regression discontinuity design
with cost-benefit analysis using Swedish administrative data and
exploiting maximum class size regulations. They show that moderate
class size reductions (from approximately 25–20 students) have
long-term positive effects on schooling attainment and adult earnings,
with an internal rate of return (approximately 18 %) substantially
exceeding teacher salary costs. Angrist and Lavy (1999) pioneered using
Maimonides' Rule (maximum class size 40 in Israel) as an instrumental
variable, finding significant effects on mathematics and reading
achievement, particularly for disadvantaged students. Krueger (1999)
analyzes Project STAR, a large-scale randomized experiment in Tennessee where students were randomly assigned to small (13− 17), regular (22− 25), or regular-with-aide classes. Small classes improved
achievement by 0.15–0.20 standard deviations in early grades, with
effects persisting into adulthood (Chetty et al., 2011), though high
implementation costs (approximately $10,000 per student-year in 1990s
dollars) raise cost-effectiveness questions relative to alternative
interventions.
Structural Models and Non-Linear Effects: Zhao (2020) empirically implements his theoretical non-linear optimization model using
Greek administrative data, estimating an inverted-U relationship between class size and performance with an optimum around 19 students.
The model incorporates adjustment costs related to hiring and firing
teachers, demonstrating that ignoring organizational frictions can lead
to biased policy recommendations. Bijou et al. (2023) propose a
Morocco-specific model using TIMSS and PIRLS 2011 data, identifying
optimal class size between 20 and 27 students with heterogeneous effects across subjects, education levels, and teacher characteristics. This
study confirms the non-linear link between class size and performance in
a developing-country context but does not include an explicit cost
function or economic optimization criterion, limiting its applicability for
budget-constrained policy design.
Evidence from Developing Countries: Duflo et al. (2011) analyse a
randomized tracking programme in Kenyan primary schools, in which
first-grade cohorts received an extra teacher, classes were split into two
streams and pupils were grouped by prior achievement. The intervention raised test scores by about 0.14–0.18 standard deviations for both
low- and high-achieving students. However, the treatment simultaneously reduced class size, modified peer composition and introduced
ability grouping, so the design does not allow a clean separation of the
effect of smaller classes from that of tracking and peer effects. The results therefore provide suggestive evidence that splitting very large
classes of around 60–80 pupils can improve learning in low-resource
settings, but they do not constitute a direct test of class-size reduction
in isolation. More broadly, the study underscores that class size interacts
with teacher incentives, tracking and pedagogy, rather than operating as
a stand-alone lever. The results underscore the importance of complementary policies: reducing class size alone may not improve outcomes
without also addressing teacher quality, incentives, and pedagogical
practices. Banerjee et al. (2007) conducted a randomized evaluation in

shortages of qualified teachers. While contract teacher recruitment has
helped fill immediate staffing gaps, concerns persist regarding job stability, training quality, and long-term pedagogical effectiveness.

The divergence between Morocco and its educational peers suggests
either compensatory quality factors—such as superior teacher training,
more effective pedagogical methods, or stronger institutional support—that allow Morocco to maintain PISA performance despite
increasing class sizes, or alternatively, untapped potential for substantial
improvement through evidence-based resource optimization. The fact
that countries with similar educational outcomes have systematically
reduced their pupil-teacher ratios while Morocco's has increased reinforces the policy urgency of identifying optimal class size
configurations.
According to the OECD (2024), centralized management of human
and financial resources in Morocco restricts schools' ability to respond to
local needs, contributing to persistent inequalities and inefficiencies.
Performance-based contracts with regional academies and the push for
preschool expansion aim to improve equity and learning outcomes, yet
structural reforms targeting teaching conditions—especially class size
optimization—remain limited. This historical context, combined with
the systematic improvement observed across all comparator countries,
highlights both the feasibility and urgency of rationalizing educational
resource allocation through models that jointly account for pedagogical
effectiveness and economic sustainability, grounded in Morocco's specific policy constraints and opportunities.

4.4.1. Plausible values
PISA reports ten PVs per domain to represent latent proficiency
under IRT conditioning; PVs must be analysed separately and then
combined to avoid underestimating uncertainty (OECD, 2024/2025;
von Davier et al., 2009).
The interaction between PISA's sampling design and plausible values
has important methodological implications for our analysis. PISA's
complex sampling design (stratified, two-stage, probability-proportional-to-size) can be addressed through replicate weights
(W_FSTURWT1–80) for full variance decomposition. However, for our
analyses, we use the primary sampling weight (W_FSTUWT) throughout,
which accounts for differential selection probabilities while simplifying
computational requirements for non-standard analyses (optimization,
fuzzy RDD). Simultaneously, plausible values introduce measurement
uncertainty, reflecting imprecision in estimating latent proficiency from
test responses. Our analytical workflow propagates measurement uncertainty through Rubin's (1987) combining rules across 10 plausible
values. Standard errors thus reflect between-imputation variance
(measurement uncertainty) while survey weights ensure appropriate
weighting of observations according to PISA's sampling design. This
approach follows standard practice in PISA secondary analyses when
custom methodologies preclude full replicate-weight integration (von
Davier et al., 2009; Rutkowski et al., 2010).
We therefore run each analysis ten times—one per PV—and combine
results via Rubin's (1987) rules, where total variance equals the
within-imputation sampling variance plus the (1+1/m)-scaled
between-imputation variance. This is the standard in international
large-scale assessments and prevents spuriously narrow confidence
intervals.

Second, it includes values suggested by the international class-size

this range [20–32] is analytically derived rather than legally mandated.

observed distribution of class sizes in Moroccan public schools, where
very few classes fall below 20 or exceed 32 students; (ii) the penalty
structure in our economic performance function, which reflects feasibility constraints documented in the international literature (Krueger,
2003; Schanzenbach, 2014); and (iii) the need to maintain sufficient
local sample sizes around each threshold for credible RDD estimation.
These thresholds therefore represent a pragmatic analytical window that
corresponds to the empirical reality of Moroccan classrooms, rather than
official policy cut-offs. For each value of c in this range, the FRDD is

5.2.6. Selection and balance of covariates
The initial set of candidate covariates includes pupil-level characteristics (gender, age, grade repetition, match between test and home
language, immigrant status, access to a computer and to the internet,
individual ESCS, urban location) and school-level variables (average
ESCS of the school and school size).
To avoid over-controlling and to respect the RDD logic, we only
include covariates that are balanced around each threshold (Imbens and
Rubin, 2015). For every candidate cut-off c and every covariate Z, we
estimate a local linear regression of Z on the cut-off indicator and the
running variable in a window of ±5 pupils around c. If the p-value of the
coefficient on Aboveic is greater than 0.10, the covariate is considered
balanced and can be included in the FRDD specification for that
threshold. Unbalanced covariates are excluded for that specific
estimation.
This procedure ensures that the vector Xisc only contains variables
that do not jump at the threshold, so that the identifying variation
mainly comes from the discontinuity in the simulated treatment probability. Detailed covariate balance test results for all thresholds are reported in Appendix C, which documents p-values and selection decisions
for each candidate covariate at each cut-off.

5.2.7. Validation and robustness checks
Several diagnostic checks are applied to evaluate the simulated
FRDD:
• Manipulation diagnostics: we inspect the number of observations
within ±5 pupils of each threshold and use local histograms of the
running variable to check that the density of class size does not show
obvious bunching at the cut-off, in the spirit of density-based
manipulation tests proposed in the RDD literature (McCrary, 2008;
Cattaneo et al., 2018)
• Instrument strength: for each specification we report the first-stage
F-statistic on Aboveic . Thresholds with very weak instruments are
interpreted with caution.
• Covariate balance: the automatic selection of balanced covariates
around each threshold serves as a formal test that pre-treatment
characteristics do not change abruptly at the cut-off.
• Robustness to specification: for all outcomes and thresholds we
compare the LATE with and without covariates and classify the
percentage change as very robust, robust, moderately sensitive or
very sensitive.

The FRDD analysis delivers two policy-relevant conclusions. First,

concentrated at threshold 23. Second, deterioration accelerates beyond

indices. Together, these findings support implementing a target corridor

7.1.2. FRDD validation of the normative optimum
The simulated FRDD provides complementary quasi-experimental
support in a context without a formal maximum class-size rule. Treatment is modelled probabilistically as a function of distance to candidate
thresholds, school socio-economic composition, and school size,
following extensions of fuzzy RDD to settings without sharp legal cutoffs (Battistin and Rettore, 2008; Bloom, 1984). For nine thresholds
between 20 and 32 students, we estimate baseline and
covariate-adjusted specifications, retaining only locally balanced covariates (p > 0.10 on the discontinuity test), in line with RDD best practice
(Lee and Lemieux, 2010).
Diagnostic tests support the credibility of this design. Density
manipulation tests show smooth distributions of the running variable,
with sufficient local sample sizes (7 out of 9 thresholds with N > 100).
First-stage F-statistics average 16.7, acceptable though slightly below
the Stock and Yogo (2005) benchmark of F > 20 for very strong instruments, reflecting the simulated (rather than rule-based) nature of
assignment. Covariate-balance procedures typically retain 5–8 variables
(individual and school ESCS, ICT access, school size), all without
detectable discontinuities.
Within this framework, the FRDD confirms a clear “high-performance corridor” around the normative optimum. At thresholds 23–26
students, pedagogical, multi-criteria, and economic indices all display
positive, statistically significant local average treatment effects (LATEs
of +0.73 to +3.09 SD, p ≤ 0.001), with LATE variation below 10 %
when balanced covariates are added. This pattern suggests that moving
towards moderate class sizes (23–26 students) yields robust causal gains
rather than artefacts of compositional differences (Imbens and Lemieux,
2008). In contrast, beyond threshold 30, multi-criteria and economic
indices show small but precisely estimated negative LATEs (− 0.10 to
− 0.22 SD, p < 0.001), while subject-specific scores become less stable.
Overall, the FRDD identifies a corridor of strong performance at 23–26
students and clear deterioration beyond 30, fully consistent with the
structural conclusion that the current average of 36.5 students lies
outside the desirable range.

7.1.4. Heterogeneity and equity implications
Heterogeneity analyses reveal minimal variation in optimal class size
across student subgroups. Disadvantaged students in the bottom ESCS
tercile have an optimum of 24.3 students, compared with 24.4 for
advantaged students in the top tercile—a difference of 0.1 students, far
below any practical threshold. This contrasts with U.S. evidence of
larger gains for disadvantaged students (Krueger and Whitmore, 2001;
Chetty et al., 2011), but is consistent with European studies reporting
limited socio-economic heterogeneity (Wößmann and West, 2006). A
universal class-size target of about 24 students would therefore benefit
all groups similarly and is likely to reduce, rather than widen, educational inequalities. In France, Gary-Bobo and Mahjoub (2013) show that
a 10-student reduction can substantially equalise promotion probabilities between working-class and professional families, suggesting similar
equity potential for Morocco.
Performance-level heterogeneity is equally small: low achievers have
an optimum of 24.4 students versus 24.3 for medium achievers. Evidence from Project STAR suggests that smaller classes raise achievement
for all students and may yield somewhat larger cumulative gains for lowachieving or disadvantaged students (Finn and Achilles, 1999; Konstantopoulos and Chung, 2009), but the gaps are too small to justify
administratively complex differentiation by ability. In practice, a universal target near 24 students appears both effective and straightforward
to implement.
7.2. Policy implications
7.2.1. Recommendations for Morocco
Taken together, the normative optimization and quasi-experimental
validation point to a clear policy recommendation: Morocco should
adopt a target class size of 23–24 students in public lower secondary
schools, to be achieved gradually between 2025 and 2030. Our multimethod analysis delineates a “policy safety zone”:
• 22–26 students: optimal zone with maximal pedagogical gains;
• 26–30 students: acceptable buffer with moderate but declining gains;
• 30 + students: zone to be avoided, where performance clearly
deteriorates.

7.2.2. Lessons for other MENA countries and global debates
These findings speak to wider policy debates in the MENA region,
where many systems face similar challenges: large classes, tight budgets,
and weak performance in international assessments. Countries such as
Egypt (average 36 students per class), Tunisia (29), and Jordan (28)
could apply a similar dual-method framework—normative optimization
combined with simulated FRDD—to derive context-specific optimal
sizes, likely in comparable ranges given institutional similarities (OECD,
2023a).
The results also contribute to long-standing global debates on education spending priorities. Critics argue that class-size reductions are
costly compared with alternative interventions such as remedial tutoring or computer-assisted learning (Hanushek, 2003; Banerjee et al.,
2007). Proponents counter that smaller classes enhance the effectiveness
of other reforms—especially pedagogical innovations—by enabling
more individualised and differentiated instruction (Blatchford et al.,
2011; Dee and West, 2011).
Our inverted-U estimates suggest that both positions are valid but
context-dependent. In high-resource systems already near 20–25 students per class, further reductions may produce small marginal gains
and lower ROI, making investments in teacher quality or curriculum
more attractive. In severely under-resourced systems like Morocco, with
average classes above 36 students, moving towards 24 students generates substantial learning gains (+25.1 PISA points) and a strong ROI
(29.1), making class-size reduction a clear policy priority that should
precede or accompany other reforms. This context-dependent perspective helps reconcile seemingly contradictory findings in the international literature.

A.1. Software and Package Ecosystem
All analyses are conducted in R version 4.4.2. Our workflow employs the following packages, each serving a distinct analytical function:
Data preparation and survey design: We use haven for reading SPSS/SAS files and tidyverse for data manipulation. We explicitly manage student
weights (W_FSTUWT) and 80 replicate weights (W_FSTURWT1–80) in all weighted calculations following OECD guidelines (OECD, 2024).
Descriptive statistics: We compute weighted means, correlations, and quantiles using base R functions (weighted.mean, cov.wt) combined with

Multi-criteria optimization: We use splines (base R) for natural cubic splines via ns(), and stats (base R) for weighted linear models (lm) and onedimensional optimization (optimize). Validation procedures (bootstrap, cross-validation, specification sensitivity) employ boot, caret, mgcv, and

A.2. Plausible Values Treatment Protocol
We implement Rubin's (1987) combining rules manually for three reasons. First, our analyses require non-standard procedures—multi-criteria
optimization, fuzzy RDD with composite outcomes—for which pre-packaged PV handling in intsvy is unavailable or insufficiently flexible. Second,
manual implementation allows us to verify each computational step and adapt variance formulas to non-standard contexts (e.g., optimization point
estimates without within-PV SEs). Third, direct implementation enables seamless integration across multiple packages (splines, stats, rdrobust) that do
not natively communicate with intsvy's infrastructure.
For each analysis (descriptive statistics, optimization, FRDD), we follow a consistent protocol:
(i) Process each of the 10 plausible values separately using the same analytical procedure;
(ii) Store point estimates (θ₁, …, θ₁₀) and, when available, within-PV standard errors (SE₁, …, SE₁₀);
(iii) Combine results using Rubin's formulas:
/
θfinal = (1 m) Σθi
/
Vwithin = (1 m) ΣSE2i
/
Vbetween = [1 (m − 1)] Σ(θi − θfinal )²
Vtotal = Vwithin + (1 + 1/m) × Vbetween
SEfinal = √Vtotal

(iv) Report pooled point estimates and standard errors with appropriate degrees of freedom adjustments following Barnard and Rubin (1999).
This protocol ensures that all estimates properly reflect both sampling uncertainty (from PISA's complex survey design) and measurement uncertainty (from the IRT-based plausible value mechanism), following established best practices for international large-scale assessments (Rubin, 1987;
von Davier et al., 2009; Rutkowski et al., 2010).
Why Not Use intsvy throughout?
The intsvy package is designed for standard multilevel and regression analyses with PISA data and would be ideal for simple fixed-effects or
hierarchical linear models. However, our study requires:
(i) Multi-criteria optimization algorithms—no R package (including intsvy) provides ready-made functions combining education production
functions, economic cost models, regularization, and policy scenario analysis. Custom optimization code is mathematically necessary and
cannot be delegated to existing packages.
(ii) Integration with rdrobust for fuzzy RDD—the gold-standard rdrobust package does not integrate with intsvy, requiring custom construction of
composite performance indices before passing to rdrobust. The intsvy package cannot facilitate this two-stage workflow.
(iii) Transparency and flexibility—direct implementation of Rubin's rules allows us to verify each computational step, adapt variance formulas to
non-standard contexts (e.g., optimization point estimates without within-PV SEs), and integrate multiple packages seamlessly. This approach

Appendix B. Cost Calculation Methodology
To estimate the full annual cost per lower secondary student, we combined data from the 2022 Finance Law (Ministry of Economy and Finance,
2021) with official indicators published by the Ministry of National Education (MEN, 2023).
B.1. Salary cost
The monthly salary at the beginning of a teaching career (4973 MAD) and at the end of a career (11,299 MAD) were averaged to obtain a monthly
mean of 8136 MAD, or 97,632 MAD per year. This amount was then divided by the student-teacher ratio in lower secondary education (1781,047
students / 62,895 teachers ≈ 28.3), resulting in an average salary cost per student of 3451 MAD.
B.2. Non-salary costs
Budgetary items for infrastructure (3.6 billion MAD), textbooks (0.7 billion), school transport (1.2 billion), and digitalization (0.35 billion) were
extracted from the 2022 budget. As lower secondary education represents 11.8 % of total enrollment, each budget item was adjusted proportionally
(item × 0.118) and divided by the number of students to calculate unit costs: 258 MAD (infrastructure), 5 MAD (textbooks), 8 MAD (transport), and
118 MAD (digitalization), for a total of 388 MAD per student.
B.3. Total cost per student and optimization parameters
By aggregating salary and non-salary components, the full annual cost reaches 3839 MAD (≈ 384 USD at an exchange rate of 10 MAD = 1 USD).
This figure serves as the reference value for the economic performance function. For a class size N, the cost per student is modeled as:
Cost(N) = (97,632 / N) + 388,
And normalized between 0 and 1 using the thresholds set by the study (minimum pedagogical threshold = SEUIL_MIN, constrained maximum =
SEUIL_CONTRAINTE_MAX).
B.4. Consistency checks
We verified that the average salary (8–15k MAD), student-teacher ratio (20–35), and total cost (2000–10,000 MAD) fall within plausible ranges. All
conditions were satisfied.
B.5. International comparison
The Moroccan cost (≈ 384 USD) represents about 4.8 % of the average per-student expenditure in OECD countries (~8000 USD).
