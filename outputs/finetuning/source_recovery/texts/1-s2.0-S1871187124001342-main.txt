A B S T R A C T  
With a set of the Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2015 test items as 
the assessment tool, this study constructed a mathematics competency assessment framework 
composed of eight attributes, and formed a Q matrix using data of 4,733 students in the four 
provinces (cities) of mainland China. Through model comparisons, this study selected the mixed 
model and made in-depth analyses of attribute mastery from four aspects: international 
comparative analysis, learning path analysis, learning progression construction, and personalized 
report development. The study found obvious advantages in most attributes of Chinese students’ 
basic mathematics competencies. The learning path was rich with large knowledge status 
distributed in the second, third, and fourth level of learning progression. It provided a basis for a 
detailed understanding of the basic mathematics competencies of Chinese students and their 
current situation in international education. It also provided a methodological basis for the 
cognitive diagnosis assessment of students’ mathematics competencies.   
1. Introduction 
From the perspective of historical development, the methods and objectives of educational assessment have undergone profound 
changes along with the progress of the educational system, technology, and the human society. In the 1960s, the United States initiated 
the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) to regularly monitor the academic achievements of students (Jones & Thissen, 
2006). In the 1970s and 1980s, when the American education system was questioned, educational assessment was called to be 
expanded, including the educational decision-making and classroom teaching based on the top-down accountability system and the 
bottom-up view of teaching (Linn, 2006). Since the 1980s, more and more states in the United States have adopted the standardized 
tests in the educational accountability systems (Linn, 2010; Nichols, 1994). The No Child Left Behind act (NCLB), passed in 2001 by the 
United States reinforces this trend, requiring all states to take mandatory performance tests in all grades to improve the performance of 
primary and secondary schools in America (Linn et al., 2002; Pellegrino, 2004). It is an act that strengthened the educational 
assessment. 
International assessments have come more valuable and meaningful due to the impact of globalization in recent decades. To 
compare and assess the quality of education across the nations, many international student assessments have been carried out to

compare the learning outcomes in various countries (Hambleton et al., 2004). The international educational achievement tests have 
become a recognized source of information for evaluating and comparing education systems. Among these international tests, Trends in 
International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) has quickly attracted more than 60 countries to participate around the world as the 
largest international education assessment (Mullis & Martin, 2017). Its purpose is to assess the trends in mathematics and science 
achievement of Grade 4 and Grade 8 students in participating countries around the world, as well as to assess curriculum imple­
mentation and identify promising teaching practices (Foy et al., 2013). 
The assessment content of TIMSS is consistent with the mathematics and science curriculums of the participating countries. It 
provides valuable information about students’ academic achievements, including the characteristics of students, teachers, schools, 
curriculum and teaching, etc., aiming to provide important information for effective improvement of curriculum evaluation and 
teaching methods (Macnab, 2000). The results of TIMSS research, which have been used by countries to improve their educational 
policies, curricula, and teaching methods, have had a great impact on politics and education, thereby strengthening educational 
accountability and competitiveness in many countries. Studies have shown that participating countries react to the students’ per­
formance in various ways (Macnab, 2003), and other studies have analyzed how these reactions play a role in changes in mathematics 
curriculums and teaching methods. Many countries, including the United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Canada, Japan, 
Singapore, and South Korea, use the results of TIMSS to help develop education plans and improve education systems (Macnab, 2000). 
However, most of the research on TIMSS stay at the macro level currently, and the evaluation unit is mainly based on the country 
(Carnoy et al., 2015; Dodeen et al., 2012; Hiebert et al., 1999; Lee & Stankov, 2018; Tajudin & Chinnappan, 2016). Cognitive Diagnosis 
Assessment (CDA), as a newly generated assessment theory, is a product of the combination of cognitive psychology and educational 
measurement and can obtain diagnostic information on students’ fine-grained knowledge. Therefore, use of Cognitive Diagnosis 
Models (CDMs) can obtain more fine-grained information based on the existing assessment framework (Liu et al., 2018). Through this 
way, TIMSS, as a large-scale non-diagnostic assessment, can still be used for diagnostic purposes (Chen & de la Torre, 2014). Therefore, 
it is necessary to use cognitive diagnostic methods to analyze data from large-scale assessments such as TIMSS, to diagnose students’ 
achievements and competencies at a more detailed level in multiple dimensions of a specific discipline, so as to better understand the 
knowledge state that students possess (Dimitrov, 2007; Gierl & Leighton, 2007; Lee & Sawaki, 2009; Leighton & Gierl, 2007; Pellegrino 
et al., 1999). The knowledge state refers to the specific mastery of different cognitive attributes such as knowledge, skills, and processes 
possessed by learners, representing the overall knowledge structure characteristics of learners. Cognitive skills, also known as 
cognitive attributes, refer to the process by which examinees correctly solve tasks and the strategies they need to adopt. 
Although TIMSS has been developed for 60 years, Mainland China has not participated in this test so far, and the students’ per­
formance under the TIMSS assessment is still unknown. As we all know, China has the best basic education, which is reflected in many 
aspects such as the PISA test and the international Olympic competition. Therefore, this study selected 4,733 Grade 8 students from 
four provinces (cities) in Gansu, Guizhou, Guangdong, and Shanghai in Mainland China as the test objects and used the TIMSS 2015 
test items as the test tool to evaluate the basic mathematics competency of the students.

2.1. Cognitive attributes and its Q matrix 
As one of the core concepts in CDA, attributes play a cornerstone role in the diagnosis process. The quality of attributes directly 
determines the validity of the results of cognitive diagnosis. To a certain extent, cognitive diagnosis is the diagnosis of cognitive at­
tributes. There is so far no unified definition of cognitive attributes in psychometrics. Attributes are defined as the knowledge structure 
and processing skills necessary to complete a task in the cognitive diagnosis process (Yang & Embretson, 2007); the procedures, skills, 
processes, strategies, and knowledge that students need to solve the test items (Dogan & Tatsuoka, 2008). It can also be a description of 
the hidden psychological processing in the process of problem solving for students (Cai et al., 2018). The attributes in this study are the 
mathematics skills required for students to correctly solve mathematical tasks. 
Skills are the concrete form of competency. Modern learning theory believes that skills are the competencies to complete physical 
coordination tasks or intelligence tasks. Competency is formed based on the long-term practice in accordance with certain operating 
procedures and rules (Pi, 1996). Mathematics skills are the embodiment of mathematics competencies, which are a series of habitual 
operations that mathematics competencies show in the process of mathematical problem-solving. This expression has been used many 
times in the international large-scale tests such as TIMSS, PISA and NAEP, but the connotation of mathematics skills is not clearly 
defined. 
In the cognitive diagnosis process of TIMSS, there are a large number of studies that divide mathematics skills into 11 attributes 
(Dogan & Tatsuoka, 2008; Tatsuoka et al., 2004; Xin et al., 2004): ①Unit conversion; ②Application of numeric attributes and re­
lationships, number sense, and number axis; ③Using images, tables, graphs and charts; ④Approximation and estimation ⑤Evaluation, 
testing, and checking options; ⑥Modes and relationships (inductive thinking skills); ⑦Use proportional reasoning; ⑧Solving inno­
vative or unfamiliar problems; ⑨Comparison of two or more entities; ⑩ Open-ended items without giving answers and ⑪ Compre­
hension of verbally asked questions. There are also studies that similarly divide mathematics skills into ① Transformation; ② 
Computing applications; ③ Computing applications; ④ Applying algebraic rules; ⑤ Logical reasoning; ⑥ Exploring solutions; ⑦ 
Visual graphics; ⑧ Data management; ⑨ Quantitative reading (Chen, 2012; Chen et al., 2008). Combining the above research on 
mathematical cognitive skills, this research divided the basic mathematics skills attributes into eight attributes, as shown in Table 1. 
2.2. Q-matrix 
The Q matrix is the core component of CDA. The Q matrix builds a bridge between the students’ observable responses and un­
observable cognitive states (Wu et al., 2020). It not only reflects the internal relationship between the test items and cognitive at­
tributes, but also expresses the relationship between the subject’s knowledge state and test attributes, and therefore forms the basis of 
CDA. In this study, a set of TIMSS 2015 Grade 8 mathematics test items was selected as the assessment tool, which is restricted to the 
public and we have obtained approval to use it. Twenty experts were selected to independently calibrate the Q matrix. The consistency 
of the expert calibration has been calculated, and for the items with low consistency, the calibration results are generated through 
expert discussion and agreement. The Q-Matrix was finally formed in Table 2. 
According to Table 2, it can be seen that, except for attribute SR (Spatial Reasoning), which has only 3 items tested, the other 
attributes all had more items tested. Therefore, the attributes are relatively comprehensive in testing.

2.2.1. Model selections 
To find the best fit between CDMs and the data, this study employed the commonly used CDMs: the Deterministic Input, Noisy 
‘‘And’’ Gate (DINA) model (Junker & Sijtsma, 2001), the Deterministic Input, Noisy ‘‘Or’’ Gate (DINO) model (Templin & Henson, 
2006), Reduced Reparametrized Unified Model (RRUM, DiBello et al., 1995), Additive Cognitive Diagnosis Model (ACDM, de La Torre, 
2011), Linear logistic model (LLM, Maris, 1999), log-linear cognitive diagnostic model (LCDM, Henson et al, 2009) and Mixed Model 
(von Davier,2010) for model comparison. A comparison and selection of models play a vital role in the process of CDA (Wu et al., 
2020). Many cognitive diagnosis practices show that choosing an appropriate cognitive diagnosis model is an important prerequisite 
for accurate diagnosis or classification of subjects (Tatsuoka, 1984). The assessment process was implemented using the GDINA 
language package in the software R, and the results were shown in Table 3. 
In the comparison and selection of models, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) are the 
main fit indicators. The smaller the values, the better fit of the model (Vrieze, 2012). It can be seen from Table 3 that the fit effect of the 
Mixed Model is the best. 
From the perspective of relative fit, the Mixed Model has the best fit effect, but this does not mean that the Mixed Model absolutely 
fits the data. Some research believes that CDM should always focus on absolute fit, because absolute model fit is based on the model fit 
of the observed response data without comparison with the other models (Templin & Henson, 2010). The Root Mean Square Error 
(Limited information of The Root Mean Square Error of Approximation, RMSEA2) of the absolute fit index of the mixed model is 
0.0348 (Houts & Cai, 2013), which is less than 0.05, the standard cutoff score for model fit (Hu et al., 2016). According to this standard, 
the Mixed Model is a good fit. 
2.3. Reliability 
CDM usually employs two kinds of reliability tests. One is the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient under the classical evaluation theory. 
Cronbach’s alpha = 0.9079 > 0.7, which has a relatively high reliability. The second is the calculation of the reliability coefficient for 
each attribute developed by Templin and others under the CDA (Templin & Beadshaw, 2013). Templin’s reliabilities for each item 
were shown in Table 4. 
According to Table 4, the test-retest reliability of each attribute has all reached 0.9. Therefore, this test has a high reliability.

3. Analysis of assessment results 
3.1. International comparative analysis 
Seen from the results of previous TIMSS assessments, students in East Asia are in an absolute leading position. The top 5 regions in 
TIMSS 2015 mathematics achievement ranking are Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Japan. The lowest average 
mathematics achievement of these five regions is 586 in Japan, which is 48 scores higher than even the highest score in Russia. 
Therefore, the countries or regions in East Asia showed absolute advantages in the TIMSS math test. Therefore, the data collected in 
mainland China for this study and the data of the above five countries (regions) were used for comparisons of CDA through the Mixed 
Model and obtain the mastery of different attributes in each region. For a clear comparison, the data of the six countries (regions) were 
standardized into Z-score, as shown in Fig. 2. 
In order to further test the differences between Mainland China and other five countries (regions) in mastering different attributes, 
chi square tests were conducted and the results are shown in Table 5. 
Fig. 2 showed the probability distribution of the mastery of basic mathematics skill attributes of Grade 8 students from six countries 
(regions). From Fig. 2, Mainland China has shown obvious advantages in these eight basic skill attributes, in which the probabilities of 
mastering these five attributes Number Crunching, Algebraic Transformation, Shape Operation, Spatial Reasoning, and While Generalization 
were the highest among the six countries or regions. There is still a certain gap with Singapore in the attribute Proportional Reasoning 
and there is room for further improvement. As the No.1 country in mathematics, Singapore showed a clear advantage in all the at­
tributes for the basic mathematics skills, and the performance of each attribute was relatively balanced, all above the average. 
Especially in attribute Proportional Reasoning and Functional Idea, and Data analysis and evaluation Singapore performed the best among 
all the countries or regions, while the performance of attribute Spatial Reasoning was relatively mediocre and needed to be improved.

3.2. Analyses of learning path and learning progression 
3.2.1. Analysis of learning path 
The cognitive diagnostic model evaluates the mastery of different attributes of each student based on their response data and makes 
a diagnosis on the mastery status of each attribute (marked as 1 if mastered, marked as 0 if not mastered). Therefore, each student’s 
mastery of different attributes forms a multidimensional vector composed of 0 or 1, which is commonly referred to as the knowledge 
state. Represented as Ki = (α1, α2, ⋯, αm), αj ∈{0, 1}(j = 1, 2, ⋯, m), where Ki refers to the knowledge state of subject i, m 
represents the number of attributes, 1 indicates that subject i has mastered the attribute j, and 0 indicates that subject i has not 
mastered attribute j. 
The learning path constructed in this article is a hypothetical learning path, not an actual one for students. The hypothetical 
learning path is based on two assumptions: ① the acquisition of knowledge or skills by students is progressive; ② The different 
knowledge states of a group can reflect the developmental stages of each individual. The order of knowledge acquisition can be re­
flected from different knowledge states, that is, αj as elements of Ki, their sequential relationship is the inclusion relationship of Ki (Wu 
et al., 2021). Therefore, knowledge states can be layered based on their inclusion relationships and learning paths can be marked. This 
method has been supported by some studies (Chen et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021). But its actual effect needs further 
practical testing. Through the above process, the learning path was shown in Fig. 3. 
Fig. 3 included the knowledge status of 4444 students, and 289 students were not included in the learning path. The coverage rate 
of the learning path in Fig. 3 was 93.9 %, which was a good representation of the dataset. Seen from the distribution of the entire 
learning path, there were more types of knowledge states in the level 2, 3, and 5, and students had indicated diverse learning statuses at 
these levels. In the level 1, there were 213 students with knowledge status (00010000), which showed that these students had mastered 
the fourth attribute Spatial Reasoning first without mastering other attributes. This showed to a certain extent that Spatial Reasoning was 
relatively easy to master in the student’s cognitive level. There were also main learning paths marked in red in Fig. 3: (00000000)→ 
(00010000)→(00011000)→(00011100)→(00011101)→(01011101)→(11011101)→(11111101)→(11111111). It reflected that in the 
students’ mastery of skills, there existed a cognitive sequence order of Spatial Reasoning→ Proportional Reasoning → Functional Idea→ 
Data analysis and evaluation→ Algebraic Transformation→ Number Crunching→ Shape Operation→ While Generalization. There definitely 
existed other possible learning paths according to Fig. 3 since it was a hypothetical path. The cognitive sequential order of some 
students was different from the logical order of mathematics (Su, 2013). The knowledge status of the students can be obtained through 
learning path in CDA, and then a suitable learning path can be adjusted to individuals based on the students’ existing learning re­
sources, the students’ learning interest, and the teacher’s teaching style. For example, for students with knowledge status (10010000), 
according to the learning path in Fig. 3, students can have two options for further learning. After obtaining the first and fourth 
attribute, this student can learn the second attribute Algebraic Transformation or the eighth attribute While Generalization. Based on the 
hypothetical learning path, this learning process is supported and more reasonable.

4. Conclusion, discussion and limitation 
4.1. Conclusion 
Cognitive Diagnostic Assessment (CDA), as a new type of assessment model, can obtain more personalized diagnostic information 
about students. Based on the TIMSS test, this research has formed a cognitive diagnostic assessment tool to assess the mathematical 
competency of 4733 students in Mainland China. Through the Mixed model, four aspects of analysis were conducted: international 
comparative analysis, learning path construction, learning progression analysis, and personalized report development. Research has 
found that (1) students in Mainland China have significant advantages in most attributes of mathematical competency, except for a 
significantly lower mastery of attribute PR compared to Singapore; (2) There are clear paths for students to learn, with multiple paths 
to choose from, and one of them being the main learning path; (3) A large number of knowledge states are distributed in the second, 
third, and fourth levels of learning progression, and students with intermediate abilities have a complex knowledge state; (4) There are 
significant differences in the quality, quantity, and distribution of attribute mastery among students with the same score. This study 
provides a detailed understanding of mathematical competency of students in Mainland China and their position in international 
comparison, a methodological reference for conducting cognitive diagnostic assessments of mathematical competency, and ideas for 
exploring diagnostic information in large-scale assessments. 
4.2. Discussion 
It is generally believed that the prerequisite for the existence of competency is individual difference. Different people not only differ 
in the types of competency they possess, but also in the degree of mastery of the same competency. Some students progress smoothly 
and quickly in certain fields and show higher achievements than other students in this field. Therefore, this competency can be used to 
describe the individual differences of students in school activities. The Soviet Union psychologist В.А.Крутецкий analyzes the psy­
chological characteristics of students in solving mathematical problems through a variety of methods such as vocal thinking, in-depth 
interviews, case studies, and questionnaires (Bao & Zhou, 2009). Mathematics skills are the embodiment of mathematics competency, 
which are a series of habitual operations that mathematics competency show in the process of solving mathematical problems. As one 
of the most critical elements in solving mathematical problems, mathematics skills are the focus of mathematics education. The 
acquisition of mathematics skills is closely linked to the learning of mathematics knowledge, and the acquisition of mathematics skills 
is usually measured by solving mathematics problems. With an employment of the large-scale dataset TIMSS, this research conducted a 
CDA of the eight attributes of the basic mathematics skills of Grade 8 students in four provinces or cities in China and provided detailed 
information for further understanding of the mastery of basic mathematics skills of Chinese students. Refined feedback for cognitive 
diagnosis provided detailed information for teaching and teaching intervention (Nichols, 1994). At the same time, the results of 
cognitive diagnosis had important implications for education policy and education improvement (Ma, 2014). 
CDA integrates theories of cognitive science and psychometrics to guide the assessment and diagnose the learning status of stu­
dents. As a new generation of evaluation theory, it provides a brand-new diagnostic assessment method, which aims to infer the 
learner’s cognitive skills mastery through cognitive science and statistical models, further form fine-grained reports, and provide 
formative evaluation feedback. The purpose of cognitive diagnostic testing is to infer more detailed information about individual 
attributes, focusing on the knowledge, skills, and cognitive processes required by subjects to solve problems, and their response 
patterns in different test items. Similar to the traditional tests, test evidence and theoretical basis are needed to clarify the basis of item 
responses, so as to support inferences and explanations from the diagnostic information (Yang & Embretson, 2007). 
To introduce the CDA method into the TIMSS mathematical assessment, Chen and others proposed a system process that adapts to 
the large-scale assessment in the CDM environment, which were the construction of attributes and their Q-matrix, assessment of the 
simplified CDMs and validation tests of the selected model (Chen et al., 2013). Tatsuoka and others used the Rule Space Model to 
sample and analyze the mathematical performance of the Grade 8 students with TIMSS in 1999 (Tatsuoka et al., 2004). Dogan and 
others used RSM to study the mathematical performance of Turkish students on TIMSS-R (Dogan & Tatsuoka, 2008). Eunkyoung et al. 
conducted a similar study, comparing the mastery of student attributes in South Korea, the Czech Republic and the United States (Um 
& Dogan, 2003). Lee and others analyzed TIMSS 2007 mathematics to compare the performance of Grade 4 students in Massachusetts

and Minnesota of the United States. They used the IRT and DINA model to compare the models with the information criterion, AIC and 
BIC. The analysis results showed that the DINA model is more informative than that provided by the IRT model (Lee et al., 2011). Their 
research focused on two aspects: item feature identification and attribute mastering investigation. However, the study also had two 
main limitations. One was that it only relied on the field experts to perform attribute specifications without verifying the correctness of 
the attribute specifications; the other was that it assumed that the DINA model was a basic correct model without evaluating the 
model-data fit. Based on the above research, this study constructed a cognitive model of Grade 8 mathematics basic skills composed of 
eight attributes and selected the mixed model as the method of data assessment through model comparison. With absolute fit of the 
data and high reliability, it met the requirements of CDA and formed a relatively standardized case. 
The construction of learning path and learning progression was the main innovation of this research, which formed the basis of 
inferential analysis of CDA. First, this research formed a learning path based on the state of knowledge. It described the relationship 
between the state of knowledge with partial order. It reflected the order of formation of different cognitive attributes of a group as a 
whole. CDA starts from the cognitive process of students’ learning, considers the relationship between the attributes necessary for each 
student to solve mathematical problems, and fully reflects the cognitive law of students’ mathematical problems-solving. This study 
presented this cognitive law in the form of a learning path diagram. According to this path, individualized teaching methods can be 
arranged for different students to provide a basis for different students’ individualized remedial teaching. Secondly, this research 
further formed a learning progression based on the learning path. The progress of students’ knowledge development can be monitored 
according to their learning progressions. However, most of the existing course content arrangement, textbook content compilation and 
examination content setting were based on the logical relationship between mathematics content. The curriculum is arranged ac­
cording to the logical order of mathematics, and the teacher teaches according to the logical order of mathematics. However, the 
students’ cognitive order and the logical order of the textbooks are often not the same. Therefore, a teaching arrangement that deviates 
from the students’ cognition is formed, which has an impact on students’ learning. Based on the learning progressions, it provided 
evidence for rationally arranging courses, compiling teaching materials, and test compilation. Studies have shown that if the cur­
riculum does not coherently help learners to establish connections within and between disciplines or does not form a meaningful 
structure for integrating knowledge, students may be lacking the basic knowledge for future learning and problems solving encoun­
tered in life (Schmidt et al., 2005). The learning progression provides a cognitive sequence for the coherence and consistency of the 
curriculum. It makes possible for the research on the coherence of the curriculum to move from empirical exploration to empirical 
analysis. 
4.3. Limitation 
Although this research strictly followed the design of the cognitive diagnosis test and explored the analysis of learning paths and 
learning progression based on the results of the cognitive diagnosis test, there still existed some shortcomings, and future research is 
needed. First, due to the lack of human, material and financial resources, this study may conduct the same sample sampling as the 
TIMSS test. Therefore, the sample size of this study was relatively small, and the representativeness of the sample was limited. Second, 
the learning paths and learning progressions involved in this research are all constructive (Wu et al., 2021). They considered the 
differences in the skill levels of the same group as different stages of individual development. Although this assumption was supported 
by some research (Chen, 2017; Wu et al., 2020), the results of the research have not been tested in practice. In addition, the conclusions 
drawn in this study are all data driven, and there may be a certain gap with the actual situation. Therefore, follow-up research still 
needs some qualitative research materials to be corroborated to make the research more convincing.
