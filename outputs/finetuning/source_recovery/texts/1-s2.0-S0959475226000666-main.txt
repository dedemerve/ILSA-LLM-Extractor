S T R U C T U R E D A B S T R A C T
Background: Preparing preservice teachers to foster students' critical thinking through productive and 
constructive teaching practices is a central goal in teacher education, yet difficult to accomplish in clinical 
settings. Virtual reality (VR) simulations, featuring artificial intelligence (AI)-powered student agents, offer a 
promising avenue for developing and rehearsing core teaching skills. Despite this potential, limited research has 
explored preservice teachers’ emotional experiences, and their development of dialogic teacher talk in such 
simulations.
Aims: This study investigated emotions and dialogic teaching practices of preservice teachers as they engaged 
with AI-powered virtual students in a VR-supported teaching simulation.
Sample: Twenty-two preservice teachers from teacher preparation programs participated in the study.
Methods: An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was employed. Quantitative data were analyzed using 
data mining and heuristic evaluation techniques, followed by qualitative analysis to interpret emotions and 
patterns of dialogic teaching practices.
Results: The findings suggested that preservice teachers' feelings of “frustration and anger,” accompanied by 
“satisfaction” (i.e., when observing AI-powered “students” improve and make progress), contributed to positive 
emotions such as “joy.” The findings also support the critical idea that the dialogic teacher talk enacted in the 
teaching simulation allowed for many of the activities seen in real word discussions, including probing students’ 
ideas, building on those ideas, and moving toward consensus in group knowledge construction.
Conclusions: VR teaching simulations with AI-powered students can elicit authentic emotions and foster mean­
ingful dialogic teaching practices among preservice teachers. These tools show promise as a complement to 
traditional field experiences in teacher education.
1. Introduction
Preparing preservice teachers to effectively interact with students to 
foster thinking and reasoning is an essential goal in contemporary 
teacher education (Darling-Hammond, 2006; Lampert et al., 2013). 
Teachers are expected to enact teaching practices that develop students' 
knowledge and skills to be successful in an ever-evolving world 
(Darling-Hammond, 2006). One way to achieving this is through ambi­
tious teaching practice (Horn & Garner, 2022; Windschitl et al., 2012, 
2018), which aims to transform teachers' pedagogies by positioning 
teachers to initiate constructive and productive approaches to orches­
trate students' reasoning and thinking for effective learning (e.g.,

emphasizes helping students explore math concepts (Horn & Garner, 
2022) and science phenomena (Windschitl et al., 2018) through the lens 
of their personal experiences and communities, integrating disciplinary 
content with students’ conceptions and resources (Furberg & Silseth,

One of the important qualities of ambitious science teaching is dia­
logic teacher talk (e.g., Dale et al., 2022; Juuti et al., 2020), defined as a 
core teaching practice that emphasizes interactive, idea-centered dia­
logue rather than mere idea transmission (see Section 2.3 for a detailed 
discussion). From a sociocultural perspective (Vygotsky, 1978), dialogic 
teacher talk positions teachers as facilitators of classroom discourse, 
supporting students’ critical thinking through guided interaction 
(Gonz´alez-Howard & McNeill, 2019; Shaughnessy & Boerst, 2018). 
Moreover, ambitious teaching and dialogic teacher talk promotes 
interactive, active, and constructive engagement, rather than passive 
reception of information. This focus on active engagement is consistent 
with the ICAP framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014), which emphasizes that 
deeper learning occurs when teachers guide students to actively 
construct their understandings as they interact with ideas. However, it 
remains a challenge to prepare preservice teachers to effectively practice 
productive dialogic teacher talk when practicing teaching (B¨oheim 
et al., 2022; Lehesvuori et al., 2011).
Although mentoring preservice teachers in the context of field 
practice with dialogic teacher talk is well-founded, the potential “reality 
shock” for preservice teachers when transitioning from teacher educa­
tion courses to field practice is a persistent issue (Veenman, 1984; Voss 
& Kunter, 2020). Veenman (1984) suggested that “reality shock” is “the 
collapse of the missionary ideals formed during teacher training by the 
harsh and rude reality of everyday classroom life” (p. 143). Using virtual 
reality (VR)-supported simulations for approximation of practice 
(Grossman et al., 2009) — “opportunities for novices to engage in 
practices that are more or less proximal to the practices of a profession” 
(p. 2058) — is one way to addressing such issue in preservice teacher 
learning (Cohen et al., 2020; Faldet et al., 2025; Ledger et al., 2022). 
Specifically, implementing artificial intelligence (AI)-powered virtual 
student agents — defined as virtual characters driven by generative AI 
technologies that act as synthetic students, capable of carrying out 
real-time conversations and interacting dynamically with preservice 
teachers (Dai et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2025; Marquez-Carpintero et al., 
2025) — in educational simulations has been increasingly recognized as 
a viable approach in teacher education. These student agents can facil­
itate authentic, core teaching practices, particularly when designed to 
foster dialogic teaching and promote meaningful discourse (e.g., Dai 
et al., 2024; Datta et al., 2021).
While AI-powered virtual student agents provide opportunities for 
preservice teachers to practice dialogic teacher talk in VR-supported 
simulations, an important yet underexplored factor for preservice 
teacher learning is their teaching emotions during the practice. Prior 
research has primarily studied the relation between teachers' emotions 
and teaching in classroom settings (e.g., Trigwell, 2012; Veenman, 1984; 
see Section 2.2). Given this, it seems reasonable that in both field-based 
and simulation-based approximations of practice (Grossman et al., 
2009), teachers' emotions may influence teaching actions and instruc­
tional decisions. However, the increasing use of VR-supported simula­
tions introduces alternative instructional conditions under which 
teachers’ emotions remain insufficiently theorized and empirically 
documented.
In sum, limited studies have investigated preservice teachers' 
teaching practices (i.e., dialogic teacher talk in the current study) in 
simulation-based learning with AI-powered virtual agents; those 
focusing on teachers' emotions during the practice of dialogic teacher 
talk are especially lacking. Understanding how dialogic teacher talk and 
teaching emotions emerge and evolve during practice provides critical 
insights if we are to hone teacher preparation experiences (Dale et al., 
2021; Trigwell, 2012). This understanding constructed by focusing on 
salient or consistently observed patterns of interactions identified in the 
analysis of practice. Specifically, we explored recurring instances of 
teaching emotion (e.g., frustration, joy, satisfaction; see Table 1 for the 
full list) and dialogic teacher talk instances (e.g., displays, 
counter-argues; see Table 2 for the full list), as well as the associations 
between them. In addition, documenting preservice teachers’ practices 
in such simulation is also necessary if the field is to better optimize and 
refine such learning environments (Ledger et al., 2022).
Therefore, this study aimed to explore salient patterns of preservice 
teachers’ emotions and dialogic teacher talk as they practiced teaching 
with AI-powered virtual students in a VR-supported teaching simula­
tion. To do this, we adopted a mixed-methods approach that combines 
association rule mining (see Sections 2.4. and 3.4.1. for details) with an 
explanatory case study design.
Specifically, we ask the following research questions (RQs). 
RQ1. What are the salient patterns of preservice teachers' emotions 
when practicing teaching with AI-powered virtual students in a VR- 
supported teaching simulation?
RQ2. What are the salient patterns of dialogic teacher talk enacted by 
preservice teachers with AI-powered virtual students in a VR- 
supported teaching simulation?
2. Theoretical background
2.1. Teacher education in VR-supported simulation-based learning with 
AI-powered virtual student agents

as valuable tools for teacher education (Cohen et al., 2020; Ledger et al., 
2022; Quintana & Fern´andez, 2015). The ground for simulation-based 
learning can be established in Grossman et al.’s (2009) research on 
how professional practices can be developed through representations, 
decomposition, and approximations of practice. Specifically, unlike full 
enactments of practice, approximations allow learners to rehearse spe­
cific aspects of teaching—such as eliciting student thinking or

preservice teacher education has shown promising results, much of it 
focused on areas such as teacher self-efficacy, classroom management, 
and communication (Ade-Ojo et al., 2022; Dalinger et al., 2020).
Thus, further research is needed on how simulation-based learning 
can support preservice teachers’ enactment of ambitious teaching 
practices, such as student engagement and discussion orchestration, 
particularly in mathematics and science. To elaborate, simulation-based 
learning has been studied in teacher education (e.g., Faldet et al., 2025; 
Huang et al., 2025) and in other disciplines such as language learning 
(Pitura et al., 2025). However, our focus is on ambitious mathematics 
(Horn & Garner, 2022) and science (Windschitl et al., 2018) teaching, as 
this focus offers an established lens for addressing pressing contempo­
rary educational challenges. Clearly, there is a pressing need for further 
research on simulation-based learning with AI-powered virtual students 
for preservice teachers, particularly in mathematics and science.
It has been reported that enacting ambitious teaching practices can 
be challenging due to the complexity and dynamic nature of ambitious 
teaching (Mikeska et al., 2022). Preservice teachers may be accustomed 
to using the initiate-respond-evaluate (IRE) model, which is a more 
traditional approach that guides students’ thinking, reasoning, and 
cognitive engagement in a more structured way (Lehesvuori et al., 2011; 
Mikeska et al., 2022).
Practice-rooted teacher education programs emphasize pedagogical 
reasoning and decision making that are adaptive to the students and 
dynamic in the classroom contexts (Kavanagh et al., 2020). Practice, 
defined from a sociocultural perspective, “incorporates both intellectual 
and technical activities and that encompasses both the individual 
practitioner and the professional community” (Grossman et al., 2009, p. 
2059). To this end, teaching practice is a professional endeavor that 
involves teachers' active attention to the components of practice (e.g., 
students' ideas and resources that integrated their personal experiences 
and communities with disciplinary content) when enacting profession­
ally accepted routines and actions (Furberg & Silseth, 2022). Connecting 
students' ideas and resources, maintaining students’ interests, and 
prompting them to think is another important endeavor. In other words, 
“learning to manage transitions in a classroom is another discrete 
component of teaching practice, one that becomes almost invisible over 
time” (Grossman et al., 2009, p. 2070).
These complex teaching practices can be realized in VR-supported 
simulation-based learning when AI-powered virtual students act as 
both dynamic environmental stimuli and interactive agents to provide 
real-time interactions (Dai et al., 2024; Marquez-Carpintero et al., 2025; 
Zhang et al., 2025). Compared to using human-pupeteered students in 
VR-supported simulation-based learning, AI-powered virtual students 
offer greater flexibility and cost-effectiveness, addressing the challenges 
of intensive human resources while allowing preservice teachers to 
practice whenever the technology is available (Kaufman & Ireland, 
2016; Ke et al., 2020). Despite fruitful research on teacher education in 
VR-supported simulation-based learning (Ade-Ojo et al., 2022), preser­
vice teachers’ practices with AI-powered virtual students have not been 
fully explored.
2.2. Teachers’ emotions in teaching
When preservice teachers are learning to teach, their sentiments and 
emotions are critical aspects to be monitored and nurtured because 
holding low or negative emotions may lead to preservice teachers' 
leaving the profession (Veenman, 1984) along with negative student 
learning outcomes (Hargreaves, 1998; Trigwell, 2012). Specifically, 
Sutton and Wheatley (2003) pointed out the connections between 
teachers' sentiments and emotions and their cognition, motivation, 
students' learning. These connections are complex, and how sentiments 
and emotions shape teachers' teaching depend on the classification and 
types of sentiments and emotions, which remain an open area of 
exploration (Sutton & Wheatley, 2003). Teachers’ sentiments can be 
generally classified as positive and negative (Sutton & Wheatley, 2003; 
Trigwell, 2012). Effective teachers are enthusiastic, confident, and filled 
with positive sentiments and emotions (Hargreaves, 1998; Trigwell, 
2012). Hargreaves (1998) emphasized that effective teachers are deeply 
emotional and passionate individuals who forge strong connections with 
their students, bringing joy, creativity, and a sense of challenge into 
their teaching and classrooms.
Teachers' emotions are dynamic connections between the teachers, 
students, and pedagogies, thus a critical part of transformative teaching 
practices and pedagogical enactment (Hargreaves, 1998). Teachers who 
hold positive emotions are more capable of enacting transformative 
pedagogy, make sense of the phenomenon that go beyond cognitive 
instruction, and take on ambitious teaching practices that embrace 
students' unique contributions in constructing knowledge. Similarly,

2.3. Dialogic teacher talk
According to sociocultural theory (e.g., Vygotsky, 1978), knowledge 
is viewed not merely as an individual asset, but as collectively created 
product and shared within communities. These communities use cul­
tural tools, such as language, both spoken and written, along with re­
lationships and institutions like classroom/schools to facilitate this 
shared understanding (Mercer & Howe, 2012). To this end, in a math or 
science classroom, the teacher plays a facilitator role to foster students' 
knowledge construction processes. That is, conceptual knowledge and 
scientific 
sensemaking 
are 
collaboratively 
constructed 
during 
student-teacher interactions when students throw out new ideas and 
converge resources to gradually build a consensus facilitated by dialogic 
teacher talks and tactical questioning (Chin, 2007). For example, Juuti 
and colleagues (2020)
compared the impacts of dialogic and 
non-dialogic/authoritative teacher talk on students' science learning 
interests. The authors suggested that dialogic teacher talk is more 
effective because it engages students by giving importance to their ideas 
and concerns, which makes the learning experience personally mean­
ingful to the students. Furthermore, it facilitates students’ thinking and 
scientific reasoning by encouraging them to actively participate in the 
conversation and explore different perspectives.
Juuti and colleagues study (2020) highlighted the importance of 
promoting dialogic teacher talk to enhance students’ learning in math 
and science. Nevertheless, practicing dialogic teacher talk is still a 
challenge in the field experience because preservice teachers are prone 
to implement lecturing or teaching with closed questions following the 
IRE-patterns (Lehesvuori et al., 2011).
Despite the challenges, dialogic teacher talk needs to be enacted to 
create and maintain a classroom discourse that encourages students to 
think independently and critically (Reznitskaya & Gregory, 2013). In 
another study, Mikeska et al. (2022) used a simulation-based learning 
environment 
to 
support 
beginning 
teachers’ 
practicing 
of 
argumentation-focused discussions in mathematics and science in an 
elementary education methods course. Their results suggested a signif­
icant effect of simulation-based learning in facilitating preservice 
teachers to develop productive discussion-facilitation skills. However, 
their findings pertain to a single type of dialogic teaching talk (i.e., 
argumentation-focused discussions), suggesting the need to investigate 
additional forms of talk.
Dialogic teacher talk is dynamic, involving interaction between the 
teacher, students, and the classroom ecology, with students' resources 
playing a key role in mediating the teacher's talk moves (Furberg & 
Silseth, 2022). In their study, Furberg and Silseth (2022) emphasized the 
significance of student resources in whole-class conversations, high­
lighting how these resources can serve as mediational tools to support 
learning. The teacher's ability to facilitate productive discussions re­
quires a delicate balance between introducing canonical scientific con­
cepts and making the content personally relevant to students. By 
drawing on students' experiences, ideas, and assumptions, teachers can 
create inclusive learning environments that encourage active 
participation. However, this approach involves the complexities of 
navigating students' diverse perspectives and skillfully connecting stu­
dent resources with academic content.
The distribution of authoritative roles between students and teachers 
is also a key factor in shaping effective dialogic teacher talk. When 
teachers create space for students to express their ideas and relate them 
to scientific concepts, it fosters a sense of ownership and encourages 
deeper engagement with the material (Keys & Bryan, 2001). This 
approach helps to cultivate an environment where students feel 
comfortable sharing their thoughts and building on each other's ideas.
2.4. Assessing teaching practices using natural language processing and 
machine learning
Natural language processing (NLP) is increasingly used to support 
teaching and learning. For example, De Luise and colleagues (2023)
developed an NLP-based conversational frameworks to facilitate natural 
interactions in an intelligent mathematics tutoring system for secondary 
school students. NLP is also used in the educational data analysis that 
informs on education practices and the designs of learning technologies. 
For instance, Dai et al. (2023) used NLP along with unsupervised ma­
chine learning approach to classify learners' interactions in a digital 
learning environment. In another study, Zhang and colleagues (2022)
explored preservice teachers’ collaborative problem solving by con­
ducting NLP and an epistemic network analysis with the discourse data. 
Their findings suggested that higher performing preservice teachers 
engage in more effective negotiations of ideas than their lower per­
forming counterparts during online collaborative problem solving.
Litman (2016) suggested various ways in which natural language 
processing (NLP) can be utilized in educational research. One such 
application involves using language processing to meet the needs of 
students, teachers, and researchers. For simulation-based teacher 
learning, NLP assists education researchers and practitioners in evalu­
ating whether the interventions were designed and developed with fi­
delity and stability across different settings. For example, Anglin and 
colleagues (2021) used NLP to examine preservice teachers’ perfor­
mance in TeachSIM, a mixed reality simulation for teacher learning and 
reported advantages of using NLP in their research context. They also 
proposed several directions for future research such as triangulating NLP 
with different methods and algorithms. Dale and colleagues (2022) used 
automatic speech recognition technology in analyzing teacher talk in 
English Language Arts.
In this study, we leveraged NLP and machine learning as data mining 
techniques to understand teaching with AI-powered virtual students in 
VR-supported simulation-based teacher learning. Specifically, we used a 
data mining technique called association rule mining (Agrawal et al., 
1994) to delineate salient patterns from the datasets. Association rule 
mining has been applied across domains to uncover meaningful patterns 
and relationships in complex datasets, including recommendation sys­
tems and personalized representation learning in e-commerce settings 
(Li et al., 2022) and supporting decision-making in learning environ­
ment design (Xia, 2023). Specifically, Xia (2023) used association rule 
mining to analyze interaction data from an interactive learning envi­
ronment, identifying frequent co-occurrences among learners’ online 
activity patterns and deriving association rules to support personalized 
decision making and adaptive learning environment design. By mining 
the correlations among diverse learning interaction variables, this 
approach provided actionable insights for improving interactive 
learning systems and guiding instructional decisions.

3.2. Simulation-based teacher learning environment with the AI-powered 
virtual agents
To facilitate and support practice-oriented teacher learning, we 
referred to principles of representational scaffolding (e.g., Fischer et al., 
2022), building on approximations of practice (Grossman et al., 2009). 
These principles are grounded in concepts such as agency scaffolds, 
including “adjusting the comprehensiveness of required activities” 
(Fischer et al., 2022, p. 651); for example, the simulation is designed in 
segments as part tasks with limited guidance; and dynamic scaffolds, 
including “adjusting the progression of situation dynamics (Fischer 
et al., 2022, p. 651), specifically, although the practice has no defined 
progression or sequence, participants generally followed a path from 
initiation to consensus building.
We used OpenSimulator, an opensource, accessible VR platform, to 
develop a simulation-based learning environment—Enactive Virtual 
Environment for Teaching practice (EVETeach)(Ke et al., 2021). We 
designed and developed AI-powered virtual students using a fine-tuned 
large language model (LLM). In this study, the pretrained LLM was 
GPT-2 (Generative Pre-trained Transformer 2) (Radford et al., 2019).

During a pretraining orientation stage, preservice teachers were 
introduced to the platform by a trained facilitator. They were informed 
that they could practice teaching within the designated disciplines (i.e., 
mathematics, physical science, and biology). Their primary objectives 
were to initiate the teaching session with the AI-powered virtual students 
and to foster consensus among the students regarding the presented 
concepts and scientific discourse.
3.3. Participants, data collection, and procedures
Twenty-two preservice teachers participated in this exploratory 
study. Three of them were male and 19 of them were female. Partici­
pants were purposefully sampled and recruited from various courses 
within the College of Education at a southeastern U.S. university. 
Eligibility criteria required that participants be enrolled in a teacher 
education program with a focus on math- or science-related areas and be 
18 years of age or older. After signing an institutional review board (IRB) 
approved consent form, each participant completed a VR-supported 
simulation-based online session lasting two to four hours. Each partici­
pant taught math topics including numbers, algebra, measurement and 
geometry, as well as physical science topics such as gas laws, or biology 
topics such as fungi, during the session. The participants voluntarily 
chose the topics they wanted to teach, receiving either a $30 e-gift card 
or research-participation coursework credits after the study.
In the teaching practice sessions, preservice teachers participated in 
an online videoconferencing environment (Zoom) with a facilitator. 
They were first oriented to the session and familiarized with interactions 
in EVETeach (Ke et al., 2021). The facilitator activated the AI-powered 
virtual student agents, allowing the preservice teachers to engage with 
EVETeach's features through screensharing. The preservice teachers 
interacted with virtual students via text-based chat boxes and attended 
to these students' real-time, verbal and non-verbal (e.g., posture and 
gesturing) reactions in the virtual world. All virtual conversations were 
logged by the system, resulting in 1269 records. These logs, collected 
from twenty-two preservice teachers, were analyzed using association 
rule mining, with unit of analysis defined at the sentence level. In 
addition to the computer-logged records, we also gathered data through 
participatory

Drawing from these data and prior literature (Juuti et al., 2020;

3.4. Data analyses
3.4.1. Association rule mining
To address our research questions, we first used association rule 
mining to delineate the patterns governing teachers’ emotions and 
dialogic teacher talk. Association rule mining is a useful technique for 
analyzing datasets and discovering associations between variables or 
attributes (Agrawal et al., 1993). It is traditionally used in market basket 
analysis, with transactional data to provide insights regarding products 
frequently bought together (Agrawal et al., 1993). The rule is typically 
represented as: {X} → {Y}, in which {X} represents antecedent (the item 
(s) or condition(s) that are known to occur), and {Y} represents conse­
quent (the item(s) or condition(s) that are likely to occur given the 
antecedent). Simply put, “if X occurred, then Y is also likely to occur.” 
For example, an association rule states that 90% of transactions that 
include both bread and butter also include milk (Agrawal et al., 1993).
In this study, the itemsets—sets of items that co-occur frequently in 
the dataset—for the association rule mining were the codes and labels 
indicating participants’ demonstrated emotions and pedagogical talk 
moves. These emotion and talk-move codes were delineated using the 
theory-informed and data-driven coding heuristics frameworks (see 
Tables 1 and 2 above).
We then used Apriori Algorithm in Python (apriori-python 1.0.4) to 
conduct the association rule mining (Agrawal et al., 1994). Apriori al­
gorithm is the foundational algorithm for association rule mining. Ac­
cording to Agrawal et al. (1994), apriori algorithm is based on the 
Apriori property, which states that any subset of a frequent itemset must 
also be frequent (i.e., occurrences exceeding a certain threshold). This 
allows the algorithm to discard larger combinations early if any subset is 
infrequent, reducing computational effort. The algorithm proceeds 
iteratively: (1) identify individual items that meet the minimum fre­
quency threshold (1-itemsets), (2) combine frequent items to test pairs, 
triples, and larger combinations, (3) retain only those itemsets that meet 
the frequency threshold at each step.
Based on the suggested Apriori algorithm process (Agrawal et al., 
1994), we first identified individual items that appear frequently (called 
1-itemsets) and then combined those items to see if pairs, triples, or 
larger groups also appeared often. The algorithm kept checking larger 
combinations of items gradually, but only retained the ones that meet a 
minimum frequency requirement, making it efficient by eliminating less 
likely combinations early on.
Basic metrics used to assess the strength of association rules are 
support (Formula 1) and lift (Formula 2). In this study, the support 
threshold was set at 0.01 and the lift threshold at 1.2. The choice of cut- 
off values is context-dependent, balancing rule coverage and practical 
interpretability (e.g., Aggarwal, 2015). A support of 0.01 was used to 
capture meaningful patterns while excluding very rare associations, and 
a lift threshold of 1.2 was chosen to highlight stronger positive associ­
ations. Values above 1 indicate positive correlation, meaning the items 
co-occur more often than expected by chance; the slightly higher 
threshold ensures more conservative and meaningful rules.

a phenomenon and, to a certain extent, explain why and how persons or

Two trained researchers first familiarized themselves with the

4. Results
4.1. RQ1. Patterns of preservice teachers’ emotions in AI-powered VR 
teaching simulations
4.1.1. Association rule mining results
To address RQ1, we first reported the participating preservice 
teachers' emotions with descriptive statistics. The findings suggested 
that preservice teachers were mostly in the “neutral” emotion state (n =
404), followed by feelings of “joy” (n = 371), “satisfaction” (n = 311), 
and “anxiety and uncertainty” (n = 141). Positive emotions (i.e., joy, 
excitement, and satisfaction) (npositive states = 791) were experienced 
more frequently than negative states (i.e., anger, frustration, anxiety and 
uncertainty) (nnegative states = 184). These results suggested that in­
teractions with AI-powered virtual agents tended to evoke more positive 
emotions than negative ones for the preservice teachers. The negative 
emotions mainly stemmed from concerns about departing from teaching 
goals and the uncertainty about achieving them (i.e., nanxiety and uncer­
tainty = 141 is the most frequently observed negative state), highlighting 
that interacting with the AI-powered virtual students had challenged the 
preservice teachers to respond to the students and address virtual stu­

the emotions experienced by the participants. Table 3 presented four

4.1.2. Qualitative results
Qualitative data analysis findings provide deeper insights into the 
emotional dynamics experienced by preservice teachers during in­
teractions with AI-powered students. They further support that the 
emotions experienced by the participating preservice teachers played a 
crucial role in shaping their overall involvement in interacting with AI- 
powered virtual students. Observing virtual students’ progress, which 
elicits feelings of teacher satisfaction, is vital for preservice teachers to 
remain engaged in the teaching process. This, in turn, fosters positive 
emotions such as “joy” (i.e., teachers experienced joy when students are 
responsive and cooperative) and “excitement” (i.e., teachers feel excited 
when students offer unexpected responses or novel ideas). At the same 
time, challenges are prevalent, as preservice teachers inevitably expe­
rience feelings of “frustration” and “anger” when struggling to maintain 
the expected flow of discourse, particularly when it deviates from what 
is perceived as the ideal or expected conversation, a common scenario in 
real classroom settings.
These emotion dynamics suggest that the design of AI-powered stu­
dent responses within the simulation, ranging from cooperative to un­
expected or off-topic contributions, directly shapes preservice teachers’ 
engagement and emotion experiences, highlighting the importance of 
carefully calibrated student agent interactions to support both positive 
and challenging teacher learning moments.
Evident examples emerged from Sage (Participant 02) and Robin 
(Participant 20). Specifically, in multiple rounds of teaching events, 
Sage experienced “anger” when her virtual students repeatedly deviated 
the conversation toward other topics and acted non-participatory, for 
example, in a classroom discussion, Sage attempted to guide students in 
understanding how temperature influences fungal growth. She promp­
ted them to consider both hot and cold conditions, but students 
responded with statements like, “I don't know,” or “I'm still standing 
there.” Throughout the processes of teaching episodes, Sage noticed that 
students were not focused on what she was teaching. She navigated the 
situation and elaborated: “I'm just gonna kind of ignore her because I don't 
want to encourage like sidetracking the conversation.” Sage identified an 
effective strategy that she used to support students in “making progress,” 
which reflected her “satisfaction” with her teaching practices: “I think the 
most effective ones were the ones where I took the words that they knew, and 
rephrased it, because then once they understood the concept they were able to 
build off.” Later, Sage suggested that she could build a conversation 
based on student's responses, when they responded with meaningful 
ideas: “… but a lot of times, like I took what the students were saying and 
rephrase that, except when it was like off topic ….” This pattern indicated 
that unpredictable student interactions in the simulation not only eli­
cited frustration but also prompted adaptive teaching strategies, such as 
rephrasing questions or redirecting discussion, showing how preservice 
teachers learned to navigate emotional and pedagogical challenges in a 
practice environment.
Robin's (Participant 20) experience suggested that she initially felt 
“angry” due to the virtual students' unexpected and disruptive responses 
(she referred to them as “class clowns”): “Yeah, I learned that. With certain 
of the students, I had to go back and re-question them to get them on track. I 
learned that there are class clowns in there.” She also identified virtual 
students that were not actively engaging in responding and contributing 
to the discourse, for instance: “I think it was, was … Thomas didn't 
contribute a whole lot.” Robin's experience demonstrated that encoun­
tering moderate emotional challenges in the simulation can catalyze 
reflective teaching practices, suggesting that simulations which inten­
tionally vary student engagement levels may foster both emotional 
resilience and pedagogical flexibility.
However, she persevered with more ambitious teaching practices, 
continuing to probe into and build on students' ideas. This ultimately led 
to productive and responsive interactions, leaving her satisfied and 
appreciative of both the students' responses and her own teaching 
practices: “… … because I feel like maybe your goal from this is to have 
them actually gain in some knowledge. And, and, and that they can, they 
can learn. And so, it was more important to teach them. You know, and 
so to guide them back into the way that we were supposed to be going 
with this scenario.” She made a reflective summary, acknowledging her 
feeling of anger while also felt joy about students' knowledge con­
struction and progress made: “I mean, there were a couple of them that 
actually ‘irritated’ me. And you have to look at them the same way you do 
with [human] students. I mean, these are, they might have a different 
way of thinking. But it was interesting with Linda that she, she sat there 
very quietly for most of the time, but at the end, when she came out, she was 
very strong, and she had obviously absorbed a bunch of the concepts.” She 
added: “I really enjoyed it. This was fun.”

5. Discussion and implications
In this study, we investigated the emotional patterns and dialogic 
teacher talk of preservice teachers as they engaged in simulation-based 
learning with AI-powered virtual students. The existing literature on 
teachers' emotions highlights their crucial role in shaping teachers' 
professional practices, well-being, and retention within the profession in 
real-world school settings (Hargreaves, 1998; Mo`e et al., 2022; Zem­
bylas, 2002). Our study extends this body of work by demonstrating how 
preservice teachers' emotions emerge in relation to interactions in 
VR-supported simulations, and how the design of AI-powered learning 
environments can actively shape preservice teachers’ emotions and 
dialogic teacher talk during approximations of practice. While positive 
emotions are important—and we observed a predominance of positive 
emotions in our dataset—we emphasize the need for a balance between 
frustration and satisfaction (defined as “teachers gain satisfaction when 
their students make progress in learning”) to foster growth for preservice 
teachers. To elaborate, when practicing teaching, preservice teachers 
encounter a level of uncertainty and engage in continuous experimen­
tation within the simulation-based environment, interacting with 
AI-powered virtual students in VR. Although a purely positive experi­
ence can potentially help to maintain engagement, encountering and 
overcoming moderate challenges are also essential for skills 
development.
While teachers' emotions have been studied in the literature, tar­
geted emotions such as need satisfaction and frustration have primarily 
been explored via self-report questionnaires. As an example, Mo`e et al. 
(2022)'s findings indicate that teachers' need satisfaction is key to 
adopting motivating teaching styles, whereas need frustration tends to 
drive demotivating teaching styles. Our study partially aligns with this 
view but extends it to preservice teachers in VR-supported simulations 
with AI-powered student agents. We argue that experiencing frustration 
can be a crucial learning experience for preservice teachers, but it is 
most productive when combined with satisfaction, which facilitates 
more positive teaching emotions, such as excitement during instruction.
Moreover, our findings highlight the role of the design of AI-powered 
virtual students in shaping preservice teachers' emotional experiences. 
Interactions with these virtual students could either support or challenge 
teachers' emotional regulation, suggesting that designing such a virtual- 
student-based participatory simulation has meaningful implications for 
teacher learning and instructional practice, as an alternative experien­
tial space (Dai et al., 2021; Ledgers et al.,2022) with stochastic student 
behaviors. This space enables preservice teachers to encounter 
emotional dynamics—such as frustration when students deviate from 
expected responses or satisfaction when students demonstrate under­
standing—that closely mirror real classroom experiences. By situating 
preservice teachers in these realistic yet manageable teaching scenarios, 
VR simulations create a valid context to explore how emotional expe­
riences influence engagement, teaching strategies, and professional 
growth. Insights from the study results using association rule mining (e. 
g., Xia, 2023) further indicate that incorporating features that promote 
emotion regulation—manifested through AI-powered student respon­
ses—can enhance preservice teachers’ engagement and foster produc­
tive, emotionally balanced teaching practices.
Additionally, when preservice teachers navigate these challenges 
with “satisfaction,” it can spark excitement and foster continued 
engagement in the teaching simulation, as in the case in field placements 
(Kaya & Schellinger, 2023). This finding can be explained by Flow 
Theory (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990), which posits that optimal learning 
occurs when there is a balance between learners’ capabilities and the 
challenges they face (Quintana & Fern´andez, 2015). In this context, 
AI-powered virtual student agents provide a controlled yet unpredict­
able environment that allows preservice teachers to experience chal­
lenges and successes in a balanced way, supporting emotional 
regulation, engagement, and the development of constructive teaching 
practices.
Our focus on preservice teachers' emotions during episodes of science 
teaching in teaching simulation sessions extends the context and career 
stage of Zembylas's (2004) research, which focused on a teacher with 25 
years of teaching experience in a school context. We align with Zem­
bylas's view that: “… there are two dimensions in emotional labor: a 
positive and a negative one. It seems reasonable to suggest that these 
two dimensions will act distinctly but not completely unrelated with 
regard to the antecedents and outcomes associated with emotional 
labor” (Zembylas, 2004, p. 319). That is, teachers' management and 
navigation of emotions in situ are linked to other emotions that influence 
their teaching actions and experiences during simulations with 
AI-powered virtual students. The emotional dynamics are to be under­
stood as the interplay between teachers' internal emotions and their 
enacted behaviors, which are continually shaped by both their personal 
emotional experiences and the demands of the teaching context.
Regarding dialogic teacher talk, we found it crucial to support pre­
service teachers in adopting ambitious teaching practices—approaches 
in which teachers initiate constructive and productive discussions that 
guide students’ reasoning and thinking for effective learning (e.g., 
Stroupe & Gotwals, 2018; Windschitl et al., 2018).
Consistent with sociocultural perspectives on classroom discourse 
(Mercer & Howe, 2012; Vygotsky, 1978), we found that dialogic teacher 
talk—particularly practices such as probing, building on student ideas, 
and orchestrating consensus—was associated with more productive 
exchanges than directive or lecture-oriented instruction. These findings 
align with empirical studies showing that dialogic approaches foster

deeper engagement and reasoning in mathematics and science class­
rooms (Juuti et al., 2020; Reznitskaya & Gregory, 2013), while high­

Importantly, our study extends this literature by demonstrating how

current study that simulation-based learning environments—when 
designed to foster dialogic teaching—can serve as powerful pedagogical 
tools for supporting both the emotional development and pedagogical 
growth of preservice teachers. By enabling guided discovery alongside 
open-ended practice (de Jong et al., 2024), AI-powered VR simulations 
offer opportunities for preservice teachers to rehearse dialogic teaching 
strategies, reflect on their pedagogical choices, and gradually develop 
the professional judgment required to enact ambitious teaching prac­
tices in complex classroom settings.
Theoretical and practical implications arise from our study findings. 
Specifically, this exploratory study provides a reference for designing 
and developing localized AI systems to enhance preservice teachers’ 
learning experiences (Dai & Ke, 2022). Future research can build on 
these findings to create customizable AI systems that support teacher 
decision making. For example, algorithms and systems could be devel­
oped to recognize patterns in teacher talk and assist teachers when they 
encounter challenges or experience frustration and anger. The findings 
also offer valuable insights for the design of generative AI-powered 
virtual agents. The stochastic nature of fine-tuned virtual student 
agents can be enhanced through advanced reinforcement learning 
techniques and mechanisms such as reinforcement learning with AI 
feedback or human-in-the-loop fine-tuning, allowing for more focused 
interactions while maintaining the openness necessary for human-level 
engagement.
6. Limitations and future research
There are several limitations of the current study. First, given the 
exploratory nature of the study, the sample size was relatively small. 
Although we primarily relied on relatively large amount of session data 
to capture preservice teachers’ actual experiences and observed 
repeated phenomena across different sessions, the small sample size 
remains a limitation. Further, we realize that, related to the sample, the 
use of purposeful sampling from a single region limits the transferability 
of the findings, and the resulting sample may reflect context-specific 
characteristics. However, we argue that this purposeful sample does 
provide an existence proof of claims that can be further tested in future 
research and can inform the design of related investigations.
Second, the categories of emotions and dialogic teacher talk were 
limited to those observed in the dataset and identified in the reviewed 
literature. Future studies may identify a broader range of phenomena 
with regard to teachers' emotions. In addition, future research could 
investigate other emotions and explore whether combinations of posi­
tive and negative emotions support preservice teachers' emotional well- 
being, potentially fostering sustained participation in the profession. 
Additionally, while beyond the scope of this current study, it would be 
important to study how preservice teachers’ experienced emotions 
relate to their enacted teaching practices, such as whether frustration or 
excitement associates with the use of dialogic teacher talk, as this re­
mains an open and promising area of inquiry.
Third, our data analysis method focused on association rule mining 
and qualitative analysis, which revealed associations between itemsets 
and offered insights about the association rules. However, it cannot 
suggest causal effects, and caution should be exercised when interpret­
ing the results. Additionally, due to the nature of association rule min­
ing, it falls short in detecting preservice teachers’ pedagogical practices 
over time. Time series techniques could be applied in future studies to 
explore teaching experiences longitudinally. Fourth, our study is specific 
to teaching practices in simulation-based learning using desktop VR 
with our localized large language model and existing classroom data. 
Future research could explore different VR technologies and contexts.
7. Conclusion
This study contributes to teacher education research by examining 
the use of simulation-based learning with AI-powered virtual student 
agents, focusing on teachers' emotions and dialogic teacher talk. Our 
findings highlight the value of balancing positive emotions (e.g., satis­
faction) and negative emotions (e.g., frustration, anger) in preservice 
teachers’ emotional experiences during VR simulations, suggesting that 
this emotional complexity fosters growth by encouraging continued 
constructive practices with optimal challenges. The study indicates the 
potential promise of practicing productive dialogic teaching strategies in 
simulation-based environments, which supports highly interactive, 
iterative, student-centered, and ambitious teaching practices. The study 
also offers insights into the design of AI systems that support both 
emotional and pedagogical development, proposing that future research 
could enhance these systems by integrating personalized emotional and 
cognitive learner support. Ultimately, this research underscores the 
potential of AI-powered simulations to offer meaningful, dynamic 
learning experiences with ample practice opportunities that prepare 
preservice teachers for modern classrooms.
