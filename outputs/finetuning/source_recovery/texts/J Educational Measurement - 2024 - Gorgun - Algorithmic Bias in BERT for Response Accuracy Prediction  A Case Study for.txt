Prediction: A Case Study for Investigating Population

Guher Gorgun
University of Alberta

Concordia University of Edmonton
Pretrained large language models (LLMs) have gained popularity in recent years
due to their high performance in various educational tasks such as learner mod-
eling, automated scoring, automatic item generation, and prediction. Nevertheless,
LLMs are black box approaches where models are less interpretable, and they may
carry human biases and prejudices because historical human data have been used
for pretraining these large-scale models. For these reasons, the prediction tasks
based on LLMs require scrutiny to ensure that the prediction models are fair and
unbiased. In this study, we used BERT—a pretrained encoder-only LLM for pre-
dicting response accuracy using action sequences extracted from the 2012 PIAAC
assessment. We selected three countries (i.e., Finland, Slovakia, and the United
States) representing different performance levels in the overall PIAAC assessment.
We found promising results for predicting response accuracy using the ﬁne-tuned
BERT model. Additionally, we examined algorithmic bias in the prediction models
trained with different countries. We found differences in model performance, sug-
gesting that some trained models are not free from bias, and thus the models are
less generalizable across countries. Our results highlighted the importance of in-
vestigating algorithmic fairness in prediction models utilizing algorithmic systems
to ensure models are bias-free.
In the last decade, machine learning and data mining methods have become pop-
ular for analyzing assessment data to discover hidden response patterns and provide
a nuanced understanding of response behavior (e.g., Goldhammer et al., 2017; He
et al., 2021; Nagy et al., 2022; Ulitzsch et al., 2022). Computer-based assessments
have facilitated the use of these methods by providing rich information about exam-
inees (Jiao et al., 2023). With computer-based assessments, supplementary informa-
tion, in addition to traditionally captured response accuracy, can be obtained about
examinees. Such supplementary information, typically referred to as process or log
data, may include response time spent for each item (e.g., Gorgun & Bulut, 2021),
the number of actions taken by examinees (e.g., Sahin & Colvin, 2020), hint requests
(e.g., Gorgun & Bulut, 2022b), response changes (e.g., Kroehne & Goldhammer,
2018), action sequences (e.g., Ulitzsch et al., 2022), keystrokes (e.g., Conijn et al.,
2019), or eye tracking data (e.g., Orange et al., 2017).

To date, researchers have leveraged log data to uncover behavioral patterns of ex-
aminees with correct and incorrect responses (e.g., Plewan et al., 2016), investigate
problem-solving strategies (e.g., Peji´c & Molcer, 2021), identify disengaged or un-
motivated examinees (e.g., Yildirim-Erbasli & Bulut, 2021), and detect anomalous
responses (e.g., Shao et al., 2022). These studies typically employed computer al-
gorithms for modeling examinee responses. However, algorithmic systems trained
with historical human data may discriminate against certain groups, leading to un-
warranted inferences about examinees (Kizilcec & Lee, 2022). For example, this
may include assigning unrealistically low scores to a certain group (Smith, 2020)
or not selecting a certain racial group during the admission process due to the re-
liance on the historical data (Kleinberg et al., 2018). In this study, we ﬁrst propose
to use a pretrained encoder-only large-language model (LLM), Bidirectional En-
coder Representations from Transformers (BERT; Devlin et al., 2018), for predict-
ing response accuracy using action sequences extracted from the 2012 Programme
for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) log data. Then,
we aim to investigate algorithmic bias in ﬁne-tuned BERT for predicting response
accuracy in countries that performed well (i.e., Finland), average (i.e., Slovakia),
and worse (i.e., the United States) in the 2012 PIAAC assessment. This allows us
to examine the population validity of the ﬁne-tuned BERT models across countries
with different performance levels. Investigating fairness issues in LLMs is of utmost
importance because LLMs are trained with historical human data and may carry
human biases and prejudices (e.g., Papakyriakopoulos et al., 2020). Recent studies
have highlighted that LLMs may hallucinate and generate incorrect information with
seemingly high conﬁdence (Zhang et al., 2023). Another challenge associated with
using LLMs for modeling response behavior is the lack of model interpretability due
to LLMs being black-box models (e.g., Lin et al., 2023). These models are consid-
ered less interpretable because they were trained with a deep learning-based archi-
tecture. For these reasons, this study not only proposes to use BERT for response
accuracy prediction but also investigates the extent of model generalizability (i.e.,
algorithmic fairness) across countries with different performance levels. The contri-
butions of this study include understanding the feasibility of using a pretrained LLM,
BERT, for predicting response accuracy using action sequences and examining the
extent of algorithmic fairness in LLMs for the model’s generalizability across all
subpopulations.
Literature Review

ﬁles capture each action, sequences of actions, and time spent in each state, process

inee response processes (Tang et al., 2020). Typically, two forms of approaches have
been followed by the researcher when analyzing the process data: (1) preprocess
the process data to reduce dimensionality, such as by creating aggregated features
(e.g., total response time spent on each item or the total number of actions taken;
Gorgun & Bulut, 2022b; Jiang et al., 2024) or (using a dimension reduction tech-

In recent years, researchers have focused on linking response processes (i.e., pro-
cess data) to response products (e.g., response accuracy, not-reached items) (Gold-
hammer et al., 2017) to disentangle problem-solving strategies and identify aberrant
test-taking behaviors. It is found that considering process data improves the estima-
tion of examinee ability levels (He et al., 2019; Liao et al., 2019) and can support
assumptions made about interactive tasks (Goldhammer et al., 2017).
To investigate how problem-solving strategies differed for examinees with cor-
rect and incorrect responses, He and von Davier (2015) examined action sequences
and found that different performance groups have distinct patterns, yet these pat-
terns were consistent across countries taking the PIAAC assessment. Similarly, re-
searchers analyzed the relationship between examinees with different performance
levels and how their action sequences differ from optimal action sequences identi-
ﬁed by experts (He et al., 2021). They found that high-performing examinees pro-
duced action sequences more similar to the expert-deﬁned ones (He et al., 2021).
Other studies corroborated this ﬁnding and found that those with correct responses
showed more efﬁcient problem-solving strategies and were more engaged in the
task (Xiao et al., 2021; Stadler et al., 2019). Whereas those with incorrect re-
sponses showed deviance in their action sequences compared with those who an-
swered the task correctly, and their action sequences were more heterogeneous
(Ulitzsch et al., 2022).
Furthermore, process data have been used for identifying anomalies or outliers
in the data and as a data cleaning procedure to enhance the data quality for sub-
sequent analysis (Aggarwal, 2016; Blázquez-García et al., 2021; Gorgun & Bulut,
2022a). For instance, Liao and colleagues (2021) identiﬁed examinees with aber-
rant test-taking behavior by determining common test-taking processes by employ-
ing the k-means clustering technique and ﬂagging those deviating from the common
test-taking processes as aberrant. In a similar study, researchers employed unsuper-
vised anomaly detection methods (e.g., isolation forest, elliptic envelope) to identify
responses deviating from normal response behavior using the aggregated features
extracted from the log ﬁles of a learning platform (Gorgun & Bulut, 2022b).
Despite the increased use of algorithmic systems for identifying aberrances and
problem-solving strategies of examinees using log ﬁles, studies have not considered
the algorithmic fairness of models developed for assessing generalizability across
different populations. Nonetheless, the population validity of algorithmic models is
fundamental for analyzing examinee responses to ensure that predictive models are
generalizable across different populations.

Algorithmic Bias
The development of algorithms often neglects a crucial aspect: their potential to
perpetuate biased outcomes, thus conserving historical and societal inequalities. De-
spite the wealth of accessible data and advanced analytical techniques, there is a
prevalent misconception that these systems inherently guarantee fairness. However,
similar to human decision-making, algorithms constructed using individual-collected
data are susceptible to biases inﬂuenced by prior experiences and personal beliefs
(Timmons et al., 2023).
Deﬁnitions of algorithmic bias may differ depending on the context, stakehold-
ers involved, and goals of the analysis. A common deﬁnition includes the existence
of discrimination and unfairness in an algorithmic system that results in dispropor-
tionate outcomes for certain groups or individuals (Hutchinson & Mitchell, 2019;
Kizilcec & Lee, 2022). An algorithm is fair when discrimination or inequity based
on group membership is absent. This entails that the algorithm’s decision is indepen-
dent of group membership. Many prediction models are trained with historical data
and may carry biases and societal injustices that may be reﬂected while predicting
outcomes about learners or examinees (Gándara et al., 2023). Especially, when al-
gorithmic systems are used to make high-stakes decisions about different actors of
education (e.g., examinees, teachers), researchers and practitioners need to scrutinize
whether components of the decision-making pipeline are free from bias, discrimina-
tion, or inequity (Kizilcec & Lee, 2022; von Winckelmann, 2023).
Assume that an algorithmic system has been used to detect cheating behavior using
process data. If there is bias in the algorithm toward a certain group of individuals,
wrong inferences and conclusions could be made about this group. For example, if
the algorithm suggests that members of a certain group are more likely to cheat, we
may mistakenly remove the answers of these individuals leading to erroneous perfor-
mance estimations. Continuing in this vein, Xiang et al. (2022) found differences in
model performance across subgroups of learners when two machine learning mod-
els were trained to predict learner success and retention. Recently, students in the
UK have been assigned ﬁnal grades using an algorithm because students could not
take the ﬁnal exam due to the COVID-19 pandemic. They found that some students
received unrealistically lower grades which led to public pressure for re-evaluating
students’ grades (Smith, 2020). This further highlights the importance of scrutinizing
the algorithm’s performance for different groups to ensure the validity of inferences
made.
Two broad families of algorithmic bias have been adopted in the literature
(Awasthi et al., 2020): group fairness and individual fairness. Group fairness is of-
ten operationalized by delineating protected subgroups based on attributes like race,
gender, ethnicity, or sexual orientation. Metrics such as the false-positive rate, false-
negative rate, or accuracy are then employed to gauge fairness, aiming for unifor-
mity in these metrics across all identiﬁed subgroups. In contrast, individual fairness
demands that the model treats similar individuals similarly, regardless of their char-
acteristics. This principle emphasizes a more granular approach to fairness within al-
gorithmic systems. For example, group fairness would involve ensuring that the pre-
dicted performance outcomes of students from different demographic backgrounds

The focus on fairness in algorithms has elevated in recent years as more ed-
ucational settings incorporate artiﬁcial intelligence to model learners. Most stud-
ies analyzed algorithmic bias in prediction models in the presence of class imbal-
ances. These studies found that using an oversampling technique such as SMOTE or
ADASYN helped improve algorithmic fairness (Deho et al., 2023; Jiang & Pardos,
2021; Sha et al., 2022, 2023). Even though oversampling techniques are useful for
improving model accuracy across different populations, researchers also suggested
that more marginalized and underrepresented populations (e.g., racial and gender
groups) should be included in training sets (Baker et al., 2023). In addition to repre-
sentational issues of minority groups, studies also documented biases in prediction
models when individuals have different native languages, leading to the underesti-
mation of examinee performance in assessments or learning environments (Gaskins,
2022; Loukina et al., 2019).
Traditionally, studies have examined algorithmic bias in prediction models by
considering groups based on factors such as gender, age, race, and religion. Yet,
the list of groups or populations can be expanded to align with the speciﬁc pur-
pose of the algorithmic system. For instance, additional factors such as school re-
gion (e.g., urban, suburban, or rural), learner speed (e.g., slow or fast learners),
cultural variations (e.g., native language or home country), and socioeconomic sta-
tus (e.g., low-income students) can be considered (Doroudi & Brunskill, 2019; Li
et al., 2021; Loukina et al., 2019; Ocumpaugh et al., 2014; Sackett et al., 2009).
In the context of international large-scale assessments, it is important to investi-
gate algorithmic fairness in modeling student performance across countries. This
ensures that valid comparisons can be made among countries participating in such
assessments.
Current Study
In this study, we ﬁrst investigate the utility of using an LLM, BERT, for predict-
ing response accuracy employing action sequences extracted from the log ﬁles of
Problem-Solving in Technology-Rich Environments (PSTRE). To date, a few studies
employed BERT for identifying anomalous responses in process data (e.g., Chen &
Liao, 2022; Guo et al., 2021; Landauer et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2022), yet BERT has
not been used for predicting response accuracy using action sequences. As empha-
sized, action sequences provide rich yet unstructured information about the examinee
behavior. In most cases, it is difﬁcult to directly use the action sequences data due
to their high dimensionality. For this reason, researchers may use dimension reduc-
tion techniques (e.g., multidimensional scaling) or may extract useful features (e.g.,
number of actions, duration of actions) from action sequences to be used in machine
learning models. A novelty of this study is that using BERT allows to directly use ac-
tion sequences without the need of using such preprocessing approaches, permitting
us to keep the rich information as is.

BERT is a pretrained transformer composed of token embeddings (i.e., capture the
meaning and context of individual words in a text; Huang et al., 2020), segment em-
beddings (i.e., differentiate between sentences A and B for next sentence prediction
loss; Devlin et al., 2018), and position embeddings (i.e., encode absolute positions
from 1 to a maximum length with each position having a learnable vector; Huang
et al., 2020). For example, in the sentence “The quick brown fox jumps over the
lazy dog,” the token embeddings would represent each individual word as numerical
vectors capturing their semantic meaning and context within the sentence. Regard-
ing segment embeddings, for example, consider two sentences: “The cat sat on the
mat” (Sentence A) and “The dog barked loudly” (Sentence B). Segment embeddings
differentiate between these two sentences and enable BERT to predict the relation-
ship between them, such as whether Sentence B follows Sentence A logically or not.
Regarding the position embeddings, consider a 20-token passage where the posi-
tion embeddings would assign a unique learnable vector to each token based on its
position from 1 to 20. These position embeddings enable BERT to understand the
sequential order of tokens and capture the contextual relationships between them,
regardless of their absolute positions in the input text.
BERT is an encoder-only model and differs from other decoder-style transformers
(e.g., GPT-4 or Llama-2) that are capable of generating sentences, answering ques-
tions, or producing a new output in general. Thus, to re-train BERT for this speciﬁc
task of response accuracy prediction, we ﬁrst tokenize action sequences and add a
linear output layer on the top of the BERT model for predicting the response accu-
racy of examinees taking PSTRE items in PIAAC 2012. This process is also known
as ﬁne-tuning an LLM.
Next, we examine the algorithmic bias in ﬁne-tuned BERT models when countries
with different performance levels have been used as training sets. Speciﬁcally, we
identify top-, average-, and low-performing countries based on the availability of
the log data. The top-performing country is Finland, the average-performing country
is Slovakia, and the low-performing country is the United States. We ﬁne-tune the
BERT model using the overall sample including all countries, only Finland, only
Slovakia, and only the United States, and compare the response accuracy across these
four different models obtained by having different countries as training and test sets.

The purpose of this research question is to investigate the population validity of

Method
Data
Data for this study comes from the log ﬁles extracted from three PSTRE items ad-
ministered in the 2012 PIAAC. PIAAC is a large-scale international assessment de-
veloped by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)
to assess the proﬁciency levels of adults in literacy, numeracy, and problem-solving.
PSTRE items in the 2012 PIAAC target adults’ problem-solving skills and include
14 items in total (OECD, 2019). We selected three dichotomously scored PSTRE
items—U03A, U07X, and U16X because (1) log data extracted from PSTRE items
included action sequences that provided rich information about examinee behavior,
and (2) we can train a binary classiﬁcation model using BERT. The action sequences
included human-computer interactions recorded for each PSTRE item, such as open-
ing a folder, viewing a folder, clicking on a link, or using the help function.
The log data were not publicly available for all countries that participated in the
2012 PIAAC cycle. Based on the overall country performance across all domains, we
identiﬁed three countries with different performance levels1: Based on the availabil-
ity of log data, the top-performing country was Finland, followed by Slovakia located
in the middle of the performance distribution, and the worse-performing country was
the United States. The ﬁrst dichotomously scored item, U03A—CD Tally, selected
for this study was located in Booklet 1 and included nFinland = 1,482, nSlovakia =
1,096, and nUnited States = 1,337 participants. The second and third dichotomously
scored items, U07X—Book Order and U16X—Reply All, selected were located in
Booklet 2 and included nFinland = 1,445, nSlovakia = 1,160, and nUnited States = 1,340,
and nFinland = 1,440, nSlovakia = 1,160, and nUnited States = 1,342 participants, respec-
tively. Across all items included in the study, the distribution of participants from
Finland, Slovakia, and the United States was balanced. The descriptive statistics,
including the number of participants, min, max, mean, standard deviation of item
length, the number of cases with action sequences longer than 512 tokens, and the
proportion-correct scores across all countries are given in Table 1.

Next, we split the data into training and test sets, and 20% of the data is used as the
hold-out test set. We also created subsets of training and test sets based on the country
to train the data with the overall sample (i.e., including all countries), Finland-only,
Slovakia-only, and the United States-only samples as well as to evaluate the model
accuracy on the test set using the overall sample, Finland-only, Slovakia-only, and
the United States-only samples. Following training and test set preparations, we tok-
enized the action sequences for each item using the BERT base cased model. During
the tokenization process, we selected a max token length of 512 and padding = True,
to achieve equal sequence lengths for each action sequence. Thus, examinees with
action sequences longer than 512 actions were trimmed to a length of 512 tokens, and
those with action sequences shorter than 512 actions were added [PAD]—a special-
ized token used to adjust the token length. Concerning Item U03A, none of the action
sequences were longer than 512 tokens, for Item U07X, only one case in the United
States sample has action sequences longer than 512 tokens, and for Item U16X, 26
cases (0.7%) have action sequences longer than 512 tokens (Table 1). Given that no
or only a very few cases have action sequences longer than 512 tokens, we expect
that truncation has a minimal inﬂuence on the models developed.
Based on the tokenization process, we obtained a 768-dimensional embedding
vector of action sequences. The tokenization process also adds two special tokens:
CLS at the beginning of each action sequence and SEP at the end of each action
sequence. We ﬁne-tuned BERT for a downstream task (i.e., classiﬁcation) for pre-
dicting examinees’ response accuracy using the tokenized action sequences. Specif-
ically, using the AutoModelForSequenceClassiﬁcation function, we added a linear
output layer for predicting the response accuracy on the top of the pooled CLS to-
ken. We used a learning rate of 5e-6 and 3 epochs with a batch size of 8. We repeated
the same process using the training data including overall data (i.e., including all
countries), Finland, Slovakia, and the United States for all three items. We then eval-
uated model performance on the hold-out test sets, including the overall data, only
Finland, only Slovakia, and only the United States test sets. This yielded 16 models
per item with a total of 48 models. The preprocessing steps were conducted in R
(R Core Team, 2022), and the analysis was conducted in Python with NVIDIA T4
Tensor Core GPU.

Results
Descriptive Statistics
The descriptive statistics for the items selected are given in Table 1. All items
selected had moderate difﬁculty (.36 < proportion-correct < .67), yet the easiest
item was U16X. Except for item U07X, the other two items were relatively more
difﬁcult for the United States compared to Finland and Slovakia. Item U07X had the
same difﬁculty for all countries included in the study, Item U16X was easiest for
Finland, and Item U03A was easiest for Slovakia.
Regarding the length of action sequences, for Item U03A, the min sequence
lengths were similar across Finland, Slovakia, and the United States, whereas the
average sequence and max sequence lengths were longer for the United States and
Finland when compared to Slovakia. Regarding Item U07X, the min sequence length
was the shortest for Finland, and the max sequence length was the longest for the
United States. Interestingly, although the max sequence length was drastically longer
for the United States compared to Slovakia and Finland, the average sequence length
was the longest for Finland. Although sequence lengths were dissimilar across the
countries, the proportion-correct scores were almost identical. Concerning the ﬁnal
item, Item U16X, the average and max sequence lengths were longer for Finland
compared to the other countries, yet the proportion-correct score was also relatively
higher for Finland. The descriptive statistics suggested that the difﬁculty of the items
across countries was more or less the same, yet the sequence length for some items
was dissimilar across the countries selected.

Model Performance across Different Training and Test Sets
We evaluated the model performance when different training and test sets were
used by accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 measures. Tables 2–4 provide results
of model performance when trained with Finland-only, Slovakia-only, the United
States-only, and overall data using all countries in the dataset.
Item U03A. The performance of models trained with Finland, Slovakia, and the
United States, and the overall data were similar for predicting Finland or Slovakia-
only test sets (Table 2). However, we observed different model performances for pre-
dicting the United States and overall test sets. Speciﬁcally, the precision was signiﬁ-
cantly low when Slovakia was used for predicting response accuracy for the United
States test set, yet recall was signiﬁcantly higher for the same training-test set pair.
Additionally, precision and accuracy were relatively low when Finland was used as
the training set to predict response accuracy in the overall test set. Overall, for Item
U03A, the models trained were performed similarly across different test sets used.
Item U07X. For Item U07X, we did not have similar results to Item U03A, and
we observed differences in model performance when different training and test sets
were used (Table 3). For predicting response accuracy in the Finland test set, we
found that Finland, the United States, and the overall data performed relatively sim-
ilar (Precision was between .75 and .78, Recall was between .80 and .87, and Ac-
curacy was between .82 and .84); however, when Slovakia was used as the training
set, precision (Precision = .62) and accuracy (Accuracy = .75) dropped while recall
increased (Recall = .93) signiﬁcantly. For predicting response accuracy in Slovakia
only test set, we observed that Finland performed the worst (Precision = .86, Re-
call = .26, F1 = .42, and Accuracy = .64) compared to other models trained with
Slovakia, the United States, and overall data. The same trend was observed for the
United States and the overall test sets where Finland was used as the training set.
Speciﬁcally, the recall values were lower when Finland was used for training the
model. Overall, there were more differences among the models trained, and speciﬁ-
cally the model trained with the Finland only training set performed worse for pre-
dicting other test sets except for Finland.
Item U16X. An interesting pattern emerged for Item U16X (Table 4). For all test
sets, the model trained with the overall dataset including all countries performed
better than using a single country as the training set. Speciﬁcally, the accuracy of
the overall dataset for predicting response accuracy in Finland, Slovakia, the United
States, and overall data was .91, .93, .86, and .86, respectively, whereas the accuracy
ranged between .73 and .83 when Finland, Slovakia, or the United States was used as
a training set. Therefore, using the overall dataset was better for predicting response
accuracy in Item U16X, suggesting that researchers may beneﬁt from using the total
datasets when training prediction models for different countries. Yet, we need to
underscore that this is not a uniform observation, highlighting the importance of the
trial-error process in data-driven approaches.

Discussion
In recent years, an increased number of research studies have leveraged LLMs
to develop prediction models for learner modeling, automated scoring, automated
item generation, and prediction (e.g., Attali et al., 2022; Beseiso & Alzahrani, 2020;
Chen & Liao, 2022). Yet, a major pitfall of LLMs is that they are trained with his-
torical human data using a deep learning architecture (Yan et al., 2023) and may
carry human biases and prejudices. Historical human data and less interpretability
inherent in LLMs may lead to unfair and discriminatory prediction models against
certain population groups. Our purpose was twofold in this study: We examined the
utility of BERT for predicting response accuracy using action sequences without the
need for preprocessing the log data extracted and algorithmic bias in the ﬁne-tuned
BERT models for response accuracy prediction when different countries were used
as training data.
Our results indicated that pretrained LLMs could be re-trained for predicting re-
sponse accuracy using action sequences directly, especially when the overall data
including all countries (i.e., Finland, Slovakia, and the United States) were used.
This ﬁnding is consistent with previous research investigating the associations be-
tween action sequences and response accuracy (e.g., He et al., 2021; Ulitzsch et al.,
2022). We also illustrated that action sequences modeled with pretrained LLMs pro-
vide very accurate predictions regarding response accuracy. These models can be

solving items to nudge learners regarding whether their action sequences are feasible
for correctly answering an item or to provide feedback and tutoring services during
the assessment (Heffernan & Heffernan, 2014). Thus, using action sequences as early
warning models for identifying the failure or misconception of learners could be an
extension of this type of modeling approach. Furthermore, these models linking re-
sponse accuracy to action sequences can provide another strand of validity evidence
(e.g., Zumbo & Hubley, 2017). We expect that examinees who know the content well
deliberately select their actions leading to the correct response in a shorter amount
of time (Ulitzsch et al., 2022). Therefore, we had a promising result for the utility
of BERT for response accuracy prediction using only action sequences. Note that
action sequences are unstructured and high-dimensional data. Because processing
action sequences directly (e.g., without using dimension reduction techniques) re-
quires higher computational power and might be difﬁcult to directly use in prediction
models, previous research has typically extracted aggregated features (e.g., number
of action sequences, total response time; Gorgun & Bulut, 2022b) or used dimension
reduction techniques (e.g., multidimensional scaling; Tang et al., 2020). Nonetheless,
our proposed approach provided a feasible method to directly use action sequences
in prediction models. This result also provides evidence for using pretrained LLMs
for prediction tasks that do not necessarily involve language-related prediction (e.g.,
essay scoring vs. response accuracy).
In the second fold of the study, we investigated the presence of algorithmic bias
in the ﬁne-tuned BERT models when different training data were used. Speciﬁcally,
we investigated the population validity (i.e., group fairness; Awasthi et al., 2020) of
BERT-based prediction models when different training sets were used. We found
differential results regarding the existence of bias in the ﬁne-tuned models. For ex-
ample, for the ﬁrst item, U07X, models performed very similarly regardless of the
training or test data used. However, this ﬁnding did not hold for Item U16X when
different training and test data were used. This underscores that each item and sub-
population should be scrutinized separately in order to fully capture the extent of
algorithmic bias occurring in a given assessment. Furthermore, we also found that
the accuracy of the models was highest when the overall sample where all countries
included was used for training the prediction models. This is consistent with previous
studies emphasizing the importance of sample size and better representation of sub-
groups in the training data to enhance model performance and minimize the bias in
the prediction model (e.g., Baker & Hawn, 2022; Deho et al., 2023; Kizilcec & Lee,
2022). Achieving higher accuracy when the overall sample was used could be due
to having almost a balanced distribution of countries, where class imbalances were
minimized (e.g., Deho et al., 2023; Sha et al., 2022). Yet, in studies incorporating
class imbalances among the subgroups of interest, researchers may use techniques
such as random oversampling, SMOTE, or ADASYN to achieve a balanced class
distribution to better represent all subgroups, especially when a high-stakes decision
is to be made about these subgroups. Furthermore, the lower performance of predic-
tion models when individual countries are used could be due to reduced sample size
(Zhang et al., 2020).

The ﬁndings from this study can offer actionable insights with practical impli-
cations for educational stakeholders. Our exploration of algorithmic bias across
different training sets sheds light on the challenges and opportunities in ensur-
ing fairness in predictive modeling. The observed small accuracy differences be-
tween countries highlight the potential for minimizing bias through careful training
data selection and model ﬁne-tuning. In practice, our ﬁndings suggest that training
prediction models using data from all countries can lead to improved model per-
formance and reduced bias. This reiterates the importance of inclusivity and repre-
sentativeness in training datasets, especially in international educational assessments
like PIAAC. It is important to underscore that we focused on group fairness as we
compared the prediction accuracy of the ﬁne-tuned BERT model across different
countries participating in the PIAAC assessment. This could be a viable method
when the goal is to make inferences about groups rather than individuals (i.e., pop-
ulation validity of an algorithmic system). This approach needs to be further reﬁned
when the goal is to assess the fairness of an algorithmic system across individuals.
To this end, researchers or practitioners may consider individual-level features (e.g.„
overall ability, gender, race) to test whether the language models are fair across indi-
viduals with similar traits. For instance, an algorithmic system is considered fair at
the individual level when individuals at the same ability levels are predicted with
similar model accuracy, regardless of their race or gender. Such characteristics are
less relevant when the focus is at the group or population level, where an algo-
rithm is fair if the prediction accuracy is similar across the groups. Therefore, re-
searchers and practitioners should carefully delineate the target of the inference in
order to ensure that an algorithmic system is fair. Additionally, our study emphasizes
the importance of investigating algorithmic bias as one strand of validity evidence
when algorithmic models are used for making conclusions about populations or
individuals.
Furthermore, our results highlighted the need for a careful data curation process
for accurately representing subgroups and being careful regarding the extent of pre-
dictive model utility when diverse subgroups are included in the decision-making
process. While modeling action sequences with language models seems a feasible
approach for response accuracy prediction, the utility of such models should be repli-
cated across different subgroups. As researchers use action sequences in predictive
models, it is worthwhile to investigate when an action sequence becomes informative
regarding the response accuracy (i.e., after how many actions taken by the examinee)
to develop effective and preventive intervention models.
Limitations and Future Directions
Our study has some limitations. First, we used only three countries and only three
PSTRE items to investigate the utility of using BERT for response accuracy predic-
tion and the presence of algorithmic bias in the trained prediction models. Future
research may beneﬁt from including other countries as well as other PSTRE items to
examine the algorithmic bias in the prediction models developed. Another limitation
of the study is that the maximum number of tokens for the BERT base model is 512
and action sequences of those longer than 512 actions were truncated. This might

differences in the length of action sequences between countries included in this study.
While this could be due to various reasons, such as achievement level differences,
educational systems, or curriculum differences, future research may beneﬁt from un-
derstanding the root cause of this difference. Finally, we have not discussed ways to
mitigate algorithmic bias in the prediction models developed using different coun-
tries. Future studies should also consider not only the presence of algorithmic bias,
but also methods to alleviate bias in the prediction models.
