A B S T R A C T
International comparative assessments are crucial for examining country-level educational changes over time, 
but requires a comparable achievement scale. This study develops a unified science achievement scale that links 
the First and Second International Science Studies with the Trends in International Mathematics and Science 
Study for upper primary school students. The science measures in each study are analyzed with respect to 
construct consistency, inferences, population differences, and test conditions. Analyses showed that, despite 
some evolution in frameworks and a transition from age-based to grade-based definitions, the core constructs 
remain consistent, enabling similar inferences. By identifying common items among the three studies, we 
rigorously assessed measurement invariance using item response modeling, with partial invariance models 
supporting reliable long-term comparisons. We applied latent regression models using contextual student 
data, creating imputed scale scores for each student. These results enable vast further research to investigate 
long-term inequalities in educational outcomes.
1. Introduction
For over 60 years, international comparative educational assess-
ments have provided essential data for researchers and policymakers to 
monitor and evaluate the performance of educational systems and the 
association between performance and variables pertaining to students, 
parents, teachers, and principals. These assessments enable meaningful 
comparisons across different cycles and education systems globally. 
International large-scale assessments (ILSAs) have had a profound im-
pact on educational policy and school accountability (Ehren, 2022) 
while also attracting global interest in political discussions and societal 
debate (Carvalho & Costa, 2014; Liu & Steiner-Khamsi, 2022; Nóvoa & 
Yariv-Mashal, 2003).
Although international large-scale assessments (ILSAs) were first 
introduced in the early 1960s, they have since evolved significantly 
in terms of participating countries, assessment governance, and overall 
design (Ehren, 2022). Despite these changes, ILSAs remain highly 
relevant for comparative studies that examine long-term trends by in-
tegrating early assessments with more recent ones. Research examples 
include studies of gender achievement disparities over 40 years (Rosén 
et al., 2022; Steinmann et al., 2023), socioeconomic status achievement 
gaps analyzed using various ILSAs over more than 50 years (Broer 
et al., 2019a; Chmielewski, 2019; Hanushek et al., 2022), trends in 
motivation scales towards mathematics over 30 years (Majoros et al.,

One of the primary organizations that conducts widely discussed

International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achieve-
ment (IEA). The IEA evaluates primary and secondary school students 
across various domains, including reading (PIRLS), mathematics and 
science (TIMSS), and civic education (ICCS). Contemporary trends in 
educational assessment in mathematics and science often reference the 
Third International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS), conducted 
in 1995, linking subsequent assessments from 1999 to the present. 
However, connecting these recent studies with the earlier First Interna-
tional Science Study (FISS) from 1970–71 and the Second International 
Science Study (SISS) from 1983–84 for upper primary school students, 
specifically students aged 10 to 11 years, has not been done. Exist-
ing research has only assessed cross-system comparability in the FISS 
and SISS for this age group, which identified substantial violations 
of measurement invariance (Sosa Paredes & Andersson, 2024). This 
suggests that direct cross-country comparisons are infeasible in FISS 
and SISS but points to the opportunity to analyze trends over time 
within each system. While current TIMSS scales cover the period from

There are several successful examples in the literature of link-
ing early large-scale assessment studies with more recent ones. Re-
searchers aimed to link scores from earlier reading studies for fourth 
graders (Strietholt & Rosén, 2016), mathematics studies for eighth 
grade students (Afrassa, 2005; Majoros et al., 2021), and science studies 
for middle school students (i.e., fourteen-year-olds) (Majoros, 2023). 
However, no attempts have been made to link science studies over time 
at the upper primary school level (i.e., for ten-to-eleven-year-olds).
This paper addresses this gap through three primary contributions. 
First, it examines the validity of comparing science achievement scores 
across the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS in multiple educational systems, to 
ensure that these comparisons are meaningful and supported by their 
respective theoretical frameworks. Second, it links the scores of these 
studies using current methodologies applied in contemporary ILSAs, 
thereby creating a long-term trend scale for science achievement and 
establishing a framework for linking early assessments to more recent 
studies. Third, it provides researchers with a unique science scale that 
leverages the richness of early IEA data and serves as a foundation 
for future secondary analyses, considering that these early studies also 
include valuable information on various student and school-related 
variables, as highlighted in previous research examples. For instance, 
researchers could now examine long-term science achievement gaps 
based on socioeconomic status (SES) using data from early studies, 
yielding absolute gaps rather than relying on relative variations with 
standardized scores. In addition, it is possible to extend the work 
by Broer et al. (2019b), who examined SES-achievement gaps in TIMSS 
from 1995 to 2015 among 8th-grade students in science and mathemat-
ics across various educational systems, to include 4th-grade students 
as well. Thus, the advantage of using this comparable scale is that 
it facilitates interpretable and robust calculations of science achieve-
ment differences, allowing researchers to study long-term educational 
inequalities along with other contextual variables.
1.1. The present study
In this study, we aim to link the early science studies (FISS and 
SISS) with TIMSS to offer a comparable long-term scale of science 
achievement for upper primary school students. This scale will facil-
itate investigations into long-term educational trends, which enables 
comparisons of achievement both in a relative and absolute sense.
To achieve this, we will first evaluate measurement invariance 
between FISS, SISS, and TIMSS in several educational systems using 
item response theory. Next, we will link the science scores by using 
anchor items across the studies and construct plausible values using 
estimated latent regression models. Finally, we will present science 
performance trends for each selected system, adjusting these trends 
based on the average age of the students and grade level. Additionally, 
we will outline several potential uses and applications for the proposed 
scale.
The study intends to answer the following research questions:
1. To what extent can science achievement, as defined by the IEA, 
be compared across the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS 1995 in the 
selected educational systems?
2. To what extent can we link the science scores onto the same 
scale across the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS 1995 in the selected 
educational systems?
3. How do trends in science achievement vary across the FISS, SISS, 
and TIMSS 1995 in the selected educational systems?
Before proceeding with the statistical analyses, it is necessary to 
establish a qualitative foundation for the study. This foundation in-
cludes examining the conditions that influence our study’s validity. 
We will thus describe the degree of similarity across the three stud-
ies, focusing on constructs, inferences, populations, and measurement 
characteristics (Kolen & Brennan, 2014). This evaluation will ensure 
that the comparisons made in this research are grounded in a solid 
understanding of the underlying theoretical frameworks while also 
identifying potential limitations. In addition, identifying common items 
across the studies is necessary to enable a linking across them. We will 
therefore examine the items of the studies and identify which items 
should be considered as anchors to link them.
2. Examining the comparability of the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS
Here, we conduct a careful review of the assessment frameworks of 
the three studies FISS, SISS, and TIMSS as a prerequisite to proceeding 
with the quantitative analysis. We first examine the degree of similarity 
across the studies using an established framework (Kolen & Brennan, 
2014). Then, we identify common items across the three studies to 
evaluate their equivalency and potential inclusion as anchors in our 
analyses.
2.1. Degrees of similarity
The framework proposed by Kolen and Brennan (2014) includes 
four key points: constructs, inferences, populations, and measurement 
characteristics of the tests. We thus aim to answer the following four 
questions, largely based on the technical reports of the respective ILSAs: 
(a) To what extent do the tests assess similar constructs?, (b) To what 
extent were the assessments designed to draw similar inferences?, (c) 
Were the assessments intended for the same target populations?, and 
(d) To what extent were the testing conditions similar?
2.1.1. To what extent do the tests assess similar constructs?
The assessment frameworks of the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS have 
evolved over time, although all three assessments consistently measure 
similar science constructs. These updates are primarily reflected in 
the updates of the curriculum grid or matrix of dimensions used to 
categorize curriculum units or achievement items.
The FISS framework was developed through extensive analysis of 
science education, with input from representatives of multiple coun-
tries. This framework employed a two-dimensional curriculum grid 
based on the well-known study titled Taxonomy of educational ob-
jectives (Bloom et al., 1956), designed to classify learning objectives 
into content — the specific subject matter to be achieved — and 
hierarchical behavioral dimensions, which denote the cognitive skills 
students should have when faced with the tasks (Bloom, 1969; Comber 
& Keeves, 1973; Kind, 2013). The FISS framework included four content 
categories: Earth sciences, biology, chemistry, and physics; and four hi-
erarchical behavioral categories: functional information, understanding 
or comprehension, application, and higher-order processes (Comber & 
Keeves, 1973).
Building on the FISS, the SISS introduced refinements while main-
taining consensus on the proposed grid across participating countries. 
The SISS framework was based on Klopfer’s study (Klopfer, 1971), 
who slightly updated the two-dimensional curriculum grid used in 
the FISS (Rosier, 1987; Rosier & Keeves, 1991). Klopfer’s matrix re-
defined the behavioral dimension. It not only included the categories 
proposed by Bloom but also aspects related to curriculum structure. 
As a result, the redefined dimension included Bloom’s behavioral cate-
gories, processes of scientific inquiry, skills of performing laboratory 
work, students’ attitudes toward science, and students’ orientations 
to the relationships of science to other aspects of culture and to the 
individual (Kind, 2013).
However, the inclusion of more categories within the same dimen-
sion introduced a ‘‘dimensionality problem’’, complicating the hierar-
chical structure that Bloom proposed. In addition, some of the new 
categories did not fit neatly into the traditional content-behavior grid.

For instance, categories such as scientific inquiry and orientation posed 
challenges: the author noted the difficulty in determining whether 
these should be classified under ‘‘behavior’’ or ‘‘content’’ dimension. 
This ambiguity resulted in a framework that resembled a list of objec-
tives rather than Bloom’s hierarchical interpretation of the cognitive 
behavior dimension (Kind, 2013).
Despite these issues, the solution was to relabel the behavioral 
dimension as the objective dimension and renaming the new cate-
gories to align them more closely with previous behavior categories. 
Thus, the SISS framework retained the same four content categories 
while introducing a simplified version of the objective dimension pro-
posed by Klopfer, which now included three categories: information, 
comprehension, and application (Rosier & Keeves, 1991).
TIMSS continued the approach of previous IEA studies, but they 
addressed the dimensionality problem posed by Klopfer’s matrix. The 
TIMSS committee resolved this issue by subdividing the objective di-
mension into two components: performance expectations and perspec-
tives. The TIMSS framework then expanded the concept of content 
and objective/behavior matrix to three dimensions: (subject-matter) 
content, performance expectations — which combined Bloom’s behav-
ior categories with scientific inquiry processes — and perspectives, 
including attitudes and orientation (Kind, 2013; Martin, 1996). In 
this new framework, the performance expectations dimension was 
intended to describe, in a non-hierarchical way, the types of perfor-
mance or behaviors that a given item might generate as responses 
from students (Martin, 1996), differing from the previous hierarchical 
approach. Meanwhile, the perspectives aspect was relevant for analyz-
ing documents like textbooks, allowing the categorization of curricular 
components according to the discipline’s nature as reflected in the 
context in which it is presented (Martin, 1996).
The content dimension in TIMSS included earth sciences, life sci-
ences, physics, and environmental issues and the nature of science; 
while the performance expectations dimension encompassed (a) un-
derstanding; (b) theorizing, analyzing, and solving problems; (c) using 
tools, routine procedures and science processes; and (d) investigation of 
the natural world. The perspectives dimension addressed attitudes, ca-
reers, participation, increased interest, safety, and habits of mind (Mar-
tin, 1996). Importantly, for test development purposes, TIMSS used 
two dimensions of the framework: content and performance expecta-
tions (Garden & Oprwood, 1996)
One additional difference between the SISS and TIMSS frameworks 
is that SISS delineated three curriculum levels: intended (national 
educational goals), implemented (teachers’ classroom practices), and 
achieved (students’ learning and attitudes). In contrast, TIMSS retained 
these levels but renamed the achieved level to the attained level.
In summary, all three assessments share a core structure consisting 
of two dimensions: content and behavioral/objective or performance 
expectations. Although TIMSS introduced a new content category (envi-
ronmental issues and the nature of science), the assessments maintain a 
significant focus on biology or life sciences, earth science, and physics, 
aligning with similar behavioral/objective or performance expectations 
categories. Table 1 includes the distribution of items (in percentages) 
across content and behavioral/objectives or performance categories in 
the three studies.
2.1.2. To what extent were the assessments used to draw similar inferences?
The FISS, SISS, and TIMSS assessments share a similar purpose 
of investigating the performance of various educational systems in 
science, with the SISS also designed to track changes between the FISS 
and SISS administrations. The FISS aimed to evaluate science education 
globally, standardizing the measurement of students’ scientific knowl-
edge and skills. Its goal was to assess the effectiveness of different 
educational practices and curricula, ultimately informing international 
educational policy (Comber & Keeves, 1973). Expanding the scope of 
the FISS, the SISS examined changes in educational systems and in-
cluded variables such as curricular emphases and pedagogical practices, 
enabling more nuanced inferences about factors potentially influencing 
science achievement. It also sought insights into the impact of different 
educational contexts on student learning outcomes (Rosier & Keeves, 
1991). TIMSS continued this comparative approach by focusing on 
student achievement in mathematics and science. It facilitated inter-
national comparisons while examining background variables related to 
the social and cultural contexts of learning, enhancing interpretations 
of student performance (Adams & Gonzalez, 1996).
In summary, despite variations in approach, the FISS, SISS, and 
TIMSS fundamentally aimed to derive similar inferences. They were 
all designed to be informative, low-stakes, and valuable for examining 
changes in science achievement for various educational systems over 
time and their relation with educational and contextual variables.
2.1.3. Were the assessments designed for the same target populations?
All the studies originally addressed several target populations. This 
analysis focuses on the definition of population 1 across all the studies, 
representing students aged 10 to 11 years old for the FISS and SISS, 
and students in grades 3 and 4 in TIMSS. Table 1 summarizes the 
definitions of the target population and the number of educational 
systems that officially participated, along with the number selected for 
this investigation. A notable change is the transition from an age-based 
to a grade-based definition in TIMSS.
Despite this change, our study utilizes all the available data of 
defined population 1 from educational systems that participated in both 
the FISS and SISS, as well as those involved in the SISS and TIMSS 
1995. This decision is motivated by the need to address gaps in the 
analysis of early science education data for this targeted population, 
as comparative studies have largely concentrated on older groups of 
students. We acknowledge that this shift can impact mean score gaps 
due to differences in age. To mitigate this, we present adjusted trends 
based on grade level and average age of the students. Table 2 displays 
the age distribution in each selected system.
2.1.4. To what extent were the test conditions similar?
We examined the similarities in test conditions across the studies, 
focusing on three aspects: (1) test design, (2) test length and number 
of sessions, and (3) test and item format. It is important to note that 
all three studies employed standardized procedures based on specific 
guidelines and utilized a pencil-and-paper format in classroom settings, 
divided into two testing sessions. These features represent the main 
common test conditions across the assessments.
In FISS, students completed two 30 min sessions consisting of 
two blocks (A and B) with 40 binary-scored multiple-choice items 
administered uniformly (Bloom, 1969). In contrast, the SISS included 
five blocks: one core (M) with 24 items and four rotated (A, B, C, 
D) with eight items each. Similar to the FISS, all items in the SISS 
were binary-scored multiple-choice. Students typically responded to 
two rotated blocks alongside the core block (Postlethwaite & Wi-
ley, 1992). TIMSS introduced a rotated block design format with 17 
blocks: one core (A) and 16 rotated blocks categorized into focus, 
breadth, and free-response. This structure allowed for up to eight 
different test booklets, with varying item numbers based on estimated 
response times. For the targeted population, two individual testing 
sessions lasting 37 and 27 min were administered, with a 15 to 20 min 
break in between. TIMSS uniquely assessed both mathematics and sci-
ence within the same instrument and included multiple-choice, short-
answer, and extended-response items totaling 97 items (93 binary and 
four polytomous) (Adams & Gonzalez, 1996).
In summary, while test conditions such as length, pencil-and-paper 
format, number of sessions, and standardized administration were con-
sistent across the studies, notable differences emerged in test design and 
item types. Table 1 summarizes the comparison of the studies based on 
these factors.

2.2. Summary of degrees of similarity analysis
Overall, the curriculum frameworks of the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS 
consistently focused on similar science constructs, primarily utilizing a 
two-dimensional matrix based on Bloom’s taxonomy, with refinements 
in the cognitive dimension, namely behavioral/objectives or perfor-
mance expectations. Each study aimed to assess student science per-
formance within educational systems while maintaining a low-stakes 
testing environment. Notably, a significant divergence occurred with 
the definition of the target population shifting from age-based to grade-
based, which may introduce biases in trend analysis and necessitate 
adjustments for further mean comparisons.
Furthermore, while the testing conditions of the three studies were 
largely consistent — each employing a pencil-and-paper format, a 
similar number of sessions, and standardized administration — there 
were noticeable variations in test design and item types. For instance, 
the original two-booklet format evolved into a more complex rotated 
matrix design; in addition, TIMSS included some polytomous items and 
open-response formats. Ultimately, TIMSS integrated the mathematics 
and science assessment into the same test administration.
2.3. Identification of common items
The identification of common items centered on textual consistency 
in questions and alternatives, the number of alternatives, and content 
coverage. To conduct this analysis, we requested the TIMSS 1995 in-
struments from the IEA. This request was made under a confidentiality 
agreement, ensuring that all materials would be used solely for the 
research objectives outlined in this study. The instruments used in 
the FISS and SISS are available in an online repository (University of 
Gothenburg, 2025).
We identified by visual inspection common items across the three 
studies. First, the core block in SISS contained 24 items, with 22 of 
them being extremely similar to those in FISS. While there were minor 
differences, such as the number of alternatives — five (a to e) in FISS 
and four (a to d) in SISS — the item description remained largely 
the same. For instance, one item in FISS stated, ‘‘The student ate four 
apples (...)’’ while in SISS, it was phrased as ‘‘Maria ate four apples 
(...)’’. Despite these slight variations, the quantity of common items 
represented nearly half of the FISS test, leading to a count of 22 
common items used in our subsequent analysis. Next, we examined 
the test instruments of the SISS and TIMSS. We followed the same 
procedure as in the FISS and SISS analyses. We reviewed each item in 
TIMSS to determine its similarity to those in SISS, and as a result, we 
identified 16 common items across these two assessments. These items 
appeared in both the core and rotated blocks of the SISS and TIMSS. 
Table 3 shows the distribution of the common items by content across 
the three studies.
In the following sections, the methodology employed at each step to 
link the scores and creating the scale are described. We then provide the 
results obtained, and conclude with a discussion section that summa-
rizes findings, addresses limitations of this investigation, and outlines 
the potential applications of the scale.
3. Materials and methods
3.1. Samples and data
The study included the upper primary school students from common 
educational systems who participated in the FISS, SISS, as well as 
students who participated in TIMSS. In these studies, the defined groups 
are referred to as population 1 (See Table 1 for a detailed description). 
Only students who provided at least one valid answer to the selected 
items were included in the analysis. Table S1 (supplementary material) 
details the original and final sample distributions for each common 
educational system, including the retention rates for each. The specific 
proportions of excluded students can be derived from these data. The 
maximum exclusion rate was 2.8% in FISS (Sweden), 0.2% in SISS 
(Hungary), and 0.12% in TIMSS 1995 (Hungary).
FISS was conducted in 1970–1971 across 16 educational systems
(Peaker, 1975), SISS in 1983–1984 across 18 educational systems
(Postlethwaite & Wiley, 1992), and TIMSS in 1994–1995 across 45 
countries (Martin, 1996), with 26 of those countries participating in 
the third and fourth-grade students assessment (Martin et al., 1997). 
In this study, we specifically utilized data from common educational 
systems between the FISS and SISS, and between the SISS and TIMSS. 
This resulted in the inclusion of seven common educational systems 
between the FISS and SISS (England, Finland, Hungary, Italy, Japan, 
Sweden and the United States) and eleven common systems between 
SISS and TIMSS (Australia, Canada, England, Hong Kong, Hungary, 
Israel, Japan, the Republic of Korea,1 Norway, Singapore, and the 
United States). Hence, there are four common systems across all three 
studies (England, Hungary, Japan, and the United States).
Some specific notes for Sweden, the United States (USA) and Canada 
should be mentioned. In Sweden, SISS assessed grade 3 and grade 
4 as population I, and in this study we included only grade 4 due 
to the bigger sample size. In the USA, assessments occurred in two 
phases: the first in 1983 (aligning with other countries) and the sec-
ond in 1986 (Postlethwaite & Wiley, 1992); only the first phase was 
included in this study. Canada was assessed in two languages of testing 
for TIMSS: French and English. We assessed measurement invariance 
between the two Canadian samples and based on those results we 
analyzed both samples as one for this study.
This study did not require ethical approval, as it involved the 
analysis of pre-existing, publicly available, and anonymized datasets. 
Nevertheless, confidential permissions from the IEA were obtained 
concerning the content of the common items between SISS and TIMSS 
1995.
3.2. Descriptive statistics
We compiled descriptive statistics to examine the characteristics of 
the sample based on the common educational system and the items 
administered in the three assessments. Initially, we calculated the 
response rate per common item for each common educational system 
to ensure that we had enough responses for further analyses. Subse-
quently, we computed classical test theory statistics to gain insight into 
the difficulty and discrimination of the items. The item difficulty was 
calculated as the proportion correct for each item (𝑝), while the item 
discrimination was estimated with the item to total score correlation 
coefficient (Wu et al., 2016). Items with a negative correlation coeffi-

3.3. Item response theory analysis
This study uses item response theory (IRT) for the analysis of the 
ordinal item scores. IRT is a modeling framework that establishes a 
probabilistic relationship between a latent variable and its observable 
manifestations. IRT allows for the estimation of item parameters which 
can be used to enable comparable individual and group level measure-
ments even though individuals or groups do not answer exactly the 
same items. Once the item parameters have been estimated, the latent 
variable scores can be directly compared based on the observed item 
responses and the model parameter estimates. In contrast with classical 
test theory (CTT), which primarily focuses on making inferences about 
an individual’s test score without explicitly generalizing it to a latent 
trait, IRT explicitly incorporates a latent trait in and aims to measure 
the level of this trait for each individual (Wu et al., 2016).
We fitted three IRT models to the binary observed data: the one-
parameter logistic (1-PL), two-parameter logistic (2-PL), and three-
parameter logistic (3-PL) models. For the four polytomous items ad-
ministered in TIMSS, we used the Graded Response Model (GRM) (de 
Ayala, 2022). Each model was independently applied to each common 
educational system, first between the FISS and SISS, and then between 
the SISS and TIMSS.
For fitting these models, we primarily used the 40 items from the 
FISS (with noted exceptions for England and the USA) and the 24 core 
items from the SISS. In the analysis comparing the SISS and TIMSS, 
we employed the core block of 24 items from the SISS, along with the 
12 items from the rotated blocks that were common to TIMSS. For the 
TIMSS analysis, we utilized the full set of 97 items.
The 3-PL IRT model is defined by the probability of obtaining a 
correct answer to an item 𝑌𝑗 given the latent construct and includes 
the item parameters 𝛼𝑗, 𝛿𝑗, and 𝜒𝑗. The probability has mathematical 
expression2
𝑃(𝑌𝑗= 1|𝜃) = 𝜒𝑗+ (1 −𝜒𝑗)
exp (𝑎𝑗(𝜃−𝛿𝑗))
1 + exp (𝑎𝑗(𝜃−𝛿𝑗)),
where 𝛼𝑗 represents the discrimination, 𝛿𝑗 represents the location, and 
𝜒𝑗 represents the pseudo-guessing parameter (lower bound probability) 
for item 𝑗. We obtain the 2-PL model by setting 𝜒𝑗= 0. Additionally, 
considering 𝜒𝑗= 0 and setting the discrimination parameter (𝛼𝑗) equal 
across all items, we obtain the 1-PL model (de Ayala, 2022). For 
polytomous items, we estimated a generalization of the 2-PL model 
which also includes the thresholds for each item (𝛿𝑗𝑣), where 𝑣 denotes 
the category threshold.
To select the model for further analyses, we calculated the Bayesian 
Information Criterion (BIC) and preferred the model with the lowest 
BIC value. Following model estimation, we evaluated the model fit 
using the M2 test statistic (Maydeu-Olivares & Joe, 2014) with a

significance level of 0.05, the Root Mean Square Error of Approxi-

It is important to note that in the FISS and SISS comparison, we used 
only the common items to compute fit statistics such as RMSEA and 
SRMSR. However, it was not feasible to select only the common items 
in TIMSS to compute the fit statistics, as those items are not necessarily 
connected across the test design in all the participating countries.
3.4. Measurement invariance evaluation
To investigate measurement invariance across studies, we first 
aimed to establish configural invariance and then employed three 
methods to evaluate differential item functioning (DIF). This subsection 
details these methods. In a previous study (Sosa Paredes & Andersson, 
2024), unidimensionality of the test items was established in the 
included educational systems from the FISS and SISS (England, Finland, 
Hungary, Italy, Japan, Sweden, and the USA). However, for the analysis 
of SISS and TIMSS, the subset of items in SISS is different than this 
prior study, since some items from the rotated blocks (12 items) and 
from the common booklet were now included. This required evaluating 
configural invariance of the included items in the eleven common 
educational systems (Australia, Canada, England, Hong Kong, Hungary, 
Japan, Korea, Israel, Norway, Singapore, and the USA).
To assess configural invariance, we estimated a unidimensional IRT 
model for each system and evaluated the model fit as described in 
the previous subsection. We then aimed to determine if the chosen 
items were invariant across these educational systems. For this, we 
first estimated a restricted multiple group IRT model, defining each 
study as a group. Two groups were thus defined for each system and 
comparison: FISS vs. SISS and SISS vs. TIMSS. Equality constraints were 
imposed on all item parameters in each of the individual educational 
systems.
For this analysis, we utilized adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature
(Andersson et al., 2023; Cagnone & Monari, 2012) approximations 
to the marginal log-likelihood using seven quadrature points, as the 
EM algorithm in the mirt package did not successfully converge for 
several systems. Estimation used direct maximization of the marginal 
log-likelihood with the R package lamle version 0.3.1 (Andersson & 
Jin, 2023). We used likelihood ratio hypothesis tests with a significance 
level of 0.05 to compare the configural and restricted models in the 
mentioned analyses. If these tests did not yield statistically significant 
results, we concluded that there was no DIF present (Leitgöb et al., 
2023). With no DIF, comparing the science achievement means across 
the studies is appropriate. However, if the tests produce statistically 
significant results, we need to examine if partial invariance holds.
Although we compared only two groups each time (FISS vs. SISS 
and SISS vs. TIMSS), we conducted multiple comparisons due to the 
numerous item parameters evaluated. Achieving full invariance across 
all item parameters is uncommon in ILSAs (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). 
As a result, allowing some measurement invariance violations is practi-
cal by freely estimating one or more parameters across groups. Partial 
invariance implies that some item parameters remain invariant, while 
others do not. We used three approaches to evaluate partial invari-
ance: a traditional stepwise method (Wang et al., 2012), the multiple-
group alignment method (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014), and inspection 
of the root mean square deviation (RMSD) values, following current 
TIMSS (Foy et al., 2020) and PISA (OECD, 2023) procedures.
To proceed with the partial invariance approaches, in the analysis 
between the FISS and the SISS, we utilized the full set of 40 items 
from the FISS and the core block of 24 items from the SISS. For the 
comparison between the SISS and TIMSS, we used the 16 common 
items from the SISS along with the full set of 97 items from TIMSS.

3.4.1. Traditional stepwise method
This method involves a series of model comparisons between a 
model with all item parameters restricted to be equal and models where 
restrictions on the parameters of each item were removed. The steps are 
as follows (Wang et al., 2012):
1. Estimate the restricted model and define it as the baseline model, 
assuming all item parameters are identical across educational 
systems.
2. For each item, estimate a model relaxing restrictions on its 
parameters. These models are then compared to the baseline 
using likelihood ratio tests. The number of models estimated 
in this step between the two assessments corresponds to the 
number of common items included (i.e., 22 models between the 
FISS and SISS, and 16 models between the SISS and TIMSS).
3. Apply the Bonferroni-Holm correction (Holm, 1979) for multiple 
comparisons to control for false positives.
4. If a test is statistically significant, it indicates the model with 
freely estimated parameters for a particular item fits better than 
the baseline, identifying non-invariant parameters.
We repeat those steps until no more anchor items are found. After 
identifying the items with invariant parameters (anchor items) in each 
educational system, we estimated a new model labeled as the tradi-
tional partial invariance model by restricting the parameters of these 
anchor items to be equal between the groups. We then computed the 
BIC and likelihood values for model comparisons.
3.4.2. The alignment method
The second partial invariance approach is the multiple-group align-
ment method (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014). This method is particu-
larly useful for many groups and items (Leitgöb et al., 2023; Marsh 
et al., 2018; Muthén & Asparouhov, 2014). Despite having only two 
groups in each analysis, we employed this method as an additional 
check of the robustness of the evaluation. The approach starts with 
the configural model as a baseline and uses an ad-hoc procedure to 
maximize invariance by allowing factor means and variances to vary 
across groups (Muthén & Asparouhov, 2014).
We implemented the alignment method using Mplus 8.9 (Muthén & 
Muthén, 2017), employing maximum likelihood estimation with fixed 
alignment optimization. For this process, we selected the reference 
group as the study with the highest likelihood of non-invariant item 
parameters as indicated in the final invariance output. This group was 
assigned a mean of 0 and a variance of 1, while the mean and variance 
for the other group were estimated. We included the R-squared value 
(𝑅2) which measures the degree of invariance explained by the align-
ment. High 𝑅2 values generally indicate invariant parameters, while 
low values suggest non-invariance (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2022).
This approach often creates a complex pattern of restrictions across 
item parameters and groups. We used these restrictions to estimate 
a new model labeled as the alignment partial invariance model and 
computed the BIC and likelihood values for further comparisons.
3.4.3. RMSD fit statistic
As an additional robustness measure, we computed RMSD values for 
each item across studies. The RMSD measures the item fit by comparing 
observed item characteristic curves (ICCs) with model-based ICCs from 
the estimated IRT models. Essentially, it quantifies the average squared 
differences between observed and predicted item performance across 
groups, weighted by the proficiency distribution. Higher RMSD values 
indicate greater misfit, suggesting that an item behaves differently 
across groups, thus violating measurement invariance (Davier et al., 
2019).
In ILSAs such as TIMSS (Foy et al., 2020) or PISA (OECD, 2023), 
RMSD was used to identify non-equivalent items across groups. Al-
though no fixed cutoff exists for an acceptable RMSD value (Davier 
et al., 2019), the TIMSS 2019 assessment considers RMSD values below 
0.10 to indicate a good fit in cognitive assessments, thus marking an 
item as invariant (Foy et al., 2020). We used this cutoff to evaluate 
item invariance and identify anchor items. Subsequently, we fitted a 
new model termed the RMSD partial invariance model and computed 
the relevant statistics for further comparisons.
From the outcome of the traditional stepwise, alignment, and RMSD 
approaches, we selected the partial invariance model with the lowest 
BIC value and used the item parameter estimates from these models in 
the subsequent analyses.
3.5. Scaling scores across studies
To create scale scores with high measurement precision while still 
being comparable across different studies, we fitted latent regression 
models using contextual data and the science achievement scale, fol-
lowing procedures consistent with those used in ILSAs (see Chapter 
11 in OECD (2023)) (Bezirhan et al., 2023; Bezirhan & von Davier, 
2024). Thus, we first retrieved the estimated anchor item parameters 
across studies in each educational system from each previously selected 
partial invariance model. Following this, we proceeded to select the 
contextual data using all available and documented variables from the 
student questionnaires in each study. Contextual variables from the 
FISS and SISS student questionnaires are available in the University of 
Gothenburg (2025) repository, while contextual data for TIMSS can be 
found in the IEA (2025) repository.
For example, the FISS and SISS student questionnaires include 
variables such as age, sex of the student, number of books at home, and 
items from the interest in science scale, among others. For TIMSS, we 
used all available data from the student questionnaire, such as sex, age, 
language spoken at home, the number of books at home, etc. However, 
in some educational systems, certain items were not administered, or 
the data were not internationally comparable (see details for each 
system in Appendix 1 in Peaker (1975) for FISS, Appendix G in Postleth-
waite and Wiley (1992) for SISS, and Supplement 3 in IEA (2025) for 
TIMSS). For a complete list of variable names, types, descriptions, and 
related scales used for each selected educational system, see Tables S48, 
S49, and S50 in the supplementary material.
The variables included in the student questionnaires contained miss-
ing data. To address this issue, we recoded the mentioned covariates 
using contrast coding (Allen et al., 2001; Fishbein et al., 2024; OECD, 
2023). For categorical variables, we recoded each variable into valid 
categories and added a category for missing values. For example, if a 
variable represented responses to a statement as yes/no, we constructed 
two dummy variables, coded as 0 or 1, to replace the original variable 
(new variables 𝑋1 and 𝑋2 are coded as follows: 𝑋1 = 0 and 𝑋2 = 0
if the original 𝑋 is 1; 𝑋1 = 1 and 𝑋2 = 0 if 𝑋 is 2; and 𝑋1 = 0 and 
𝑋2 = 1 if 𝑋 is missing.). For continuous variables, we created two new 
variables: the first retained the non-missing values of the continuous 
variable, while assigning a value of zero for missing entries; the second 
variable was coded as zero for non-missing values and one for missing 
values.
However, the recoded variables were not used directly in the anal-
ysis, as their dimensionality could be too high to estimate regression 
parameters in a reasonable time and could lead into overspecification 
of the conditional model. Instead, we applied principal component 
analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction (Bezirhan & von Davier, 
2024; OECD, 2023). In line with current TIMSS procedures, we selected 
principal components for each country such that either 90% of the 
common variance was explained, or the number of components did 
not exceed 5% of the unweighted student sample size (Bezirhan & von 
Davier, 2024).
We then fitted latent regression models for each system separately, 
fixing the anchor item parameters to their estimated values and includ-
ing other items along with the previously defined principal component 
scores as regressors. For each system, we independently generated a 
set of five plausible values from the estimated model. Using these

4.3. Measurement invariance
4.3.1. Configural invariance
We assessed measurement invariance using the multiple group 2-PL 
model. The first step involved estimating the configural model, where 
we freely estimated common item parameters across each educational 
system in the studies. Fit indices for the 2-PL model of systems par-
ticipating in both the FISS and SISS were computed in a previous 
study (Sosa Paredes & Andersson, 2024), and exhibited excellent val-
ues, adhering to the recommendations outlined by Maydeu-Olivares 
and Joe (2014). Table 7 shows the fit indices for the FISS and the SISS.
However, we did not provide fit indices for the 2-PL model of 
systems participating between the SISS and TIMSS for two reasons. 
First, some common items from these studies belonged to rotated blocks 
in the matrix design, resulting in an incomplete dataset that prevented 
us from computing the M2, RMSEA, and SRMR fit indices. Second, in 
TIMSS, the common items belong to different rotated blocks, and thus 
it was not possible to fit a model using only the common items.
4.3.2. Evaluation of differential item functioning
The evaluation of DIF utilized a model with equality constraints on 
all item parameters across studies, serving as a baseline model for each 
system separately. We then tested equivalence between the baseline 
model and the configural model using a likelihood ratio test. A rejection 
of the null hypothesis of item parameter equality indicates that the less 
restricted model (configural) fits the data better, thus requiring a DIF 
evaluation. Table S3 (supplementary material) presents the test results 
for each system participating in both the FISS and SISS, while Table 
S4 (supplementary material) provides analogous results for the systems 
that participated in the SISS and TIMSS. In all cases, we rejected the 
null hypothesis, leading us to evaluate partial measurement invariance.

This evaluation was conducted primarily through three approaches: 
the traditional stepwise method, the alignment method, and the in-
spection of RMSD values for the FISS and SISS. Detailed results from 
all approaches are available in the supplementary material (Table S5 
to S47). For the FISS and SISS, the model derived from the alignment 
partial invariance approach exhibited lower BIC values in most systems. 
However, in Finland, the traditional partial invariance model showed a 
lower BIC value compared to the other models. In contrast, for the SISS 
and TIMSS assessments, the preferred model across all systems was the 
partial invariance model based on the traditional approach. With these 
selected partial invariance models, we established the anchor item 
parameters as equal across the studies, which enabled the estimation of 
distinct latent trait distributions for each study. This approach allowed 
us to accurately account for differences in science achievement between 
the studies in each system. It is important to note that the multiple 
group alignment method was not applied in this instance due to the 
complexities of the matrix design in TIMSS. In all cases, the selected 
partial invariance models fit the data better than the less restricted 
configural model, both for the comparisons between the FISS and SISS 
and for the SISS and TIMSS.
Table 8 provides a summary of the comparison between the partial 
invariance models for the FISS and SISS. It highlights the lower BIC val-
ues obtained for each model and the number of anchor items identified 
in each approach. For the alignment partial model, we have specified 
the number of anchor items that had at least one parameter restricted. 
Similarly, Table 9 presents model comparisons for the common systems 
in the SISS and TIMSS, including BIC values and the number of anchor 
items. Notably, the RMSD approach identified a greater number of 
anchor items: 20 or 21 between the FISS and SISS, and between 
11 and 16 for SISS and TIMSS. In contrast, the traditional approach 
identified fewer anchor items, ranging from 5 to 12 for the FISS and 
SISS comparison, and from 3 to 8 for the SISS and TIMSS comparison. 
The parameter estimates resulting from the selected partial invariance 
models in each system were then used to estimate student proficiency 
for each system participating across the science studies.
4.4. Scaling scores across studies
With the estimated item parameters from the partial invariance 
models, we proceeded to the next step: estimating latent regression 
models. We recoded the missing values as described in the methods 
section and then applied PCA to reduce the number of predictors. The 
number of components retained in the PCA ranged from 54 (Japan 
- SISS) to 238 (Singapore - SISS), and the percentage of variance 
explained was approximately 90% except for Japan (FISS, 85%), Israel 
(TIMSS, 82%), Finland (FISS and SISS, 76% and 85%), and Sweden 
(FISS, 87%). For the systems that participated across all three studies, 
we fitted the model considering the anchor items found across the 
FISS and SISS, and the SISS and TIMSS, with those item parameters 
constrained to be equal across studies and estimating the remaining 
item parameters using the respective sample from each system in each 
study. In such a model, we used TIMSS as the reference group, setting 
the mean to 0 and the variance to 1. For systems participating only in 
the FISS and SISS, the FISS served as the reference group. For those 
participating solely in the SISS and TIMSS, the SISS was used as the 
reference group.
We used the estimated latent regression models to obtain imputed 
plausible values of student proficiency based on item responses and 
contextual data. We imputed five plausible values and computed the 
mean and variance using the weights from each study and system. To 
estimate the trends in each system across the relevant assessments, 
we subselected the sample based on grade and average age across 
studies and systems, using the upper level in population 1 in TIMSS 
as the reference point. This particular group was utilized to facilitate 
linking our results to the TIMSS assessments from 2003 to 2023 for 
further investigations. Table 4 presents the groups used for creating 
trend scores in all selected educational systems, while Fig. 1 illustrates 
the mean science trends for these systems across the studies.
Notably, if researchers wish to use the generated plausible values 
alongside existing TIMSS data, they can easily do so. The scores of the 
systems participating in all three studies — namely England, Hungary, 
Japan, and the USA — can be rescaled into the current TIMSS metric 
through a linear transformation. The scale scores (five plausible values) 
for each system in each study are available in this repository (Rdata and 
sav files).
Examining the trends, we first focus on the four systems — England, 
Hungary, Japan, and the USA — that participated across all three 
studies. England shows a flat trend, with the sample in each study com-
prising students of similar average age and grade. A similar situation is 
observed in Japan, where the sample was adjusted, resulting in a slight 
increase for TIMSS scores compared to the SISS and FISS. In contrast, 
both Hungary and the USA exhibit a peak in the SISS; however, in 
the USA, the students in that study had a higher average age (more 
than one year older on average) and a different grade compared to the 
FISS and TIMSS, which may explain this peak. This pattern is similar 
for Hungary despite very similar student samples in each study. These 
results are consistent with a previous study of Hungarian science scores 
in grade 8 of population 2 (Majoros, 2023).

5. Discussion
The rich data from comparative large-scale assessments collected 
over the past six decades enable an improved understanding of how 
educational systems evolve over time. Data from these assessments 
present a unique opportunity for researchers and policymakers to study 
educational inequalities and identify the potential effects of educational 
policies on student outcomes. A primary resource for such analyses is a 
comparable achievement scale over time, as it allows for the evaluation 
of differences in achievement in both absolute and relative terms. This 
paper investigated the validity of linking science achievement scores 
from the early FISS and SISS with the current TIMSS, and contributes a 
unified, comparable long-term scale of science achievement for upper 
primary school students which gives insights into the development 
of science achievement over an extended period of time in multiple 
systems and enables many further studies that can investigate diverse 
research questions in the educational sciences.
Our investigation focused on three research questions. First, we ex-
amined the similarities in constructs, inferences, population definitions, 
and test conditions across the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS to determine how 
comparable these assessments are. The greatest similarity lies in their 
intended purpose: all studies aim to be low-stakes and assess potential 
changes in performance over time. When it comes to constructs, differ-
ences are primarily terminological rather than substantive; the studies 
maintain a consistent focus on the same content categories, particularly 
emphasizing biology, life sciences, and physics. Furthermore, while test 
designs differ, the test conditions are largely comparable, featuring two 
testing sessions of similar duration, all conducted in paper-and-pencil 
format, with a predominance of multiple-choice questions. Neverthe-
less, a significant shift from age-based to grade-based definitions of 
the population between TIMSS and earlier assessments was observed. 
We attempted to address this change by adjusting the trends to reflect 
more comparable groups of students within each educational system. 
Overall, these findings align with previous studies focused on reading, 
mathematics, and science among older populations, where researchers 
have identified population differences as a primary concern in linking 
analyses with earlier studies (Majoros, 2023; Majoros et al., 2021; 
Strietholt & Rosén, 2016).
Second, we successfully linked science scores across various educa-
tional systems, identifying anchor items, the number of which varied 
by system. Our methodology deviated slightly from previous studies, as 
we conducted a more in-depth analysis of measurement invariance and 
followed contemporary procedures to estimate plausible values. These 
findings offer an additional resource to the existing scales available 
in the COMPEAT repository (University of Gothenburg, 2025), which 
supplies scale scores for mathematics and science assessments among 
students aged 13 to 14 years or in the eighth grade.
Third, we described trends in mean science scores across the se-
lected educational systems. After adjusting the samples for grade and

average age, we found that most participating systems in TIMSS ex-
perienced a significant decline in average science scores compared 
to the SISS, such as Canada, Hungary, Israel, Norway, and the USA; 
while Japan, Korea, and Singapore showed positive trends. Systems like 
Australia, England, and Hong Kong exhibited stable results. However, 
in cases such as Israel, Korea, Norway, and Sweden, we could not obtain 
sufficiently comparable groups, as the sample compositions varied in 
both grade and average age by one or more years.
This study has several limitations. A central limitation concerns the 
number of anchor items identified for linking the studies. A significant 
number of common items exhibited DIF across assessments, undermin-
ing the strength of the link in systems like Canada, where only three 
out of sixteen common items showed measurement invariance. The 
shift in population definitions from age-based to grade-based in TIMSS 
may have influenced the detection of DIF in common items. Cognitive 
developmental differences in students within each system among the 
studies could have played a role since the age group and grade level 
can be very different.
For instance, in Canada, students were assessed at grade 5 in SISS, 
while in TIMSS they were assessed in grade 3 and grade 4. This resulted 
in an average age difference of about one year, and one additional year 
of schooling. Similarly, in Norway, students were assessed in grade 4 
for SISS but in grades 2 and 3 for TIMSS, leading to an average age 
difference of more than two years. Conversely, in systems like Israel 
and Hong Kong, we found a more robust set of anchor items. In Israel, 
both SISS and TIMSS assessed students at grade 4, and while there was 
a mean age difference of one year, the students had been exposed to 
the same years of schooling. For Hong Kong, students were assessed in 
grade 4 for both SISS and TIMSS, with the addition of grade 3 in TIMSS. 
The sample composition for this system then intersects with students 
assessed in grade 4, and the average age difference was less than one 
year.
Another limitation concerns differences in test conditions. Although 
the length of testing are similar across studies, TIMSS assesses both 
mathematics and science in a single administration, whereas the FISS 
and SISS focus solely on science. This difference could affect achieve-
ment estimation, as students may experience different factors during 
testing, such as fatigue and other item-related effects, including po-
sitioning effects, which we did not investigate. Moreover, based on 
prior analyses of the FISS and SISS (Sosa Paredes & Andersson, 2024), 
we were unable to link scores across systems, meaning that the scales 
provided are independent of each other and can only be used to 
investigate changes within each educational system.
Despite these limitations, our study supplies researchers with the 
tools to evaluate long-term relationships in educational outcomes via a 
unified science achievement scale for upper primary school students—a 
population that has not been extensively studied before. For instance, 
researchers can utilize our generated plausible values directly to com-
pare the FISS, SISS, and TIMSS 1995 in the selected systems. For 
long-term analyses, they may use our plausible values; however, it is 
essential to first rescale them with the current TIMSS 1995 plausible 
values using a linear transformation. Jewsbury et al. (2024) provide 
clear considerations on how to use plausible values in large-scale 
assessments. Importantly, our results represent just the beginning of a 
more in-depth analysis. Future research should delve into the impact 
of specific educational policies, considering additional variables for 
a more comprehensive understanding. Potential variables to explore 
alongside our proposed scale include the student’s sex, socioeconomic 
status (SES), school composition, and attitudes toward science.
For instance, our scale could extend the work of Steinmann et al. 
(2023), who analyzed long-term changes in gender gaps in reading 
comprehension at the end of primary school using a unified scale from 
1970 to 2016. She found a general advantage for girls over boys, with

a significant increase in international gaps between 1971 and 2001, 
followed by a slight decrease thereafter. This highlights the importance 
to examine long-term sex-achievement gaps to understand historical 
trends.
Turning to SES achievement gaps, a notable example is the influ-
ential paper by Chmielewski (2019), which was pioneering in studying 
these gaps in math, science, and reading using multiple ILSAs, including 
the early studies like FISS and SISS. Although her research did not 
utilize a unified scale, it leveraged standardized scores within each 
country-study-year-subject to ensure comparability across tests and 
years. Similarly, Hanushek et al. (2022) examined SES-achievement 
gaps in the USA, drawing on large-scale national assessments and 
international data from cohorts born between 1961 and 2001, again 
using standardized scores. Therefore, our scale can enhance this type 
of research and provide greater interpretability of mean achievement 
differences in various systems.
In terms of SES indices in long-term studies, Broer et al. (2019b) 
proposed a new approach for constructing an SES index based on 
data from IEA’s TIMSS grade 8 mathematics and science assessments. 
Their study examined changes in achievement gaps between high- and 
low-SES students from 1995 to 2015 across 13 educational systems, 
operationalizing SES with a modified version of the TIMSS home ed-
ucational resources index, which includes variables such as highest 
parental education levels, home possessions, and the number of books 
at home.
In the FISS and SISS studies, similar variables were included to 
assess the home background of students. For the FISS, variables in-
cluded were father’s occupation, use of dictionary, number of books, 
and family size. In contrast, the SISS used indicators such as father’s 
occupation, parental education, and family size, as well as data on the 
number of books and the use of dictionary are available.
Regarding school inequalities, a recent investigation highlighted 
disparities in the effects of school composition over time, utilizing data 
from ten educational systems — including England, Japan, Hungary, 
Italy, Norway, Singapore, and the USA — participating in TIMSS from 
2003 to 2019. Ali et al. (2024) explored how achievement varied 
within and between schools at different grade levels (grades 4 and 
8). Their findings indicate that, for grade 4, inequality within schools 
is far more significant than inequality between schools—a pattern 
that has remained stable over two decades. However, there is emerg-
ing evidence of increased within-school inequality linked to specific 
student subpopulations, particularly in lower-achieving schools. Our 
proposed scale can enhance such studies, specially at grade 4, as school 
identification is available for the FISS and SISS.
Additionally, both the FISS and SISS included scales to capture 
students’ attitudes toward science, such as ‘‘interest in science’’ and 
‘‘importance of science’’. While the items are not identical to those in 
the current TIMSS scale ‘‘Students like learning science’’, they share a 
common aim: to measure students’ self-reported enjoyment of science. 
Items in SISS include statements like: ‘‘I like science’’, ‘‘Science is an 
enjoyable school subject’’, ‘‘The science taught at school is interesting’’, 
and ‘‘The teacher makes science lessons enjoyable for us’’. For more de-
tails, see Appendix G in Postlethwaite and Wiley (1992) and Appendix 
1 in Peaker (1975) for the FISS items, along with Tables S48, S49 and 
S50 in the supplementary material.
Another point to consider is that some educational systems, such 
as Italy, Finland, and Sweden, participated in the FISS and SISS but 
not in TIMSS 1995. However, these systems eventually participated 
in later TIMSS cycles. This creates opportunities for researchers to 
investigate the linking between Italian students’ science achievement in 
the early studies and TIMSS 2003, as that was the cycle in which Italy 
participated. Additionally, Swedish students were assessed at grade 4 in 
TIMSS starting in 2007, while Finnish students were assessed in grade 
4 in TIMSS 2011. The existence of common items across the relevant 
studies is a requirement for linking these studies for the mentioned 
systems, which needs to be further investigated.
Lastly, while our study faced challenges regarding sample composi-
tion due to the shift from age-based to grade-based categorization, the 
issue of comparability in ILSAs persists in current IEA studies. Adjusting 
sample composition is crucial for accurately interpreting gaps and 
trends over time. In this study, we adjusted samples by age and grade 
level; however, additional strategies are available. For example, Stri-
etholt et al. (2013) proposed a corrected model that incorporates the 
month of test administration along with age and grade for improved 
comparability. Similarly, research examining the Swedish sample us-
ing register data and PISA 2018 highlighted that PISA overestimated 
achievement levels in Swedish results. This overestimation was mainly 
attributed to changes in exclusion and non-participation rates at student 
level across years (Borger et al., 2024). The authors advocated for 
further enhancements and revisions to PISA methodology to address 
comparability biases. Emphasizing these adjustments is essential for 
providing meaningful educational insights.
In conclusion, this study allows for reasonable comparisons of sci-
ence scores over time across multiple educational systems while ac-
knowledging certain dissimilarities in construct definitions, popula-
tions, and measurement characteristics. It presents an opportunity for 
further investigations into educational research questions concerning 
inequalities. We also emphasize the importance of adjusting group 
compositions over time for better interpretation of trends and gaps.
