This study used the Trends in International Math Science (TIMSS) 2015 Advanced data from nine countries to
examine the relationship between students’ sense of school belonging and their attitudes towards mathematics,
and the extent to which student gender moderated these relationships. Results showed that students’ sense of
school belonging emerged as a statistically significant, positive predictor of students’ liking and valuing of
mathematics. For most countries, no significant moderating effect of student gender was observed. However,
among Portuguese students, the relationship between students’ sense of school belonging and each attitude
measure was stronger for males than for females.

1. Introduction
School belonging is a construct that has held the interest of researchers for several decades. Definitions of this construct primarily
relate to the degree to which students feel that they identify with school
or belong at school (Finn, 1989), or “the extent to which students feel
personally accepted, respected, included, and encouraged by others in
the school social environment” (Goodenow, 1993, as cited in Moallem,
2013, p. 3). Some terms that have been used synonymously with school
belonging include school climate, school attachment, school identity,
school bonding, school involvement, school connectedness, emotional
engagement, or positive orientation toward school (Gillen-O’Neel and
Fuligni, 2013; Whitlock, 2006). Prior research has shown that students
in early adolescence show decreases in school belonging (Eccles et al.,
1993), and that this decline continues through the secondary school
experience (Anderman, 2002; Ding and Hall, 2007). Other research,
however (e.g., Meeus and Dekovic, 1995), has suggested that school
belonging does not decline in later adolescence, and may even show
slight increases. Gender differences in school belonging also have been
observed. Gillen-O’Neel and Fuligni’s longitudinal study of U.S. high
school students found that boys’ sense of school belonging tended to
remain stable, while girls’ sense of school belonging—which initially

was higher than that of boys—tended to decline.
Although school belonging has been demonstrated to be related to
academic, social, and personal attitudes; participation and engagement;
academic achievement, and basic psychological mechanisms essential to
student success (Finn, 1989; Osterman, 2000; Sasson, 2019; Voelkl,
1997), other studies are mixed in their findings regarding the specific
relationship of school belonging to academic success, with some studies
(e.g., Anderman, 2002; Walton and Cohen, 2007) showing a positive
relationship, and other studies (e.g., Dotterer et al., 2007; Liu and Lu,
2011) showing no relationship. Among American students, Singh et al.
(2010) found ethnic differences in the relationship between school
belonging and school achievement as measured by self-reported grades,
with African American high school students showing a positive association, but European American students showing no such association.
Korpershoek et al. (2019) in their meta-analysis of 82 correlational
studies, found that school belonging had a small positive effect on student achievement (student grades and standardized test scores), while it
negatively predicted school absence and dropout rates. Importantly,
Korpershoek et al. found moderately strong positive associations between school belonging and self-concept and self-efficacy.

gender differences in math performance are quite small, with effect sizes

performance differences, larger gender gaps existed in math-related
career choices (Piatek-Jimenez et al., 2018). Similarly, Ganley and

confident and more anxious about math than boys, but these gender
differences in confidence and anxiety are larger than actual gender
differences in math achievement. Men and women also tend to prioritize
different values when selecting a profession. For example, women tend
to care more about working with people, while men tend to be more
interested in working with things, and this difference relates to gender
gaps in selection of math-related careers (Eccles and Wang, 2015).
However, some gender differences in attitudes toward math and math
skills appear during elementary school, and ultimately, boys are much
more likely than girls to pursue careers in some key math-intensive
fields, such as engineering and computer science.

2. Review of literature
2.1. Sense of belonging and learning engagement
In the past decade, there is a growing trend of interest in science
identity (Avraamidou, 2019). According to the model posited by Carlone
and Johnson (2007), science self-identity consists of three dimensions:
personal competence (knowledge and understanding of the subject);
individual performance (social performances of relevant scientific
practices); and self-recognition (recognition of oneself and being
recognized by others as a member of the community). This conceptual
model formed the framework for the present study, particularly in the
sense of how a student’s sense of school belonging might related to their
attitudes and identity as a STEM community member. Recent studies (e.
g., Avraamidou, 2019, 2020) highlight the role that belongingness plays
in the development of science identity, and indicate that the barriers of
gender, religion, cultural expectations, sociopolitical factors, and
negative stereotypes can be critical in hindering or enhancing the
development of a science identity. In this sense, a student’s sense of
school belonging might be expected to play a formative role in the
development of science identity, with heightened sense of belonging
potentially enhancing science identity, and a lack of belonging serving
as a barrier. Further, Avaamidou (2019) calls for additional research
that examines the role of “the complex mixture of political, social, cultural, and personal factors on the formation of a science identity…” (p.
337) so as to cultivate conditions that enhance individuals’ science
identity.

The data include variables pertaining to math and science achievement,
student background characteristics, and attitudes. For the present study,
we used data from the Advanced Mathematics Population (Martin et al.,
p. 3.4). Students in the TIMSS sample were selected from the population
of advanced mathematics students using a two-stage stratified cluster
sampling procedure where the first stage involved a random sample of
schools, and the second stage involved the selection of one or two intact
classes of students (Martin et al.). School type (e.g., public vs. private)
and location (e.g., metropolitan, non-metropolitan), and school performance on national exams served as stratification variables. Additionally,
data from the teachers of the students advanced mathematics courses
were collected. The total unweighted sample size was N = 35,853.
To address the research questions, two measures of attitude towards
mathematics were considered: Students Like Learning Advanced Mathematics (SLM), and the Students Value Advanced Mathematics (SVM). Scale
scores for the SLS (12 items) and SVS (9 items) were based on student
responses pertaining to statements such as “I enjoy figuring out challenging mathematics” (SLM) and “When I do mathematics problems, I
sometimes get completely absorbed” (SLM), “I like telling people I am
studying advanced mathematics” (SVM) and “Learning advanced
mathematics will give me more job opportunities” (SVM). Each item was
associated with four Likert response options (Agree a lot to Disagree a lot).
Students’ sense of school belonging was assessed based on student responses to the inquiry, “What do you think about your school? Tell how
much you agree with these statements,” where provided statements (see
Table 1) included statements such as: “I enjoy school,” and “My classmates respect students who excel in school subjects.” Each of these items
was associated with five Likert response options (Agree a lot to Disagree a
lot). Composite scores for the mathematics attitude and sense of school
belonging scales were computed by the survey developers using item
response theory (IRT) procedures. In each country/region, scores from
each scale have demonstrated evidence of reliability for the populations
of interest, with reliability (alpha) indices ranging from 0.74 to .91
(Students Like Learning Advanced Mathematics), .67–.83 (Students Value
Advanced Mathematics), and .82–.89 (Students’ Sense of School Belonging).
To address RQ1, multiple regression analyses were carried out for
each country/region, where the students’ sense of school belonging was
entered as predictor of the two mathematics attitude measures. Based on
prior literature suggesting sex/gender differences, student gender (female/male) was included in the model. Other covariates included
parental education level (consisting five levels ranging from some primary, lower secondary, or no school to university or higher), and the TIMSS
measure of math achievement. To address RQ2, a country × schoolbelonging interaction term was incorporated to assess potential
moderation effects. For all regression analyses, supplied student-level
sampling weights were used and, to account for the complex, multistage sampling design, standard errors were adjusted using jackknife
replications. The TIMSS math achievement scores consisted of a set of
five plausible values. Thus, regression analyses were carried out five
times, and obtained parameter estimates averaged across these regressions. For the regression analyses, error degrees of freedom were
computed as the number of PSUs (clusters) minus the number of strata
(unless a stratum had a single PSU), minus the number of regressors.

5. Discussion
Although prior research has addressed students’ sense of school
belonging as a construct that may be related to students’ academic
development and learning, there is little work that relates this construct
to students’ attitude towards math and, in particular, the attitudes of
students enrolled in advanced mathematics courses. The present study
aims to address this gap in existing research. Results show that school
belonging emerged as a significant and important predictor of student
attitude towards mathematics. Among this population of students—students who may consider pursuing post-secondary education, and
perhaps considering science, technology, engineering, and mathematics
(STEM) as fields of study, fostering positive attitudes towards mathematics is critical. The results from this study suggest that school context
and perceptions of school context could serve as important factors in this
regard. In particular, in several countries, students’ sense of school
belonging was more important than math achievement in predicting the
extent to which they value mathematics. This indicates that a positive
school environment might instill into students a sense of the importance
and utility of mathematics and mathematics achievement, and that efforts to improve school climate and environment might yield benefits
that go beyond those immediately visible and extend to the future ambitions of students and the trajectories of their lives, education, and
careers.
The effects of students’ sense of school belonging on these math
attitudinal outcomes align with and highlight the role that a sense of
belonging can play in the formation of an adolescent’s or young adult’s
conception of a “STEM identity,” and are consistent with the notions put
forth Avraamidou (2019, 2020). Such identities certainly don’t develop
apart from the social milieu in which individuals find themselves, and
cultivating healthy, safe, positive school environments can provide
necessary conditions that promote and sustain students’ interest in
STEM subjects, including mathematics.
The current study found that the effects of students’ sense of school
belonging generally did not vary by gender, with school identity
showing fairly equal, but positive effects on math attitudes for both females and males. In Portugal, however, the effects of school belonging
on both liking and valuing advanced mathematics were stronger for
males than females. This is interesting, as Portugal has been noted for its
recent rapid emergence in academic outcomes relative to other countries/regions and a concomitant lessening of the gender gap in STEM
fields (Saavedra et al., 2014), with some studies even observing that
females outperform males in school mathematics (Saavedra, 2001). As
Saavredra et al. note, however, increases in performance and a lessening
of the gender gap do not necessarily equate with increased interest
among females in all types of scientific pursuits. Moreover, the current
study suggests that in Portugal (and to a more limited extent in Sweden
and the Russian Federation), certain environmental factors such as
school climate and milieu may have more effect on male’s attitudes
towards math than they do for females. Perhaps female attitudes are
shaped more strongly by other factors, such as family influences/expectations, experiences with particular math teachers or courses, or
even media portrayal of scientists/mathematicians.
There are important limitations in this study. First, the survey results
are fully dependent on student responses to attitudinal prompts, and it is
possible that some students may not validly report their own attitudes.

junior high school students, found that nearly 19 % of observed students
who reported that they disliked math were “fake math-dislikes”—that is,
