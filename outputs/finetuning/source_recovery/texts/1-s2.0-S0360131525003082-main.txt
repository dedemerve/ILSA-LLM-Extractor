This study investigates the relationships among students’ use of ICT for learning and leisure, selfefficacy in digital competencies, and achievement in math, reading, and science, and compares
differences based on gender and immigrant background. Previous studies show inconsistent relationships among these variables. Student background affects the use of ICT, and while selfefficacy may vary depending on the subject, it has had positive effects on academic achievement. In this study, self-efficacy in digital competencies is viewed as two different competence
beliefs: computer and information literacy (CIL) and computational thinking (CT). Although selfefficacy in CIL and CT has had varying effects on digital skills, the effects on math, reading, and
science are not well studied. We analyzed Finnish data (N = 10,239) from PISA 2022 using
general and multigroup structural equation modelling. We found that ICT use for learning had
little to no practical significance across groups. ICT use for leisure and self-efficacy in CT were
associated with being approximately a year or more behind in math, reading, and science across
groups. Self-efficacy in CIL was associated with being two or more years ahead and played a
protective short-term role as a mediator, especially in immigrants. These findings imply that
closer integration of ICT use for learning with subject-specific goals in authentic learning contexts
may promote their contribution to achievement. Further research should examine how different
uses of ICT and CT skill development interact with subject learning in practice and how schools
can be supported in adopting more pedagogically purposeful digital activities.

The importance of using information and communication technology (ICT) in learning has grown alongside increasing availability
of and reliance on digital technology. Prior research has evinced that basic skills (e.g., reading, math, and science) and higher-order
skills (e.g., critical thinking, self-regulation, collaborative problem-solving, and socioemotional skills), as well as motivation, interest,
and engagement, can be facilitated by technology-enhanced learning environments (Bhutoria & Aljabri, 2022; Faber et al., 2017;
Hatlevik et al., 2018; Hietajärvi et al., 2020; Hori & Fujii, 2021; Muis et al., 2015). However, simply providing access to ICT does not
increase achievement in these skills (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2015). Thus, large-scale

studies have recently shifted focus toward how ICT is used to develop students’ cognitive performance, well-being, and digital skills
(OECD, 2023). These studies have found that digital technologies are rarely used for such pedagogically relevant purposes as
enquiry-based activities, self-assessments, processing and analyzing data, collaborative problem-solving, simulations, serious games,
and computational thinking (European Commission, 2019; Fagerlund et al., 2024; Fraillon, 2024; Fraillon et al., 2020; Oinas et al.,
2023; Smahel et al., 2020).
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) study assesses the availability, purpose, and frequency of ICT use and
its relationship with students’ performance in math, reading, and science to understand how students’ ICT skills and competencies
interact with their backgrounds and school achievement (OECD, 2023). Previous studies have generally examined the relationships
among different ICT constructs or between ICT constructs and achievement. Odell, Cutumisu, and Gierl (2020) have found varying
results depending on the country, subject, and ICT construct. These ICT constructs have centered on “uses of ICT,” such as entertainment, practical, school- or learning-related, and ICT use at school, and have exhibited links to academic performance (Courtney
et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2025; Odell, Cutumisu, & Gierl, 2020; OECD, 2015). In addition, there are individual differences between
students that affect the use of ICT. For example, boys tend to be interest-driven ICT users, while girls use ICT for social communication
(Lahti et al., 2021) and boys are more likely to use ICT excessively (Hooft Graafland, 2018).
Other ICT constructs studied in PISA have been “perceived competence in using ICT” and “autonomy in using ICT,” which have had
varying relationships with and effects on academic performance (Courtney et al., 2022; Li & Petersen, 2022; Liang et al., 2025; Meng
et al., 2019). While in 2018 and earlier cycles PISA measured “perceived competence and autonomy in using ICT”, in PISA 2022 it was
replaced by “self-efficacy in digital competencies” (Lorenceau et al., 2019; OECD, 2023). This self-efficacy consists of CIL and CT
competencies. CIL covers searching for and assessing information online and working with word-processing and spreadsheets software. CT covers programming, troubleshooting computer code, and algorithmic problem-solving. As self-efficacy in digital competencies is new to PISA, its interactions with ICT use and achievement have not yet been studied. However, other assessments of
self-efficacy in the ICT domain have found significant effects on students’ digital skills, as well as differences based on gender and
type of self-efficacy (Hatlevik et al., 2018; Rohatgi et al., 2016).
In Finnish lower secondary schools, digital technology is rarely used for learning and its use is focused on basic operations, such as
searching for, editing, and storing information (Fagerlund et al., 2024; Oinas et al., 2023). In addition, Finnish students report
acquiring most of their digital competencies outside of school (Fagerlund et al., 2024; Hietajärvi et al., 2020; Leino et al., 2019). Oinas
et al. (2023) reported that while there is a negative correlation between ICT use and achievement, partly due to the targeted use of ICT
with students with immigrant backgrounds and special needs, digitalization does not impair learning and can improve motivation and
engagement. Moreover, differences in CIL and CT skills favored girls and nonimmigrants, and few differences in self-efficacy in CIL and
CT between boys and girls have been discovered (Fagerlund et al., 2024). There has been an increasing number of immigrants coming
to Finland, and this trend of net positive immigration is expected to continue (Statistics Finland, 2024). However, research into how
students of different genders and immigrant backgrounds use ICT and how to better address their needs is still in its infancy in the
Nordic nation.
In summary, there is a need to better understand how students’ digital practices and confidence with technology connect with their
learning outcomes, especially as digital tools are increasingly integrated into education. PISA 2022 provides a large representative
sample to investigate these relationships. To respond to this need to assess how different students living in Finland use ICT, the current
study examines the relationships among ICT use for learning and leisure purposes, self-efficacy in digital competencies (CIL and CT),
and student achievement. The objective is to elucidate whether ICT use for learning and for leisure are beneficial for students’
achievement and how different student backgrounds affect these relationships. In addition, the current study will examine the yet
unstudied self-efficacy in digital competencies construct that was introduced in the PISA 2022 ICT questionnaire, thus adding new
insights to academic literature.
1. Literature review
1.1. The diverse effects of ICT use on achievement
Lorenceau et al. (2019) describes the following changes to PISA 2022. In earlier cycles, ICT use for learning has been evaluated by
measuring how much time students spend using a computer to do homework, communicating with peers or teachers about school
assignments, and using learning apps. This changed in PISA 2022, where ICT use for learning is evaluated by measuring the purposes
for which ICT is used, asking about specific learning activities such as writing or editing assignments, collecting and analyzing data,
and planning projects. ICT use for leisure has been measured similarly in earlier cycles and in PISA 2022, asking students about the
time they spend on specific activities such as playing video games and browsing the internet or social media.
The use of ICT has had varying effects on achievement. These effects can be broken down based on the uses of ICT, the signs and
magnitudes of effects, and in relation to which subject they are examined. We have included a table presentation of these studies’
findings in Appendix 1. Regarding the uses of ICT, uses for specifically school- or learning-related and social communication purposes
have had negative effects on math and science (Courtney et al., 2022; Ünal et al., 2022) but varying effects on reading (Hu & Yu, 2021).
This kind of use has also had negative effects on reading (Kunina-Habenicht & Goldhammer, 2020) and science (Odell, Galovan, &
Cutumisu, 2020) but positive effects across all subjects (Li & Petersen, 2022). The effects of ICT use outside of school were found to be
either detrimental or beneficial for math and science (Courtney et al., 2022; Ünal et al., 2022), detrimental only for science (Odell,
Galovan, & Cutumisu, 2020) and beneficial only for science (Örnek et al., 2023). The effects also varied across subjects (Li & Petersen,
2022). There also appears to be some dependency on the frequency and type of ICT usage. Moderate (2–6 h) ICT use outside of school

science.
1.2. Self-efficacy in digital competencies
In PISA 2022 self-efficacy is defined as “students’ beliefs regarding their ability to execute a specific task or to achieve a given goal.
A related construct is self-concept, which corresponds to students’ global judgement of how they perceive their abilities in relation to a
particular domain” (OECD, 2023, p. 277). It is measured by asking students, “To what extent are you able to do the following tasks
when using < digital resources>?” and listing various tasks related to ICT use.
According to Bandura (1993), stronger perceived self-efficacy generally results in taking on more challenges and putting in more
effort, which typically results in better performance, while weaker self-efficacy generally results in worse performance and avoidance
of challenges. Analyzing the PISA 2022 dataset in a hierarchical two-level regression model, Palimo (2024) found that self-efficacy in
digital competencies had a positive impact on math, reading, and science. Thus, self-efficacy in digital competencies can be considered
directional based on Bandura’s (1993) interpretation of it affecting students’ self-regulation of learning, as well as motivation and
achievement aspiration.
In the present study, “self-efficacy in digital competencies” is examined as a combination of self-efficacy in CIL and in CT. This is
because the PISA ICT Assessment Framework refers to relying on the existing International Computer and Information Literacy Study
(ICILS) framework (OECD, 2023, p. 242). CIL is defined as “an individual’s ability to use computers to investigate, create, and
communicate in order to participate effectively at home, at school, in the workplace, and in society” (Fraillon et al., 2023, p. 31). CT is
defined as “an individual’s ability to recognize aspects of real-world problems which are appropriate for computational formulation
and to evaluate and develop algorithmic solutions to those problems so that the solutions could be operationalized with a computer”
(Fraillon et al., 2023, p. 47). While self-efficacy in CIL has had a positive impact on CIL scores (Hatlevik et al., 2018; Rohatgi et al.,
2016), its impact on achievement in other domains has not been studied in detail. The case for CT is similar, mostly centering around
assessment of students’ skills in programming and computer science (Tang et al., 2020).
There is not much research on self-efficacy in digital competencies in PISA 2022 yet, either as a single construct or as a combination
of CIL and CT, and how it interacts with academic achievement. However, we accept that the concepts “perceived competence and
autonomy in using ICT” from earlier PISA cycles and its successor, “self-efficacy in digital competencies” from PISA 2022, are comparable, and thus results from studies of previous cycles could hint at how “self-efficacy in digital competencies” may behave. This
connection between the two concepts is further detailed in Lorenceau et al. (2019, pp. 42–44). In 2018 and earlier cycles, PISA
measured “perceived competence and autonomy in using ICT” as part of motivation for achieving (OECD, 2019, p. 231). It is measured
by items such as “If I have a problem with digital devices I start to solve it on my own.”
A table presentation of the following studies’ findings is available in Appendix 1. Odell, Galovan, and Cutumisu (2020) and Ünal
et al. (2022) found a positive effect of both perceived autonomy in using ICT and perceived competence in using ICT on science.
Perceived autonomy in ICT has had consistently positive effects: on reading (Lezhnina & Kismihók, 2022; Ma & Qin, 2021), on math
and science (Courtney et al., 2022), and across subjects (Li & Petersen, 2022; Li & Zhu, 2023; Meng et al., 2019). Perceived competence
in ICT has had varying effects. It has had a positive effect on students’ ICT use in and out of school (Örnek et al., 2023), on math and
science (Courtney et al., 2022), and across subjects (Li & Zhu, 2023). Li and Petersen (2022) found that perceived competence had a
negative direct effect across subjects. In Eastern countries, perceived competence had a negative effect on reading (Ma & Qin, 2021)
and across subjects (Meng et al., 2019), while these relationships varied in Western countries.
In addition to the effects discussed thus far, Li and Petersen (2022) found that perceived autonomy in ICT positively mediated the
effects of school ICT use on schoolwork, leisure ICT use, social interaction ICT use, and perceived competence in ICT. Further, Li and
Zhu (2023) found that perceived autonomy and competence in ICT had a positive mediating effect on the relationship between ICT use
and achievement.
1.3. The role of gender and immigrant status in ICT use and self-efficacy
When it comes to ICT use, boys lean more toward excessive use than girls, which could lead to more negative psychoemotional
experiences (Hooft Graafland, 2018). In addition, interest-driven ICT users tend to be boys, have the highest number of low achievers,
and have the highest percentage of problematic social media use (Lahti et al., 2021). Friendship-driven ICT users tend to be girls, have
the highest number of moderate and high achievers, and have the highest levels of parental and peer support (Lahti et al., 2021).
Usher and Pajares (2008) found that there were no differences between genders regarding general self-efficacy; however, when
factoring in the domain, differences appeared. Usher and Pajares (2008) suggested that this could be a result of gender orientation or
stereotypical beliefs about gender, meaning that students would perform better in areas in which their own gender is perceived to
perform better—boys in math, science, and technology, and girls in language and arts. In addition, Campos and Scherer (2024) found
differences in digital skills partially due to the gender differences in self-efficacy in CIL, and that gender differences in attitudes toward

In the Finnish school context, digital technologies are used more frequently with immigrant students, lower achievers, and students

boys and girls in self-efficacy in CIL.
1.4. The present study
There is evidence that perceived autonomy and competence in using ICT mediate the relationship between ICT use and
achievement (Li & Petersen, 2022; Li & Zhu, 2023). However, self-efficacy in digital competencies as a mediator of experiences
affecting achievement (Bandura, 1993) has not been examined in PISA. Considering the Finnish context, we took into account that
Hiltunen (2023) reported high variability within schools and low variability between regions in Finland. This indicated a need to look
at the differences between students, therefore we limited the study’s scope to the student level. Thus, the aim of the study is to clarify
how students’ use of ICT and their self-efficacy in digital competencies are tied with achievement in math, reading, and science, In
practice, the study examines the relationships among students’ use of ICT, self-efficacy in digital competencies, and their achievement
in math, reading, and science, as well as compares differences based on gender and immigrant background. The current study sets the
following research questions (RQs):
(1) What are the relationships between ICT use for learning and leisure and students’ achievement in math, reading, and science?
(2) What are the relationships between self-efficacy in digital competencies and achievement in math, reading, and science, and
does self-efficacy in digital competencies mediate the effect of ICT use on achievement?
(3) What roles do gender and immigrant status have in these relationships?
To answer these questions, we tested the model shown in Fig. 1, where both direct relationships between ICT use and achievement,
and indirect relationships through self-efficacy in digital competencies were examined.
2. Methods
2.1. Data source
This study uses Finnish data from PISA 2022 (N = 10,239) in the overall model and the gender grouping model. In the case of
immigrant grouping, there are fewer entries (Ntotal = 9,874; Nnonimmigrant = 8,066; Nsecond-generation = 790; Nfirst-generation = 1,018) due to
missing data, as immigrant status is calculated based on the country of birth of the student and both parents (OECD, 2024). This means
that if one of these questions is not answered, the student does not get a valid immigrant value.
The variables used, shown in Table 1, are all the items in questions regarding the use of ICT in enquiry-based learning activities
(IC174), weekday leisure use (IC177), self-efficacy in digital competencies (IC183), all replication weights, student weights, gender
(ST004D01T), and immigrant status (IMMIG). Please note that for the sake of brevity, the remainder of the text refers to ‘ICT use for
learning’ and ‘ICT use for leisure’ instead of the full names, respectively ‘use of ICT in enquiry-based learning’ and ‘weekday leisure use
of ICT.’
The PISA 2022 Technical Report (OECD, 2024) and the PISA Data Analysis Manual (OECD, 2009) provide further details about data
and how they should be analyzed. The questionnaires, frameworks, and other documentation are openly available in the OECD library
(OECD, n.d.), including a detailed report on the changes to the 2022 ICT questionnaire (Lorenceau et al., 2019).

2.2. Analysis
As self-efficacy in digital competencies is a new concept in PISA 2022, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis of its questionnaire items and found a clear separation. CIL competencies cover the first 11 items of question IC183, which include searching for
and assessing information, using text- and presentation-editing software, and collecting and recording data. CT competencies cover the
last 3 items, which include creating a computer program, troubleshooting code, and solving problems algorithmically. This separation
is also based on PISA’s ICT framework (OECD, 2023), where self-efficacy in digital competencies is largely based on the definitions of
CIL and CT conceptualized in ICILS (Fraillon et al., 2023). Reliability tests of all latent factors, shown in Table 2, were conducted using
SPSS version 28 and showed good reliability. In addition, the standardized factor loadings were good as none were below .400 and the
averages for each factor were above .600 (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013). The average loadings for each factor, in order of appearance in
Table 2, were .697, .678, .785, and .858.
We also ran a model where self-efficacy was a single 14-item factor, however, model fit indices were worse than the current model.
The factor loadings for the last 3 items were below .600, and the average for the factor was .710, which was also worse than the 2-factor
version in the current model. Appendix 2 contains a more detailed comparison between the current model and the rejected model. We
chose 2 factors for self-efficacy because the model had a better fit, the factor loadings for CIL and CT did not drop below .600, the
average loadings for the CIL and CT factors were also better, and McDonald’s Omega still showed good reliability. In addition, Rohatgi
et al. (2016) found that self-efficacy in basic ICT skills and self-efficacy in advanced ICT skills had opposing direct and indirect effects
on CIL scores. This hinted at the need to investigate whether there were similar opposing effects of self-efficacy in the current study.
The relationships between the ICT constructs were investigated using structural equation modelling (SEM), as it allows examining
multi-factor and multi-group relationships at the same time, as well as allowing complex operations such as missing data imputation,
multilevel analyses, and weighting of clustered data (Byrne, 2011; Hoyle, 2011; Maruyama, 1998). The SEM analysis was conducted in
Mplus version 8.11, which generates descriptions of the relationships among continuous latent variables (factors) among observed
variables of various types, and between factors and observed variables in multivariate regression models (Muthén & Muthén,
1998–2017, pp. 55–56). The overall model was analyzed using general SEM and the other models using multi-group SEM. We followed
the OECD’s recommendations on how to work with PISA data (OECD, 2009) in order to ensure unbiased and reliable estimates that
account for measurement error and the complex sampling design of the PISA study. We used Fay’s version of the balanced repeated

RMSEA <.05 and an acceptable model fit when TLI, CFI >.90, SRMR <.08, and RMSEA <.06 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). Note that all model

such as in this study, it does not matter which PV is picked, as type I error is not substantial, and population statistics are still calculated
in an unbiased way with just one PV (OECD, 2009) and can thus be used to estimate model fit. It is, however, strongly recommended to
report final results with the averages of the tests with all 10 PVs to ensure more accurate and unbiased estimates, especially in smaller
samples (OECD, 2009) such as the second- and first-generation immigrant groups. The mediation effects (MacKinnon, 2008) of
perceived self-efficacy in CIL and CT on the relationship between ICT use and achievement were investigated. Mediating variables
(self-efficacy) intervene in and clarify the relationship between the independent (ICT use) and dependent (achievement) variables.
Model diagrams and mediation effects were calculated and reported with the averages of all 10 PVs for each subject. All group
models were tested for invariance and considered to hold invariance when ΔCFI ≤.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤.015 (Chen, 2007). We found
metric invariance for the gender grouping (RMSEA = .046; CFI = .923; TLI = .917; SRMR = .063; ΔCFI = .003; ΔRMSEA <.000). We
found scalar invariance for the immigrant status grouping (RMSEA = .046; CFI = .918; TLI = .916; SRMR = .062; ΔCFI = .002;
ΔRMSEA = − .001). The full results of the invariance tests are included in Appendix 3.
In addition, we tested the models with ESCS as a confounder control variable which resulted in a worse model fit than without the
control and the immigrant grouping model did not converge. In Appendix 4 we have included the model fit and regression coefficient
tables for all models that converged with and without the ESCS control. The control variable caused almost all coefficients to drop by a
small amount, c.a.040 on average. Some of these coefficients also became statistically significant, although still smaller than .100, thus
not practically significant. We expand the interpretation regarding practical significance in the discussion section. Considering the
poorer model fit, the convergence issues, the minor changes that ESCS brought to the models, and our aim to represent immigrant
students in our analysis, we chose to present the results without controlling the ESCS.
3. Results
3.1. The relationships between ICT constructs and achievement in the overall model
The overall model, pictured in Fig. 2, had an acceptable fit (χ 2 = 9937.020; RMSEA = .043; CFI = .930; TLI = .921; SRMR = .065).
There were no significant direct relationships between ICT use for learning and achievement. However, ICT use for learning had a
positive effect on self-efficacy in both CIL and CT. ICT use for leisure had a negative relationship with achievement and self-efficacy in
CIL, but it had a positive relationship with self-efficacy in CT. Self-efficacy in CIL had a positive direct effect on achievement in all three
subjects, while self-efficacy in CT had a negative direct effect.

4. Discussion
The discussion section will first go over the effects of ICT on achievement (RQ1), then the effects of self-efficacy in digital competencies on achievement (RQ2), and finally the role of gender and immigrant status (RQ3). The practical significance of the findings
will be discussed in the context of standardized regression coefficients functioning as effect sizes (Flora et al., 2025). These will be
interpreted according to Funder and Ozer’s (2019) guidelines, where an effect size of .050–.100 indicates a small short-term effect that
offers little to no practical significance, .200 indicates a medium short-term effect that is practically significant, .300 indicates a large
short- and long-term effect that is also practically significant, and .400 and above indicates a large effect that is possibly an overestimation. In the context of PISA 2022, 20 score points, or .200 standard deviations, is considered to reflect a year’s worth of
development in math, reading, and science (OECD, 2023b, p. 156).
4.1. The effects of ICT use on achievement (RQ1)
Our overall model shows that ICT use for learning does not have a statistically significant direct effect on achievement among
Finnish students. This is different from earlier findings, where negative relationships were found between ICT use for school- or
learning-related purposes and achievement in math and science (Courtney et al., 2022), computer use for schoolwork practice and
math (Kim, 2018), and ICT use for enquiry-based learning and all subjects (Palimo, 2024). Perhaps our results could be related to Oinas
et al.’s (2023) findings that digital technologies used in Finnish classrooms rarely require advanced skills and are often used as breaks
during lessons instead of for pedagogically beneficial activities.
We found that leisure use of ICT had a negative relationship with achievement across subjects and groups. This is also echoed by
earlier findings that using ICT for leisure purposes is detrimental to reading (Hu & Yu, 2021; Ma & Qin, 2021) and that ICT use in and
out of school, for both learning and leisure, is associated with lower science scores (Odell, Galovan, & Cutumisu, 2020). Excessive
internet use is linked to feeling low and being tired at school (Lahti et al., 2021); hence, high use of ICT for leisure could have a negative
effect on achievement because it affects the students’ mood and sleep. The regression coefficients between ICT use for leisure and
achievement, ranging between − .224 and − .270, are also practically significant and can be interpreted as more than a year gap. In
other words, high use of ICT for leisure is associated with being over a year behind in math, reading, and science, and thus a concern for
parents, policymakers, and educators.

4.2. The direct and mediating effects of self-efficacy on achievement (RQ2)
We found that self-efficacy in CIL, which involved tasks in digital contexts, such as searching for and assessing information, writing
and editing a text, and collecting and analyzing data, was positively related to achievement in all three subjects. However, self-efficacy
in CT, which involved tasks such as writing a computer program, troubleshooting software errors, and algorithmic problem-solving,
was negatively related to achievement across subjects. While such a split in self-efficacy has not yet been studied in PISA, a similar
study using ICILS data also found positive effects of self-efficacy in basic ICT skills and negative effects of self-efficacy in advanced ICT
skills (Rohatgi et al., 2016). In addition, our results confirm previous findings that ICT competencies as a whole have a positive effect
on one and/or more of the three subjects (Li & Zhu, 2023; Odell, Cutumisu, & Gierl, 2020; Odell, Galovan, & Cutumisu, 2020; Palimo,
2024; Ünal et al., 2022). However, other studies have reported negative effects in Eastern countries and varying effects in Western
countries when examining the relationship between ICT competencies and achievement (Ma & Qin, 2021; Meng et al., 2019); thus, the
varying effects for Finland continue this trend for Western countries. Additionally, others have found no significant effect of ICT
competencies on literacy (Lezhnina & Kismihók, 2022).

We also investigated the mediating role of self-efficacy in digital competencies on the relationship between ICT use and
achievement and found that self-efficacy can play a protective role. For example, in the overall model, ICT use for learning did not have
a significant direct effect on achievement, but after accounting for the indirect effects of self-efficacy in both CIL and CT competencies,
the total effect was positive. This protective mediating effect of self-efficacy on the relationship between ICT use and achievement has
also been found by Li and Zhu (2023).
Regarding the practical significance of these results, we can interpret the regression coefficients between self-efficacy in CIL and
achievement, ranging from .440 to .528, as very large effect size and possibly an overestimation. In other words, high self-efficacy in
CIL is associated with being more than 2 years ahead in math, reading, and science. Regarding self-efficacy in CT, the regression
coefficients range from − .214 to − .334, meaning that high self-efficacy in CT is associated with being over a year behind in math,
reading, and science. We can also say that the majority of the mediation effects lack practical significance as the regression coefficients
are under |.100|. The only exception is how using ICT for learning is providing a short-term (less than a year) boost to achievement via
self-efficacy in CIL (coefficients ranging between .115 and .138). This stark division between self-efficacy in CIL and in CT hints at the
need to examine what CT skills students are developing and practicing both at and outside of school. In addition, the evaluation of CT
skills should go beyond self-reported data, and incorporate scores and log data of digital assessments of CT tasks.
4.3. The role of gender and immigrant status (RQ3)
Among girls, ICT use for learning had a direct positive effect on math (.065), and the total effects on all 3 subjects were significant
and positive (between .076 and .133). Among boys, ICT use for learning only had a significant total effect on math (.043). However,
these coefficients are small and reflect little to no practical significance, except for short-term (less than a year) boosts in math and
science among girls. Since boys tend to use ICT more excessively than girls (Hooft Graafland, 2018) and in accordance with their
interests (Lahti et al., 2021), it is possible that boys have a limited use of ICT for learning due to lack of interest and distractions from
using ICT for purposes other than learning. We found no gender differences in the effects of self-efficacy in both CIL and CT on
achievement. In contrast, studying ICILS data, Campos and Scherer (2024) found gender differences in self-efficacy in CIL. As the new
self-efficacy concept in PISA 2022 is modelled on ICILS (Lorenceau et al., 2019; OECD, 2023), we can expect to gain more information
about how background variables such as gender may affect its function within the PISA study as more secondary analyses of PISA 2022
data emerge.
We found statistically and practically significant differences between the immigrant groups. ICT use for learning had negative
direct relationships with math and science among second-generation immigrants and with reading and science among first-generation
immigrants. These results echo findings that computer use for schoolwork has a negative effect on math for both first- and secondgeneration immigrants (Kim, 2018). This result was unexpected because in Finnish schools, ICT is used more often as a support
tool with immigrant students (Oinas et al., 2023) and was expected to have a positive impact. Immigrant students also scored

in their ability to use ICT for various tasks. Thus, the negative direct effects and the nonsignificant total effects of ICT use for learning in
immigrant groups could also be related to overall lower digital skills and using ICT for basic tasks. It was unexpected that self-efficacy
in CIL negatively mediated the effects of ICT use for leisure among nonimmigrants, but for both second- and first-generation immigrants, these indirect effects were not significant. Kim (2018) found similar differences based on immigrant status, although their study
measured mediation through self-efficacy in math and found that computer use for programming had positive indirect and total effects
for second-generation students but negative effects for first-generation and nonimmigrant students. Thus, the current study adds a
novel perspective to the topic. It is also possible that the difference in group sizes and the exclusion of students due to missing
immigrant status data also affected the significance levels of the results. The practical significance of the findings is that immigrant
students experienced a short-term (less than a year) gap in achievement compared to nonimmigrants when considering their use of ICT
for learning. However, this gap was bridged by the short-term boost provided by self-efficacy in CIL, resulting in the total effects of
using ICT for learning being neither statistically nor practically significant. Thus, self-efficacy in CIL plays an important protective role
in immigrant students and should be developed alongside other digital skills.
5. Conclusion
This study contributes to the literature investigating how different uses of ICT affect achievement in math, reading, and science,
highlighting the mediating effect of ICT competencies on this relationship, especially in immigrant students. ICT use for learning
lacked practical significance across groups, except for some short-term benefits for math among girls. Across groups, using ICT for
leisure was related to being over a year behind in math, reading, and science. Furthermore, the study highlights the different effects of
self-efficacy in CIL (positive) and CT (negative), which reflect over a year’s worth of achievement in math, reading, and science. In
immigrant students, ICT use for learning was associated with being less than a year behind in achievement, however, this short-term
gap was bridged by students’ self-efficacy in CIL, indicating its protective role.
The limitations of the current study need to also be considered in order to contextualize the results. One limitation is that the
current study focuses only on Finnish data. Therefore, the results are not generalizable because they reflect the Finnish society and
educational system, and our models might not apply to other countries. Another limitation concerns the split in the “self-efficacy in
digital competencies” construct – although we chose the 2-factor model, a 1-factor model was also possible and could have yielded a
different result. Also, self-efficacy in CT consisted of only 3 items, writing a program, troubleshooting software, and solving algorithmic problems. Similarly, ICILS 2023; 2018 respectively use a 3-item and a 4-item construct for self-efficacy in CT. These may be too
few to represent the construct holistically as it typically consists of additional elements, including but not limited to decomposition,
abstraction, and generalization (see e.g., Tang et al., 2020). This uncovers a larger need to balance between concise items and conceptual breadth in large scale assessments. In addition, in our model we assumed that self-efficacy in digital competencies was having
an effect on achievement, however, the direction of the relationship could be reverse or bi-directional. Being newly introduced to PISA,
self-efficacy in digital competencies requires further study. Regarding the immigrant group comparisons, the significance levels of the
coefficients for second- and first-generation immigrants could have been affected by the large difference in group size and the exclusion
of students with missing immigrant values, which would have introduced some bias. We attempted to control for ESCS, as shown in
Appendix 4, and found little to no change in terms statistical significance, the magnitude of the relationships, and the practical significance in the two models that converged. It is, however, possible that accounting for ESCS in a smaller model that converges in all
multigroup analyses could have provided further explanations for the associations. In addition, while we have not discussed the relationships between the observed variables, math, reading and science, these are highly correlated, as shown in Appendix 5. Thus, it is
possible that, for example, poor reading skills would be detrimental to achievement in math and science.
Our findings point to the need to further investigate the different roles that different ICT competencies play and how they can be
addressed through ICT use for learning. Given that ICT use for leisure was associated with being a year behind in achievement, we
recommend a more balanced engagement with digital leisure activities. It is particularly puzzling why self-efficacy in CT competencies
has a consistently negative effect across groups, reflecting a gap of a year or more. It is not clear whether this has to do with how CT was
measured in PISA 2022, whether it is related to students’ understanding of what CT is, or if it reflects how CT is studied at school.
