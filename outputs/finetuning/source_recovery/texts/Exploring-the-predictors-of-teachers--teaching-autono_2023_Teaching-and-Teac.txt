Concerning the critical function of teachers’ teaching autonomy in instructional practice and professional
development, this study explores the relative importance of three predictive mechanisms: a. professionalism of
teachers, b. complexity of teaching practice, and c. culture of individualism. Using the sample of 118,347
teachers from 8262 schools in 27 countries derived from the 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey
(TALIS), we applied a three-level hierarchical linear model (HLM) for analysis. We found that teachers’ teaching
autonomy was mainly predicted by the professionalism of teachers and the culture of individualism rather than
the complexity of teaching practice. Implications for individual teachers, school leaders, and policymakers are
discussed.

1. Introduction
Over the last few decades, teachers’ teaching autonomy has been a
heated debate in global public discourse, and there has been a growing
research interest (Guo & Wang, 2021; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2021). There
is little question about whether teaching autonomy plays a significant
role in explaining effective educational practices and whether increasing
teaching autonomy links to beneficial outcomes. For example, teaching
autonomy fosters a sense of ownership and empowerment among
teachers who strive to advance their professional careers and assume
increasing responsibilities (Khan, 2019). It is also considered a basic
professional need and is closely associated with teachers’ job motivation
(Börü, 2018), work engagement, and emotional exhaustion (Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2020). Additionally, teaching autonomy has a significant
impact on teachers’ job satisfaction (Worth & Van den Brande, 2020),
professional identity (Skinner, Leavey, & Rothi, 2021), job commitment
(Collie, Granziera, & Martin, 2018; Skinner et al., 2021), and retention
intention (Worth & Van den Brande, 2020).
Out of this importance, enhancing teachers’ autonomy in the classroom is critical. Consequently, researchers have emphasized that the
predictors of teachers’ autonomy should be carefully considered and
evaluated (Prichard & Moore, 2016a; Prichard and Moore, 2016b).
Recent research on the predictors of teaching autonomy has focused
mainly on individual characteristics, such as teachers’ teaching experiences and personal capability (Lamb & Simpson, 2003), excluding

contextual factors. According to Lepine (2007) and Salokangas and
Wermke (2020), teaching autonomy is also a context-dependent
concept, and can be impacted by a multi-layer of factors. For instance,
existing literature increasingly emphasizes the historical and sociocultural nature of teaching autonomy (Vangrieken, Grosemans, Dochy, &
Kyndt, 2017); thus, we must consider local and nation-specific structures. School-level characteristics such as principals’ leadership style
and organizational climate can be key predictors of teaching autonomy
(Dieudé & Prøitz, 2022). The literature proposes that teaching autonomy
tends to reach a high level within schools with supportive and collaborative working environments (Liu, Keeley, Sui, & Sang, 2021; Nguyen
& Walkinshaw, 2018). Furthermore, teaching autonomy is a notion that
is closely related to different education systems’ broader social, cultural,
and political backgrounds. Existing research has also demonstrated that
teaching autonomy differs by country. For example, teachers from East
Asia usually sense less teaching autonomy, whereas those from Western
countries have a great sense (Guo & Wang, 2021). This variation has also
been discovered in the Nordic region (Mausethagen & Mølstad, 2015),
indicating international differences and large variances owing to
country-level predictors. Although nearly no study has addressed predictors of teaching autonomy from a comprehensive perspective so far,
only one empirical study has included indicators from three levels. Li
and Allen (2021) used data from The Schools and Staffing Survey (SASS)
to examine the relationship between teachers’ autonomy and individual,
school, and state characteristics in the United States (US). Even though,

However, as in previous research, the underlying influence mechanism between predictive variables remains unclear. Existing studies
have primarily focused on the superficial predictors of teachers’ teaching autonomy, which results in this field being shallow and lacking
deeper explanation. Meanwhile, in different studies, these variables are
always treated as separate, isolated, and disconnected, rather than
united, to claim the integrated effects (Lamb & Simpson, 2003; Wilches,
2007). Therefore, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive examination of the mechanisms that synthesize related predictions. While
mechanism usually refers to an assembly of moving parts that perform a
particular function, the predictive mechanisms in this study potentially
reflect the core and fundamental principles of how teachers’ teaching
autonomy is systematically determined and functioning. Predictive
mechanisms can relate multiple pieces of information with similar features and are critical for a collective and clear understanding of a
complex system (Zhang, Chen, Stan, Zhou, & Maciejowski, 2008). Unlike individual factors, mechanisms provide a merged view of the
operation of teachers’ teaching autonomy. As Vansteenkiste and Ryan
(2013) stated, empirical indicators are but an operationalization of the
more fundamental mechanisms that are at play. It seems to be more
important for the latter to be revealed. Therefore, a framework that
enables the organization of predictors more comprehensively is needed
so researchers and practitioners can focus on reconsidering teachers’
teaching autonomy.
To date, this study aims at exploring the effects of different predictors
and potential mechanisms on teachers’ teaching autonomy. These impacts will be quantitatively analyzed at the teacher, school, and country
levels in an international context. We contribute to filling the current
research gap in three ways. First, we construct a three-dimensional
mechanism framework based on the literature to apply a theoretical
lens to understand the various predictors of teaching autonomy. This
framework redirects attention away from a plethora of separated indicators and onto the more fundamental mechanisms. Moreover, the
framework may be utilized to organize the empirical results of this study
rationally and can be applied to future research. For example, while
maintaining the consistency of the framework in this study, all the
findings, discussions, and conclusions will be organized and explained
according to the three mechanisms. As the clue of teachers’ teaching
autonomy, different mechanisms will include various predictors which
will provide us an incorporated but detailed elaboration of the results.
Thus, it avoids an overly biased interpretation of one or some factors.
Second, we intend to provide a complete analysis of teacher, school,
and country predictors rather than discrete descriptions of isolated indicators. Similar to the previous point, we persist in the integration
perspective; but here, mainly through the lens of factor structure, it
forms a vertical, multi-level hierarchy for this study to comprise multiple factors from the individual and context properly. This enables us to
consider variables together, form a dynamic interaction through a threelevel model, and gain more thorough knowledge of this notion. In this
way, the differences and relationships between variables are allowed to
be interactively connected, analyzed, and compared across the three
levels, which will reveal the relative force that causes teaching autonomy to change continuously. Together with the mechanisms mentioned
before, we can develop a more flexible and multi-dimensional approach
to further understand the prediction effects under a two-way scheme.
Third, we perform our analysis in an international setting, attempting to generalize the findings of prior studies that mainly focused on
Anglo-American and Nordic nations to countries worldwide. Thus, the
conclusions of this study will give an end to some previously contradictory findings. In addition, this study will provide a broader database
with sufficient empirical evidence of teachers’ teaching autonomy and
decision-making regarding supporting policies, which has strong implications, both globally and individually.

2. Literature review
2.1. Teaching autonomy
Teaching autonomy is rooted in the concept of autonomy. Individual
autonomy is widely understood to encompass freedom and the internal
ability for decision-making and action (Benson, 2010; Mausethagen &
Mølstad, 2015). Researchers further expanded this definition to teachers. Accordingly, Gwaltney (2012, p. 22) defined teacher autonomy as
“the degree to which teaching provides substantial freedom, independence, power, and discretion to participate in scheduling, selecting, and
executing administrative, instructional, and socialization and sorting
activities both in the classroom and in the school organization at large.”
As a complicated notion, the numerous elements of teacher autonomy can be classified into two categories (Friedman, 1999; Littlewood,
1996). In the first category, teaching autonomy refers to teachers’ degree of autonomy and control over critical parts of the classroom
(Nguyen & Walkinshaw, 2018). The second factor is autonomy in school
functions, which focuses on teachers’ internal abilities to act and
participate under contextual restrictions (Benson, 2010). Previous
studies have demonstrated that a lack of autonomy in classroom
teaching is significantly more intolerable than a lack of participation in
school decision-making (Wermke, Olason Rick, & Salokangas, 2019).
Therefore, the first component, teaching autonomy, deserves more
exploration and is the core concept of this study.
As previously stated, teaching autonomy focuses on daily instructional practices and classroom decision-making. Husband and Short
(1994, p. 60) conceptualized teaching autonomy as “the ability to control daily schedules, teach as one chooses, have the freedom to make
decisions on instruction, and generate ideas about the curriculum.” This
reflects teachers’ perceptions of the degree to which they exert control
over themselves and their work environments (Pearson & Moomaw,
2005). Empirical evidence suggests that teaching autonomy is critical
for instruction and classroom management, and it has also been shown
to promote teachers’ professional development (Choi & Mao, 2021),
teaching success, and students’ academic outcomes (Day, 2019).
The multi-dimensional characteristics of teaching autonomy are
widely acknowledged. Pearson and Hall (1993) used factor analysis to
divide teaching autonomy into two dimensions: general teaching autonomy and curricular autonomy. The former is primarily concerned
with classroom standards of behavior and personal on-the-job discretion, which ensures instructors’ control over the general work environment and their creative ability. The latter focuses on concerns
regarding activity selection, material choice, instructional planning, and
sequencing, logically consistent with teachers’ need for autonomy in
teaching and learning decision-making. Similarly, Vangrieken et al.
(2017) distinguished teaching autonomy into didactical-pedagogical
and curricular autonomy. Whichever theoretical division and dimensions of teaching autonomy, they all indicate the extent to which
teachers can freely decide and determine their actions within a particular classroom teaching situation.
2.2. Predictors of teaching autonomy
As we claimed earlier, teachers’ teaching autonomy is deeply connected to social, cultural, and political contexts. Teachers in different
countries/regions are embedded in and profoundly influenced by
certain sociocultural settings (Bjork, 2004). These settings may be
similar or they may also differ. However, it is under this premise
assumption that macro social and cultural factors can gradually penetrate down to the school and teacher levels, and eventually influence
teachers’ autonomy. Also, in this way, it will generate various predictors
of teaching autonomy at each level and allow us to compare them across
different units.
Although many studies have focused on the factors influencing
teachers’ teaching autonomy, almost no study has developed a

predictions and probabilistic estimates of future events (Brunborg,
2008). The predictors help us to understand how the target factor will
change from an individual variable perspective. There is no doubt that
this study will follow the effects of different predictors; however, an
excessive emphasis on predictors may divert attention away from what
matters most: the underlying mechanism (Van den Borre, Spruyt, & Van
Droogenbroeck, 2021). Unlike predictors, mechanisms incorporate
related information together and emphasize essential principles and
synthesized systems regarding how something is determined or regulated (Vespignani, Canal, Molinaro, Fonda, & Cacciari, 2010). On the
one hand, mechanisms can classify and include different predictors to
analyze their impacts clearly and methodically, especially when many
scattered variables exist. On the other hand, through the integration of
predictors, mechanisms can reflect the most critical predictive principles
and deeper operating rules instead of simply stopping at individual
predictors. Thus, we propose developing the influence mechanisms of
teaching autonomy, organizing the predictors under the framework, and
helping visualize the different latent effects.
According to previous theoretical and empirical studies, three predictive mechanisms of individual autonomy are wildly acknowledged:
“professionalism” which is defined as the essential skill and competency
that is expected from a person or an organization that is trained to do a
job well (Rothstein, 2003; Willner, 1990), “complexity of practice”
which is regarded as the challenges and difficulty that individual will
face in the practical activities (Katerndahl, Parchman, & Wood, 2009;
Neprash et al., 2021), and “culture of individualism” that represents the
prioritization or emphasis of the individual over the entire group
(O’Brien, 2015). These three mechanisms have also been preliminarily
examined in other fields. For example, in the medical field, researchers
have found that medical staff autonomy is closely related to their
experience (Schneider, 1998), educational level (Labrague,
McEnroe-Petitte, & Tsaras, 2019), the complexity of patient care
(Neprash et al., 2021), and individualistic ideas of themselves (Armstrong, 2002). With strong implications from the studies mentioned
above, these mechanisms are also of concern to scholars of teacher education (Helgøy & Homme, 2007; Parker, 2015). Therefore, we distinguish three essential mechanisms affecting teaching autonomy based on
previous literature: a. professionalism of teachers, b. complexity of
teaching practice, and c. cultural tradition of individualism. These
mechanisms were shortened to “Professionalism”, “Complexity”, and
“Culture” in this study. Predictors of teaching autonomy will be separately and logically grouped into these three mechanisms. At the same
time, these three mechanisms can be identified at the teacher, school,
and country levels, finally forming a structured, comprehensive 3 × 3
framework for review, analysis, and interpretation (See Table 1).

Generally, an individual teachers’ professionalism can be conceptually
divided into institutional and practical. Institutional professionalism
comprises a series of established criteria and licensing requirements that
are institutionally acknowledged (Ingersoll & Merrill, 2011), such as
teachers’ qualifications, educational degrees, and employment status.
For example, studies have shown that qualified teachers with a master’s
degree are more likely to be treated as professionals and have a higher
sense of teaching autonomy (Lepine, 2007). Additionally, teachers who
are permanently employed or working full-time at schools may be more
trusted by leaders than those hired temporarily or part-time.
Practical professionalism reflects teachers’ daily teaching practices
and instructional abilities. We propose four important indicators:
teachers’ teaching experience, professional preparedness, instructional
quality, and self-efficacy. As has been widely discussed, how teachers
develop and sense their autonomy depends on their prior experience
(Benson, 2010); experienced teachers usually report a higher degree of
autonomy than novice teachers. However, a few studies have revealed
that staff experience does not affect teaching autonomy (Prichard &
Moore, 2016a), highlighting the importance of further investigating this
relationship in a broader context. In addition, teachers’ teaching autonomy is suggested to be authorized regarding teachers’ professional
preparation in teacher education programs. Those not well-prepared or
still during their induction period may be granted less autonomy
(Anderson, 1987; Bartlett & Johnson, 2010). Furthermore, scholars
argue that experts with higher instructional quality and self-efficacy
tend to enjoy greater autonomy because administrators may perceive
themselves as less qualified to supervise them (Lepine, 2007).

regarded as reliable in educational goal attainment, they will be

2.2.1. Professionalism of teachers
As one of the key mechanisms, the professionalism of teachers refers
to the degree to which they can effectively handle and manage classroom teaching issues, provide high-quality instruction, and improve
students’ academic performance convincingly and professionally (Erss
& Kalmus, 2018). Historically, teachers’ professionalism has been
closely attached to their autonomy and regarded as a central pillar of the
latter (Day, 2019). Teachers who are more professional in instruction
are enriched in autonomy and have freedom from reliance (Pearson &
Moomaw, 2005). For instance, the strong correlation between the
enrichment in autonomy and freedom from reliance was primarily
confirmed by a study showing that teachers’ autonomy steadily
increased as their professional instructional quality (Dikilitaş & Mumford, 2019). Therefore, in the following section, we consider several
predictors of the professionalism mechanism at the three levels.
Firstly, at the teacher level, it is believed that autonomy is not a
natural right for individual teachers but must be earned through their
internal capacity and degree of professionalism (Dieudé & Prøitz, 2022).

Thirdly, at the country level, teachers’ professionalism is mainly
regulated and represented by how the country, society, policymakers,

educational soft power from a global perspective and then convert that
perception into an appreciation for their country’s teachers, further
building their professional status and forming their autonomy. Thus, the
PISA score is a critical country-level characteristic that must be
considered.

2.2.3. Culture of individualism
As a structural construct, autonomy is inextricably linked to individual agency and institutional constraints, both shaped by the hidden
culture (Murray, 2021; Paradis, Lutovac, Jokikokko, & Kaasila, 2019;
Pearson & Moomaw, 2005). According to Hofstede’s culture theory,
individualism-collectivism is essential for understanding people’s autonomy (Hofstede, 1980; Hofstede, Hofstede, & Minkov, 2010). Members of individualistic cultures identify themselves as distinct and
unique, pursue their own goals, and gain self-esteem from their capacity
for self-expression. Consequently, their autonomy was enhanced.
Members of a collectivistic culture define themselves through their
affiliation with a social group, advocate for the public interest, and draw
self-esteem from their capacity to maintain group harmony. As a result,
they value collaboration over autonomy. Based on this, we can infer that
whether a nation, organization, or person advocates for the individualistic culture and encourages independence largely determines the
degree of teachers’ teaching autonomy.
Nevertheless, other researchers have suggested that teachers’ common sense beliefs and the conceptual notion of autonomy connect autonomy with individualism or independence (Street & Licata, 1989).
Teachers’ culture has a long tradition of relating to and amplifying their
interpretations of individualized autonomy as independence and individualism, forming a reactive attitude that seems to exclude collaboration. Overall, the concepts of teaching autonomy and individualistic
culture were logically consistent. Teachers’ teaching autonomy will be
greater when culture is more aligned with individualism on the
individualism-collectivism axis.
From a macro perspective, Hofstede et al. (2010) indicated that
individualistic cultures vary in different nations based on the countries’
individualism index he generated. For example, while Anglo-American
countries tend to score higher individualism index, East Asian countries usually score lower (Helgøy & Homme, 2007; Hofstede et al.,
2010). Generally, a higher individualism index in a country predicts a
more robust individualistic culture and influences the decentralization
tendency of educational policies and curricular orientation. Taking this
one step further, it will finally authorize power to the bottom and
strengthen teachers’ teaching autonomy through various institutional
conditions (Hammersley-Fletcher, Kılıçoğlu, & Kılıçoğlu, 2021).

2.2.2. Complexity of teaching practice
One of the most important reasons for equipping teachers with
teaching autonomy is the complexity of their daily teaching practices.
Teachers are expected to encounter various complex issues during the
teaching process, which requires autonomy and freedom to enact subject
content effectively, responsibly monitor student learning, manage
classroom discipline promptly, and complete the instructional goals.
However, practical complexities may vary when teachers face different
classroom situations and issues directly influencing their autonomy.
Although there is no identical operational definition of teaching
complexity, we recognize two key ideas for representing this mechanism
from existing relevant studies. On the one hand, the number of students
each teacher educates is an essential component that reflects the
complexity of the teaching practice. It is self-evident that when a teacher
educates a larger class size and is confronted with more students, they
will need more autonomy to manage the disorder, address unanticipated
problems, and focus on each student’s cognition and emotions.
Conversely, teachers can easily identify and react to students’ needs in
smaller classes (Nye, Hedges, & Konstantopoulos, 2004). On the other
hand, the diversity of student compositions that teachers encounter is
also significant. When a teacher educates a group of diverse students,
particularly those with a high percentage of social-economically status
(SES) disadvantaged students, they will also require additional autonomy to focus their attention on those students (Li & Allen, 2021), as well
as ensuring a friendly environment that supports their diverse needs
(Núñez, Fernández, León, & Grijalvo, 2015).
These two predictors of the “Complexity” mechanism can be identified at the teacher, school, and country levels. The class size and percentage of low-SES students in the class can be easily recognized as

In a meso way, the last vital resource of the “Culture” mechanism to
predict teaching autonomy is school principals’ distributed leadership.
Wermke et al. (2019) claim that schools’ cultural contexts are critical for
realizing teaching autonomy. There will always be a conflict and a
balance between teacher autonomy and leadership styles in an organization. Thus, principals’ distributed leadership, as how school leaders
exert control, affects teachers’ perceived teaching autonomy (Katzenmeyer & Moller, 2001). Although distributed leadership seems to be a
collaborative approach for school leaders, it is also a supporting and
empowering style for individual teachers. Meanwhile, it has been
empirically confirmed that distributed leadership empowers and increases individual teachers’ autonomy (Liu et al., 2021; Skaalvik &
Skaalvik, 2021), consistent with individualistic cultures’ emphasis on
authorizing individual teachers’ teaching autonomy.
In sum, for the “Culture” mechanism, this study hypothesizes that
teachers’ teaching autonomy will be higher when teachers show preferences and devote more time to individual work rather than colleague
collaboration; principles’ leadership style is more distributed, and

3.2.2. Professionalism of teachers
At the teacher level, we included teachers’ qualification (0 = no; 1 =
yes), educational level (0 = no master’s degree; 1 = master’s degree),
contract type (0 = temporary contract; 1 = permanent contract), and
work time (0 = part-time; 1 = full-time) as dummy variables. Teachers’
teaching experience was collected as a continuous variable based on their
responses regarding their working years. Professional preparedness was
measured using 12 items on a 4-point Likert scale to investigate how
well teachers felt prepared for different elements of teaching, such as
subject content, general pedagogy, and subject pedagogy (Cronbach’s
alpha = 0.920). Furthermore, teachers were required to respond to a 4point Likert scale consisting of 12 statements (e.g., “I set goals at the
beginning of instruction”; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.831) from three dimensions (clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, and classroom
management) to generate a mean score for instructional quality. Teachers’ self-efficacy was also measured using 12 items on a 4-point scale (e.
g., “I get students to believe they can do well in school work”; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.906) composed of three subscales referring to selfefficacy in classroom management, instruction, and student
engagement.
At the school level, the academic focus was measured by a 4-point
Likert scale with four statements administered by each school’s principal (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.780). Items such as “Teachers succeed in
implementing the school’s curriculum” refer to how teachers in the
school concentrate on the subject teaching. In addition, team innovativeness was operationalized using a scale of four items regarding
teachers’ perceptions of their colleagues’ innovativeness in the school
(e.g., “Most teachers in this school strive to develop new ideas for
teaching and learning”; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.918).
At the country level, we included two indicators. How teachers are
valued as professionals in the country was assessed by three items on a 4point scale investigating teachers’ perceived value of their teaching
profession (e.g., “Teachers’ views are valued by policymakers in this
country/region”; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.814). The mean score of each
country’s teachers for this variable was calculated to represent countrylevel outcomes. Countries’ PISA scores were adopted from the 2018
round of the Programme for International Student Assessment conducted by OECD to align with other data collection times. We added the
Reading, Mathematics, and Science scores for each country to generate
the total PISA score for the nation.

3. Methodology
3.1. Data
The data for this study were derived from the third wave (2018) of
the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) launched by the
Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD).
TALIS investigated the daily practices, professional development, and
working conditions of teachers and principals in lower secondary education (ISCED 2) in 48 countries and regions, allowing us to analyze
from an international perspective. During the sampling period, schools
were selected using systematic two-stage random sampling with a
probability proportionate to size within defined categories by nation
(OECD, 2019a), and teachers were randomly selected within each
selected school. Teachers and principals answered questionnaires individually, either on paper or electronically. The data were collected at the
end of the school year. After excluding cases with missing values for the
variables included in this study, we finally reached 118,347 teachers
from 8262 schools in 27 countries for the analysis.

3.2.4. Culture of individualism
As we mentioned in the literature review, teachers’ individualistic

At the country level, the individualism index was adopted from a

generated each country’s individualism index by multiplying the mean
score of the scale by 25 and adding a constant number of 50. The individualism index ranged from 6 (Guatemala) to 91 (US). Although the

3.2.5. Control variables
We also included several demographic indicators as control variables

urban area) as school-level control variables, and log gross domestic

4.1. Professionalism of teachers
For teacher-level predictors, Model 2 included eight variables related
to individual professional characteristics. The results showed that
teachers who were qualified or held a master’s degree did not significantly predict their teaching autonomy (β = 0.008, p > 0.05; β =
− 0.011, p > 0.05). We found significant positive parameters for teachers’ contract type and employment status (β = 0.015, p < 0.05; β =
0.022, p < 0.01), indicating that teachers who were permanently contracted or employed full-time perceived higher teaching autonomy than
those who were temporarily contracted or employed part-time. Teachers’ teaching experience did not seem to have a significant relationship
with the degree of teaching autonomy, however, indicating a slightly
negative effect (β = − 0.003, p > 0.05). Based on this result, novice
teachers were considered to own similar teaching autonomy as experienced teachers. Furthermore, the findings showed that teachers reported
higher teaching autonomy if they were well-prepared for the teaching
profession (β = 0.072, p < 0.001). In addition, teachers’ instructional
quality and self-efficacy played significant positive roles in predicting
teaching autonomy with relatively high coefficients (β = 0.065, p <
0.001; β = 0.188, p < 0.001). Whether teachers implemented highquality instruction or perceived greater self-efficacy in classroom management and student engagement had significant effects on the level of
autonomy.
Two school-level indicators under the “professionalism” mechanism
were examined in Model 5. Higher teaching autonomy was found when
teachers and their colleagues focused more on subject teaching and
academic achievement (β = 0.069, p < 0.01). Nevertheless, team
innovativeness was also a significant predictor of teaching autonomy (β
= 0.183, p < 0.001). Teachers tend to be empowered with more autonomy when they are creative and innovative in teaching and learning.

3.3. Analytic approach
Although previous studies analyzed predictors from different levels,
most of them did not take an appropriate approach to deal with the
multilevel structural issue among factors. According to the research
question and framework established in this study, different factors are
distributed across the three levels, forming a nested structural relationship. For example, individual teachers are grouped and nested in
different schools, and schools are grouped and nested in different
countries. In fact, these influencing factors have shaped a three-level
pyramid layout. Given the nested data structure, it is supposed to
build a hierarchical linear model (HLM) for categorizing and analyzing
all variables based on different levels rather than treating all data at the
same level (Hofmann, 1997). This can help address the problem of
between-group independence and within-group correlation of the data
(Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002, p. 100).
Therefore, we applied a three-level hierarchical linear model
approach to analyze the effects of predictors from the teacher, school,
and country levels, professionalism, complexity, and culture mechanisms on teaching autonomy. Following the HLM analysis procedure, we
added the variables into the model step-by-step according to the level
and entered the predictors from three mechanisms separately for each
level. Hence, we have gotten ten models, including one null model and
nine conditional models. The full conditional model is as follows:

4.3. Culture of individualism
Finally, we examined the relationships between the three hypothesized cultural predictors and teaching autonomy. The time allocation
ratio between individual work and colleague collaboration at the
teacher level in Model 4 strongly predicted teaching autonomy (β =
0.114, p < 0.001). This indicated that teachers who prefer to work
independently over collaboratively are expected to have a higher degree
of teaching autonomy. When considering distributed leadership at the
school level, it also significantly predicted the teaching autonomy of
teachers, even with a coefficient much higher than individualistic tendency (β = 0.168, p < 0.001). We can infer that when school leaders
implemented democratized leadership behaviors, teachers were more
satisfied with their autonomy. Although the predictive effect of the
countries’ individualism index on teaching autonomy was not statistically significant, there was a positive relationship between these two
variables. Teachers would sense greater teaching autonomy if the culture was closer to individualism rather than collectivism. These findings
together proved the close relationship between the culture of individualism and teaching autonomy across all three levels.

This study examined predictors from three levels and three theoretical mechanisms, aiming at a comprehensive consideration. To

(Model 0), we found that 82.7% of the variance in teaching autonomy
could be explained by the difference between individuals, 10.43%
explained by the difference between schools, and 6.87% explained by
country-level characteristics. Correspondingly, the full conditional
model (Model 9), which included the three mechanisms, demonstrated
that all predictors explained 10.18% of the total variance in teaching

5.2. Complexity of teaching practice
Regarding the second mechanism, “complexity of teaching practice”,
most predictors were not significantly correlated with teaching autonomy. This also reflects that, compared with the other two mechanisms,
the complexity of teaching practice is not considered the main predictive
mechanism of teachers’ teaching autonomy.
However, it is noteworthy that the percentage of low-SES students in
class and the student–teacher ratio showed negative predictive effects.
These findings imply a struggle of teachers in maintaining a balance
between teaching for equity and teaching for equality. Studies have
shown that the ratio of low-SES students and the class size are drawbacks
rather than advantages of teaching autonomy (Mustafa & Cullingford,
2008). In reality, it is difficult for teachers to offer individualized or
differentiated instruction to students from lower socioeconomic backgrounds in large classrooms. Even though the teachers know that some
low-SES students are struggling, they have to teach in a standardized
and identical way because of their limited time and resources. Therefore, the number and diversity of students restrict their teaching
possibilities.

5. Discussion
Based on the above findings, we offer further insight into how
different mechanisms contribute to teaching autonomy and provide
some potential explanations.

5.3. Culture of individualism

5.1. Professionalism of teachers

Regarding the final mechanism under analysis, we distinguished a
strong relationship between the “culture of individualism” and teaching
autonomy at three levels. Teachers have a greater feeling of autonomy
when they display preferences to work independently, which supports
the consistency between individualistic culture and teaching autonomy
from a personal perspective. In addition, principals’ distributed leadership is another important indicator of how favorably teachers’
teaching autonomy is influenced. Skaalvik and Skaalvik (2021) also
revealed that teachers perceived more freedom in classroom
decision-making when school leaders insisted on a distributed leadership style and holistic approaches to school governance. Finally,
teachers’ autonomy increases as nations’ individualism indexes rise,
although the relationship is statistically weak. After Li and Allen (2021)
identified the link between state policy cultures and teacher autonomy
in the US, we extended this conclusion to a broader international
context.
Culture is an endogenous mechanism systematically embedded in
society and education (Hallinger & Leithwood, 1996). People employ
culture as their action framework; however, it can also be used to
interpret their educational outcomes. Our findings across the three
levels suggest that autonomy is deeply rooted in individualistic culture.
Because, in an individualistic country, autonomy and freedom are seen
as the most important values. People tend to cherish themselves over
group, and independence over collaboration. Thus, not only will individual teachers ask for their power, but leaders and institutions will also
proactively empower teachers. And the stronger individualistic the
culture, the higher the teaching autonomy teachers will own.

Our findings showed that the “professionalism of teachers” has a
strong predictive effect on teaching autonomy. Among the predictors of
professionalism, as expected, teacher contract type, work time, professional preparedness, instructional quality, and self-efficacy were positively related to teaching autonomy. These findings are consistent with
previous studies (Bartlett & Johnson, 2010; Lepine, 2007). However,
other predictors, such as teachers’ qualifications, educational level, and
teaching experience, had little influence on teaching autonomy. These
findings contribute to existing knowledge that teacher autonomy was
less influenced by teaching experience (Pearson & Moomaw, 2006) and
teachers’ academic backgrounds (Benson, 2010). One possible explanation is that teachers with higher degrees or richer experience will
receive higher expectations or requirements from principals (Prichard &
Moore, 2016a). Therefore, they may feel a bit more constrained rather
than free in their daily practice.
At the school level, we found important context effects; teaching
autonomy was higher in schools where teachers and their colleagues
focused more on academic achievement and showed greater capability
for team innovativeness. Teachers in the schools will build a form of
collective professionalism if they uniformly show improved attitudes
and quality skills, such as implementing successful curriculums and
developing new ideas for instruction (Nguyen et al., 2021). As a result of
their professionalism, they will gain the organizational trust of school
leaders and be empowered in order to enact their autonomy better
(Zheng, Yin, & Liu, 2019).
Finally, how a country values teachers as professionals positively
determine their teaching autonomy, with an insignificant effect. This
finding supports the relationship between teachers’ professional images
in society and their degree of autonomy. Furthermore, connecting
Spruyt’s (2021) discovery of a strong correlation between countries’
PISA results and teachers’ perceived social status, we furtherly conclude
that how students perform in the international test also has a positive
association with teachers’ teaching autonomy. As an objective fact, how
students in a country perform in international academic assessments and
their rank globally represent teachers’ capacity to improve students’
academic achievement. As a subjective perception, the recognition of
teachers as professionals by parents, leaders, and other stakeholders also
matters significantly to teachers’ social status (Anderson, 1987). These
two factors will shape teachers’ professional reputations at the country
level and impact whether policymakers and the general public approve

6. Conclusion and implications
In this study, we investigated how three mechanisms a). professionalism of teachers, b). complexity of teaching practice, and c). culture
of individualism at the teacher, school, and country levels, were associated with teachers’ teaching autonomy. We used three-level hierarchical linear models to account for three different theoretical
mechanisms as well as the nesting structure of the education system.
This study indicates that teachers’ teaching autonomy is mainly predicted by their professionalism and culture of individualism rather than
the complexity of teaching practice. Even though, the multilevel analysis, including determinants under the two-way framework, highlights
the need for and importance of considering the three different

mechanisms and teacher, school, and country characteristics for a
thorough understanding of teaching autonomy.
Based on our findings, we developed implications for diverse subjects. First, individual teachers are acknowledged as having teaching
autonomy, which is earned. As a result, teachers should develop their
professionalism, especially in classroom practice. For example, teachers
should be well prepared in pre-service teacher education and novice
teacher induction while simultaneously building their competency and
capacity for classroom instruction and autonomy. Only in this manner
will they gain their leaders’ and policymakers’ trust and respect. Second,
principals and administrators must employ a distributed leadership style
in school governance and teacher instruction supervision to empower
individual teachers. Additionally, it is necessary to understand the
relevance of team innovation and academic focus in schools for school
leaders to establish professional learning communities for teachers and
help improve their innovativeness and creativity in classroom instruction and student learning. Third, for policymakers to create a social
environment that values and respects teachers as professionals, it is vital
to shape teachers’ professional image in the media and public discourse.
While formulating educational policies, they are expected to listen to
teachers’ voices at the bottom of the hierarchy and consider more crucial
viewpoints from teacher associations.
Two limitations exist in this study. On the one hand, although we
considered most possible predictors and built an inclusive framework,
maybe there are still some indicators that cannot be fully covered and
should be explored further in other studies. For instance, a nation’s
degree of decentralization, as its macro socio-political background,
might also be a potential predictor worth examining. On the other hand,
the cross-section data in the 2018 wave of TALIS was collected simultaneously. Therefore, it is challenging to determine the causal relationships between the predictors under different mechanisms and
teaching autonomy. Longitudinal studies are expected to be performed
in future work to address this.
Despite these limitations, this study contributes to the field of
research in three ways. First, this is the first study to integrate multiple
determinants at the teacher, school, and country levels in an international context, enabling the evaluation of teaching autonomy and
responding thoroughly to prior research contradictions. Second, we
propose a 3 × 3 framework comprising mechanisms and hierarchies to
understand teaching autonomy comprehensively. It can also be used as a
foundation for future research. Third, we reveal the underlying mechanisms’ distinctive importance and identify the predictors’ diverse roles
within these mechanisms. In this way, we fill the research gap and
contribute to practical adjustment.
