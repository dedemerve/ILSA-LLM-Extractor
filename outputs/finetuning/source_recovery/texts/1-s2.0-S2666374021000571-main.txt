Contemporary European and North American societies have experienced changing demographics due to migration. School classrooms reﬂect this dynamic. To-date, few studies have examined migration eﬀects on both reading skills and subjective wellbeing from an international comparative perspective. Using the 2018 Programme
for International Student Assessment (PISA) dataset, this paper addresses the issue by examining migrant-native
gaps in reading, happiness, and life satisfaction among 15-year-old students in four education models. Regression
results illustrate that migrant students in general tend to experience a double inequality: they have lower reading performance and report lower life satisfaction when compared to native students (although in some cases
they report higher happiness levels). However, such eﬀects are also context-dependent. While a migration background has signiﬁcant negative eﬀects on students’ reading in Germanic and Nordic schools, its negative eﬀects
in Anglo-Saxon systems are above all manifested in students’ level of life satisfaction. In addition, compared to
ﬁrst-generation migrants, being of second generation is positively associated with reading performance in all education models, with the strongest eﬀect in the Nordic countries. Such positive generation eﬀects are statistically
insigniﬁcant, on the other hand, on students’ wellbeing. Other individual factors such as socio-economic status
and gender present mixed correlations with reading and wellbeing. Such ﬁndings invite scholars and practitioners to reﬂect, through the prism of migration, on how reading proﬁciency and wellbeing can diﬀer in diverse
educational approaches, and correspondingly seek context-speciﬁc intervention measures.

biggest negative eﬀect on students’ reading performance, followed by
life satisfaction, while it has a slightly positive eﬀect on happiness. Such
ﬁndings notwithstanding, this article posits that diﬀerent education systems provide conducive contexts in diﬀerent ways (reading or wellbeing) for migrant students. Based on the comparative analysis, I show
that the Anglo-Saxon approach (Ireland, UK, USA) proves conducive
in achieving parity in reading performance and level of happiness between migrants and natives. Students in the Germanic-inﬂuenced (Austria, Germany, Netherlands, Switzerland) and Nordic (Finland and Sweden) models, in turn, are less likely to experience a life satisfaction gap
due to their migration status. Results also reveal that, across the board
(but especially in the Nordic system), being of second-generation is positively associated with higher reading scores vis-à-vis ﬁrst-generation
migrants. This generation eﬀect on subjective wellbeing is, on the other
hand, insigniﬁcant. Furthermore, eﬀects of individual factors, notably
speaking a diﬀerent language at home (often a proxy of international migration) and grade retention, on reading and wellbeing display strengths
and signiﬁcance to varying degrees in diﬀerent systems.
Findings of this study inform context-speciﬁc policy formulations
in mitigating inequality between migrants and natives in both reading
competence and subjective wellbeing. Education performance and wellbeing during adolescent years play a crucial role in later-stage achievements and quality of life (Eurostat 2019; Heinrich and Galan 2018).
For migrant youths, the stakes are even higher, as education integration in schools impacts later-stage economic activity and assimilation in
the receiving country (Schneeweis 2011). This article’s conclusions contribute to scholarly debates and the formulation of more inclusive policies aimed at alleviating the migrant-native gap in both reading literacy
and wellbeing domains. More inclusive policies that take into account
vulnerable groups (in this case migrants) are also more sustainable policies in the promotion of education achievement, integration, and quality
of life in school in general.

2.1. Review of current scholarship
A burgeoning trend of scholarship at the crossroads of migration and
education examined eﬀects of migration on various aspects of youths’
educational attainment. Mainly set in the context of rich European,
North American and Oceanian countries (among the largest migrantreceiving states), migration status in schools is broadly operationalised
in the literature as students with an immigration background. This encompasses both ﬁrst-generation (foreign-born students with foreignborn parents), and second-generation (students born locally but with
both parents foreign-born) students1 (Frones et al 2020; OECD 2019).
The broad consensus established by extant literature is that migration status negatively correlates with academic performance (Dockery,
Koshy, & Li, 2020; Heckmann, 2008; Jonsson & Rudolphi, 2011;
Rodríguez, 2020; Volante, Klinger, & Bilgili, 2018). For instance,
Frones et al (2020) highlighted a general and consistent gap in reading performance, to the disadvantage of immigrant minority students,
in Denmark, Sweden and Norway throughout the three rounds of PISA
in 2000, 2009, and 2018. In more speciﬁc terms, it has been found that
migrant students’ country of origin has an impact on their performance
too. In Sweden, students of non-European migration origin have the lowest school performance compared to natives and migrants of European
origin (Jonsson and Rudolphi, 2011). This eﬀect need not be negative,
however, when it comes to aspirations: among the same group of nonEuropean migrant students who could enrol in upper secondary schools,

Countries in the early tracking system (mostly Germanic-inﬂuenced)
use tracking at the end of primary school as an early selection process
to sort students into diﬀerent pathways according to their academic
results (Mons 2007). In the common core-uniform integration system
(mostly Southern European), students follow a relatively standardised
core education curriculum until 14-16 years old, with grade repetition
as the single most frequent measure to separate students with weaker
academic abilities from the cohort. In contrast, schools in the common
core-ﬂexible integration system (mostly Anglo-Saxon) attend to diverse
academic abilities by ﬂexibly grouping students either within classes or
within schools, without resort to hard and fast early tracking or grade
repetition practices. Students in this system are also exposed to a comprehensive core curriculum until 15-16 years old. Lastly, schools in the
individualised system (Nordic countries) rarely adopt tracking, ﬂexible
grouping within schools, or grade retention to deal with heterogeneity.
Instead, they rely more on individualised measures, such as individual
or small-group tutoring, to ensure all students progress (Dupriez et al
2008; Mons 2007).
Along these lines of classiﬁcation, studies using older PISA datasets
established that the migration disadvantage in reading and mathematics
is larger in continental European countries (early tracking and common
core-uniform integration systems) than in English-speaking countries
(common core-ﬂexible system), while socio-economic status has the
largest impact on reading in English-speaking countries (plus Germany)
and least in Nordic societies (Entorf and Minoiu 2005; Schnepf 2007).
Due to the lack of focus on subjective wellbeing in these studies, such
results do not allow us to conclude if an education system that is
good in alleviating migrant disadvantage in, say, reading performance

Using mean PVs in analyses might lead to (i) underestimation of standard deviation, (ii) underestimation of within-school variance, and (iii)
overestimation of correlations between student performance and some
background variables (OECD 2009: 128). The ﬁrst two issues, however,
are of little concern to this research. First, standard deviations and standard errors are averaged out due to the high number of observations in
the regression models of this study. Second, school-level factors are not
part of the analysis, hence within-school variance is of little concern. The
only potential issue is the third point. To address this concern, all 10 PVs
were separately tested in the regression model where reading score is
the dependent variable. Results were then compared to the same model
where the mean reading score (derived from the 10 PVs) was used. The
comparison shows no change in both the signiﬁcance and direction of
coeﬃcients, and only slight and insigniﬁcant changes in the magnitudes
of the coeﬃcients in a minority of cases. Crucially, these changes all lie
within the 95% conﬁdence interval. Hence, insofar as objectives of this
study are concerned, using mean PVs does not lead to an overestimation of correlations between student performance and the background
variables used in this study.
Together with reading performance, subjective wellbeing is an important analytical outcome in this article. This study understands subjective wellbeing as manifested by the following two indicators: life satisfaction and happiness. This is in line with current practices in the empirical operationalisation of subjective wellbeing in social sciences research
(Happiness Research Institute 2020; Veenhoven 2012; 2015). In current
literature, happiness relates more to emotional moods (hence aﬀective
dimension of wellbeing), whereas life satisfaction involves reﬂections on
one’s achievements and possessions and comparisons with other reference groups (hence cognitive dimension of wellbeing) (Adler and Seligman 2016; Happiness Research Institute 2020). It is expected that these
two dimensions yield diﬀerent results as regards the migrant-native gap.
Findings hence contribute to further theorising in extant literature on
subjective wellbeing by showing how diﬀerent dimensions of life contentment (whether aﬀective of cognitive) correlate to diﬀerent indicators. This study recognises that both happiness and life satisfaction
ﬂuctuate in natural settings and the accuracy of individual respondents
is hard to conﬁrm. However, given such a representative sample size,
reliability limits are expected to average out (Kahneman and Krueger
2006).
In PISA 2018, students were asked the following question on happiness: “Thinking about yourself and how you normally feel, how often do you feel happy?” Responses are computed on a 1-4 scale from 1
“never”, 2 “rarely”, 3 “sometimes” to 4 “always”. As for life satisfaction,
students were asked: “Overall, how satisﬁed are you with your life as a
whole these days?” The original responses in the dataset ranged from 0
to 10, with increasing values corresponding to higher levels of life satisfaction. In this study, life satisfaction was rescaled to match the 4-point
scale for happiness. Hence, for life satisfaction, answers from 0 to 4 were
grouped as 1 “Not satisﬁed”, 5 and 6 as 2 “Somewhat satisﬁed”, 7 and
8 as 3 “Moderately satisﬁed”, and 9 and 10 as 4 “Very satisﬁed”, as per
OECD’s practice (OECD 2019).

3. Data, variable descriptions, and methods
3.1. Sample: Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2018
The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development
(OECD) administers the large-scale PISA study in three-year intervals.
The goal is to assess key skills in reading, mathematics, and science,
as well as various aspects of educational competencies of 15-year-olds
among participating countries and regions. The international and standardised nature of assessment also facilitates cross-country comparisons
to improve educational policies and outcomes (Gomez-Fernandez and
Mediavilla 2021). To-date, it is one of the largest samples comprising students from diverse national, cultural, linguistic, and educational
backgrounds (Borgonovi and Han, 2021). Data used in this study come
from the latest round of PISA 2018, and are all extracted from publicly available data ﬁles on OECD’s website. The subset of 15 countries
(mentioned in section 2.2) in this study adds to a total of approximately
118,000 students in the 15 countries for the analytic sample.
3.2. Variable description
3.2.1. Outcome variables
To test for correlations between migration on the one hand, and reading and subjective wellbeing on the other hand, multiple linear regressions were utilised. Hence, we do not only examine the relationship
between two variables (e.g., migration and reading) in model 1, but
crucially, how such a relationship holds when eﬀects of other variables
(e.g., socio-economic status, gender) were controlled in model 2. This
study has three outcome variables: reading performance, happiness, and
life satisfaction. Reading performance is used in this study for the following two reasons. First, among the three competency areas tested by PISA,
international migration experience of students and their families has
a more direct and signiﬁcant impact on language-based reading skills
(Frones et al 2020). This is because linguistic knowledge (vocabulary
and grammar) is more closely associated with reading comprehension
(taught in language and humanities-based classes) than in mathematics
and science. It is expected that international migration experience, coupled with insuﬃcient host society language knowledge (also the variable
“language at home”, see below), result in a reading performance gap.
PISA 2018 deﬁned reading literacy as understanding, using, evaluating,
reﬂecting on and engaging with texts in order to achieve one’s goals,
to develop one’s knowledge and potential, and to participate in society
(OECD 2019). Understood in this way, reading proﬁciency measures students’ ability to engage in a wide variety of activities both within and
beyond the conﬁnes of their classroom. Potential reading gaps hence
underscore future challenges in leading a socially active and enriching
life.
Second, reading was the main focus of academic competency in
PISA 2018. Hence, compared to mathematics and science, a wider
range of tasks in reading than mathematics or sciences was assessed
(Borgonovi and Han, 2021). What is more, all participating 15-year-olds
received at least some reading assessment, whereas not all were assessed
in mathematics or science. In the interest of generating as rich a set of
analyses as possible, and simultaneously remaining relevant and comparable across the board, reading performance was therefore the most
suitable choice insofar as the aims of this study are concerned. Individual mean reading scores for each respondent were computed using the
average of 10 plausible values (PVs) for the reading assessment. The
author is aware of the three potential issues for averaging the PVs and
using such mean values in analyses, as raised by the OECD (OECD 2009).

or she was born outside the test country with two foreign-born parents
(ﬁrst-generation immigrant student) or if he or she was born in the test
country but whose both parents were foreign-born (second-generation
immigrant student) (OECD 2019). As per OECD deﬁnition, those who
do not belong to either category was classiﬁed as non-immigrant native
student in this analysis. For the ﬁrst set of regressions (Tables 2-5), a
dichotomous indicator was used to show if a student is a native (coded
as 1) or migrant (coded as 2).

4.1. Migration status and inequality in reading competence and subjective
wellbeing: general patterns

Results in Table 2 answer the ﬁrst research question: To what extent
is there a gap in reading performance and subjective wellbeing between
native and migrant students in major migrant destination countries in
Europe and North America? The general picture emerging from the analysis is that, all things considered, migrant students experience a double
gap in both reading performance and life satisfaction (model 2). Regardless of education systems, having a migration background is associated
with a 12.82-point drop in mean reading score, as well as a 0.077 drop
on the life satisfaction scale. Such negative correlations are statistically
signiﬁcant at the 99.9%. However, there is a caveat. Results in model
2 also indicate that, for both reading and life satisfaction, the eﬀect of
migration status does not have the strongest magnitude once we consider other factors. For reading, a one-unit change in grade repetition,
the ESCS of a family, as well as the language spoken at home all result in
a stronger magnitude of change in reading scores than migration status
does (at the same 99.9% signiﬁcance level). While ESCS has a positive
correlation with reading, grade repetition and a diﬀerent language spoken at home have negative correlations.
For life satisfaction, a one-unit change in happiness, gender, and fear
of failure is associated with larger eﬀects on life satisfaction (at the same
99.9% signiﬁcance level) than migration status does. While happiness
has a positive correlation with life satisfaction, gender (being a male)
and fear of failure have negative correlations.
The situation is diﬀerent for happiness, since migration status is positively associated with happiness (model 2) when controlled for other
factors. This positive relationship is, however, on a lower signiﬁcance
level (95%) than in the case of reading and life satisfaction (both 99.9%).

5. Discussion and conclusion
The data analysis in this study provides evidence to suggest the association between migration on the one hand, and reading performance
and subjective wellbeing on the other. Results revealed that, compared
to natives, migrant students achieve lower reading performance, experience lower life satisfaction, but report higher happiness (albeit at lower
signiﬁcance level and magnitude) across the board. Crucially, however,
comparative analyses highlight the fact that eﬀects of migration on reading and subjective wellbeing manifest diﬀerently for diﬀerent outcome
variables and in diﬀerent education systems. While migration background is not associated with a negative reading gap for migrants in the
common core-ﬂexible integration system, it is associated with a 51.448point drop in mean reading score in the individualised system, and a
19.208-point drop in the early tracking system. However, whereas migration negatively aﬀects students’ life satisfaction in the two common
core systems, its negative association with life satisfaction loses signiﬁcance in the early tracking and individualised systems.
Prior comparative research aﬃrmed that migrant students experience the largest reading disadvantage in continental Europe (the early
tracking and common core-uniform systems in this study) (Entorf and
Minoiu 2005; Schnepf 2007). It has also been shown that migrant students have the most conducive environment for successful academic in-

general migrant-native gap in reading is the largest in the individualised
system, this system might also be more conducive to upward mobility
for second-generation migrants, in terms of acquiring reading competence.

satisfaction, regardless of education system and after controlling for all
other variables. Insofar as migrants’ subjective wellbeing inequality is
concerned, the negative impact of migration on life satisfaction (and
the signiﬁcant correlation) manifests in the two common core systems
only. As for happiness, migration experience has a slight positive impact on the two common core systems, and loses statistical signiﬁcance
in the early tracking and individualised systems. More than migration
status, a series of other factors displayed consistent eﬀects on the two dimensions of subjective wellbeing across all education systems. Whereas
female students reported higher levels of happiness but lower levels of
life satisfaction in all cases, students who had higher fear of failure reported lower levels of both happiness and life satisfaction. While extant research established the proﬁle of students who are likely to report
higher fear of failure (Borgonovi and Han, 2021), results here contribute
to scholarship by highlighting the importance to address the issue from
a wellbeing perspective, for it negatively aﬀects students’ happiness and
life satisfaction across the board.
Results of this study advance current debates by casting the question
of migrant-native inequality in a new light: that eﬀects of migration on
reading performance and subjective wellbeing manifest diﬀerently in
education systems with diﬀerent diversity management practices. While
the common core-ﬂexible integration system proves good in alleviating
the migrant-native gap in reading, migrant students in the early tracking
and individualised systems are less likely to experience a life satisfaction
gap due to their migration status. Lastly, the two common core systems
appear to successfully mitigate negative eﬀects of migration on happiness.
This paper contends that it is important for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers in education to consider the wellbeing and reading performance of secondary school students for several reasons. First,
recent studies have shown that adolescents’ wellbeing has positive associations with their academic performance, as well as other positive educational outcomes such as engagement in social life and smoother transition from one school phase to another (Clarke 2020; Lindorﬀ 2020). Second, implications of students’ reading performance go beyond the conﬁnes of secondary school classrooms. As I pointed out in section 3.2.1,
assessments in PISA 2018 deﬁned reading as understanding, using, evaluating, reﬂecting on and engaging with texts in order to achieve one’s
goals, to develop one’s knowledge and potential, and to participate in
society. In a broader sense, secondary students’ reading proﬁciency is
one useful tool to gauge their present and future ability to engage in
public social life. This is all the more pertinent in the context of in-

ternational migration, in which migrant students might more likely be
placed in a disadvantaged position in gaining reading literacy, due to
(initial) language barriers. Lastly, as secondary school students are at a
critical stage of growing up, what they experience, especially as regards
personal and emotional wellbeing, will have an impact on life achievements in later adult years (Brule and Veenhoven 2014; Heinrich and
Galan 2018; Lindorﬀ 2020)
Moreover, results suggest that diﬀerent combinations of indicators –
migration background plus grade repetition, home language use, and/or
socio-economic status – should be used to identify most vulnerable
groups (in reading and subjective wellbeing) in diﬀerent education systems, so that more nuanced intervention measures can be devised. This
has practical relevance in making potential changes to pedagogical approaches. For instance, policymakers in systems with individualised approach might want to consider introducing more standardised support
programmes (e.g., extra language and reading remedial classes for those
in disadvantaged positions, of which migration can be one indicator)
coupled with quality control/evaluations by senior educators. Practitioners in schools adopting the common core approaches could pay
particular attention to students from migration and lower ESCS backgrounds as regards their state of wellbeing, and if need be, design special
counselling sessions.
This study has several limitations. First, it only considered reading
competence as an indicator of academic performance. As explained earlier, international migration is directly linked to issues of language behaviour, notably the acquisition of new languages in host societies. This
in turn concerns language-related performance such as reading. The fact
that reading was the focus of PISA 2018 also meant that it was more
reliable than mathematics and science scores (see section 3.2.1). Do
correlations between migration background and mathematics and science manifest in the same way and to the same signiﬁcance level in
each of the four clusters? This is a noteworthy issue for future research
to pick up. Second, it is worth reiterating that ﬁndings in this paper
pertain to the sample of 15 countries analysed in this study. They are
therefore not generalisable to the entire PISA 2018 sample (approximately 70 countries/regions). Further enquiry with mixed-method research in context-speciﬁc educational settings across regions would allow more generalisable conclusions across the entire PISA sample of
countries/regions. What is more, the cross-sectional data analysed here
do not permit the establishment of causality. Further examination of
causations of the correlations that emerged from the analysis is needed.
A lead for future studies in examining possible causality would be to
consider societal attitudes towards migration and diversity in the educational contexts of the diﬀerent countries (or groups) discussed here.
This could be achieved with a mixed-method approach incorporating
qualitative research (in-depth interviews and classroom observations)
to explore if and how educators’ and peers’ attitudes towards migration
contribute to performance and wellbeing gaps observed in this study.
Also, in keeping with the current research focus on eﬀects of individual level migration on reading performance and subjective wellbeing,
school-level factors were not included in the present analysis. This could
be examined by future studies with corresponding foci on school-level
factors.
In addition, this study shows that even in similar educational systems, the eﬀect of migration and gender can be positive for one aspect
of subjective wellbeing and negative for the other. This prompts the
thought that although happiness and life satisfaction may overlap, they
are not the same. Future studies could build on this empirical evidence
to further theorise and operationalise subjective wellbeing measures in
the context of comparative education research with migration and gender perspectives. I also acknowledge that care needs to be taken when
interpreting cross-cultural comparisons on happiness and life satisfaction. Partly for this reason, the scope of this study is limited to European and North American contexts in which happiness and life satisfaction are construed in broadly similar ways (Uchida and Ogihara 2012).
To be sure, internal diﬀerences on understandings and expressions of

This article highlights the fact that inequality, to migrants’ disadvantage, in reading performance and subjective wellbeing exists even in
some of the worlds’ richest and most developed countries. This comes at
a critical juncture in which migrant students’ academic continuity and
wellbeing needs tend to be more at stake than the general population, as
a result of intermittent school closures due to the novel coronavirus disease (Covid-19) (Wang, 2020). The Covid-19-induced school closures
not only threaten the learning rhythm of all students (Kuhlfeld and
Tarasawa 2020), but more importantly, exacerbate the existing structural inequality between native and migrant students (Geis-Thöne 2020;
European Commission 2020). Reading literacy has a fundamental role
in promoting individual development both in academic attainment and
beyond the conﬁnes of classrooms (Vedechkina and Borgonovi 2021).
Seen in this light, results of this paper invite scholars and practitioners
in education to reﬂect, through the prism of migration, on how reading
proﬁciency and wellbeing can diﬀer in diverse educational approaches,
as well as the implications on longer-term personal developments in and
out of the classroom.
