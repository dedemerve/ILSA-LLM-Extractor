Does between-school tracking really result in more achievement 
homogeneous schools? Evidence from 40 countries around the globe
Silvia Salchegger *
, Markus Hirczy 1

A B S T R A C T
A key argument for tracking is that teaching is easier and more effective in groups of students with more ho­
mogeneous abilities. These positive effects are based on the implicit assumption that tracking renders learning 
groups more academically homogeneous. The present study investigated whether within-school achievement 
variation (WSAV) really decreases after between-school tracking and how at the same time between-school 
achievement variation (BSAV) develops. Individual student data of the Progress in International Reading Lit­
eracy Study (PIRLS) 2011, the Trends in Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2011, and the Programme for 
International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 were integrated in a pseudo-panel including 631,368 students 
from 40 countries. Differences in WSAV and BSAV between fourth grade students (before tracking in all coun­
tries) and 15-year-olds (after tracking in early- and mid-tracking countries) were established for the domains of 
reading, math and science and then meta-analyzed. Results show that in early- and mid-tracking countries WSAV 
decreased significantly only in the domain of reading but not in math or science. In late-tracking countries WSAV 
did not change significantly in any domain. On the other hand, BSAV increased largely in early-tracking 
countries across all domains, whereas it did not change significantly in mid- and late-tracking countries 
(except for the domain of reading in mid-tracking countries). In sum, a small homogenization of students within 
schools comes of the expense of a markable heterogenization between schools in early-tracking countries. Results 
show that between-school tracking is less efficient in establishing homogeneous learning environments than 
often assumed.
1. Introduction
Between-school tracking (i.e., selecting students into different 
schools by their achievement) has been discussed controversially. One 
major point of discussion concerns the age of selection: Should students 
be tracked into different schools as early as the age of 10 (like in Ger­
many, Austria and Hungary) or should they stay in a comprehensive 
school till the age of 16 (like in the United States, Finland, Poland, 
Australia or Sweden)? The central argument in favor of (early) tracking 
is that teaching and learning is more effective in homogeneous groups 
(e.g., Baumert, Maaz, Neumann, Becker, & Dumont, 2017; Betts, 2011; 
Esser & Seuring, 2020). Opponents claim that early selection increases 
achievement variance and social inequality whereas it does not increase 
achievement level (Strello, Strietholt, Steinmann, & Siepmann, 2021; 
Terrin & Triventi, 2023; Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010).
The central belief behind between-school tracking is that 
academically homogeneous schools have advantages for teaching and 
learning. However, it has yet to be shown whether between-school 
tracking really increases achievement homogeneity. The present study 
establishes the efficacy of tracking as a strategy for increasing achieve­
ment homogeneity by creating a pseudo-panel analyzing data from the 
extensive databases of the large-scale assessments TIMSS 2011 (Trends 
in International Mathematics and Science Study), PIRLS 2011 (Progress 
in International Reading Literacy Study), and PISA 2018 (Programme 
for International Student Assessment). This will be achieved by applying 
a very new data analysis approach: Two-stage individual participant 
data meta-analysis (Brunner et al., 2023; Campos, Cheung, & Scherer, 
2025).

Tracking is defined as “the practice of assigning students to instructional 
groups on the basis of ability” (Hallinan, 1994, p. 79; see also Betts, 
2011, p. 342; Esser & Seuring, 2020, p. 277; LeTendre, Hofer, & Shi­
mizu, 2003, p. 44). The structure of tracking serves different needs in 
different nations and interacts with the dominant cultural, social, eco­
nomic, and political settings (LeTendre et al., 2003; Triventi, Kulic, 
Skopek, & Blossfeld, 2016). Therefore, education systems actively apply 
or passively enable different types of tracking.
Between-school tracking refers to separating students into different 
school types based on formally stated placement criteria (Chmielewski 
et al., 2013; LeTendre et al., 2003; Salchegger, 2016; Trautwein, Lüdtke, 
Marsh, K¨oller, & Baumert, 2006;). These criteria usually include speci­
fications about the onset and duration of tracking (e.g., at what age 
tracking occurs), the depth or intensity of tracking (e.g., the number of 
different school types), and the requirements for attending a specific 
school type. These requirements are primarily based on student 
achievement (e.g., school marks, entrance examinations, or placement 
tests).
Between-school tracking must be distinguished from within-school 
tracking. Chmielewski et al. (2013)
differentiate two forms of 
within-school tracking: Course-by-course tracking, which means sepa­
rating students within schools with respect to certain subjects, and 
within-school streaming, which means assigning students to a certain 
stream for all subjects.
The above-mentioned forms of tracking (between- and within-school 
tracking) are both explicit (i.e., formal and intended). These forms of 
tracking must be distinguished from implicit tracking, which refers to the 
informal division of students into schools by social background. Implicit 
tracking does not have an official basis but is primarily the result of 
economic divides between urban and rural areas, residential segregation 
in cities, and/or family choices (Salchegger, 2016; Trautwein et al., 
2006). While explicit tracking (either between or within schools) intends 
to make schools or classrooms more academically homogeneous, 
academically homogeneous schools may be also a result of implicit 
tracking.
1.2. Controversial views on between-school tracking2
Explicit between-school tracking, the focus of the present study, is 
seen as the most rigid form of sorting students (Chmielewski et al., 
2013). A central indicator of explicit between-school tracking is the age 
of first selection. The age of first selection varies greatly across the OECD 
countries: It is lowest in Austria, Germany, and Hungary where students 
are usually first selected at the age of ten, and highest in countries with 
comprehensive school systems, like Australia, Finland, Poland, or the 
United States, where students are first selected at the age of 16 (see 
Table 1). The age of first selection is hotly debated in school systems that 
separate students into different school types at an earlier stage, like 
Austria and Germany (e.g., Engelbrecht, 2014; Nikolai & Helbig, 2019) 
and it is a prominent issue in tracking research (e.g., Hanushek & 
W¨oßmann, 2006; Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Devel­
opment [OECD], 2020a; Salchegger, 2016).
The main arguments against early tracking are that it contributes to 
social and academic inequality (Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010), ex­
poses low-track students to a low-quality learning environment (Oakes, 
2005), stigmatizes weak students (Bardach et al., 2023) and ignores the 
fact that students’ potential not always follows a fixed trajectory but 
may develop individually and at different ages (Hallinan, 1994). 
Moreover, as students of the same school type vary markedly in 
achievement and large achievement overlaps exist between students of 
different school types (e.g., Baumert, Maaz, Stanat, & Watermann, 2009; 
Reinhold, Reiss, Diedrich, Hofer, & Heinze, 2018; Schiepe-Tiska, 
R¨onnebeck, & Neumann, 2018; Schreiner & Toferer, 2009; Schreiner 
& Schmich, 2009; Weis et al., 2018), tracking obviously does not sepa­
rate students by achievement in an effective manner and therefore ho­
mogeneous learning groups are considered as a fiction even in selective 
school systems (Tillmann, 2004, p. 8). This indicates that achievement 
may not be the only sorting criterion. Actually, social status was shown 
to be another important one (Brinkmann, Huth-St¨ockle, Schunck, & 
Teltemann, 2024). For example, in Austria and Germany students from 
families with a high social status more often attend higher tracks than 
equally able peers from families of low social status (Bruneforth, Weber, 
& Bacher, 2012; Wohlkinger & Ditton, 2023).
On the other hand, the central argument for tracking is that homo­
geneous classes permit a focused curriculum and appropriately paced 
instruction that leads to better learning by all students (e.g., Baumert, 
et al., 2017; Chiu, Chow, & Joh, 2017; Esser & Seuring, 2020; Hallinan, 
1994; Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006). Following this argument, homo­
geneous learning groups are the central basis of all possible benefits 
from explicit tracking, i.e., tracking may only be beneficial when aca­
demic sorting is efficient and actually results in homogeneous class­
rooms or schools (cf. Triventi et al., 2016, p. 5). But do schools really 
become more achievement homogeneous by explicitly allocating stu­
dents to different schools on the basis of their abilities? And what more 
do we know about the consequences of explicit between-school 
tracking?
1.3. Consequences of between-school tracking for student achievement
Research on between-school tracking is usually comparing school 
systems with different between-school tracking policies. Such data can 
either be drawn from large-scale assessments that include a broad range 
of countries with different tracking regimes (e.g., Contini & Cugnata, 
2020; Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006) or from studies that compare 
educational outcomes before and after the reform of a country’s tracking 
regime (e.g., Jakubowski, Patrinos, Porta, & Wisniewski, 2017 on 
Poland; Kerr, Pekkarinen, & Uusitalo, 2013 on Finland; Malamud & 
Pop-Eleches, 2011 on Romania; Meghir & Palme, 2005 on Sweden; 
Piopiunik, 2014 on Bavaria; Van de Werfhorst, 2018 on reforms in 
Finland, France, England, and Scotland compared to five countries 
without reforms). The latter studies each focus single or very few 
countries and the findings may not be easily generalized to different 
contexts (c.f. Contini & Cugnata, 2020). Also research so far is mostly 
based on country-comparing studies which investigated the effects of 
between-school tracking on the equality of opportunity, the mean level 
of achievement and the level of achievement dispersion (see Terrin & 
Triventi, 2023; Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010, for reviews).
Concerning equality of opportunity, the OECD (e.g., OECD, 2004, 
2013, 2020a) showed that social background has a greater impact on 
achievement at the end of compulsory schooling in systems that apply 
early between-school tracking. Similarly, studies of various large data 
sets showed that the effect of family background on educational 
attainment increases with between-school tracking duration (e.g., Bru­
nello & Checchi, 2007; Strello, Strietholt, & Steinmann, 2022). There­
fore Brunello and Checchi conclude that the length of school-type 
differentiation increases inequality of opportunity. In a review of 
various studies, Van de Werfhorst and Mijs (2010), come to the 
conclusion that “the dependence of achievement on social class and 
race/ethnicity are higher in educational systems that track students in 
different school types and school locations relative to systems that offer 
comprehensive schools” (p. 421).
Concerning achievement dispersion and mean achievement, 
Hanushek and W¨oßmann (2006) compared learning outcomes in pri­
mary education to those in secondary education. This study showed that 
total variation in student achievement increases more from primary

The study by Hanushek and W¨oßmann (2006) established the dif­
ference-in-differences (DID) approach for large-scale assessments in ed­
ucation. The DID approach aims to create a pseudo-panel by performing 
two steps: The first step compares the difference in achievement varia­
tion between Grade 4 (PIRLS or TIMSS Grade 4) and secondary school 
students (PISA and/or TIMSS Grade 8) for each country with data 
available for both younger and older population. The second step 
compares these differences (found under Step 1) between tracked and 
untracked countries. This enables controlling for country-specific char­
acteristics that may affect within-country achievement variation beyond 
explicit between-school tracking (like, for example, implicit tracking). 
Therefore, by comparing countries with different tracking regimes, the 
impact of explicit tracking on achievement variation can be estimated. 
The work of Hanushek and W¨oßmann (2006) is today regarded as 
“seminal” (Contini & Cugnata, 2020; Strello et al., 2021, p. 139) and the 
two-step DID-approach is highly recommended because it can appro­
priately compare test scores of different surveys that are not measured 
on the same scale (Contini & Cugnata, 2020).
Despite these methodological advances, the study by Hanushek and 
W¨oßmann (2006) as well as newer studies using the two-step DID-ap­
proach (e.g., Contini & Cugnata, 2020; Strello et al., 2021) are limited by 
two points: First, they did not consider population coverage. For 
example, the large decrease in achievement variation for Turkey was 
interpreted as a decrease in inequality in the study by Hanushek and 
W¨oßmann (2006) and it did not consider that population coverage was 
low for Turkey in PISA (e.g., 36 % in PISA 2003). Therefore, the decrease 
in achievement variation between 4th graders and 15-year-olds could 
also be explained by the fact that at the age of 15 the lowest achievers in 
Turkey already dropped out of school and hence are not tested in PISA 
because the target population of PISA are 15-year-old students at Grade 7 
or higher (and not 15-year-olds in general; Spaull, 2017). Second, 
studies using a two-step DID approach did not differentiate between the 
two components of achievement variation: between-school achievement 
variation (BSAV) and within-school achievement variation (WSAV). This

However, without separately analyzing BSAV and WSAV, we cannot 
know whether between-school tracking actually serves to homogenize 
schools (i.e., reduce WSAV) or whether the higher achievement varia­
tion in tracked countries (found by Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006 and

Research on the effect of between-school tracking on student aca­
demic homogeneity within schools is still largely missing. Probably 
because such research requires the combination of methods from 
different research areas: DID models, commonly used in sociology, 
public health, and economic research (e.g., Wing, Simon, & 
Bello-Gomez, 2018) and multilevel analyses, common in psychology and 
education research (e.g., Hox, 2010). Attempts to combine these 
methods were made by Brinkmann et al. (2024) and Sucha´n and Berg­
müller (2010).3 Brinkmann et al. (2024) compared the Intraclass Cor­
relation (ICC) of achievement and social status between PIRLS/TIMSS 
Grade 4 and TIMSS Grade 8/PISA across 63 countries. The ICC indicates 
the proportion of variance between schools in relation to total variance 
(total variance is the sum of variance within and between schools). 
Results concerning achievement showed that ICC increases more in 
early than in late tracking countries. The authors interpret this result in 
the way that early between-school tracking contributes to the homoge­
neity of learning environments. However, Brinkmann et al. standardized 
measures on the basis of the total variance of each individual country 
and not on the basis of the mean total variance of the country sample 
under comparison (like the present study, see Section 2.3.2). This means 
that for each country and each assessment, the total achievement vari­
ance was set to 100 (100 %), regardless of how large a country’s total 
variance actually was. When comparing two countries with the same 
amount of BSAV, but one with a high total variance (country A) and one 
with a low total variance (country B), this method results in a smaller 
ICC for country A than for country B. In this sense, a within-country 
standardization flaws DID analyses and conveys only limited informa­
tion on the evolution of achievement inequalities over time (Contini & 
Cugnata, 2020). Due to this limitation, Brinkmann et al. (2024) were not 
able to provide information on the development of WSAV and BSAV in 
tracked vs. untracked countries.
The study by Sucha´n and Bergmüller (2010) included such infor­
mation and compared WSAV and BSAV in 19 countries that participated 
in both TIMSS 2007 (Grade 4) and PISA 2006. Results showed that the 
earlier students had been tracked between different school types, the 
greater the increase in BSAV after selection. This result was expected as 
it had had already been demonstrated that tracking is related to BSAV. 
For example, in Poland, after a reform towards a more integrated edu­
cation system, a dramatic reduction in BSAV was observed (OECD, 
2004, p. 164). Beyond this expected result of growing BSAV after 
tracking, the study by Sucha´n and Bergmüller (2010) showed surpris­
ingly, WSAV was not significantly related to the first age of selection: 
There was nearly no change in WSAV between age 10 and age 15 for 
countries that first tracked students into different schools at the age of 10 
and those in which students remained untracked until the age of 15. This 
indicates that one major aim of between-school tracking is not being 
reached: Between-school tracking does not significantly increase 
within-school achievement homogeneity. On the other hand, it increases 
BSAV. Therefore, the increase in total achievement variation in tracked 
countries (demonstrated by Hanushek and W¨oßmann, 2006; Strello 
et al., 2021) may be an effect of lacking homogenization within schools, 
but at the same time increasing achievement differences between 
schools.
It must be noted that also the study by Sucha´n and Bergmüller (2010)
has shortcomings. First, it did not compare students of the same birth 
cohort: Students participating in TIMSS 2007 were mostly born in 1997 
and students participating in PISA 2006 in 1990. Moreover only 19 
countries were included, and the central domain of reading was not 
included. A methodological shortcoming was that no meta-analytical 
methods were used to summarize country-level results and also coun­
tries with low population coverage in PISA were included. Moreover, 
Sucha´n and Bergmüller (2010), like Hanushek and W¨oßmann (2006), 
only differentiated between tracked and untracked countries and 
ignored the duration of tracking. The duration of tracking, however, 
could play a role for the size of WSAV and BSAV. For example, WSAV 
might become even smaller the longer tracking takes as relatively weak 
students in higher tracks progress more than the other students (e.g., Bos 
& Scharenberg, 2010; Zurbriggen, 2015). Moreover, stringent tracking 
often goes along with a cascade system in which failing students fall 
down to lower tracks which means that sorting and homogenization 
continues after tracking (e.g., Dvoˇr´ak, Meyer, Kuˇcerov´a, Vyhn´alek, & 
ˇSmíd, 2020; Van Praag, Boone, Van Houtte, & Stevens, 2012). With 
regard to BSAV, achievement progress was shown to be higher for stu­
dents in high tracks than for students in low tracks (e.g., Angelone, 2019; 
Baumert, Stanat, & Watermann, 2006; Becker, Lüdtke, Trautwein, & 
Baumert, 2006; Becker, Lüdtke, Trautwein, K¨oller, & Baumert, 2012; 
Dockx, De Fraine, & Vandecandelaere, 2019; Guill, Lüdtke, & K¨oller, 
2017; K¨ahler, Hahn, & K¨oller, 2024; K¨oller & Baumert, 2001; Traini, 
Kleinert, & Bittmann, 2021; Van Damme, De Farine, Van Landeghem, 
Opdenakker, & Onghena, 2002). This would be associated to an 
increasing BSAV the longer tracking takes. However, research results are 
not clearly consistent. There are also studies that did not find such 
scissor effects (see Traini et al., 2021, for an overview).
1.4. The present study
The present study is one of the first to investigate whether between- 
school tracking actually renders schools more academically homoge­
neous and how the increase in within-school homogeneity relates to an 
increase in heterogeneity between schools. The present analyses are 
based on a large sample of 40 countries that participated in PIRLS 2011 
and/or TIMSS 2011 (Grade 4) and PISA 2018 and had a high population 
coverage of at least 80 % in PISA. The countries are very diverse with 
regard to their tracking policies. In these countries in sum 631,368 in­
dividual students from 22,901 schools were tested. These students were 
born in or around the years 2001/2002. Because students were all born 
in the early 2000s, a comparison between PIRLS/TIMSS 2011 and PISA 
2018 is well suited for creating a pseudo-panel and applying a DID 
design. Unlike studies so far, the present study takes into account the 
duration of tracking in order to investigate if early tracking (tracking 
students before the age of 13) has different effects on WSAV and BSAV 
than late tracking (tracking students at age 14 or 15). Moreover, the 
present study extends the DID approach by a two-stage individual 
participant data meta-analysis which is currently state of the art for 
country-comparisons based on large-scale assessment data (Brunner 
et al., 2023; Campos et al., 2025).
The homogenization of the student body to facilitate teaching and 
learning is the central aim of between-school tracking (e.g., Baumert 
et al., 2017; Chiu et al., 2017; Esser & Seuring, 2020; Hallinan, 1994; 
Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006). Therefore, we expect that between-school 
tracking will result in more homogeneous schools (i.e., a reduction in WSAV; 
Hypothesis 1). We expect a reduction in WSAV despite the results of 
Sucha´n and Bergmüller (2010) who indicated that WSAV might keep 
almost the same after tracking as this study had several shortcomings. 
We assume that with a larger sample, using a pseudo-panel, and 
including more sophisticated statistical methods, as in the present study, 
we will see a reduction of WSAV. Moreover, we suggest that WSAV de­
creases more in early-tracking countries (in which students had been tracked 
four to six years at the time of the PISA test) than in late-tracking countries 
(in which students are still untracked at the time of the PISA test; Hypothesis 
2). This will test that it is indeed tracking that makes schools more

students in higher tracks relative to other students (Zurbriggen, 2015).
Further we investigate the relationship between tracking and BSAV. 
So far it has been shown that the size of BSAV is related to between- 
school tracking with a higher BSAV in tracked countries (e.g., Salcheg­

Grade 4 to upper secondary school (students aged 15) to rule out the 
alternative explanation that a high BSAV was not due to tracking but 
would have been high in tracked countries even before explicit 
between-school tracking (e.g., due to implicit tracking). Following the 
results of Sucha´n and Bergmüller (2010), we expect BSAV to increase after 
tracking (Hypothesis 4). Further, we expect that this increase is higher in 
early-tracking countries than in late-tracking countries (and therefore related 
to tracking and not just a general development; Hypothesis 5). In addition, 
we expect that BSAV increase is higher the earlier tracking occurs and thus 
higher in early-tracking countries than in mid-tracking countries (Hypothesis 
6) as various studies have shown that achievement of students in 
different school tracks diverge over time because students’ achievement 
progress is higher in high tracks and lower in low tracks (e.g., Angelone, 
2019; Baumert et al., 2006; Becker et al. 2006; Becker et al., 2012; Dockx 
et al., 2019; Guill et al., 2017; K¨ahler et al., 2024; K¨oller & Baumert, 
2001; Traini et al., 2021; Van Damme et al., 2002).
In sum, we propose that tracking is a moderator of both WSAV and 
BSAV change between 4th graders and students aged 15 (Hypothesis 7). 
Concerning the relationship between WSAV and BSAV, we hypothesize 
that in tracked countries the decrease in WSAV between age 10 and age 15 is 
smaller than the increase in BSAV (i.e., decrease in WSAV does not outweigh 
increase in BSAV; Hypothesis 8). Such a result would be in accordance 
with both, the study by Hanushek and W¨oßmann (2006) which showed 
that total variation increases after tracking, and the study by Sucha´n and 
Bergmüller (2010) which revealed that WSAV keeps almost the same 
after tracking whereas BSAV increases.
2. Methods
Analyses are based on publicly available data from the large-scale 
assessments PISA 2018, PIRLS 2011 and TIMSS 2011. The data is 
explored in a new way by combining the DID approach with multilevel 
modeling and finally integrating the results from individual student data 
into a meta-analysis thereby performing a two-stage individual partici­
pant data (IPD) meta-analysis (Brunner et al., 2023; Campos et al., 
2025).
Campos et al. (2025) describe IPD meta-analysis as methodological 
framework for analyzing and synthesizing IPD from two or more studies. 
They see the relative advantage of IPD meta-analysis over other ap­
proaches from the level of data pooling. Access to individual participant 
data, rather than summary data, provides the opportunity to standardize 
analyses across studies, directly derive the effect sizes of interest, and 
increase the power to assess interactions between outcomes and mod­
erators. The two step approach also has several advantages over a 
one-step approach, like tree-level modeling, because a broader spectrum 
of effect sizes can be integrated, studies using different methods can be 
integrated, and very large data sets with complex hierarchical structure 
can be analyzed (as random-access memory in MLM is often too low for a 
one-step approach; Brunner et al., 2023; Campos, et al., 2025). In the 
present study a one-step approach even is methodologically not possible 
because the central effect size “differences in variation coefficients be­
tween Grade 4 (PIRLS/TIMSS) and PISA” (see Section 2.3.2 for 
computation details) cannot be modeled AND integrated into a model in 
one step.
Like Campos et al. (2025) and Brunner et al. (2023), who also used 
data from educational large-scale assessments to perform IPD 
meta-analyses, we first analyze data from each country independently 
and then combine resultant effect size estimates into a meta-analysis. 
Specific to the present study these steps include: 
1. Compute effect sizes and their sampling variances for each country 
using individual participant data. In the present study effect sizes are 
the differences in WSAV and BSAV between the assessments of fourth 
graders (PIRLS/TIMSS) and 15-year-olds (PISA). Effect sizes are 
computed separately for the domains of reading (PISA–PIRLS), math 
(PISA–TIMSS), and science (PISA–TIMSS).
2. Meta-analyzing the effect sizes produced under Step 1. First, het­
erogeneity of effect sizes across countries are determined and second, 
we analyze how effect sizes are moderated by countrys’ tracking 
regimes.
The implementation of these steps is described in more detail under 
Section 2.3.
2.1. Sample
We used individual student data from the three educational large- 
scale assessments: PIRLS 2011, TIMSS 2011, and PISA 2018. These as­
sessments are designed and conducted to compare education systems 
worldwide and to provide information about reading, mathematics, 
and/or science achievement and learning contexts over time.
PIRLS assesses students’ reading achievement in Grade 4 every fifth 
year (Mullis, Martin, Kennedy, Trong, & Sainsbury, 2009). In TIMSS, 
students’ mathematics and science achievement are assessed every 
fourth year in Grades 4 and 8 (Mullis, Martin, Ruddock, O’Sullivan, & 
Preuschoff, 2009). The present study only includes Grade 4 as students 
at this grade are not yet tracked in any country. In 2011 TIMSS and 
PIRLS assessments fell into the same year and in many countries the 
same students were tested in both PIRLS and TIMSS (Foy, 2013). Stu­
dents participating in these studies were in the 4th grade of formal 
schooling and most of them were born in the year 2001 (see Supple­
mental Materials, Table S1).
PISA assesses students’ skills and knowledge in the core domains 
mathematics, reading, and science at the age of 15 every third year 
(OECD, 2019a). Students participating in PISA 2018 were nearly all born 
in the year 2002 (see Supplemental Materials, Table S1).
In sum, TIMSS and PIRLS follow a grade-based sampling definition 
and include students of 4th grade (Joncas & Foy, 2012, p. 4); PISA fol­
lows an age-based sampling definition and includes students who are 15 
years old at the time of the test. PISA uses a two-stage sampling pro­
cedure, with schools as the primary sampling units and individual stu­
dents as the secondary sampling units (OECD, 2020b), PIRLS and TIMSS 
use a two-stage sampling procedure, with schools as the primary sam­
pling units and entire school classes as the secondary sampling units 
(Martin & Mullis, 2012). This means that in TIMSS and PIRLS intact 
classes are tested while in PISA students may come from different classes 
within a school.4 All studies yield country-representative results. TIMSS 
and PIRLS for math, science and reading achievement of 4th grade 
students, PISA for math, science and reading achievement 15-year-olds.
In the present study, only countries were included that participated 
either in PIRLS 2011 and PISA 2018 (N = 35) or in TIMSS 2011 and PISA

2.2. Measures
2.2.1. Reading, math, and science achievement
Reading, math, and science achievement scores were the basis to 
compute WSAV and BSAV for each domain. PIRLS assesses reading 
achievement of 4th graders, TIMSS assesses math and science achieve­
ment of 4th graders5 and PISA assesses reading, math and science 
achievement of 15-year-old students. Achievement was measured with 
standardized tests. In PIRLS and TIMSS 2011 the tests were paper-based 
and in PISA 2018 computer-delivered. There is evidence of gradual 
mode-of-assessment effects that may partly explain declines in 
achievement level between PISA 2012 (paper-based assessment) and 
PISA 2015 (first fully computer-based assessment; e.g., Jerrim, Mickle­
wright, Heine, Salzer, & McKeown, 2018; Robitzsch, Lüdtke, Gold­
hammer, Kroehne, & K¨oller, 2020). However, there is no indication that 
achievement variance, which is central for the present analyses, was 
affected by the mode of delivery: In PISA 2015 the mean SD across OECD 
countries was almost the same as in PISA 2012, and the changes between 
PISA 2015 and PISA 2018, two adjacent computer-based assessments, 
were even slightly larger (OECD, 2019, Annex B1, Tables 28–30).
The assessment framework of each study describes in detail what is 
meant by reading, math, and science achievement (Mullis, Martin, 
Kennedy, et al., 2009; Mullis, Martin, Ruddock, et al., 2009; OECD, 
2019a). The TIMSS framework aims to assess the attained curriculum (i. 
e., what students have learned from the curriculum) while PISA focuses 
on the concept of literacy, aiming to assess the abilities of students to use 
their knowledge and skills to solve problems encountered in life. As 
curricula aim to ensure that children develop the knowledge, skills and 
attributes they will need for life (e.g., Education Scotland, 2024), a 
curriculum-based test (like TIMSS) and a literacy-based test (like PISA) 
have a large overlap. Consequently, quite a number of items related to 
real life problems can be found in the TIMSS assessment (Foy, Arora, & 
Stanco, 2013). Also between PIRLS and PISA, a high similarity of the 
reading construct was established: “Both surveys attempt to capture the 
practical and functional aspects of the skills acquired in school, 
including the ability to understand text and use it to solve problems. This 
implies that both surveys assessed comparable subjects” (Zuckerman, 
Kovaleva, & Kuznetsova, 2013, p. 65). In sum, the underlying constructs 
of reading, math, and science are highly similar but not identical across 
TIMSS/PIRLS and PISA. Identical constructs are also not necessary for 
the DID approach as we are not comparing absolute achievement mea­
sures between 4th and 10th grade, but rather the relative change of 
achievement variation across different countries (cf. Brinkmann et al., 
2024).
In all TIMSS, PIRLS, and PISA assessments, the achievement scores 
for mathematics and reading were originally scaled to have an inter­
national mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100. The reliabilities 
of achievement scales were satisfactory in all included assessments in all 
participating countries (Martin & Mullis, 2012; OECD, 2020b).
2.2.2. Student age at first selection
In the present analyses countries are grouped into early, mid, and 
late tracking systems according to the first age of selection in an edu­
cation system (OECD, 2020a, Table B.3.3.3). The cut point between 
early and middle tracking is set at age 13 because no country selects 
students at age 13 but either before or after. Like Hanushek and 
W¨oßmann (2006), the upper cut-off point (between middle and late 
tracking) is set at age 16 because PISA tests 15-year-old students and 
thus provides data of tracked and non-tracked students for this age 
group. Countries that track students before age 13 are classified ear­
ly-tracking countries (Austria, Belgium, Czech Republic, Germany, 
Hungary, Indonesia, Netherlands, Singapore, Slovak Republic), coun­
tries that track students at age 14 or 15 are classified mid-tracking 
countries (Chinese Taipei, Croatia, Denmark, France, Georgia, Hong 
Kong, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Kazakhstan, Republic of Korea, 
Lithuania, Portugal, Qatar, Russia, Serbia, Slovenia, United Arab Emir­
ates) and countries that apply tracking only at age 16 are classified 
late-tracking countries (Australia, Canada, Chile, Finland, Malta, New 
Zealand, Norway, Poland, Spain, Sweden, United Kingdom, United 
States). There are no countries that track students after the age of 16 
(OECD, 2020a).

(Brunner et al., 2023; Campos et al., 2025). In the first stage individual 
participant data are analyzed and in the second stage the separate results 
of Stage 1 are summarized by using meta-analytical techniques. Before 
describing these analyses in more detail, data preparation is explained.

Analyses are based on publicly available data from the large-scale

2.3.2. Stage 1: analysis of IPD and estimation of effect sizes
In Stage 1, multilevel analyses were performed to identify WSAV and 
BSAV in the domains of reading, math, and science for each country and 
assessment. The results are further used to determine differences in 
WSAV and BSAV between fourth graders and 15-year-olds for each 
country and domain. These differences are the central effect sizes in the 
present study.
Multilevel analysis decomposing variance between and within 
schools were performed using the statistical software Mplus Version 
7.31 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 1998–2017). This software can appropriately 
weight the data from large-scale assessments in multilevel situations (i. 
e., implement school weight at Level 2). Multilevel analyses follow the 
procedure proposed by Mang, Küchenhoff, Meinck, and Prenzel (2021)
for educational large-scale assessments. Specific to this procedure is that 
school weight is used as Level 2 weight and no weight is used at Level 1 
(see Supplemental Materials S3 for Mplus code).
In order to bring variance components on a meaningful metric, we 
set the mean total variance of all countries for each study to 100 % and 
express the within and between school variation7 of student perfor­
mance as a fraction of this total mean variance (like OECD, 2019c, 
Table II.B1.4.1).8 As variance components of PIRLS, TIMSS and PISA are 
standardized separately, an increase in BSAV or WSAV of a country does 
not refer to an absolute increase in BSAV or WSAV but to an increase in 
its relative position to the 35 countries included in PIRLS–PISA com­
parisons or to the 37 countries included in TIMSS–PISA comparisons.
After establishing standardized coefficients of variation between 
schools and within schools for each country and study, differences in 
variation coefficients between PIRLS and PISA (reading) and TIMSS and 
PISA (math and science) were computed. These differences correspond 
to the first-order differences in a DID analysis and compare the variation 
of performance between younger students (at grades before tracking is 
introduced in any country) and older students (after some countries 
have started tracking) using early outcomes in each country as the 
control (Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006; Lavrijsen & Nicaise, 2016). Ta­
bles 2 and 3 shows the resulting effect sizes (i.e., differences) for each 
country and each domain. Standard errors for differences and for 
country-averages were computed according to OECD (2009).
In order to become a quick impression of the relationship between 
selection age and BSAV/WSAV change, we performed country-level 
correlation analyses (excluding the outliers United Arab Emirates and 
Qatar). Results established correlation coefficients varying between 
r = 0.20 (math), r = 0.34 (science) and r = 0.36 (reading), for the 
relationship between selection age and WSAV change. The correlation 
coefficients between selection age and BSAV change ranged between 
r = –0,59 (reading), r = –0,53 (science), and r = –0,47 (math). There­
fore, correlation coefficients are small to medium indicating that a 
higher selection age is associated with a higher WSAV and they are large 
in indicating a higher selection age is associated with a smaller BSAV. 
We do not give p-values here as these results are not corrected for dif­
ferences in standard errors across countries. However the following 
meta-analytical approaches will take differences in standard errors into 
account.
2.3.3. Stage 2: meta-analysis of the effect sizes
To build a meta-analytic data set, we extracted each country’s dif­
ference in BSAV and WSAV and its standard errors for all three domains. 
To establish the context of these estimates, we added the first age of 
selection for each country.
Stage 2 synthesizes these differences and performs meta-analyses to 
test whether there is heterogeneity in effect sizes across countries and 
whether between-school tracking moderates changes in WSAV and 
BSAV. We first computed meta-analytic baseline models (i.e., random- 
effects models) for each domain separately as well as a general model 
for all domains together to examine the extent to which heterogeneity 
exists across countries. A high heterogeneity indicates that it is worth 
investigating context variables as moderators of this variation. Viecht­
bauer (2010, p. 14) included the I² statistic as measure of heterogeneity 
as it “estimates (in percent) how much of the total variability in the 
effect size estimates (which is composed of heterogeneity and sampling 
variability) can be attributed to heterogeneity among the true effects.”
We implemented all meta-analytic models using the rma.mv function 
in the R package metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010). Supplemental Material S4
shows the respective model specification codes. In all models, we first 
specified the vector of observed effect sizes and the corresponding 
standard errors via the yi and sei arguments. In further analyses we fitted 
mixed-effects models and included two dummy-coded moderator vari­
ables, mid and late. In the mid variable early-tracking countries were 
coded as 0 and mid-tracking countries were coded as 1. In the late var­
iable early-tracking countries were coded as 0 and late-tracking coun­
tries were coded as 1. These moderator variables were included in the 
model via the mods argument (Viechtbauer, 2010).

and BSAV
In further random-effects models, we included time of tracking as an 
additional grouping variable to establish average effect sizes by tracking 
regime (Table 4). Moreover, we tested if the significant variance be­
tween countries (demonstrated above) can be explained by different 
tracking regimes. Therefore, we performed mixed-effects models and 
included early vs. mid selection and early vs. late selection as dummy 
variables (Table 5).
Weighted average effect sizes (Table 4) show that in the domain of 
reading WSAV significantly (p < .05) decreased by 15.33 %age points

domains of math and reading (although there is a clear tendency in the 
expected direction), and it is not confirmed for mid-tracking countries.
4. Discussion
The present study used a two-stage individual participant data meta- 
analytical approach and included 40 countries and more than half a 
million students. It showed that homogenization effects (i.e., decrease in 
WSAV) are usually small after between-school tracking and only sig­
nificant in the domain of reading. This indicates that between-school 
tracking is not very effective in making schools more achievement ho­
mogeneous. This applies to both early- and mid-tracking. Therefore, 
results only partially support our central hypothesis that tracking makes 
schools more homogeneous.
Results concerning BSAV however showed that achievement differ­
ences between schools increase markedly across all domains in early- 
tracking countries with an increase in BSAV that is almost three times 
as large as the reduction in WSAV. In mid-tracking countries achieve­
ment differences between schools increase only slightly. Moreover, in 
late-tracking countries (tracking after age 15) both WSAV and ВSAV 
remain very constant between students aged 10 and 15. These results are 
broadly in line with expectations, although the sharp increase in BSAV 
in early-tracking countries is surprising. Central questions arising from 
the present results are discussed below.
4.1. Why is tracking not more effective in increasing student homogeneity 
within schools?
The homogenization of the student body to facilitate teaching and 
learning is the central aim of between-school tracking (e.g., Baumert 
et al., 2017; Chiu et al., 2017; Esser & Seuring, 2020; Hallinan, 1994; 
Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006). Reaching this aim is based on two 
propositions: First, students are tracked by their ability (and nothing 
else) and second, students keep their ability position permanently. 
Concerning the first proposition, tracking policies that are put into 
practice often include only raw approximations of student achievement 
and additionally rely non-ability-based criteria. In countries like Austria, 
Germany or Switzerland prior grades play a key role in track placement. 
Prior grades however are not standardized and often do not reflect a 
criterial reference-norm orientation but are mostly given based on a 
social reference-norm orientation that reflect the student’s achievement 
in relation to the achievement of their class rather than to the criteria set 
out in the curriculum (e. g., Baeriswyl et al., 2011; Bergmüller & Steger, 
2009). Moreover, school grades contain non-cognitive factors reflecting 
what teachers value in student work, like engagement, persistence, or 
commitment to school (Brookhart et al., 2016). Moreover, criteria for 
between-school tracking are often not only based on ability but also on 
educational decisions of parents (Brunello & Checchi, 2007; Esser & 
Seuring, 2020). This makes social status an important sorting criterion: 
Students from families with a high social status more often attend higher 
tracks than equally able peers from families of low social status 
(Bruneforth et al., 2012; Wohlkinger & Ditton, 2023). The important 
consequences of tracking decisions based on social background become 
evident by the fact that “the dependence of achievement on social class 
and race/ethnicity are higher in educational systems that track students 
in different school types and school locations relative to systems that 
offer comprehensive schools” (Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010, p. 421). 
In sum the desired achievement homogenization effect after tracking 
may be low because sorting is not only based on achievement-related 
criteria but moreover on non-cognitive criteria, like student discipline 
(Brookhart et al., 2016), and social status-related criteria, like parental 
decisions (cf. Brunello & Checchi, 2007; Gr¨ohlich, Scharenberg, & Bos, 
2009). The present results show that the (small) homogenization effect 
in tracked countries varied between domains and was statistically sig­
nificant only in reading but not in math and science. This indicates that 
tracking decisions are more based on reading abilities than on math or 
science abilities, which has already been suggested by Gr¨ohlich et al. 
(2009).
Even if the sorting of students were based solely on their abilities, 
there would still be the possibility that the ability development would 
progress at different rates after tracking. For example, students showing 
later development will be sorted in a low track in early tracking coun­
tries. In this case, students may not be able to develop their full potential 
after tracking as they had already been tracked into a school with lower 
demands and challenges at an early age (e.g., Baumert et al., 2010). A 
meta-analysis showed that there is less long-term stability of cognitive 
abilities measured at the age of 10 than at the age of 14 (Breit, Scherrer, 
Tucker-Drob, & Preckel, 2024). Accordingly, academic achievement 
was shown to undergo substantial reordering over the course of the 
school career (Scherrer, Breit, & Preckel, 2025) which indicates that 
“rigid placement decisions determining one’s placement over long pe­
riods of time do not appear to be an adequate approach to schooling 
because with increasing time intervals, it becomes very likely that a 
substantial portion of students in the various placement settings are no 
longer ideally served there” (Scherrer et al., 2025, p. 26). Consequently, 
the problem of student misallocation was shown to be more severe when 
tracking occurs early (Borghans, Diris, Smits, & de Vries, 2020). The 
present data propose evidence that sorting is not perfect from the 
beginning (as WSAV decrease is only small) and that WSAV does not 
decrease by prolonged tracking (as WSAV decrease is about the same in 
early- and mid-tracking countries). These results speak against the 
expectation that WSAV would increase by the time of tracking. There­
fore, mechanisms such as an ongoing downward sorting of relatively low 
achievers from high tracks to low tracks (e.g., Dvoˇr´ak et al., 2020; Van 
Praag et al., 2012) or a higher achievement gain of weak students (than 
other students) in high tracks (Zurbriggen, 2015) obviously do not 
contribute remarkably to increasing homogenization within schools 
after tracking.
4.2. Why is BSAV increasing after tracking?
The aim of between-school tracking is to produce more achievement 
homogeneous schools, even at the cost of increasing variance between 
schools. However, the increase in BSAV should not be greater than the 
decrease in WSAV so as not to increase total variance. The present re­
sults, however, show that the increase in BSAV clearly outweighs the 
decrease in WSAV in early-tracking countries. In mid-tracking countries 
this BSAV increase is decisively smaller than in early tracking countries 
(except for the domain of reading). In sum, a small homogenization 
effect of students within schools comes of the expense of a markable 
heterogenization of students between schools in early-tracking coun­
tries. Consequently, total variance increases in these countries (cf. 
Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006).
What may be the reason for the decisive increase of BSAV in early- 
tracking countries? It should be noted that BSAV increases over time if 
high achievers progress at a higher pace than low achievers. In fact, it 
has been shown that achievement progress is higher for students in high 
tracks than for students in low tracks (e.g., Angelone, 2019; Baumert 
et al., 2006; Becker et al. 2006; Becker et al., 2012; Dockx et al., 2019; 
Guill et al., 2017; K¨ahler et al., 2024; K¨oller & Baumert, 2001; Traini 
et al., 2021; Van Damme et al., 2002). This leads to the question: Are 
there particular characteristics of schooling after tracking that promote 
the learning progress of students in high tracks more than in low tracks? 
Baumert et al. (2006) propose three possibilities (see also Brunello & 
Checchi, 2007):
First, students in higher tracks possess more prior knowledge which 
might enhance further learning progress (individual Matthew effect). 
Empirical findings to this point are inconsistent and meta-analytical 
results on reading even indicate a small compensatory effect (Pfost,

Hattie, D¨orfler, & Artelt, 2014). Therefore, an individual Matthew effect 
does not seem to play an important role for the widening of achievement 
differences between schools.
Second, students in higher tracks may benefit from an advantageous 
peer composition in terms of ability and social and ethnic background 
(institutional Matthew effect). Research so far indicates that students 
tend to show higher achievement progress in classrooms and schools 
that are characterized by high average prior achievement level of the 
student body (Becker, Kocaj, Jansen, Dumont, & Lüdtke, 2022; Dumont, 
Neumann, Maaz, & Trautwein, 2013) and high average socioeconomic 
status (Dumont et al., 2013; Holzberger, Reinhold, Lüdtke, & Seidel, 
2020; Van Ewijk & Sleegers, 2010). The ethnic composition of class­
rooms seems to be of little relevance for individual student achievement 
(Rjosk, Richter, Lüdtke, & Eccles, 2017). It must be noted that different 
compositional characteristics (social, ethnic, achievement-related) are 
highly interrelated and moreover highly related to track placement 
(Dumont et al., 2013; K¨ahler et al., 2024). Therefore, the independent 
effects of separate composition aspects are difficult to determine. 
Moreover, conditions which are related to both student composition and 
achievement (e.g., instruction quality), need to be considered as po­
tential influences. Hattie (2002, p. 463) outlined: „It seems that the 
quality of teaching and the nature of the student interactions are the key 
issues, rather than the compositional structure of the classes.” This leads 
to the next point.
Third, higher tracks might pose advantages for learning due to 
institutional characteristics, such as more challenging curricula, a 
higher level of instructional quality, and better school resources. Insti­
tutional differences were shown to be very relevant for students’ 
learning progress (Angelone, 2019; Baumert et al., 2006; Becker et al. 
2006; Becker et al., 2012; Dockx et al., 2019; Guill et al., 2017; K¨ahler 
et al., 2024; K¨oller & Baumert, 2001; Traini et al., 2021; Van Damme 
et al., 2002). Moreover, there is indication that instructional quality is 
higher in high tracks and lower in low tracks (e.g., Brunello & Checchi, 
2007; Demanet & Van Houtte, 2014; Guill et al., 2017; Hattie, 2002; 
Maaz, Trautwein, Lüdtke, & Baumert, 2008; Weber et al., 2019). For 
instance, in the early tracking countries Germany, Austria, and 
Switzerland there are specific curricular and didactic traditions of 
different school types (Baumert et al., 2006; Neumann et al., 2007; 
Tischler & Wakounig, 2013). Baumert et al. (2010) demonstrated that 
teachers in high-track schools show higher performance both in general 
intellectual and in teaching-specific matters as well as in pedagogical 
content knowledge than teachers in lower-track schools. Moreover, 
there is a higher amount of cognitive activation in academic-track 
schools (Klieme, et al., 2001). In sum, instruction in lower tracks may 
be characterized as less demanding and activating, more fragmented, 
more routine and repetitive, and delivered by less engaged and less 
competent teachers (Maaz et al., 2008). In actual learning and teaching, 
the institutional context (e.g., curriculum, instruction quality) interacts 
with the compositional context (e.g., student socio-economic status, 
student abilities), making different school types to differential environ­
ments for learning and development (Baumert et al., 2006 p. 98).
The present results show that the increase in BSAV is higher in early- 
tracking countries than in mid-tracking countries. This indicates that 
achievement of students in different school tracks diverge the more the 
longer they had been tracked and therefore the more time they have 
passed in differential learning and development environments. When 
students have passed only one or two years in tracked schools (like in 
mid-tracking countries) BSAV has increased to a smaller amount than 
when they have been tracked between schools for four to six years. It has 
been speculated that achievement differences of students from different 
tracks would increase further in the course of tracking (Eder & Neuweg, 
2024, p. 216). However, longitudinal studies that include three mea­
surement points (the first just after tracking, the second 1–2 years after 
tracking and the third 3–4 years after tracking) indicated that achieve­
ment gaps between tracks do not increase anymore beyond measure­
ment Point 2 (Pfost & Artelt, 2013; Retelsdorf, Becker, K¨oller, & M¨oller, 
2012; Traini et al., 2021). It must be noted that these results are only 
based on Germany. Our results indicate that such a continuous spread 
after tracking may indeed occur on an international level. However, our 
results are not longitudinal and therefore we do not know whether BSAV 
still increases after tracking in early-tracking countries or whether BSAV 
is larger in early- than in mid-tracking countries right from the start of 
tracking. Future longitudinal studies including different countries and 
more than two measurement points may shed light on this issue.
4.3. Implications for policy and practice
4.3.1. Implication from small WSAV reduction in tracked countries
The present results show that a homogenization effect of between- 
school tracking is only small. This indicates that there is still need for 
differentiated instruction even in tracked education systems. However, 
in early-tracking countries teachers are often less prepared to manage 
heterogeneity and actually use methods of adaptive instruction less 
often than teachers in other countries. For example, figures of the OECD 
(2019d, p. 100) based on PISA 2018 show that among the 15 OECD 
countries with the lowest level of teacher support there are nine 
early-tracking countries (Austria, Netherlands, Germany, Luxembourg, 
Czech Republic, Slovak Republic, Belgium, Switzerland, Hungary). 
Among the 15 OECD countries with the highest level of teacher support, 
however, there is only one early-tracking country (Turkey). In the same 
vein Bacher (2007) showed that students perceive teacher support as 
lower in early-tracking countries. These results suggest that in 
early-tracking countries teaching is organized as if homogeneity were a 
given (although it is not) and instruction comes with a low level of 
adaption to student needs. This is an urgent issue for intervention as 
instructional quality is considered decisive to students’ learning prog­
ress (e.g., Hattie, 2002) and adaptive teaching is seen as an important 
component of effective, high quality instructional practice (Hardy, 
Decristan, & Klieme, 2019).
4.3.2. Implication from growing BSAV the longer tracking takes
The present results show that mid-tracking countries are not less 
efficient than early tracking countries in establishing homogeneous 
learning groups, although mid-tracking countries are better at limiting 
BSAV increase as in mid-tracking countries WSAV reduction is not 
outweighed by BSAV increase. In sum, mid-tracking countries come 
closer to the goal of increasing homogeneity within schools without 
increasing total variance than early-tracking countries. Early-tracking 
countries (tracking at age 10–12) could therefore consider delaying 
tracking a few years (to the age of 14 or 15). Postponing tracking is a 
popular issue of discussion in early-tracking countries (e.g., Brinkmann 
et al., 2024; Brunello & Checchi, 2007). However, one must be careful to 
assume that postponing early tracking will automatically result in a 
reduced BSAV at the end of compulsory schooling as quality of in­
struction is deemed more important for student learning than the time of 
tracking. As Hattie (2009, p. 95) pointed out: “Simply placing students 
in small or more homogeneous groups is not enough.” Hattie (2002)
emphasized that tracking only plays a minor role for learning, but it 
often goes along with differential quality of instruction. Similarly, Lav­
rijsen, Dockx, Struyf, and Verschueren (2022) pointed out that teachers’ 
responses to student diversity might be more decisive for improving 
student achievement than homogenizing classes in terms of ability.
BSAV could therefore (also) be reduced by increasing the instruc­
tional quality, especially in low tracks. As Betts (2011) emphasized, 
separating students by initial achievement opens up the possibility that 
school systems could tailor school resources to the given type of student. 
Examples of such customized resource allocation are class size reduction 
for disadvantaged students or assigning the best teachers to the most 
disadvantaged students (OECD, 2016).

4.4. Strengths and limitations, and implications for future research
So far, the question of whether tracking leads to more homogeneity 
has been characterized by a) the strong conviction that tracking would 
result in more achievement-homogeneous student bodies and b) by 
weak data, as almost no research has systematically examined this issue. 
The present study made a big step forward in investigating the role of 
between-school tracking for the development of achievement variance. 
Up to now studies have focused on total variance. We know from this 
research that total achievement variance is increasing in early tracking 
countries. Despite this well-documented finding, early tracking has still 
been justified by the argument that homogeneous learning groups would 
make teaching and learning more effective. Since the finding of 
increasing total variance does not provide information about WSAV, 
there has been no hard evidence so far to disprove this argument (as also 
a decrease in WSAV together with an even larger increase in BSAV may 
yield to an increase of total variance). The present study is the first to 
show that the empirical evidence for this argument is weak as WSAV 
reduction is on average very small in early-tracking countries. This 
yields to a new question that has not been asked before: What is the 
benefit of early between-school tracking if it only marginally increases 
achievement homogeneity within schools while substantially amplifying 
achievement differences between schools?
The present study not only systematically differentiated between 
WSAV and BSAV, but also between early- and mid-tracking. This makes 
it possible to distinguish between long- and short-term effects of 
tracking. Education policy in early-tracking countries is often not 
interested in radical reforms that would postpone the selection age by, 
for example, five years, whereas a first step of about two years is seen to 
be more feasible.12 The present paper gives an indication on what 
happens in both scenarios (postponement of selection for a shorter or 
longer period) and thus provides a more detailed basis for political de­
cisions. It must be noted however that the number of countries per group 
becomes quite small (e.g., nine countries in the early-tracking group). 
Therefore, more research is needed to substantiate the present results, 
especially concerning differences between early- and mid-tracking 
countries.
Unlike many studies so far, it followed a pseudo-panel design and 
included students of about the same birth cohort. This prevents the 
alternative explanation that different cohorts were educated under 
different circumstances even in the same country, for example, if there 
were educational reforms in primary school that were in force only for 
the PIRLS/TIMSS cohort but not yet for the PISA cohort. Ultimately, the 
present study follows an IPD meta-analytical approach, which is state of 
the art, but to our knowledge it has not yet been used to analyze the 
effects of tracking. Despite these various strengths, the present study has 
also limitations that need to be considered:
The DID approach assumes that achievement variance (including 
WSAV) would evolve identically across countries if there were no dif­
ferences in tracking regimes. One potential criticism behind this 
assumption is that countries with early- and late-tracking regimes may 
differ in respects that are correlated with tracking policies. Some of these 
factors may also affect the evolution of WSAV even in the absence of 
tracking. For example, WSAV will decrease independently of explicit 
tracking when students with a high social status (who are often also high 
performing students) attend private schools after primary education or 
when secondary teacher education differs from primary teacher edu­
cation in a way that differentiated instruction is less emphasized. The 
present study included a large number of countries and therefore spe­
cific country policies other than tracking are supposed to average out 
and to not meaningfully bias the results. An exception are the countries 
Qatar and United Arab Emirates, where an enormous decrease in 
achievement variation (both WSAV and BSAV) was evident from 4th 
graders to 15-year-olds. This is probably not an effect of tracking. 
Possible reasons could lie in varying proportions of students who were 
tested in English and Arab between PIRLS/TIMSS and PISA as well as in 
increasing demands on teacher qualification at the secondary level 
(Kelly, Centurino, Martin, & Mullis, 2020; Mullis, Martin, Minnich, 
Drucker, & Ragan, 2012; Ridge, 2014). Therefore, we computed extra 
models that excluded these Gulf States. As long as we are not clear about 
the reasons for this enormous decrease in variance, we consider analyses 
that exclude these two countries as more trustworthy. Therefore, in the 
present study all result interpretations for the group of mid-tracking 
countries exclude Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, and in the ta­
bles results for both (including and excluding Qatar and the United Arab 
Emirates) are presented.
The present IPD meta-analysis is based on a pseudo-panel DID 
approach of different studies with different scalation. A direct compar­
ison of achievement results between PIRLS/TIMSS and PISA is not 
possible. As these studies are not longitudinal, we cannot follow 
achievement (variance) over time, however we can compare the same 
countries and investigate how the country’s relative positions change 
over time. To achieve this, we standardized total variance for each 
country sample and each study to an average of 100 and this total 
variation was further decomposed to WSAV and BSAV. Therefore, a 
result like “WSAV in math decreased by 7.20 points” means that the 
share of WSAV in the average total variance across the same 37 countries 
was 7.20 %age points lower for 15-year-olds (PISA 2018) than for 10- 
year-olds (TIMSS 2011). This relative decrease does not automatically 
imply an absolute decrease: For example, if there were at the same time 
an absolute reduction in total variance of 7.20 %age points between 
students aged 10 and 15, the above result would mean no absolute 
change in WSAV. When including groups of countries this approach 
allows deductions like “in relation to average variance across countries, 
BSAV increased more in early-tracking countries than in late-tracking 
countries”. This relative (and not absolute) change must always be 
kept in mind when interpreting the results of DID analyses. It must be 
noted that the DID approach is well established and recommended (e.g., 
Contini & Cugnata, 2020; Hanushek & W¨oßmann, 2006) and makes 
longitudinal questions tangible, even when real longitudinal data is not 
available.
Results show that there is still large amount of variance in WSAV and 
BSAV between countries that cannot be explained by between-school 
tracking and there are indeed countries that do not follow the average 
trend. For example, we found effective early-tracking countries with a 
remarkable decrease in WSAV and at the same time relatively stable 
BSAV (e.g., Hungary and Indonesia). On the other hand, there are early- 
tracking countries where tracking seems to be highly ineffective: WSAV 
does not decrease significantly but BSAV increases significantly in the 
Netherlands and Germany. There are also inefficient mid-tracking 
countries in which WSAV does not decrease significantly (e.g., 
Denmark, Hong Kong, Korea, Portugal, Russia) or the increase of BSAV 
clearly outweighs the decrease of WSAV (e.g., Chinese Taipei, Croatia). 
Future studies should investigate in more detail, why some tracked 
countries achieve better to produce achievement homogeneous schools 
than others and how extraordinary developments in some countries 
(especially Qatar and the United Arab Emirates) can be explained.
Studies so far have indicated that tracking is not only related to 
increased achievement variance but also to increased social variance 
(Terrin & Triventi, 2023; Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010; Strello et al., 
2021, 2022). Future studies should investigate in more detail how social 
variance within and between schools changes after tracking. In this way, 
the amount of academic homogenization could be compared to the 
amount of social homogenization to investigate in more detail whether 
between-school tracking is a driver of social segregation. This could shed 
light on whether tracking is more effective at the undesired sorting by 
social status than at the intended sorting by achievement.
WSAV does not necessarily reflect the amount of student

An issue concerning methods is that there might exist differences in 
the tracking regimes within countries which are not considered in the 
present analyses. In the figures of the OECD (2020a) the selection age is 
only reported for whole countries. However, there is indication that 
variations within countries exist (e.g., Becker, Neumann, & Dumont, 
2017 on Germany; Nagengast & Marsh, 2011 on the United Kingdom). 
Future OECD reports should include data on school tracking policies in 
greater detail with regard to regional differences to enable more specific 
analysis.
A potential limitation is that statistical power is relatively low in the 
domain-specific models and only rather large effects become statistically 
significant. There is more power in the general models including all 
domains and therefore at least 25 effect sizes per tracking group. We 
addressed this point by interpreting results in the domain-specific 
models not only in terms of significance, but also by indicating ten­
dencies and comparing effect sizes between WSAV and BSAV. Therefore, 
it is not only the lack of significance in some domains that leads us to the 
conclusion that tracking is not very effective in making schools more 
homogeneous, but also effect sizes, which show that the homogenization 
within schools is much smaller than the heterogenization between 
schools. The present results are consistent with the findings of Berg­
müller and Steger (2009), which are based on data from TIMSS 2007 and 
PISA 2006. Still, more research, including more countries and more 
different assessments, would be desirable to further substantiate the 
results. But testing a pseudo-panel with recent data (e.g., TIMSS 
2015/PIRLS 2016 to PISA 2022) is not advisable because COVID-19 
effects may have led to changes in variance that are not attributable 
to tracking but depend on country-specific COVID-19 policies. More­
over, the latest PISA assessment was postponed for one year (from 2021 
to 2022) due to COVID-19 which impairs pseudo-panel comparisons.
5. Conclusion
The present results focused the effects of tracking on achievement 
and showed that between-school tracking is not very effective in making 
schools more achievement homogeneous. However, variance between 
schools increased largely in countries with early-tracking regimes. This 
detrimental development was found to be less pronounced in mid- 
tracking countries than in early-tracking countries.
Our findings weaken the strong (and often implicit) conviction that 
tracking would result in more achievement-homogeneous student 
bodies. This central aim of between-school tracking is only achieved to a 
limited extent as achievement homogeneity does not increase to a 
considerable amount in tracked countries. Nevertheless, teachers in 
tracked countries teach as if homogeneity were a given. For example, 
adaptive teaching is especially low in early-tracking countries (OECD, 
2019d). This points to the importance of raising awareness that there is 
still 
heterogeneity 
after 
school-level 
tracking. 
Especially 
in 
early-tracking countries, more efforts should be made to address student 
heterogeneity through appropriate teaching methods. This should be an 
issue of all responsible parties: Teachers, principals, teacher education, 
and education policy.
The large increase in BSAV in early-tracking countries points to 
differences in teaching quality between tracks. Further research is 
needed to better understand the specific nature of these differences and 
to find out how teaching in the lower tracks can be improved to achieve 
comparable gains to those in the higher tracks.
Finally, the present results show that the increase in BSAV is lower if 
tracking takes place later (at age 14 or 15) instead of earlier (at age 
10–12). This could be interpreted in the way that postponing tracking by 
a few years could prevent widening achievement gaps. However, pre­
vious research revealed that changing the tracking system may result in 
higher student achievement only if teaching quality improves (Hattie, 
2002). Moreover, it is important to consider a country’s culture when 
changing the tracking system (LeTendre et al., 2003). If the tracking 
regime does not fit societal expectations and the corresponding demands 
of parents and teachers, it is likely that alternatives to the explicit ed­
ucation policy will proliferate, such as (implicit) within-school tracking 
(Trautwein, 2014), private schools or shadow education (Baker, Akiba, 
LeTendre, & Wiseman, 2001). This could lead to two-tier education even 
in formally comprehensive systems.
The central goal of all education systems should be to improve the 
quality of teaching, and all education reforms and interventions should 
be considered in terms of whether they contribute to this goal (Hattie, 
2009). In tracked school systems the focus should be on improving 
teaching quality in low tracks more than in high tracks to counteract 
widening achievement gaps between tracks.
