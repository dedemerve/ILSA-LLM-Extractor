A B S T R A C T  
Artificial Intelligence (AI) strongly influences our daily lives and work environments. To deal with the challenges 
as well as to pick up on the opportunities associated with AI, university students need to acquire a basic un­
derstanding of AI (so-called AI literacy). To design effective study programs that foster AI competencies, it is 
necessary to assess the state of students’ AI literacy. While there already are some AI literacy tests available, 
many instruments focus on specific AI courses, rely primarily on self-assessment, or do not provide detailed 
psychometric information. This study aims to develop and validate an AI literacy test and to provide initial 
insights into the current state of AI literacy among German university students. We present a validated multiple- 
choice test to assess AI literacy in higher education. The results suggest that there is a significant variance in AI 
literacy among students, with most showing a fundamental understanding of AI. AI literacy is higher among 
students with a technical study background or prior experience in AI. The results imply that there is a need for 
effective AI courses for a broader audience of students who use AI in their daily lives and will use AI tools in their 
future careers. Furthermore, educators should consider the variance of students’ prior knowledge when 
designing AI courses.   
1. Introduction 
Emerging AI tools like ChatGPT show the disruptive power of Arti­
ficial Intelligence (AI) in many areas of our lives. AI is already influ­
encing large parts of our society, such as workplaces (Lane et al., 2023) 
and educational systems (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022). Many AI applications 
offer promising opportunities for society, for example in healthcare 
(Briganti & Le Moine, 2020) or in education (Kasneci et al., 2023). 
However, AI applications such as predictive policing (Richardson et al., 
2019) or deep fakes (Verdoliva, 2020) pose risks to people and society. 
To deal with the opportunities and challenges associated with AI, citi­
zens need to develop a basic understanding of AI and the ability to use 
and evaluate AI systems. These skills are often referred to as AI literacy 
(Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021). Therefore, governmental in­
stitutions like the EU (European Union, 2023; Vuorikari et al., 2022) and 
organizations (e.g., AI4K12: https://ai4k12.org/) have declared the goal 
to develop AI literacy among the general population. 
Students in higher education are an essential target audience for AI 
education as they need to develop the required skills to handle AI 
effectively in their careers (Ng et al., 2021; Southworth et al., 2023). 
This is not only true for students in computer science-related disciplines 
who may be involved in the development of AI. It applies to students in 
all disciplines, as they will likely use AI tools in their professional lives, 
regardless of the industry in which they will work. At universities, many 
academic programs have already integrated AI courses into their cur­
riculum. However, these often focus on technical aspects of AI and are 
only accessible to students in technical departments (Southworth et al., 
2023). A growing number of initiatives (Federal Ministry of Education 
and Research, 2021; Southworth et al., 2023) have begun to develop AI 
education programs with a broader scope, including courses focusing on 
the ethical and societal implications of AI. These programs focus not 
only on engineers and computer scientists but a broader audience that 
includes students in the social sciences and humanities. To be effective, 
educational programs should be tailored to the individual student’s 
prior knowledge (Kalyuga, 2007; van de Pol et al., 2010). Assessment of 
student competencies is essential for determining entry requirements 
and evaluating and improving higher education (K¨oller et al., 2020; 
Zlatkin-Troitschanskaia et al., 2020). Using a valid AI literacy test, ed­
ucators can obtain information about the status quo of AI literacy in 
their target group and use it to adapt their teaching to the needs of the

1.1. Defining AI literacy 
Several frameworks outlining AI competencies and learning goals for 
various target groups have been published recently (Long & Magerko, 
2020; Michaeli et al., 2022; Ng et al., 2021; Touretzky et al., 2019). A 
review of AI literacy definitions relevant for higher education by Lau­
pichler et al. (2022) shows that despite the novelty of the concept, there 
seems to be a common understanding of AI literacy across many of these 
definitions (Laupichler et al., 2022). Following analogous constructs 
such as scientific literacy (Laugksch, 2000) or media literacy (Potter, 
2010), AI literacy aims to describe competencies needed for a basic 
understanding of AI (Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021). Long and 
Magerko (2020) define AI literacy as “a set of competencies that enables 
individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and 
collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and 
in the workplace”. They conceptualize AI literacy as a set of 17 com­
petencies they derived from various literature sources. These compe­
tencies are then grouped according to five questions (What is AI? What 
can AI do? How does AI work? How should AI be used? and How do 
people perceive AI?). By aligning with a widely used definition of AI 
literacy, our AI literacy test will be applicable for a wide range of re­
searchers and educators in AI education. Therefore, we will focus on the 
conceptualization of AI literacy by Long and Magerko (2020), which is 
among the most frequently cited and established definitions (Laupichler 
et al., 2022). 
1.2. Current research on AI literacy among students 
The current research on AI literacy in higher education focuses on 
the development and evaluation of specific AI courses (Kandlhofer et al., 
2016; Kong et al., 2022; Kong et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021). To date, only 
a few research projects have dealt with the measurement of AI literacy. 
An increasing number of self-assessment scales for AI literacy have been 
published recently (Carolus et al., 2023; Laupichler et al., 2023; Lin 
et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2023; Pinski & Benlian, 2023; Wang et al., 2022). 
However, since self-assessment can be biased (Dunning et al., 2004), a 
valid and objective knowledge test is needed to assess students’ AI lit­
eracy (Laupichler et al., 2023). Some knowledge tests for AI literacy 
have been developed to evaluate AI courses (Kong et al., 2021, 2022; 
Wang et al., 2022). Therefore, they often focus on course-specific topics 
and are thus not suitable for assessing students’ AI literacy in other 
contexts. As one example, Kong et al. (2021) developed an AI course for 
students with diverse study backgrounds and showed that course par­
ticipants improved in a test about concepts covered in the course. In 
addition, psychometric measures of these tests are rarely published. 
Consequently, we set out to develop an AI knowledge test for university 
students. 
Due to the lack of large-scale surveys, the current state of AI literacy 
among university students remains mostly unclear. One of the few 
studies available is a study with healthcare students which reports that 
the majority of students were unable to define AI or provided an inac­
curate definition (Teng et al., 2022). The existing literature on pre­
conceptions and misconceptions about AI may inform assumptions 
about students’ AI literacy. Research suggests that students hold mis­
conceptions about AI, such as the idea that AI is very similar to the 
human brain (Sulmont et al., 2019). In particular, students of 
non-technical domains have more knowledge gaps regarding AI, as 
mathematical skills and programming skills were identified as the two 
biggest barriers to learning about AI (Sulmont et al., 2019). In general, 
research shows that many learners have a limited technical under­
standing of AI (Antonenko & Abramowitz, 2023; Chounta et al., 2022; 
Evangelista et al., 2018; Lindner & Berges, 2020) and tend to attribute 
human-like characteristics to AI (Evangelista et al., 2018; Kreinsen & 
Schulz, 2021; Mertala et al., 2022). This research underscores the 
importance of assessing AI literacy to gain insight into pre-existing 
knowledge about AI. 
1.3. Rationale 
In this study, we aim to develop and validate a theory-based AI lit­
eracy test that is compatible with Long and Magerko’s (2020) concep­
tualization of AI literacy. Using this test, we assess the AI literacy among 
university students of different disciplines in Germany, thus contrib­
uting to closing the research gap of students’ AI literacy. In addition, we 
aim to investigate the relationship between AI literacy and other vari­
ables, such as students’ prior experience in formal and informal learning 
opportunities. Considering that AI education programs are beginning to 
gain wide popularity among instructors, researchers, and in curricula 
(Su & Zhong, 2022), we expect that most university students in Germany 
did not learn about AI at school yet (Rietz & V¨olmicke, 2020). However, 
numerous informal learning opportunities are available online (e.g., 
https://www.elementsofai.com/). Therefore, we expect that AI literacy 
among university students varies depending on their discipline and in­
dividual prior experience in formal or informal learning settings. 
2. Method 
2.1. Test development 
2.1.1. Item development 
We generated items theory-driven based on the competencies of 
Long and Magerko (2020). We decided not to include competency 5 
(Imagine Future of AI), as it is not possible to verify predictions, and 
competencies 14 (Action & Reaction) and 15 (Sensors), because they 
only focus on one application area of AI (robotics). We used established 
AI textbooks (Ertel, 2016; Russell & Norvig, 2022) for item generation. 
Research findings on learners’ misconceptions about AI were used to 
develop appropriate distractors (Bewersdorff et al., 2023; Lindner & 
Berges, 2020; Sulmont et al., 2019). We chose a multiple-choice format 
(one out of four answers is correct) to allow an economic assessment that 
is suitable for large-scale surveys. This first step resulted in a pool of 57 
items. All items were reviewed for technical accuracy and validity by 
three academic researchers (two computer scientists and one social 
scientist) in the field of AI. Identified issues (e.g., imprecise use of the 
terms AI and machine learning) led to revisions. 
2.1.2. Cognitive pretest 
We conducted cognitive interviews with five students from different 
disciplines (two computer science students, two education students, and 
one business student). We used the think-aloud method (Willis, 2004) to 
identify potential hard-to-understand words and misleading phrases. 
Identified comprehension problems led to revisions of the affected items 
(e.g., replacing an incomprehensible word with a simpler one). Based on 
the expert review and cognitive interviews, we excluded less appro­
priate, redundant, and interdependent items, resulting in a preliminary 
number of 39 items. 
2.1.3. Pilot study 
We conducted a subsequent pilot study to obtain descriptive mea­
sures of the psychometric quality of the 39 items. For this purpose, we 
recruited 24 students (11 female, 13 male, age: M = 25.11, SD = 3.23) 
from different disciplines, e.g., educational sciences, management, 
psychology, or computer science. Our aim was to include items with a 
difficulty index between 10% and 90% and a discrimination index be­
tween 0.1 and 0.7 (Moosbrugger & Kelava, 2020). The difficulty index 
represents the proportion of correct responses to each item. We 
computed a difficulty index that takes into account any potential 
guessing. The item discrimination index is a measure of how much one

2.2. Main study 
For the validation study, we recruited German-speaking students 
from six universities in southern Germany. The students were invited to 
participate in the web-based study via e-mail. Participation in the study 
was voluntary. As a reward, participants could participate in a raffle of 
vouchers. A total of 1330 students participated. Before analyzing the 
data, we excluded 44 invalid cases: Four participants were excluded due 
to more than 25% missing values in the AI literacy test. Forty partici­
pants were excluded because they completed the survey too quickly (less 
than 4 min, Mdn = 16.63), which made it implausible that they 
responded thoughtfully. The final sample size was N = 1286. The mean 
age of the participants was M = 23.62 (SD = 4.47). Among the partic­
ipants, 750 (58.32%) were male, 497 (38.65%) were female, seven 
(0.54%) were diverse, and 32 (2.48%) did not indicate their gender. 
Fig. 1 shows the distribution of participating students across univer­
sities. The majority of the students are from Technical University of 
Munich. Fig. 2 shows the composition of disciplines according to a 
category system from the Federal Statistical Office of Germany (2023). 
2.3. Analysis 
We used Item Response Theory (IRT) models to analyze the data of 
the AI literacy test. In IRT, person abilities and item characteristics are 
estimated on a joint scale (Embretson & Reise, 2009). IRT offers several 
advantages over classical test theory, such as that test data can be 
analyzed at the item level. The three most prominent IRT models are the 
Rasch (1-PL) model, the 2-PL model, and 3-PL model (Embretson & 
Reise, 2009). They differ in the number of parameters for item charac­
teristics. The Rasch model estimates only one parameter for item diffi­
culty is estimated. The 2-PL model additionally estimates parameters for 
item discrimination. The 3-PL model also includes parameters to ac­
count for guessing behavior. We assumed a guessing rate of 25% for all 
items due to the one-out-of-four response format. 
Before running the IRT models, we tested two essential assumptions 
of IRT: unidimensionality and local independence (Embretson & Reise, 
2009). Unidimensionality means that the responses to all items can be 
explained by one latent trait. We tested unidimensionality by fitting a 
unidimensional model using confirmatory factor analysis with the 
package lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) for R (R Core Team, 2022). We evalu­
ated model fit using the following indices and cutoff values: Х2/df < 2–3; 
RMSEA <0.05; SRMR <0.1 (Backhaus et al., 2015; Brown, 2015). Local 
independence means that the response to one item depends not only on 
the trait level of the examinee but also on their responses to other items 
(Embretson & Reise, 2009). We evaluated local independence using the 
Q3 statistic (Yen, 1984) and examined if the correlation of the residuals 
of a pair of items is higher than the cutoff value 0.2 (Chen & Thissen, 
1997). 
We fitted all three IRT models using the package mirt (Chalmers, 
2012) for R (R Core Team, 2022). We compared multiple model fit and 
item fit indices to determine the best model. We evaluated model fit 
using the M2 statistic (Maydeu-Olivares & Joe, 2006), a limited infor­
mation statistic suitable for sparse data. We used the cutoff ≤0.05 for 
RMSEA and SRMR suggested by Maydeu-Olivares (2013). Additionally, 
we examined the TLI and CFI indices using ≥0.95 as the cutoff. To 
evaluate item fit, we used the signed chi-squared (S - Х2) index proposed 
by Orlando and Thissen (2000). This item fit statistic is based on 
response patterns rather than on sum statistics and is therefore also 
suitable for IRT models other than the Rasch model. 
2.3.1. Validation strategy 
In addition to AI literacy, we assessed related constructs and prior 
experience with AI to validate the scale. We followed two approaches to 
determine the validity of the AI literacy test. First, we tested correlations 
with other variables that are theoretically related to AI literacy using 
IRT-based person abilities. We used rating scale models (Andrich, 1978) 
to calculate person abilities for the related constructs. Second, we tested 
whether the test could discriminate between people with presumably 
low and high AI literacy based on their prior experience. 
Self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is defined as an individual’s confidence to 
perform a task successfully (Bandura, 1997). The relationship between 
self-efficacy and performance is known in many domains (Chang et al., 
2014; Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Hatlevik, 2017). As self-efficacy is 
perceived competence and positively influences learning outcomes 
(Eccles & Wigfield, 2002), we expect self-efficacy regarding AI and AI 
literacy to be correlated. To measure self-efficacy, we developed a scale 
based on the competencies described by Long and Magerko (2020). The 
scale consists of eight items (e.g., “I have a good understanding of the 
basic principles of AI.”). The response format consisted of a 5-point 
Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Internal 
consistency was good at α = 0.85 (Tavakol & Dennick, 2011). 
Interest. Because of the positive effects of interest on motivation and 
learning strategies, interest in a subject and performance in that subject 
are related (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Krapp & Prenzel, 2011). Therefore, 
we expect that a person’s interest in AI is positively correlated to their AI 
literacy. To measure interest in AI, we adapted a scale used in the 2015 
PISA study (Mang et al., 2019) to measure interest in science. We 
modified the scale by exchanging the word “science” with “Artificial 
Intelligence”. The scale consists of five items (e.g., “I generally have fun

Attitude. The relationship between knowledge and attitudes in the 
field of public understanding of science is well established (Allum et al., 
2008; Bybee & McCrae, 2011). Based on these results, we expect AI 
literacy to correlate positively with positive attitude toward AI and 
negatively with negative attitude toward AI. We assessed attitude to­
ward AI using eight items from the General Attitudes towards AI scale by 
Schepman and Rodway (2020). We used four items measuring positive 
attitude (e.g., “There are many beneficial applications of Artificial In­
telligence.”) and four items measuring negative attitude (e.g., “I think 
Artificial Intelligence is dangerous.”). The response format was again a 
5-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. 
Internal consistency was acceptable at α = 0.77 for both scales (Tavakol 
& Dennick, 2011). 
Discipline and experience with AI. Students in a technical disci­
pline should have a higher level of AI literacy because of prior knowl­
edge from courses in their studies (Southworth et al., 2023; Sulmont 
et al., 2019). Therefore, we assessed the current discipline. Students who 
use formal and informal learning opportunities should have higher AI 
literacy than students with little experience with AI. To assess relevant 
prior experience with AI, students were asked to indicate not only their 
current discipline but also if they are currently studying computer sci­
ence or have studied study computer science in the past. Furthermore, 
they were asked whether they had ever attended a course on AI. In 
addition, they were asked to indicate how often they learn about AI in 
their free time (never/once or twice a year/less than once a month/once 
or twice a month/once a week/two or three times a week/every day).

3.2. Verifying the assumption of unidimensionality 
We verified the assumption of unidimensionality by performing a 
confirmatory factor analysis with a one-factor model. The results indi­

3.3. IRT model selection 
As outlined in 2.3, we fitted all three classical IRT models (Rasch, 2- 
PL, and 3-PL) to the data. We compared multiple model fit and item fit 
indices to determine which model describes the data best. The model fit 
indices in Table 3 show that the 3-PL model has a good model fit in all 
indices, while the Rasch model has an adequate fit only by the RMSEA 
criterion. The 2-PL model passes the cutoff values for RMSEA and SRMR 
but closely misses the TLI and CFI criteria. Comparing all three models 
with each other using AIC and BIC, the 3-PL model describes the data 
best. 
To evaluate item fit, we used the signed chi-squared (S - Х2) index 
proposed by Orlando and Thissen (2000). Table 4 presents signed 
chi-squared item fit indices for all IRT models. Due to multiple testing, 
the p-values were corrected for false discovery rate using the method by 
Benjamini and Hochberg (1995). Compared to the Rasch and the 2-PL 
model, using the 3-PL model, only a few items do not fit well. Since 
all model fit and item fit indices support the 3-PL model, we used the 
3-PL model for our subsequent analyses. 
3.4. Local independence 
We verified the assumption of local independence using the Q3 sta­
tistic (Yen, 1984) based on the 3 PL model. Therefore, we examined the 
correlation between the residuals of all items. Since all correlations were 
smaller than 0.2, local independence was not violated.

3.6. Reliability 
Fig. 6 shows the test information and the standard error of the test. 
The figure shows that the test measures more accurately in the middle 
range of ability and less accurately in the extremes. To evaluate reli­
ability, we calculated a reliability index for the EAP estimation of person 
abilities, which was r = 0.85. Additionally, we evaluated internal con­
sistency with Cronbach’s Alpha which resulted in a value of α = 0.82. 
3.7. Validity 
3.7.1. Affective measures related to AI literacy 
Table 6 shows the means and standard deviations of the raw scores of 
the affective measures we used in our study. Participants rated their AI 
literacy self-efficacy in the middle range of the scale. The mean raw 
scores for positive attitude and interest in AI were above the midpoint of 
the five-point scale, while the mean raw score for negative attitude was 
below the midpoint. 
As outlined in 2.3.1, AI literacy is related to self-efficacy, interest in 
AI, and attitude toward AI. Because of the Likert format, we used rating 
scale models (Andrich, 1978) to calculate IRT person abilities for 
self-efficacy, interest, positive attitude, and negative attitude (see 
Table 7 for reliabilities). Table 7 shows the correlations between AI 
literacy and the affective measures based on IRT scores. As expected, AI 
literacy correlates significantly positively with self-efficacy, interest, 
and positive attitude, and significantly negatively with negative 
attitude.

4. Discussion 
The aim of this study was to develop and validate an AI literacy test 
to assess the current state of AI literacy among university students in 
Germany. We developed a multiple-choice test on AI based on the 
competency model by Long and Magerko (2020). We validated the test 
with a large sample of German university students from different dis­
ciplines. The results show that the test is able to assess AI literacy in a 
reliable and valid manner. Furthermore, the findings provide initial 
insights into the state of AI literacy among German university students. 
4.1. Discussion of the test development and validation 
We took a theory-based approach to test development, using a well- 
known competency model for AI literacy (Long & Magerko, 2020). By 
aligning the test with this widely used definition, we make the test 
accessible and useable for many researchers and educators. However, 
other conceptualizations of AI literacy could also serve as a basis for an 
AI literacy test (Ng et al., 2021). In addition, we contribute to the 
research gap of an AI literacy test that is not a self-assessment, allowing 
an objective assessment of knowledge about AI (Laupichler et al., 2023). 
This complements existing AI literacy tests that rely on self-assessments 
(Carolus et al., 2023; Laupichler et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). We 
chose a multiple-choice format to allow for economic assessment. A 
disadvantage is that multiple-choice tasks might not be able to assess a 
full competency but are rather limited to assessing knowledge. 
An advantage of our approach is the use of IRT models to analyze the 
validation data. This allows us to determine the difficulty of each item 
and to assess the test’s reliability over the whole range of ability 
(Embretson & Reise, 2009). Our analyses based on IRT show that the test 
measures in a reliable manner in the middle range of person ability, i.e. 
the test measures an average level of AI literacy more accurately than a

4.2. Initial insights into the current state of AI literacy 
This study offers preliminary insights into the baseline AI literacy 
among students, shedding light on the prevailing research gap. We 
found that, on average, students solved about half of the questions. This 
finding indicates a foundational level of understanding of AI but high­
lights a significant lack of more comprehensive knowledge. This aligns 
with the results of Teng et al. (2022), who found that healthcare students 
have a limited understanding of AI. Similar results have been found for 
other groups of learners (Bewersdorff et al., 2023; Chounta et al., 2022; 
Evangelista et al., 2018). This calls for more educational programs on AI 
to address these knowledge gaps. Enhancing such initiatives would 
elevate overall AI literacy and empower students to engage more 
effectively with the increasing prevalence of AI technologies in society. 
As predicted, students with prior experience in computer science and 
students with prior experience in AI (university or informal learning) 
have a higher level of AI literacy. Furthermore, a comparison of students 
from different study backgrounds shows that students with a STEM 
background have a higher level of AI literacy than students in the social 
sciences. This finding is consistent with previous qualitative research on 
university students’ misconceptions and study backgrounds (Sulmont 
et al., 2019). Considering that students of all disciplines will be con­
fronted with AI in their professional lives (Lane et al., 2023), it is 
necessary that all students, regardless of background, have the chance to 
acquire AI literacy (Ng et al., 2021). In our study, while AI literacy varies 
greatly among students both in technical and non-technical disciplines, 
the variations between disciplines were not substantial. Only the largest 
difference - that between students from engineering and social sciences - 
was statistically significant. 
These results defy the simplistic notion that study backgrounds pri­
marily determine AI literacy. Instead, they underscore the critical role of 
individual learning experiences, whether within university or in less 
formal settings. This observation aligns with the current state of AI ed­
ucation, which, as Southworth et al. (2023) indicate, is not yet estab­
lished in many academic curricula, hence not widely accessible to all 
students. Consistent with this, our research indicates that only a mi­
nority of students have been exposed to an AI-specific lecture. These 
findings affirm an urgent need for educational reform that establishes AI 
education for both technical and non-technical disciplines and includes 
informal learning spaces. The findings also suggest that heterogeneity is 
a substantial pedagogical challenge in AI education for all students. 
Considering the heterogeneity of AI literacy across disciplines we found 
in the present results, it seems that one-size-fits-all courses are not the 
solution to the challenge of AI education. Developers of AI courses 
should consider multiple variables when designing effective courses for 
heterogeneous groups of students. According to this study, important 
variables are prior university courses about AI, prior informal learning

4.3. Limitations 
The results of this study are limited by the constitution of the sample. 
Most participants study at a technical university and are enrolled in a 
technical program. Therefore, we can hardly make any statement on the 
general level of AI literacy among all students in Germany or even 
internationally. In addition, the participation in the study was volun­
tary, so there might be a selection bias, in the sense that the students 
who decided to participate are more interested in AI or knowledgeable 
about AI than average. Another limitation is that the topic of AI gained 
in popularity after we conducted this study due to the release of 
ChatGPT. Therefore, it is possible that awareness and knowledge of AI 
have increased in the meantime. The items of this AI literacy test assess a 
general understanding of AI and do not ask for technical details. How­
ever, due to the rapid developments in the field of AI, we cannot rule out 
the possibility that some items may become outdated in the future and 
revisions of the test might become necessary. 
5. Conclusion 
Assessing the status quo of AI literacy is key to designing effective AI 
courses for all university students. We developed and validated a 
multiple-choice test on AI based on the competencies proposed by Long 
and Magerko (2020). This AI literacy test was shown to measure the 
ability of university students in Germany in a reliable and valid manner. 
It can be used by researchers and practitioners in higher education 
settings. In addition, the analysis of the present data provides initial
