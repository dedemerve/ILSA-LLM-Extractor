A B S T R A C T  
Educational research has focused increasingly on comparing student civic outcomes across various educational 
systems. However, the study of non-cognitive, attitudinal measures has assumed a subsidiary role to the study of 
cognitive measures, despite its potential to shape policy and practice. This article investigates the validity and 
cross-country measurement equivalence or invariance (ME/I) of political trust measures among adolescents. 
Drawing on student data from ICCS 2016 covering 15 European educational systems (N = 52,788), the standard 
test for exact ME/I (i.e., Multi-Group Confirmatory Factor Analysis) is juxtaposed with a novel method for 
approximate ME/I (i.e., Alignment Optimization). The results underscore a two-dimensional structure of political 
trust, separating trust in order institutions from trust in representative institutions. While MGCFA results suggest 
configural invariance, rendering correlational analyses and country mean comparisons meaningless, Alignment 
Optimization yields valid latent means with an acceptable degree of non-invariance (i.e., 16%). Implications for 
comparative research on political trust among adolescents are discussed.   
1. Introduction 
Comparing students’ civic competences across different educational 
systems stands as a vital aspect within the realm of school effectiveness 
research. However, it is only recently that non-cognitive outcomes such 
as students’ endorsement of democratic principles and practices have 
garnered increased attention, despite holding equal importance in pre­
dicting future civic behaviour as cognitive metrics such as civic 
knowledge and understanding (Blask´o et al., 2019; Isac et al., 2014; 
Wurster, 2022). Among these non-cognitive aspects, one particularly 
significant measure of democratic support is political, or institutional, 
trust (Torney-Purta et al., 2001). This attitude is widely recognized as 
‘the glue that keeps the system together and as the oil that lubricates the 
policy machine’ (van der Meer & Dekker, 2011, p. 1). High levels of 
political trust are associated with increased law compliance and voter 
turnout, thus ensuring system legitimacy (Easton, 1975; Marien & 
Hooghe, 2011; Smets & van Ham, 2013). Socialization research suggests 
that political trust emerges in early childhood, evolves during adoles­
cence through interactions with parents, peers, and teachers, and tends 
to remain relatively stable into adulthood (Flanagan, 2013). This, 
therefore, underscores the importance of comparative research on the 
political trust of young individuals, not only for informing civic 
educational practices and reforms but also for shedding light on the 
validity and generalizability of theories surrounding political trust, its 
sources and consequences. 
The aim of the present study is to contribute to the field of 
comparative (educational) research on civic and citizenship outcomes 
by investigating whether existing political trust measures among youth 
populations are valid, and whether comparisons of these measures 
across countries are meaningful. To date, little progress has been made 
in this direction. Empirical studies often compute summed or averaged 
scores from a set of survey items that measure trust in state institutions 
without initially validating the underlying structure of the scale, or 
doing this in an exploratory rather than a confirmatory, more theoretical 
fashion (Dahl & Stattin, 2014; Kiess, 2021; Kim, 2019). Moreover, 
findings regarding the dimensional nature of political trust have been 
mixed, with most studies reporting (or assuming) a unifactorial struc­
ture, some providing evidence for a two-dimensional model dis­
tinguishing between trust in order institutions and trust in 
representative institutions, and still, others proposing a multi-factorial 
scale (Breustedt, 2018; Norris, 2022). Additionally, though over the 
past decades several methodological papers investigated the compara­
bility of political trust measures across different groups (Andr´e, 2014; 
Marien, 2017; Schneider, 2017; Turper & Aarts, 2017; van der Meer &

Specifically, it investigates both the dimensionality and cross-country

civic and citizenship competences. It adds to the existing literature by 
recognizing the limitations of MGCFA in the context of International

juxtaposing it with a novel method that tests for approximate ME/I, 
namely Alignment Optimization (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014). Our 
primary hypotheses test whether, among European adolescents, political 
trust represents a one-dimensional construct or a two-dimensional 
construct (i.e., separating trust in order institutions from trust in 
representative institutions), and whether this construct can be validly 
compared across various European countries. 
The article starts by conceptualizing political trust using two 
competing dimensional models and outlining empirical evidence on the 
dimensionality and cross-country comparability of political trust mea­

ME/I tests are discussed. We conclude by describing the theoretical and 
methodological implications of the findings and providing recommen­
dations for future comparative research on political trust among young 
people. 
2. The dimensionality of political trust 
Political trust, or the trust in state institutions and representatives, is 
commonly defined as a positive evaluation that, despite uncertainty, 
political trustees (i.e., specific objects within the political system, or the 
system as a whole) (will) adhere to normative criteria (Hetherington, 
1998; Bauer & Freitag, 2017). This definition depicts political trust as 
both relational and evaluative, describing a situation where A (i.e., a 
citizen) trusts B (i.e., a political institution) to do X (Zmerli & Van der 
Meer, 2017; Bauer & Freitag, 2017). In recent years, a growing number 
of scholars have delved into the dimensional nature of political trust 
(Breustedt, 2018; Rothstein & Stolle, 2008; van der Meer & Ouattara, 
2019). Conventional approaches assume that trust in a variety of po­
litical objects (e.g., the parliament or the police) represents a unidi­
mensional attitude. Specifically, some political scientists argue that 
political trust is moulded by the prevailing political culture, and that a 
generally (non-) trusting attitude will shape specific trust judgements in 
different political institutions equally (Hooghe, 2011). In a similar vein, 
psychological and socio-cultural theories view trust as either a person­
ality trait or learned from an early age on (Uslaner, 2002), thus reducing 
political trust to its subject and implicating that people trust distinct 
political trustees similarly. Empirically, both accounts suggest that 
different political trust indicators measure a singular attitude and can 
thus be used interchangeably. 
However, other scholars have warned that such unidimensional 
interpretation ignores the relational essence of political trust, which 
perceives trust as the result of a truster, trustee, and criterion (van der 
Meer & Ouattara, 2019). Particularly the notion that citizens do not 
differentiate between political objects, rendering them interchangeable, 
lacks robust theoretical or empirical grounding. Indeed, despite 
belonging to the same political system, different state institutions 
possess unique characteristics that are apparent to citizens. Therefore, 
theoretical contributions often classify institutions into two distinct 
groups, each representing a different branch of the system with its own 
set of responsibilities and defining characteristics (Rothstein & Stolle, 
2008; Warren, 2018). The first branch includes the order institutions (e. 
g., the police, the army, the judiciary), which are responsible for real­
izing and securing agreed public services (e.g., security) and laws. These 
agencies are assumed to be impartial and have the best interests of all 
citizens in mind. Without a widespread public trust in these actors, 
scholars have warned that “a society will not have the collective action 
capacities necessary to provide for itself” (Warren, 2018, p. 78). The 
second branch encompasses the representative institutions of a demo­
cratic polity (e.g., the government, parliaments, political parties), which 
are responsible for drafting the rules and laws . These are often deemed 
the true ‘political’ institutions as they are characterized by political 
competition and partisanship. Consequently, these institutions generally 
do not enjoy the same levels of public trust such as order institutions do, 
with political parties being the least trusted (Gabriel, 2017). Hence, 
considering that (groups of) objects of political trust hold unique 
meanings, political trust attitudes should to some extent be 
object-specific (i.e., differentiating between order and representative 
institutions), which challenges the conceptual assumption of 
unidimensionality. 
In recent years, a few studies have also empirically scrutinized the 
dimensional structure of political trust. A study by Breustedt (2018) 
provides a summary of research among adults that investigated the 
dimensionality of political trust measures through factor analytical ap­
proaches. Her synthesis reveals that the majority of studies substantiate 
a unidimensional structure of political trust, several corroborate a 
two-dimensional nature, and only a few support a multi-dimensional 
perspective, signalling a need to distinguish between national and su­
pranational institutions or between institutions and representatives. 
Complementing this overview with her own investigation into the val­
idity of three dimensional models using the World Values Survey (WVS) 
data, Breustedt (2018) concludes that political trust is best represented 
by two factors which she labels trust in representative and implementing 
(e.g., order) institutions. Also in Norris’s recent work (2022, p. 66–67), 
empirical evidence emerges for the distinctiveness of these two di­
mensions, which she labels as trust in agencies of national governance 
and trust in state security agencies, respectively. As her analysis extends 
to the broader concept of institutional trust, she also separates trust in 
civil society organisations (e.g., the banks or the media) and trust in 
agencies of global governance (e.g., the European Union or the United 
Nations) as distinct but related dimensions. Finally, departing from 
another strand of research, namely Item Response Theory (IRT), van der 
Meer and Ouattara (2019) fitted Rasch models to political trust items 
across seven datasets. Their findings also cast doubt on the unidimen­
sional assumption and highlight the necessity to redevelop political trust 
measures ‘a priori from a firm conceptual footing’ (van der Meer & 
Ouattara, 2019, p. 2999). 
In sum, there seems to be no common dimensional model of political 
trust across empirical studies. This can be primarily attributed to dif­
ferences in estimation methods (e.g., exploratory or confirmatory), 
analysed data (e.g., pooled or country-specific, encompassing different 
regime types and continents), and the selection of political trust in­
dicators. Particularly the latter seems to vary considerably. Some studies 
solely include trust in representative institutions as indicators, while 
trust in the civil service and the army are only rarely considered. In 
addition, often, only three or four indicators are included, thus pre­
venting the empirical investigation of two- or multi-factorial structures 
(e.g., Ariely, 2015; Hooghe & Kern, 2015; Marien, 2011). However, 
importantly, when studies incorporated at least five indicators of trust in 
both order and representative institutions, and investigated the under­
lying factorial structure using EFA or CFA instead of PCA – which is 
considered a more pragmatic approach for dimension reduction – the 
results more often corroborated a multi-dimensional instead of a 
one-dimensional structure of political trust (e.g., Breustedt, 2018;

Scholars predominantly validate their measurement of political trust in

In conclusion, the dimensionality of citizens’ construct of political 
trust has been subject to both theoretical and empirical debate, though 
evidence among adolescent populations lacks. Building upon the 
aforementioned evidence, we examine two competing theory-driven 
dimensional models of political trust.1 The first model describes a one- 
dimensional model of political trust. The second model proposes a 
distinction between trust in order institutions and trust in representative 
institutions. Underpinned by both theoretical and empirical rationales, 
we hypothesize that adolescents have a two-dimensional as opposed to a 
one-dimensional conceptualisation of political trust. First, a two- 
dimensional structure of political trust adheres to the relational nature 
of political trust which depicts political trust as an object-specific 
judgment. Second, the substantial differences between political objects 
such as the government, political parties, and the justice system (and the 
branches they represent) are evident and have been extensively dis­
cussed and build upon in various theoretical works (Rothstein & Stolle, 
2008; Warren, 2018). Finally, in recent empirical investigations, a 
multi-dimensional structure of political trust has garnered increasing 
support, especially when the chosen indicators allowed for such an 
exploration and when theoretically grounded factor analytical models 
were employed. 
Hypothesis 1.
Adolescents have a two-dimensional conceptualisation 
of political trust, distinguishing between trust in order institutions and 
trust in representative institutions, as opposed to a one-dimensional 
conceptualisation. 
3. The cross-country measurement equivalence of political trust 
Besides the ongoing debate on dimensionality, scholars have also 
questioned whether measures of political trust tap into comparable 
meanings across time, countries, or subgroups of citizens (Breustedt, 
2018; Marien, 2017; Schneider, 2017; Turper & Aarts, 2017; van der 
Meer & Ouattara, 2019). This issue of comparability stems from the 
latent nature of political trust, meaning that this attitude cannot be 
observed or measured directly. As a result, political trust is typically 
operationalized through a set of survey items that yield observed esti­
mates of trust in political objects. These estimates are subsequently 
summed or averaged to create a general political trust score, or turned 
into latent factor scores using factor analysis. However, subsequent 
cross-country comparisons of these factor scores may be complicated 
and become rather meaningless due to the influence of several sources of 
bias (i.e., construct, method, or item bias) that unintentionally increase 
or decrease variations between countries (Davidov et al., 2014). Hence, 
to ensure the cross-country comparability of political trust measure­
ments, it is not only necessary to implement preventive measures during 
questionnaire development and data collection to mitigate the impact of 
biases, but also to empirically verify the assumption of ME/I during data 
analysis. 
Achieving cross-country ME/I implies that respondents from 
different countries who share the same level of trust in state institutions 
will provide similar responses to the presented survey items. Various 
methods exist to investigate the ME/I of latent variables, with Multi- 
Group Confirmatory Factor Analysis (MGCFA) being the traditional 
and most commonly used technique (Brown, 2014; Millsap, 2011). This 
approach assesses the equivalence of empirical relations between the 
latent factor(s) and their respective indicators across groups by imposing 
increasing cross-group equality constraints on measurement parameters, 
and assessing both overall as well as relative model fit compared to less 
restricted models. As this method demands a detailed specification of 
the measurement model beforehand, it is often regarded as one of the 
most rigorous tests of ME/I in the context of latent variable models 
(Svetina et al., 2020). In general, three different hierarchical levels of 
invariance are distinguished: configural, metric, and scalar invariance. 
As higher levels of equivalence impose stricter equality constraints, they 
are more difficult to achieve; however, they also guarantee more 
extended forms of cross-group comparisons (Davidov et al., 2014). First, 
configural invariance is tested by assessing whether the latent factors 
and their corresponding indicators are the same across groups (i.e., 
countries). Achieving this level implies the absence of construct bias, 
indicating that across countries, respondents rate political trust in­
dicators with the same construct in mind. Next, metric invariance is 
evaluated by constraining factor loadings to be equal across groups. 
Achieving this level signals that the measurement scale’s intervals for 
the latent factor are equal across countries, thus facilitating meaningful 
interpretation of the relationship between the latent construct and other 
relevant constructs across countries. Lastly, scalar invariance is tested by 
imposing cross-group equality constraints on factor loadings and item 
intercepts – or item thresholds in the ordinal-categorical approach. 
Reaching this level proves that the measurement scale has both equal 
intervals and origins, ensuring valid comparisons of latent means across 
countries. 
In previous empirical studies on cross-national ME/I of political trust 
among adult populations and using MGCFA, the strongest level of ME/I 
(i.e., full scalar invariance) has not yet been achieved. Following the 
review by Breustedt (2018), the achieved levels of invariance for a 
two-dimensional model of trust in representative and order institutions 
were either configural or partial metric (i.e., the equivalence of some but 
not all factor loadings). The majority of reviewed studies, however, 
employed a unidimensional baseline model, with one reaching partial 
scalar invariance, and others reaching metric invariance. These findings 
suggest that, at best, items measuring trust in various political objects 
relate similarly to an underlying construct (i.e., political trust), though 
this does not justify comparing country means of political trust. Among 
younger populations, these investigations are rare, except for the ones 
conducted during scaling procedures for ICCS 2016 (Schulz, Carstens, 
Losito, & Fraillon, 2018). Given that ‘Trust in Civic Institutions’ was 
included within a two-factorial model, correlated with attitudes of na­
tional belonging and pride as a second latent factor, this complicates the 
interpretation of the scale’s comparability results, as reported in the 
ICCS 2016 technical report (Schulz, Carstens, Losito, & Fraillon, 2018, p. 
171). Nevertheless, the report, assuming a two-dimensional model of 
political trust and national pride, highlights the lack of scalar invariance 
across the 24 participating educational systems, therefore voicing con­
cerns about the comparison of country means derived from both scales. 
It needs to be noted, however, that ICCS 2016 employed more lenient 
model fit criteria than the ones suggested by recent simulation studies 
for comparative research involving multi-dimensional measurement 
models, ordinal indicators and numerous groups (Rutkowski & Svetina, 
2014, 2017; Svetina et al., 2020). Specifically, the researchers deemed 
model fit unacceptable when RMSEA values exceeded 0.100, and did not 
consider relative measures (Schulz & Ainley, et al., 2018, p. 143). Given 
the recommended RMSEA cut-off of 0.055, the 0.100 threshold runs the 
risk of retaining misspecified models. As both the configural and metric

Notably, MGCFA has recently faced criticism for being too strict (i.e., 
demanding that measurement parameters are exactly the same across 
groups) and therefore difficult to reach in ILSAs (Van De Schoot et al., 
2015). Also aiming for partial ME/I (i.e., allowing some non-invariant 
parameters) is considered cumbersome and impractical, as adopting a 
stepwise approach in freely estimating one or many parameters based on 
the largest modification indices demands the estimation of many 
possible models (Byrne & van de Vijver, 2017; Marsh et al., 2018). In 
ILSAs, this could mean that acceptable fit is only achieved after hun­
dreds or even thousands adjustments. Moreover, the subjectivity of this 
process (i.e., deciding which parameters to freely estimate and when 
changes in the measurement model and the resulting model fit are suf­
ficient) can lead to arbitrary decisions and overfitting the model which 
could increase the risk of capitalizing on chance findings. To address 
these limitations, Asparouhov and Muth´en (2014, p. 505) introduced a 
new technique known as Alignment Optimization, which ‘automates 
and greatly simplifies’ the investigation of the most critical parameters 
causing non-invariance, and iteratively adjusts these parameters to 
non-invariance while maintaining adequate model fit. As this method 
estimates latent means and variances while tolerating minor (i.e., close 
enough to zero) deviations between groups, it is coined an ‘approximate’ 
ME/I approach. The procedure starts from the configural model (i.e., 
M0), freely estimating all factor loadings and intercepts, and fixing 
factor means and variances at zero and one, respectively. In a second 
step, provided that configural invariance is established, a simplicity 
function similar to rotation criteria in EFA is applied. This method fa­
cilitates the free estimation and comparison of factor means and vari­
ances without constraining factor loadings and intercepts across groups. 
It allows for a certain amount of non-invariant parameters (i.e., having a 
few large and many approximately non-invariant measurement param­
eters rather than many medium-sized ones) whilst preserving the model 
fit of the configural model. In short, ‘it identifies the solution with the 
least measurement noninvariance, and with the best possible fit, among 
all possible multiple-group factor analysis models’ (Munck et al., 2018, 
p. 695). Invariant groups are those for which the measurement param­
eter is not statistically different from the mean value of the invariant 
groups. The decision, then, to compare latent means is based on the 
proportion of non-invariant parameters, with a threshold of 25% 
(Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2014) – though its arbitrariness and other 
shortcomings have been noted and warrant further research attention 
(Kim et al., 2017; Svetina et al., 2020). To this day, only a few studies 
among adolescents have successfully applied this approach to 
non-cognitive measures in student ILSAs such as the IEA CIVED, ICCS 
and TIMMS studies (Munck et al., 2018; Wurster, 2022). To our 
knowledge, the study of Coromina and Peral (2020) is the only one that 
compares the MGCFA approach and the alignment method for the 
analysis of political trust. Using a unidimensional measurement of po­
litical trust captured by trust in the parliament, the legal system and 
politicians, the authors establish partial scalar invariance and show an 
average of 31.9% of non-invariant parameters using alignment. The 
present study will be the first in comparing both approaches to the 
two-dimensional measurement of political trust, focussing on 
adolescents. 
In sum, to date, substantial steps have been made to assess the cross- 
national comparability of political trust measures in adults, and to a 
lesser extent in adolescents. However a definite consensus remains 
elusive due to variations in baseline models, underlying items, data, and 
estimation techniques. Whereas MGCFA has been the traditional method 
to investigate ME/I of political trust, the newer alignment approach has 
proven its relevance in (only) one recent study (Coromina & Peral, 
2020). While MGCFA operates under the assumption that exact ME/I is 
imperative for valid comparisons, and that even minor deviations across 
groups can lead to the rejection of ME/I; the main assumption of 
alignment lies in allowing for approximate ME/I, meaning that some 
degree of non-invariance might be acceptable and even unavoidable in 
cross-cultural research. Though both techniques, thus, operate under 
different assumptions and different criteria for model evaluation, re­
searchers can use them in tandem in their pursuit for meaningful com­
parisons. The present study marks a first step in this direction. Based on 
the aforementioned evidence, we anticipate achieving at least configural 
invariance of a two-dimensional model of trust in order and represen­
tative institutions among European adolescents. We have no prior ex­
pectations regarding the total degree of non-invariance presented by the 
alignment method. 
Hypothesis 2.
Adolescents across Europe have an equivalent two- 
dimensional structure of political trust (i.e., configural invariance). 
4. Data and Methods 
4.1. Data 
The present study investigates the dimensionality and ME/I of po­
litical trust among adolescents living in European electoral and liberal 
democracies. We draw on data from ICCS 2016, which was organised by 
the IEA and surveyed approximately 94,000 eight-grade students across 
24 countries from Asia, Europe and South-America (Schulz, Ainley, 
et al., 2018). The data were gathered using a stratified two-stage prob­
ability sample design. In the first stage, schools were sampled with a 
probability proportional to their size. In the second stage, one class of 
the target (eight) grade was randomly selected, with all students in that 
class participating in the study. Based on the Democracy Index 2020 
(The Economist Intelligence Unit, 2021), we selected 15 countries or 
country regions within Europe that share a similar democratic tradition: 
Belgium (Flanders), Bulgaria, Croatia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Ger­
many (North Rhine-Westphalia), Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, the 
Netherlands, Norway, Slovenia, and Sweden. In total, 52,788 students, 
representative of the countries’ eight-grade student population (on 
average, 14 years old) were included. The number of cases with missing 
data on all variables was 1014 (1.92%). 
4.2. Measures 
To measure political trust, respondents were asked to what degree 
they trust the following six institutions: the national parliament, polit­
ical parties, the government, the police, the armed forces, and the courts 
of justice. The former three institutions are considered representative 
institutions; the latter three are considered order institutions. Items are 
rated on a four-point Likert scale: ‘Not at all’ (= 1), ‘A little’ (= 2), ‘Quite 
a lot’ (= 3), and ‘Completely’ (= 4). Descriptive statistics by country are 
shown in Table 1. Descriptive analyses provided no indication of ex­
tremity scoring. 
4.3. Methods 
The analysis was conducted in two stages. In a first step, we aimed to 
determine the validity of two competing theoretical conceptualizations 
of political trust in each of the 15 countries. More specifically, we 
applied CFA (Brown, 2014) to assess and compare the model fit of two 
measurement models for each country separately. The first measure­
ment model (M1) describes political trust as a one-dimensional 
construct, defined by all six indicators. The second model (M2) de­
scribes political trust as a two-dimensional construct, reflecting two 
distinct but strongly correlated factors, i.e., trust in order institutions 
(ξ1) (the police, the armed forces, and the courts of justice) and trust in 
representative institutions (ξ2) (the national parliament, political 
parties, and the government). Unlike in the case of continuous (i.e., 
multivariate normal) data, CFA for ordered-categorical data assumes

As latent factors are unobserved, their metric scale was defined using 
the reference indicator method, which fixes one factor loading per latent 
factor to one in all countries, thus assuming its invariance (Byrne, 2012). 
The appropriate reference indicators were selected using EFA per 
country. Comparative analyses alternating all possible reference in­
dicators showed that the analysis was not sensitive to the selection of 
these particular reference indicators. To evaluate model fit, the 
following overall goodness- (or badness-) of-fit indices (relevant for the 
categorical CFA approach with large sample sizes) were used: the root 
means square error of approximation (RMSEA), the comparative fit 
index (CFI), and the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) (Brown, 2014). The 
applied criteria for acceptable (i.e., marginally satisfactory) model fit 
were: RMSEA ≤0.080, and CFI and TLI ≥0.900. The applied criteria for 
good model fit were: RMSEA ≤0.060, and CFI and TLI ≥0.950 (Brown, 
2014; Wang & Wang, 2012). We also report the chi-square (χ2) statistic 
as a classic goodness-of-fit measure, although it is known to be sensitive 
to sample size (Davidov et al., 2014). We evaluated local areas of ill fit as 
input for model revision by looking at modification indices (MI) and the 
accompanying standardized expected parameter change (EPC) values. 
In a second step, we tested whether the best-fitting measurement 
model reached appropriate levels of ME/I across all 15 countries. Given 
the ordinal nature of the data, the scores of the trust indicators (i.e., item 
probability curves) are jointly influenced by the factor loadings, in­
tercepts and indicator thresholds. Hence, the invariance of factor 
loadings alone (i.e., metric invariance) does not provide adequate 
justification for comparing structural associations of the latent variables 
across groups (Bowen & Masa, 2015; Davidov et al., 2018; Muth´en & 
Asparouhov, 2002). Therefore, only two levels of ME/I were estimated:

5. Results 
5.1. Country‑specific CFA and MGCFA 
We first estimated and compared two CFA models for political trust 
in each country: a one-dimensional described by all six indicators (M1) 
versus a two-dimensional (i.e., first-order correlated two-factor model) 
distinguishing between trust in order and representative institutions 
(M2). Factor loadings and model fit indices for the 15 country-specific 
CFA models are presented in Appendix A. While trust in the army 
shows the lowest factor loadings, trust in the courts and trust in 
parliament exhibit the highest. M1 shows inadequate fit in all countries, 
whereas M2 shows an adequate fit in seven countries. We observe 
moderate standardized correlations between both dimensions (ranging 
from 0.62 to 0.85). In the eight remaining countries, neither solution 
provides an acceptable fit to the data.7 After examining sources of model 
misspecification for the best-fitting model (i.e., M2), we establish good 
or acceptable model fit in all countries by adding two measurement 
error-correlations (M3) (see Appendix A): one between trust in the po­
lice and trust in the armed forces, and another between trust in the 
national parliament and trust in political parties.8 Substantively, both 
sets of error-correlations are justifiable. On the one hand, in democratic 
systems, the police and the armed forces are both defined by their mo­
nopoly on the legitimate use of force to maintain order, which might 
translate in some unique shared variance between both trust items that 
is not shared with trust in the justice system. Second, all political parties 
are primary subjects in parliament, whereas the government consists of 
a selection of parties (i.e., the winning coalition). Moreover, whereas

CFI=0.997; TLI=0.993). This means that the same pattern of factors and 
indicators is found across all 15 European countries. As expected, model 
fit worsened in the scalar invariance test: even though CFI and TLI fit 
indices were below the thresholds, this model was rejected based on 
unsatisfactory RMSEA, ΔRMSEA and ΔCFI values (RMSEA=0.074; 
CFI=0.975; TLI=0.981; ΔRMSEA=0.029; ΔCFI = −0.020). This means 
that the assumption of equal factor loadings and thresholds does not 
hold. Estonia, Italy, Norway, and Sweden show the highest chi-square 
contribution (above 500). The lowest contributions come from Malta, 
Belgium, Germany, and the Netherlands (below 200). Examination of 
the 22 MIs for measurement parameters exceeding 100 (Appendix B) 
reveals that five countries (i.e., Belgium, Latvia, Malta, the Netherlands, 
and Germany) show no chi-square contributions above 100 for factor 
loadings or indicator thresholds. Only two countries (i.e., Estonia and 
Norway) show chi-square contributions above 100 for factor loadings. 
Further, a total of 19 thresholds exceed the 100 chi-square contribution 
limit: six countries (i.e., Bulgaria, Croatia, Denmark, Finland, Slovenia, 
and Sweden) have one chi-square contribution above 100, Estonia two, 
Lithuania three, and Norway and Italy each have four. Across all in­
dicators, particularly the third threshold (13 out of 19) – followed by the 
second (6 out of 19) – shows the most signs of non-invariance. This 
means that the point (i.e., the level of the latent variable) where students 
transition from reporting to trust ‘quite a lot’ to trust ‘completely’ varies 
most prominently across countries. Finally, MIs show that the most non- 
invariant item is trust in the national parliament (5 of 22 MI’s above 
100, among which one for a factor loading), whereas the most invariant 
item is trust in the police with only one MI above 100. 
MGCFA results among five sub-groups of countries are presented in  
Table 3 and point to the achievement of scalar invariance within the 
Western-European cluster. In the remaining clusters only configural 
invariance is reached due to RMSEA, ΔRMSEA, and ΔCFI measures 
exceeding – though mostly marginally – the cut-off criteria in the scalar 
invariance tests. 
5.2. Alignment optimization 
As an alternative to MGCFA, we searched for approximate ME/I by 
means of the Alignment Method (Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2014). Table 4 
shows the approximate measurement (non-) invariance results for the 
factor loadings and intercepts. Values in parentheses represent 
non-invariant parameters. Given 6 indicators and 15 groups, 90 factor 
loadings and 90 intercepts in total were evaluated. Our finding of 17 
non-invariant factor loadings (18.88%) and 12 non-invariant intercepts 
(13.33%) leads to an overall average of 16.11% non-invariance. As this 
non-invariance proportion is lower than the recommended 25% 
threshold (Muth´en & Asparouhov, 2014), the factor mean estimates and 
their comparison across all studied countries is considered reliable. 
Some other findings are noteworthy. First, in line with MGCFA results, 
Norway and Estonia encounter the highest number of non-invariant 
parameters (i.e., respectively 50% and 41%). Estonia even has five of 
its loadings reported as non-invariant. Eight countries have fully 
invariant intercepts, five countries have fully invariant loadings, and 
Croatia is the only country with zero non-invariant parameters. Trust in 
the courts of justice and trust in the police are the only indicators with 
fully invariant intercepts, whereas trust in the parliament has the most 
non-invariant intercepts, and trust in the army has the most 
non-invariant loadings (i.e., each in five groups). Table 4 also shows 
each item’s intercept and loading contribution to the optimized 
simplicity function and R2 value. The latter is commonly interpreted as 
the amount of variation in the parameter explained by the variation in 
factor means and variances, with values close to 1 being associated with 
invariance and values closer to 0 with non-invariance. Though we report 
R2 values, a recent study cautioned against its use as a criterion for 
parameter invariance due to its frequent deviation from the general rule 
of thumb in practical applications (Asparouhov & Muth´en, 2023). 
Therefore, looking at each item’s total contribution to the fit function, 
Table 4 shows that trust in the police has the lowest total contribution 
(−71.341) and hence, in line with the MGCFA results, shows the lowest 
amount of non-invariance. Trust in the army, on the other hand, has the 
highest total contribution (−103.243) and thus shows the highest 
amount of non-invariance. 
In a next step, we compare the factor means from the alignment 
model with the factor means from the scalar invariance model (Ap­
pendix C). The correlation between the two models is 0.989 and 0.994 
for trust in order and representative institutions, respectively. Further 
comparison of country ranking reveals that six countries (40.00%) 
preserve their ranking on trust in order institutions, eleven countries 
(73.33%) preserve their ranking on trust in representative institutions, 
and those countries that do not preserve their ranking change only 
slightly. Looking at the ranking of alignment scores (Figs. 2 and 3), 
primarily Nordic countries preceded by Germany score the highest on 
both types of trust, whereas central/Eastern European and Post-Soviet 
countries generally score the lowest. Notably, Sweden is the one coun­
try that ranks relatively high on trust in representative institutions (i.e., 
3rd), yet low on trust in order institutions (i.e., 11th). The opposite 
applies to Slovenia, which ranks in the middle regarding trust in order 
institutions (i.e., 6th) and low on trust in representative institutions (i.e., 
14th). These results also prove the relevance of distinguishing between 
both dimensions of political trust. 
6. Discussion 
Given the growing research and policy interest in adolescents’ civic 
and citizenship competencies across Europe, investigations into whether 
cross-national comparisons of these outcomes are truly meaningful are 
necessary (Blask´o et al., 2019; Malak-Minkiewicz & Torney-Purta, 
2021). The aim of this study was to investigate the dimensionality of 
political trust, a non-cognitive civic outcome, and assess its ME/I across 
15 European countries using ICCS 2016 data.

Our analyses establish two key findings. First, confirming Hypothesis 
1, country-specific CFA results show that adolescents in several de­
mocracies across Europe have a two-dimensional conceptualisation of 
political trust, differentiating between trust in order institutions and 
trust in representative institutions. Methodologically, this finding has 
two important consequences. First, as a unidimensional measurement 
model shows inadequate fit in all countries, these results caution against 
the use of a unidimensional scale of political trust when using the ICCS 
2016 European sample. Secondly, this underscores the importance of 
thoughtfully selecting political trust items to fully cover the con­
ceptualisation of political trust. We argue that future studies include 
items measuring trust in order institutions as well as trust in represen­
tative institutions, and thoroughly examine the factorial structure using 
more theoretically grounded CFA models. When possible, this implies 
the inclusion of at least two trust indicators for each branch of in­
stitutions, followed by a validation of a two-factor model compared to a 
one-factor model, as was done in the present study. In addition, this 
finding also bears several theoretical and conceptual implications. First, 
following the literature review by Breustedt (2018), it was found that 
the choice and combination of political trust indicators varies consid­
erably across studies, though the majority of research uses the same 
terms, i.e., ‘political trust’ or ‘institutional trust’. In other words, 
although there is a common conceptualisation and use of terminology, 
there seems to be no common operationalisation of political trust with 
regard to the chosen political trustees. As a result, we argue that re­
searchers should opt for terminology that is best in line with their 
operational choices, thus ensuring more conceptual clarity. For 
example, when solely representative institutions are included as politi­
cal trustees, we argue that the term ‘trust in representative institutions’ 
is more precise. Second, our findings provide support for theoretical 
perspectives distinguishing between different dimensions of political 
trust (Easton, 1975; Rothstein & Stolle, 2008; Warren, 2018), and pro­
vide additional motives for future research theorising on and empirically 
identifying the origins and consequences of each dimension. 
Our second key finding stems from ME/I tests and underlines that full 
ME/I of political trust among adolescents across different European 
countries is not guaranteed. More specifically, in line with our second 
hypothesis and previous research involving adults (Breustedt, 2018), 
our results point towards the achievement of configural invariance of a 
two-dimensional model. The highest level of invariance, scalar invari­
ance, was not reached. Hence, European adolescents share a common 
conceptualization of political trust, yet do not seem to use the response

linguistic similarities. In contrast, the exploration of approximate ME/I 
using Alignment Optimization did produce reliable estimates of factor 
means with an overall 16.11% degree of non-invariance. This method 
therefore enables substantive cross-group comparisons of latent means 
and associations. Nonetheless, as it also tolerates a certain proportion of 
non-invariance, the theoretical validity of comparisons might be 
compromised (Svetina et al., 2020). The results show that both methods 
yield different conclusions. Indeed, in essence, a choice between exact 
and approximate ME/I approaches presents a trade-off between theory 
and data. Therefore, more research is needed in order to develop 
guidelines for choosing the appropriate estimation method for one’s 
research goals. For example, Asparouhov and Muth´en (2014) emphasize 
that both methods can complement each other: given that alignment 
optimization provides a detailed account of parameter invariance in 
each group, researchers can use this information to modify the mea­
surement model or studied groups in order to achieve partial (metric or 
scalar) invariance, though always considering the substantial validity of 
adjustments. 
Our study, while informative, does have certain limitations that open 
up avenues for future research. First, as we rely on ICCS 2016 data and 
only include European countries or country regions in our sample, our 
findings are valid solely to this selection of data and items. It remains to 
be examined whether a two-dimensional construct of political trust 
among adolescents, reaching configural invariance across different Eu­
ropean countries, holds when a larger and more diverse set of countries 
is examined. This is particularly pertinent when considering countries 
with authoritarian traditions, where political trust is argued to be less 
object-specific and even less domain-specific (i.e., distinguishable from 
other forms of trust, such as social trust), as these societies often exhibit 
generalized distrust due to authoritarian legacies and the persistence of 
institutional dysfunction (Letki, 2017; Rivetti & Cavatorta, 2017; Rose & 
Mishler, 2011). Second, while this study opted for alignment optimi­
zation as an alternative approach to testing for ME/I with MGCFA, 
future studies might explore other factor analytical methodologies, such 
as multilevel CFA, multilevel factor mixture modelling, and Bayesian 
approximate ME/I testing. Each of these ME/I methods, whether strict

or approximate, offer distinct advantages but also come with specific 
limitations that require consideration (Kim et al., 2017; Svetina et al., 
2020; van Dijk et al., 2022). Third, while this study applied a 
variable-centred approach to assess the dimensionality and compara­
bility of political trust, person-centred approaches can be equally 
informative tools but are applied fewer in this field of research (Millsap, 
2011). Given the categorical nature of the used indicators, future studies 
could consider employing Latent-Class Analysis (LCA) to distinguish 
between different latent classes of individuals based on their patterns of 
responses (e.g., high or low in both dimensions, high in one dimension 
but low in the other). As such, LCA might offer a more nuanced repre­
sentation of the concept of political trust. Lastly, guidelines for the 
evaluation of model fit during ME/I testing are continuously evolving 
(Svetina et al., 2020). While this study followed recent model fit 
guidelines (Brown, 2014; Svetina & Rutkowski, 2017), thereby taking 
into account several characteristics of the data (e.g., ordered-categorical 
nature, two-dimensional construct, large number of countries, full 
sample versus country clusters, and large sample sizes), it is worth 
acknowledging that such measures might not perform optimally under 
typical ILSA circumstances, given they were originally developed and 
tested in different settings (e.g., normal distributed data, single con­
structs, relatively few groups, and small sample sizes; Svetina & Rut­
kowski, 2017). Moreover, as we also found no clear guidelines on the use 
of relative fit indices in the context of ME/I testing with ordinal in­
dicators, these observations beg for continued efforts to develop 
improved fit measures and guidelines. 
Despite the words of caution, our findings contribute to the body of 
research in two significant ways. First, we establish that 14-year-old 
adolescents across Europe distinguish between different dimensions of 
political trust and thus demonstrate that a certain level of political 
sophistication concerning citizen-state relationships already exists at a 
young age. Second, we show that exact ME/I of political trust measures 
across a large set of different European contexts is not achieved and, 
therefore, should not be assumed. Future studies investigating political 
trust or other latent civic outcomes across a large number of countries 
can consider two pragmatic avenues that still allow for meaningful 
comparisons. One option is to compare countries within theoretically 
substantiated clusters, where the necessary level of exact ME/I is ach­
ieved, nevertheless running the risk of disregarding countries that are 
particularly interesting due to cultural differences. The second option 
involves using approximate ME/I methods, such as Alignment Optimi­
zation, which permit some degree of non-invariance, nevertheless un­
derstanding the implications of such explorative approach for theory 
validation.

Research at KU Leuven, Belgium. Her research interests lie in the areas of political so­
cialization and political psychology. Specifically, she studies political trust, knowledge and 
behaviour among adolescents. 
Maria Magdalena Isac is a researcher at the Centre for Political Science Research at KU 
Leuven, Belgium. Magda’s research is focused in the area of comparative evaluation of 
educational systems, with special emphasis on understanding how different formal and 
informal educational approaches contribute to young people’s citizenship learning. 
Ellen Claes is an associate professor at the faculty of Social Sciences of KU Leuven, 
Belgium. In her work, Ellen takes a didactic perspective on political science exploring the 
roles secondary schools have in shaping democratic knowledge, skills and attitudes of 
young people. Recent studies focus on the civic and intercultural competencies of (student) 
teachers.
