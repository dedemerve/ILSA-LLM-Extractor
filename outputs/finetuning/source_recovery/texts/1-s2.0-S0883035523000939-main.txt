Peer Effects in Education: Theoretical Synthesis of Frame Factor
Theory and Opportunity to Learn using TIMSS 2011
Pontus Bäckström *
Jönköping University, School of Education and Communication, Sweden

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
frame factor theory
opportunity to learn
compositional effects
peer effects

Despite a vast literature, previous research into compositional effects and peer effects in education has remarked on the lack of consensus within the field. To date, peer effects in education and
students’ different opportunities to learn in school has been studied separately. In this study, it is
argued that these perspectives need to be synthesized. A multilevel structural equation model (MSEM) is derived from such a synthesis of the Frame Factor Theory (FFT) and Opportunity to Learn
(OTL) and tested on Swedish TIMSS 2011-data (n=3928). The results indicate that class
composition affects the presence of limitations on instruction and advanced content coverage,
both of which is related to opportunities to learn and individual students’ achievement in
mathematics.

1. Introduction
It is a well-known fact that students perform differently in school. It is equally well known that it matters which school a student
attend and which classmates they have (Coleman, et al., 1966; Beckerman, & Good, 1981; Dumay & Dupriez, 2007; Rolfe, Yang
Hansen, & Streitholt, 2022). The impact of which school a student attend differ between countries (Rolfe, Strietholt, & Yang Hansen,
2021), but on average about 20 percent of the variance in achievement can be attributed to which school a student attend (Opdenakker
& Damme, 2006; Dumay & Dupriez, 2007).
To understand why these differences occur, and why the effects differ between different school-systems, is of great importance to
educational policy. If we want to pursue higher level of educational equity, then we need knowledge about the mechanisms generating
educational inequality. In their seminal work, Coleman et al (1966) investigated the equality of educational opportunity within the US.
Alongside evidence on poorer teacher quality in minority schools, one main finding was that pupils’ achievement was related to the
educational backgrounds and aspirations of the other students in school. Contemporary US-studies have found similar results. In a
study of US TIMSS 2011-data, Morton & Riegle-Crumb (2020) found that teachers in predominantly black schools reported significant
lower levels of time spent on advanced mathematical content, than teachers in non-predominantly minority schools did. In another
study, Saw & Agger (2021) found that students in rural areas where offered limited access to advanced STEM-courses and a lower
STEM teaching capacity, which to a large extent was explained by students’ demographics and career aspirations. These results indicates that students in these different schools get different opportunities to learn, which most likely can explain some of the
achievement gap between students (Schmidt, Siwen, & Houang, 2021).

Given this, Opportunity to learn (OTL) has become an important perspective when studying educational inequality (Schmidt,
Burroughs, Zoido, & Houang, 2015). Another equally important theoretical perspective concerns peer effects in education. Within this
field, researchers are concerned with the effect of having different peers on individual students’ achievement (Epple & Romano, 2011;
Sacerdote, 2011).
In a previous study, Bäckström (2022) applied the Frame Factor Theory (FFT) to the issue of peer effects in education. The hypothesis for this was that the FFT offers a theoretical explanation for both these phenomena, i.e. OTL-differences between classrooms
and peer effects on achievement, thereby creating a synthesized theoretical understanding of the two. The main argument was that
class composition, understood as class average aptitude and prior knowledge, will steer and limit what the teacher can do in instruction
and thereby affect individual students’ achievement (Lundgren, 1972). The study indicated that one mechanism generating peer effects in education was the steering and limiting effect class composition of peers has upon teachers’ instruction. In more high-achieving
classrooms, the steering and limiting effects are smaller, thereby presumably allowing the teacher to focus on even more advanced
content which further enhance student achievement. However, the study could not verify the modus operandi of these peer effects.
Bäckström (2022) proposed that this might be due to poor construct validity of the content related variables in the model.
These empirical findings highlight the need of a further theoretical synthesis between OTL and the FFT. A synthesis of such would
also bridge the distance between research into educational inequality, educational opportunities, and peer effects in education. In this
study, such a synthesis is initiated. The aim is to further develop the theoretical framework used by Bäckström (2022) and to test a
revised model on peer effects in Swedish schools.
1.1. Previous research, Theories and Models of Compositional Effects and Peer Effects in Education
A pioneer in the research field of equality and educational opportunity was the Coleman-report (1966) (Zimmerman, 2003). Since
then, a myriad of studies has reported similar results on these peer group effects. For instance, Szulkin & Jonsson (2007) reported that
if a lower-secondary school class in Sweden had more than 40 percent foreign born students, the results for the same group decreased
by an average of .20 standard deviations. Similar, Brunello & Rocco (2013) studied 19 countries participating in PISA and found that if
the share of immigrants in secondary schools was to double, the native students’ results would decrease by 1.0-3.4 percent. Similar
results can be found in Betts (1998), Jensen & Würtz Rasmussen (2011) and Chetty, Hendren, & Katz (2016).
Studying the effect of disruptive classroom behaviour in Israeli schools, Lavy & Schlosser (2011) concluded that a larger share of
girls in class was related to positive peer effects for all students. The effect was mediated through less violence, improved relations, and
less teachers’ fatigue. In another study, Lavy, Paserman & Schlosser (2011) showed that an increase of the number of pupils who have
been retained for one year in class, was associated with a decrease of the new classmates’ achievement. The effect was mediated by
detrimental effects on teachers’ pedagogical practices, increased violence, and classroom disruptions. Similar results can be found in
Bertrand & Pan (2013), Grøne Kristoffersen et al. (2015) and Bäckström (2021).
Some researchers however, claimed that a model of compositional and/or peer effects must capture the more complex mechanisms
within classrooms (Dumay & Dupriez, 2007). One such example is found in Bourke’s (1986) study of the relations between class size,
teacher practices and student achievement, but a more comprehensive model of such was developed by Thrupp (1999) in his “School
Compositional Hypothesis” (SCH).
With the SCH-model, Thrupp contested the up until then very influential “school effectiveness”-research. One of the main arguments of Thrupp was that the SER-research had a socially decontextualized view on school quality (Dumay & Dupriez, 2007). Thrupp
instead argued that school intake, school mix and compositional effects must be seen as parts of the whole school’s functioning. The
essence of the SCH-model is that compositional effects mainly operate through a nested series of hierarchical layers, where school-,
class- and group compositional effects are intertwined, all affecting each other, and in the end also affecting students’ achievement.
Besides the qualitative evidence from Thrupp, the SCH has been confirmed in quantitative studies by both Opdenakker & Damme
(2006) and Dumay & Dupriez (2007). Similar results can be found in Weinert, Schrader, & Helmke (1989), Garrison (2004) and
Blatchford et al. (2007).
1.1.1. The Frame Factor Theory
At heart of Thrupp’s SCH-model is a notion introduced by Barr & Dreeben (1983), that the composition of the student body in class
affects teachers’ instruction. Thrupp refers to this as “the negotiated nature of teaching” (1999, p. 157). However, Hattie (2002)
challenged this viewpoint by arguing that the main line of argument in this strand of compositional research, is that different class
compositions and smaller class sizes would alter teachers’ practices in class – but they don’t according to Hattie. From this perspective,
Hattie argues that it is far more important to focus on providing high quality instruction; a bad teacher is bad no matter if he or she has
15 or 30 students. Hattie’s view is in turn contested by other empirical research, such as Blatchford, Edmonds, & Martin (2003),
Garrison (2004) and Schmidt et al. (2011). The results in Bäckström (2022) however, indicated that while Hatties’ argument – that
high-quality teaching can levitate individual students’ achievement – seem to be true on the within class level, the positive effect of
high-quality teaching seem to have a hard time delimiting the negative effects of class composition on the between class level.
Even though Barr & Dreeben (1983) often are credited for the introduction of this notion, there are others who preceded them. One
important contribution of such was the FFT, proposed by Dahllöf (1967) and Lundgren (1972). The FFT builds on a crucial concept
introduced by Carroll (1963), that every student – due to their prior knowledge and aptitude – will need a certain amount of time to be
able to learn a certain curricula unit. If the student isn’t provided with enough time, the student will not learn.
Carrolls’ model of school learning concerns how individuals learn in school. In the FFT, Dahllöf and Lundgren aggregate this
phenomenon to the class-level, theorizing how the instructional process will be affected from the teachers’ perspective (Lundgren,

1972; Barr & Dreeben, 1977). The notion of a class-aggregated steering and limiting effect on teachers’ instruction stems from a
re-analysis Dahllöf (1967) produced of a Swedish experiment in the 1950’s with non-streamed comprehensive compulsory schools
(Svensson, 1962). In the experiment, the capital Stockholm was divided into two parts, the north and the south. The schools in the
south acted as an experimental group and introduced non-streamed comprehensive schools. The north acted as a control group and
kept the streamed parallel schooling system. Analysing the results from the experiment, Svensson’s (1962) main result was that less
advantaged students in the south outperformed less advantaged students in streamed schools in the north whilst the results for
advantaged students seemed similar. Politically, this was interpreted as a go-ahead signal for the comprehensive school reform
(Dahllöf, 1967).
In his re-analysis of data from the experiment, Dahllöf (1967) added process data from the instruction within the schools during the
experiment. By doing so, he found two important novel results. Firstly, the analysis revealed that the achievement tests that had been
administered failed to capture all variation in students’ knowledge and skills. The tests were too elementary for several students,
especially for those from positively streamed control schools in the north. Secondly, he found that the mathematical content that had
taken three years of instruction to cover in the non-streamed classes in the south, had taken the positively streamed classes in the north
only one year to cover (Dahllöf, 1967; Lundgren, 1972; 1973). Considering the contrasting results from his re-analysis, Dahllöf wrote:
At a crucial point in their argumentation in the differentiation issue, the 1957 School Committee drew a conclusion, which can
be perceived and has also been interpreted as such, that homogenization through organizational differentiation disadvantages
the worst students without, for that reason, having any positive effects for the best. The pattern of results that can be deduced
from the Stockholm survey rather supports the opposite interpretation. (Dahllöf, 1967, p. 128, translated from Swedish by
author)
Dahllöf’s conclusions from this re-analysis, alongside the notion of Carroll’s model of school learning, led Dahllöf to the conclusion
that when studying the outcomes of schooling, researchers must include process variables of the instruction (Dahllöf, 1967). This was
formulated into a pedagogical theory of instruction, claiming that there are three major frame factors that will steer and limit (i.e frame)
teachers’ instruction in class. These are (i) students’ prior knowledge (operationalized as time needed to learn), (ii) the curricula (what
is to be learnt) and (iii) time at hand. If the time needed by class exceeds time at hand, then the class will not learn, and the teacher must
act in some way. The teacher’s possibilities include holding up the entire class, moving on, lowering demands or alter the content
covered (Lundgren, 1972; 1973). An original paradigm of the FFT is depicted in Fig. 1.
The FFT states that some of these steering and limiting effects on instruction can be predicted by estimating the level of the so called
“steering criterion group” (SCG), that is the students between the 10th and 25th percentile of aptitude distribution in class. The focal
point of this prediction is not the exact percentile (Arlin, 1984), but the notion that peers’ average ability will affect what the teacher is
able to do in class and individual students’ achievement (Lundgren, 1973). Studies have reported evidence on the prediction
(Lundgren, 1972). Also, as referred above, other studies have provided evidence of the same phenomena, even though it hasn’t been
theorised, nor described, in the same terminology (Bourke, 1986; Weinert, Schrader, & Helmke, 1989; Garrison, 2004).
1.1.2. Opportunity to Learn
As referred, fundamental findings in studies of both the SCH and the FFT are that the composition of the student body within classes
to some extent will affect teachers’ instructional practices, which affects students’ achievement (Lundgren, 1972; Bourke, 1986;
Dumay & Dupriez, 2007). These results highlight an important difference between the FFT and other contemporary theories on
learning, such as Blooms Mastery Learning (Bloom, 1974; 1984). Even though they share a common ground in Carrolls’ model of school
learning (1963), they differ in the sense that the FFT sees the teacher as being somewhat dependent of class composition whilst the
Mastery Learning claims that the teacher is (or should be) independent of class composition (Barr & Dreeben, 1977).
This distinction is of utmost importance for yet another theoretical perspective on students learning, namely Opportunity to learn

(OTL). As the name implies, OTL concerns students’ opportunities to learn the intended curricula. The research includes questions of
whether students are given the same access to and possibilities for learning. This is often operationalized as instructional time and
content provided (Frederick & Walberg, 1980; Porter, 2002). This means that OTL too share the common ground in Carroll’s (1963)
model of school learning with the FFT and Mastery Learning (Kurz, 2011; Elliot & Bartlett, 2016). But going beyond this, both OTL, the
FFT and Mastery Learning share the perspective that the time allocated for instruction also de facto must be used for the given purpose;
actual time spent on instruction almost always subside time allocated (Kurz, Elliott, Kettler, & Yel, 2014). This perspective has been
labelled as “time-on-task” (Anderson, 1981) and is central – pronounced or non-pronounced – for the named theoretical perspectives,
but also for research into endogenous peer effects such as disruptive behaviour (Lazear, 2001; Lavy & Schlosser, 2011). Recent
OTL-research has also demonstrated the relationship between disciplinary climate, OTL and student achievement (Wang, Liu, & Leung,
2022).
One of the first comprehensive OTL-frameworks was presented by Stevens (1993). According to Elliot & Bartlett (2016), there is a
lack of direct empirical evidence for Stevens’ framework, but since the framework of Stevens’ rests upon the crucial concepts of time
for instruction (Carroll, 1963), content coverage (Husén, 1967) and the quality of instruction (Weinert, Schrader, & Helmke, 1989),
they argue what the model is relevant for contemprorary research within the field.
In the US, the OTL-research is prominent, with perspectives dating back to the Coleman-report and with Stevens’ (1993) framework
still at play (Kurz, 2011; Elliot & Bartlett, 2016). Contemporary studies focus on the same issues as Coleman did, for instance regional
differences in provision of education (Saw & Agger, 2021), schoolings’ possibilities to perpetuate educational inequality (Schmidt,
Burroughs, Zoido, & Houang, 2015) but also differences in educational provision and opportunities between minority and majority
students.
In a recent study of the latter, Morton & Riegle-Crumb (2020) utilize US TIMSS 2011-data to study the relation between school
ethnical composition and content coverage in algebra. They found that the teacher-reported time spent on algebra and advanced 8th
grade content was lower in schools that were predominantly black, in comparison to those that were not. Under control for school
characteristics (including proxies for SES), teacher qualifications, and students’ academic performance, they found that in schools that
were predominantly black, teachers on average spent 72 percent of class time over the year covering algebra and advanced content,
while the corresponding share in non-predominantly minority schools was 82 percent.
Very often, as in the case of Morton & Riegle-Crumb (2020), these perspectives are linked to the discussion of equity in school
systems (Schmidt, Burroughs, Zoido, & Houang, 2015). A recent example from Sweden is a study by Klapp & Jönsson (2020). They
report findings that low-achieving students to a larger extent than other students perceive that they are provided with “simplifying
support” rather than “scaffolding support”, meaning that the teachers lower the standards and lower their expectations on them and
thereby limiting these students’ opportunities to learn. Significant for both Klapp & Jönsson (2020) and Morton & Riegle-Crumb
(2020) studies are that they, in reference to the theoretical differences between the FFT and Mastery Learning, where the former views
the teacher as somewhat dependent on class composition, never discuss why these results are found. The mechanisms generating the

1.1.3. The Need of a Theoretical Synthesis
The results from Morton & Riegle-Crumb, concerning differences in time spent on advanced content, highlight the need of a
theoretical (and methodological) synthesis of the OTL-perspective and the FFT when studying educational opportunity and peer effects
in education. The FFT predicts that there will be a difference in coverage of elementary and advanced content between high- and lowperforming classrooms; teachers in low-performing classrooms will have to spend more time on elementary parts of the curricula in
order to get the majority of the class to master the core elements (Lundgren, 1973). This means that the OTL-operationalization of
Morton & Riegle-Crumb (2020), but in essence the OTL-framework of Stevens (1993), could be incorporated into an FFT-model
predicting peer effects in education, effects mediated by the compositional effect on teachers’ instruction. While enhancing the precision of the FFT, the synthesis would at the same time offer a theoretical explanation to some of the empirical results reported in much
of the OTL-literature, for instance those of Morton & Riegle-Crumb (2020) and Klapp & Jönsson (2020).
It could be argued that synthesized models of such already exists. Thrupps’ SCH has been mentioned (Thrupp, 1999; Dumay &
Dupriez, 2007), there are educational productivity models such as that of Walberg et al (1982) and the “TIMSS Model of Potential
Educational Experiences” is a comprehensive model on educational delivery of school systems (Schmidt et al, 1997; Rolfe, Strietholt, &
Yang Hansen, 2021). The argument for this synthesis of OTL and FFT is mainly to merge the perspective of content delivery within OTL
with the class compositional steering and limiting perspective of the FFT, into one common theoretical perspective on peer effects in
classroom instruction, which can be modelled and empirically estimated. Such a synthesis would add precision for between class
analyses. The estimation done within this study should thus be viewed as a classroom model, subsidiary other system related general
models as that of Schmidt et al (1997).
In Fig. 2, the synthesised theoretical model is presented. The model illustrates the predicted relation between the frame factors
(Lundgren, 1972; 1973) and student achievement, but also how this is mediated trough an indirect effect via teaching practices (OTL)
(Stevens, 1993). Both the frame factors and the teaching practices are predicted to be related to class composition. Class composition is
also predicted to have a direct effect on achievement since all variation in class compositional effects on achievement is not expected to
be explained by the FFT and OTL-elements. For instance, Lundgren (1972) expected that as explanatory variables in an analysis of
student achievement, the steering and limiting effects would probably explain about 10-25 percent of the variation (p.40). In contrast
to this theoretical model, the final operationalized model is depicted in Fig. 4. This is presented after a discussion on the operationalization of measures for the study at hand.
1.2. Aim of the study
The aim of this study is to further develop the theoretical framework of the FFT, via a synthesis with OTL, so that it can be used as a
theory of peer effects in education. The synthesized model is illustrated in Fig. 2. The model predicts that the frame factors to some
extent will steer and limit teachers’ instructional practices in class, including the opportunities given for the students to learn, which in
turn will affect students’ achievement. The theory will be operationalized and tested empirically.

The gross sample contained test results and survey data for 5 816 students in 8th grade (on average 14 years old) and their 364
teachers in mathematics. The gross sample contained classes that proved to be outliers in class size. In Sweden, this is an indication that
these are special classes of some sort. Classes with less than 10 and more than 40 students were excluded. Also, classes that had more
than one math teacher where excluded. This was done because it would be impossible to decide which teachers’ assessment of the OTLvariables (content coverage and time spent during the year) that was to be used in the models. The net sample after these exclusions
was 3 928 students in 207 classes, representing 68 respectively 78 percent of the gross sample. Descriptive statistics of the gross and net
sample is reported in Table 1. Data was weighted using “house weights” on the between-class level (Rutkowski, Gonzalez, Joncas, &
von Davier, 2010).
2.2. Operationalization and Measures
In Fig. 2, the synthesized theoretical model was depicted. The different elements of the figure rests upon original descriptions of the
FFT (Lundgren, 1972) and the OTL (Stevens, 1993). These descriptions within the model do not correspond to actual variables or
constructs in TIMSS 2011. Therefore, in this section the operationalization of variables is discussed in reference to the theoretical
model. The final operationalized model is depicted in Fig. 4. Descriptive statistics for all variables in the modelling are presented in
Table 2.
2.2.1. Class composition
In a previous study (Bäckström, 2022), the FFT-prediction of the “steering criterion group”-hypothesis (SCG) was tested using data
from TIMSS 2015. This was associated with several problems of measurement since there aren´t any measures of prior knowledge in
TIMSS due to its cross-sectional design. Since the SCG was highly correlated to the average of different compositional variables in class,
such as student SES and immigrational background, this study will only utilize class compositional variables instead of a measure of the
SCG in each class.
Two class-aggregated variables of such were specified and used in two different versions of the model. The first was class-mean SES
measured by TIMSS’ index of home educational resources (BSDGHER) (Wiberg & Rolfsman, 2021), and the second was the class-mean
of students’ ages when coming to Sweden (BSBG09B).
2.2.2. Limitations on Instruction
The main FFT-prediction is that class composition to some extent will steer and limit the teachers’ instruction in class (Lundgren,
1972; 1973). In the TIMSS 2011 teacher survey, there are six different variables measuring such limitations. Two of these variables
proved to be associated with a vast number of missing cases, namely limitations due to lack of nutrition and lack of sleep. Possibly,
these are issues that are hard to assess for the teacher. Due to this, the four remaining variables were used to specify a latent variable of
“Limitations on Instruction” (LIMIT). These were:
In your view, to what extent do the following limit how you teach this class?
– Students lacking prerequisite knowledge or skills,
– Students with special needs,
– Disruptive students and
– Uninterested students.
The respondents answered in a Likert scale ranging from “not at all” (1) to “a lot” (3). The Cronbach’s Alpha was .72.
2.2.3. Instruction According to Students
A way of assessing the quality of instruction in class is via the students. As mentioned above, this has also been done within OTL7

research such as that of Klapp & Jönsson (2020). A latent variable of “Instruction According to the Students” was specified. The
variable was specified to capture different aspects of instruction that have proven to be of importance to students’ outcomes, such as
teachers’ expectations, instructional clarity through structuring of work, explaining new concepts, giving feedback and so on (Hattie,

The students answered in a Likert scale ranging from “disagree a lot” (1) to “agree a lot” (4). The Cronbach’s Alpha was .76. These

2.2.4. Content Coverage and Time Spent
In TIMSS 2011 there are a wide set of variables that measure content coverage and time spent on different topics. All these variables
were included in a descriptive analysis. Analysis of time spent revealed that a replica of Morton & Riegle-Crumb’s (2020) “time on
advanced content”-variable was inappropriate for the Swedish context. This was due to, firstly, small between class/school-variance
and secondly no significant correlations between time spent on advanced content and class composition. This is an important result for
comparative education why the Morton & Riegle-Crumb-replica is reported in Table 2. These findings are well in line with previous
findings on the low-level between school OTL-gap in Sweden (Schmidt, Burroughs, Zoido, & Houang, 2015; Rolfe, Strietholt, & Yang
Hansen, 2021).
According to the FFT, class composition will steer and limit content coverage to some extent, in the sense that the higher the mean
ability and prior knowledge is in class, the more advanced content will be possible to cover in instruction, but also that elementary
content can be covered faster (Lundgren, 1972; 1973). At the same time, one important perspective in OTL-research is that opportunity
to learn must be understood as the overlap between enacted and assessed curricula; students must be taught what is to be tested
(Husén, 1967; Elliot & Bartlett, 2016). Both these perspectives had to be incorporated into an operationalization of advanced content
coverage.
In TIMSS 2011 there were 19 topics specified in the teacher survey, topics that also were included in the tests given to the students.
The teachers were asked to answer when the class had been taught this content. They could reply by “mostly before this year” (3),
“mostly taught this year” (2) or “not yet taught or just introduced” (1). The numbers represent my coding of the answers. Given this, the
hypothesized relations by the FFT would be that, in general, the higher ability in class, the more content could have been taught
“mostly before this year” and the more advanced content might also have been introduced.
To operationalize an adequate variable, firstly, correlational analysis was conducted between class-aggregated results, SES and the
19 different topics specified in TIMSS 2011. With aggregated results, all correlations were either weak (R < .20), not significant or
none-existing. Five variables had correlations above R = .10. These were simplifying in algebra with (R = .15, p < .01), cartesian plane
(.14/<.01) and geometric properties (.13/<.01) in geometry, interpreting data (.12/<.01) in data and chance, and computing (.10/<.01)
and representing (.10/<.01) in numbers. With SES-measures, the patterns were the same. The correlations were strongest with the
average age of coming to Sweden in the classes. The higher average age of coming, the lower content coverage. For instance, the
correlation to cartesian plane was R = -.30, interpreting data -.17 and geometric properties -.16 (p < .01).
After doing this analysis, secondly, five teachers in 8th grade mathematics were asked to categorize the 19 different topics specified
in the TIMSS 2011-teacher survey, into “elementary” or “advanced” topics. Six of these topics were identified as advanced topics for 8th
grade mathematics in Sweden by all five teachers. These were simultaneous equations, functions (algebra), appropriate measurement,
cartesian plane, translation (geometry) and interpreting data (data and chance). Merging these results led to the creation of a
calculated variable where the values of cartesian plane, geometric properties (geometry), simplifying (algebra), and interpreting data (data
and chance) was summarized into a new variable measuring advanced content (ADV.CONTENT).

2.3. Validity and Reliability
There are several indices that both the internal and external validity and reliability of TIMSS is good in the Swedish case. For
instance, trends of Swedish students’ motivation, as measured by TIMSS, are verified in Swedish longitudinal surveys, indicating
external validity and reliability of the TIMSS-survey (Skolverket, 2019).
A common concern with the validity of student surveys in TIMSS – as with all self-reported survey data – regards students’
background variables. Several studies have reported significant errors in measurement concerning these survey items (Engzell &
Jonsson, 2015; Engzell, 2019). In a recent study, Wiberg & Rolfsman (2021) compare the self-reported data of TIMSS with Swedish
register data on student background and conclude that the home educational resource index of TIMSS and the number of books at
home are good indicators of students’ SES. This study therefore follows the recommendations of Wiberg & Rolfsman.

When participating in TIMSS, the Swedish National Agency for Education [Skolverket] analyse what is tested in TIMSS in reference
to the Swedish curricula. The agency has concluded that the mathematical content measured correspond to the Swedish curricula
(Skolverket, 2008; 2012; 2016). Similar results are reported by Sollerman (2019). Studies on Swedish data have shown that there are
strong correlations between students’ mathematical results in TIMSS, their final grades in mathematics (R = .74) and high-stakes

Regarding the reliability of TIMSS, and the variables used in the analysis, Cronbach’s Alpha measures were reported for each of the

2.4. Data Analysis
When studying peer effects in education, it is essential that the methods used can handle the selection problems in school systems
(Manski, 1993; Sund, 2009) and the naturally nested multilevel structure of school data (Heck, Thomas, & Tabata, 2010; Hox, 2010).
To do this, this study utilizes Multilevel Structural Equation Modelling (M-SEM) using Mplus 8.2 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998). Data
preparation and descriptive analysis was done using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.
Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) has become increasingly popular within educational research (Gustafsson, 2009; Wang,
Hefetz, & Liberman, 2017). This is mainly due to its flexible ability to combine the handling of complex relations between dependent
and independent variables, mediation analysis and analysis of latent variables (Kline, 2015). SEM is also a powerful way of ensuring
construct validity and reliability through its inherent ways of testing internal consistency between multiple indicators (Abedi, 2002;
Marsh & Hau, 2007).
Since the aim of this study was to test the theoretical synthesis of the FFT and OTL, as a theory on peer effects, the SEM-framework
was assessed as a suitable methodology since SEM is based on Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Within CFA, variables are chosen in
accordance with the theoretical perspective and then tested (i.e., “confirmed”) in the analysis (Kline, 2015). Hereby, the
SEM-framework can be seen as driven by theory.
In TIMSS, IEA (International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement) employs a method of “plausible values”.
Essentially, the plausible value methodology treats student achievement as a missing value. Responses to the limited test items and the
student background questionnaires are used in conjunction to generate a student ability distribution (Rutkowski et al, 2010). This
means that for each student there are five plausible values for mathematical achievement (BSMMATH01-05). To handle this in the
analysis, the “imputation”-command in MPLUS was used. When doing this, MPLUS runs the model on each plausible value (1-5) and
then reports the mean estimated effect and standard deviation between each estimation. The mean effect and standard deviation
between each estimation will be reported in the article, following the recommendations of Rutkowski et al (2010).

3. Results
3.1. Measurement model
The first step in SEM-analysis is to build a measurement model. The aim with this modelling is to create a model of latent variables
that fits actual data, i.e., that the theorized model can explain the variance in the actual data (Kline, 2015). In the measurement model
specified in this study, three latent variables were specified. These were “Limitations on Instruction” (LIMIT) on the between class level
and “Instruction According to the Students” (S.INSTR/SC.INSTR) on both the within- and between-class level. In Fig. 3, the results for
the measurement model are reported. Maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR) was used for estimation. All
reported coefficients are standardized (STDYX in MPLUS). The coefficients are significant (p < .05) if nothing else is stated. The
left-hand side of the model holds the within-class level. The right-hand side holds the between-class level.
The fit indices reveal that the model had a good fit to data. Such a conclusion was contradicted by the χ2-analysis which reported a
significant value of χ2 = 98.5 (df = 21, p = .00), indicating that the measurement model and actual data were significantly diverse from
one another. Given the large sample size in the analysis (n = 3 928), this result was expected since the χ2-test is sensitive to large
sample sizes (Gustafsson, 2009; Kline, 2015; Wang, Hefetz, & Liberman, 2017). All other fit indices revealed a good or even excellent
model fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Gustafsson, 2009; Kline, 2015; Wang, Hefetz, & Liberman, 2017). The Root Mean Square Error of
Approximation (RMSEA) was of .031 (recommended < .05) and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) was .977 (.95 <), which suggested
good model fit. The Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), which can be estimated for each level, was .026 for the
within-class level and .056 for the between-class level (recommended < .08).
The analysis revealed that the latent variable of Instruction according to students (S_INSTR) explained most of the variance in the
manifest variables concerning the students’ interest in what the teacher says and that the teacher gives interesting things to do (factor
loadings of .80). The least of the variance was explained in “I know what my teacher expects me to do” (.41). On the between-class
level, the same pattern emerged but with higher factor loadings (.48-.91). The latent variable of Limitations on instruction (LIMIT)
explained most of the variance in limitations due to students uninterest (.79) and the least in lack of prerequisite knowledge (.48).
The analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between limitations of instruction (LIMIT) and class-aggregated instruction
according to students (SC_INSTR) of -.29 (p < .01), indicating that the more the teacher assesses that instruction is limited by certain
factors, the less the students assess instruction in class.

4. Discussion and Future Research
Given the cross-sectional design of TIMSS, the possibilities of drawing causal conclusions from this study are limited. One major
concern for this study, in reference to the FFT-perspective, is the lack of a longitudinal design. At heart of the FFT-perspective is the
importance of students’ average prior knowledge and aptitude, which according to the theory will create the steering and limiting
effects on instruction. Since such data is not included within TIMSS cross-sectional design, the results of this study can at best be
interpreted as circumstantial evidence of the theory. With this said, the results presented in this study are in line with previous findings
on compositional and peer effects in education (Lundgren, 1972; Opdenakker & Damme, 2006; Dumay & Dupriez, 2007; Bäckström,
2022), whereas the theoretical interpretation of these results seems plausible.
The study revealed that class composition to some extent affects teachers’ instruction in class, through its’ limitations on instruction. Through these effects, individual students’ achievement in mathematics are affected by limitations on instruction (-.53),
indicating the presence of a peer effect. The results from the study thereby support previous findings on the steering and limiting effect
of class composition on teachers’ instruction (Lundgren, 1972; Barr & Dreeben, 1983; Thrupp, 1999; Garrison, 2004; Dumay &
Dupriez, 2007), as well as providing a new theoretical perspective on results from OTL-research (Stevens, 1993; Kurz A., 2011; Elliot &
Bartlett, 2016; Klapp & Jönsson, 2020; Morton & Riegle-Crumb, 2020).
The results indicate that class composition is related to students’ opportunities to learn, measured as the advanced content that can
be covered in instruction. In the full model (M3), there was a negative relation between average class SES on the between-class level
and advanced content covered (.16). Advanced content was in turn related to student achievement (.15, p < .05). Teachers in
classrooms where students’ prior knowledge and aptitude on average is higher can to some degree cover more advanced content in
instruction than teachers in other classrooms. The more and older immigrant students in class were when arriving to Sweden, the less
advanced content was covered in class (Szulkin & Jonsson, 2007; Brunello & Rocco, 2013). Interestingly, the results indicate that this
phenomenon was separated from limitations on instruction (LIMIT), which must be interpreted as the main mechanism of peer effects
12

International Journal of Educational Research 121 (2023) 102229

P. Bäckström

in the analysis. These results are in line with previous findings within the Swedish setting (Schmidt, Burroughs, Zoido, & Houang,
2015; Rolfe, Strietholt, & Yang Hansen, 2021).
It is relevant to ask how these effects should be interpreted; are these effects of such magnitude that they deserve our attention?
First of all, the limiting effects on instruction – the main mechanism of peer effects according to the FFT – seems to be of significant
magnitude. Secondly, the effects of advanced content coverage seem to be of less magnitude. Analysed separately, a model specification of M3 – but with the OTL-variables of advanced content coverage excluded – explained 66.2 percent of the between class
variance in achievement. With OTL content coverage added, as in the original M3, 68.7 percent was explained.
In reference to this, it is yet again important to point to the special conditions for the Swedish setting, with small between-school
OTL-gaps and with highly equitable results (Schmidt, Burroughs, Zoido, & Houang, 2015; Rolfe, Strietholt, & Yang Hansen, 2021). It
highlights the need to apply the synthesized FFT-model in an international setting, going beyond the Swedish context.
To enhance the understanding of these mechanisms, future research is also summoned to test the synthesised FFT presented in this
study in a longitudinal design. Also, since the findings reported are average effects for all students in class, it summons future research
to explore possible interaction-effects between compositional effects on teachers’ instructional practices and different students’
achievement.
In a wider perspective, these findings are interesting to discuss from a historical point of view. In Sweden, the predominant aim of
educational policy throughout the post-war era has been to increase school equity, mainly by decreasing streaming and tracking by
students’ background and abilities (Beach, 2018). This policy has over time created a school system which in an international
perspective has a heterogenous school composition regarding students’ background, small between-school OTL-gaps and with highly
equitable results (Schmidt, Burroughs, Zoido, & Houang, 2015; Rolfe, Strietholt, & Yang Hansen, 2021). From this perspective, the
findings in this study can be seen as both promising and discouraging. The findings are promising in the sense that the compositional
effects aren’t greater. To some extent, the Swedish comprehensive compulsory schooling model seem to work. But at the same time, the
findings are discouraging since they reveal compositional effects on content coverage and student achievement of the same nature as
those reported by Dahllöf in the 1960’s (Dahllöf, 1967). Despite the aim of Swedish educational policies, it still matters – as it did in the
1960’s – who students are, what background they have and who their classmates are. Perhaps this is something that is impossible to
reform? The collective nature of classroom teaching (Nuthall, 2005), or “the negotiated nature of teaching” (Thrupp, 1999, p. 157),
might force us to be somewhat realistic about to what extent educational inequalities are possible to fully eliminate.
