Abstract
The COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly affected academic performance,
intensifying existing disparities among students from lower socioeconomic status
(SES) backgrounds, especially in the subjects mathematics and reading. This study
examines the influence of SES on scientific literacy—a critical competency for
informed participation in an increasingly science-driven society. Employing linear
models and meta-analytic techniques, we analyzed data from the 2018 and 2022
PISA assessments across 69 participating countries (N = 1,060,311). Findings reveal a
positive association between SES and scientific literacy (ß = .28, CI[.25, .31]). Notably,
a significant interaction between SES and PISA cycle indicates that educational
inequalities widened during the pandemic period (ß = .06, CI[.03, .09]). These results
contribute to a deeper understanding of how socioeconomic factors shaped learning
outcomes during COVID-19 and introduce a novel modelling approach for analyzing
effects across two assessment cycles in large-scale education data.
Keywords COVID-19, Socioeconomic status, PISA, Scientific literacy
Introduction
The impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on academic performance are still a debated
topic in public and empirical educational research, as the pandemic-related school closures have impacted approximately 90% of students globally (UNESCO, 2020). Students
experienced significant performance declines during the COVID-19 pandemic in these
domains, and learners from families with lower socioeconomic status (SES) were particularly affected (Betthäuser et al., 2023). These findings suggest that the longstanding achievement gaps associated with SES (e.g., Sirin, 2005) have been substantially
exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the extent to which these effects
manifested likely varied substantially across countries due to structural and systemic
differences (Betthäuser et al., 2023). To capture these cross-national variations, it is

Theoretical background
Scientific literacy in PISA

Scientific literacy is considered one of the most essential competencies students should
possess as part of the 21st-century skills to successfully participate in a science- and
technology-driven society (National Research Council, ). In this context, learners should
be able to draw on their scientific literacy to critically engage with online information,
particularly when such information may influence their behavior or personal health, to
have a solid understanding of the environment and how to protect it, and that learners know how scientific knowledge is generated (OECD, 2024b). Reflecting its importance, the PISA study has included scientific literacy from its inception to provide an
international overview of the current status of scientific literacy. According to the
OECD framework (2016), scientific literacy is a comprehensive understanding beyond
mere knowledge of scientific concepts and theories. Scientific literacy further includes
familiarity with the methodologies and practices of scientific inquiry. Hence, a scientifically literate person not only grasps the core concepts and principles underlying scientific and technological ideas but also comprehends the processes through which this
knowledge is obtained and appreciates the extent to which this knowledge is supported
by evidence or theoretical reasoning. Accordingly, various knowledge components are
central to scientific literacy. These range from content-based knowledge, which focuses
on pure factual knowledge relevant to science, to procedural and epistemic knowledge.
Procedural knowledge involves understanding how specific tasks or processes must be
performed and how they function. Epistemic knowledge encompasses understanding
scientific methods, such as hypothesis formulation, data collection, and data analysis. In
addition to the relevant knowledge components for scientific literacy, the OECD framework (2016) identifies three core competencies essential for scientifically literate students: explaining phenomena scientifically, evaluating and designing scientific inquiry,
and interpreting data and evidence scientifically. These competencies enable students
to apply their scientific knowledge and critically engage with science-related topics. For
example, they allow students to assess whether sufficient valid data exists to make a scientifically accurate statement or to plan their scientific investigations.
In addition to knowledge components and competencies, attitudes are crucial to scientific literacy. Scientifically literate students should exhibit a positive attitude toward
science and show interest in science-related topics and technology [27]. Thus, fostering
these attitudes seems essential. In summary, scientific literacy is a comprehensive and
highly relevant construct that students must possess sufficiently to participate successfully in a society shaped by science and technology.
Socioeconomic status and its effect on scientific literacy under the lens of the COVID-19
pandemic

An individual’s socioeconomic status (SES) is frequently considered in educational
research to explain student performance differences or uncover social inequalities. SES
can significantly impact various aspects of a person’s life, including access to education,

support of parents and social mobility. In the literature, SES is often defined as a multidimensional construct that describes an individual’s or their parents’ economic and social
standing. Typical indicators used to measure SES include family income, educational
attainment, and occupational status (see Cowan et al., 2012; Avvisati, 2020). Bourdieu’s
(1983) theory on the forms of capital has profoundly influenced the operationalization of
socioeconomic status. According to Bourdieu’s (1983) theory, the dimensions of socioeconomic status can be articulated through various forms of capital, specifically economic, cultural, and social capital. Economic capital includes resources such as financial
assets or a family’s income. Cultural capital encompasses aspects such as the educational
attainment of a family. Social capital involves affiliations or membership within particular groups or social strata.
Again, substantial body of research has demonstrated that students’ socioeconomic
status (SES) significantly impacts their academic achievement. One explanation for this
relationship is that parents with higher economic capital, such as those with more significant family income, are often better equipped to provide resources and materials that
facilitate access to additional educational tools for the home learning environment compared to students from households with lower family incomes (Sirin, 2005). Higher family income is frequently associated with higher parental educational levels. Parents with
advanced education might therefore often more capable of offering thoughtful support
related to their children’s academic performance, as they might better understand complex content due to their education and have more time to assist due to job prestige and
flexibility. This mix of the mere availability of digital devices on the one hand and their
meaningful use for educational purposes on the other reflects social disparities that are
typically discussed in terms of the second and third digital divide (e.g., Ragnedda, 2017;
Robinson et al., 2020). While the second digital divide refers to differences in digital
skills and usage patterns among individuals who already have access to technology, the
third digital divide concerns inequalities in the favorable outcomes derived from digital
engagement—such as learning success or academic achievement. These divides became
particularly relevant during the COVID-19 pandemic, when students had to rely on digital resources at home to sustain their learning processes.
With the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, concerns emerged that pre-existing performance disparities influenced by the SES could exacerbate (Haeck & Lefebvra, 2020;
Woessmann, 2020), as regular participation in classroom instruction is particularly crucial for students with low SES, as they may not have the necessary support at home and
may not have the necessary support at home and may require external resources to facilitate learning effectively. This posed a particular risk for low-achieving students, who
often have a low SES, potentially exacerbating their educational disadvantages. Studies
verified these assumptions, showing that the pandemic led to significant learning losses,
disproportionately affecting students with low SES (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Engzell et al.,
2021; Hammerstein et al., 2021; Grewening et al., 2021). In summary, substantial evidence indicates that the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated educational inequalities
in academic achievement, particularly for children from lower SES backgrounds (Betthäuser et al., 2023; Jacobowski et al., 2023).
Moreover, while the relationship between SES and academic achievement in mathematics and reading is frequently and extensively explored in studies, there is a shortage
of research investigating the connection between SES and scientific literacy (Betancur

research consistently indicates that socioeconomic status, similar to its influence on
reading and mathematics competencies, significantly impacts scientific literacy (e.g.,
Maerten-Rivera et al., 2010; OECD, 2023).
Notably, studies on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on educational inequalities have primarily focused on mathematics and reading (Hammerstein et al., 2021). In
particular, findings in mathematics indicate more pronounced learning losses compared
to reading (Betthäuser et al., 2023), possibly because families were better able to compensate for reading deficits than for mathematical ones during school closures. Given
that science learning, like mathematics, often relies on structured instruction and the
development of abstract concepts (e.g., Anderson et al., 2010), it is plausible that similar challenges arose in maintaining scientific literacy—especially among students from
lower socioeconomic backgrounds. However, as direct evidence on pandemic-related
science learning losses remains limited, this assumption should be interpreted with caution and warrants further empirical investigation. Moreover, it is plausible that science
learning was especially affected by pandemic-related restrictions, as science instruction
often involves hands-on experiments and laboratory work that are difficult to replicate
through digital teaching formats. Such limitations may have further amplified existing
inequalities in access to high-quality science education during the pandemic.
Country-specific characteristics, SES and COVID-19 pandemic

It is plausible to assume that the COVID-19 pandemic—which affected the majority of
students in countries worldwide (UNESCO, 2020)—had varying degrees of impact on
students and, consequently, on their academic performance in different countries. In
countries where the education system benefits from comparatively higher per-student
funding (see overview: OECD, 2024b), schools might be better equipped with financial
and structural resources to mitigate the negative consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic, such as prolonged school closures. Accordingly, research shows that the extent
of learning loss due to the COVID-19 pandemic was generally more severe in lowerincome countries than in wealthier nations (Betthäuser et al., 2023). However, many
studies examining the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on academic achievement
primarily rely on national datasets (for an overview, see Hammerstein et al., 2021). This
focus presents a limitation, as country-specific indicators—such as a nation’s economic
wealth—can significantly influence how the pandemic has affected educational outcomes. For example, Kennedy and Strietholt (2023) demonstrate substantial variation
in pandemic-related learning losses across different countries. They argue that further
research is needed to understand why some countries have experienced greater deficits than others. An international perspective that includes a broader range of countries
could provide more nuanced and generalizable insights into educational research. However, the authors note that cross-country comparisons may be constrained by factors
such as cultural differences and variations in developmental levels, whereby during the
COVID-19 pandemic the digital developments of a country might also have been particularly relevant to keep up with contact restrictions and school closures. These considerations have led to two key methodological decisions in the present study. First, this
study utilizes data from the 2018 and 2022 cycles of the PISA assessment in the domain
of scientific literacy, with both cycles using identical testing instruments. Second, the

Methodological strategies for deriving longitudinal insights from cross-sectional designs

International large-scale assessment (LSA) studies, such as PISA, ICILS, and PIRLS, are
cross-sectional investigations conducted across several cycles and years. Unlike longitudinal studies, which track individual development over time, LSAs provide “snapshots”
of student performance that serve as central indicators of the efficiency of education systems at specific points in time. Their high degree of standardization and regular administration makes them a valuable resource for examining the potential effects of major
global events, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, even though they were not explicitly
designed for this purpose. Jakubowski et al. (2023) illustrate such an approach by analyzing PIRLS data to estimate the impact of pandemic-related school closures on reading achievement. They projected linear performance trends for each country based on
earlier assessment cycles and compared the 2021 results against these projections. Their
findings revealed significant negative deviations, particularly in countries with prolonged closures and among lower-achieving students. The model accounted for student
characteristics (e.g., age, grade, gender) and fixed country effects, thereby providing a
rigorous methodological design. In a follow-up study, Jakubowski et al. (2024) applied a
similar strategy to PISA 2022 data, again identifying pandemic-related learning losses,
most prominently in mathematics. A clear strength of these studies is their use of multiple assessment cycles to establish long-term performance trends in international
comparison.
Complementary insights are provided by Kennedy and Strietholt (2023), who examined the relationship between the duration of school closures and students’ reading
achievement in a cross-country perspective. Drawing on internationally comparable
trend data, they assessed changes in student performance before and after the pandemic
while controlling for factors such as infection rates, vaccination policies, and lockdown
stringency. Their findings indicate substantial learning losses associated with prolonged
closures, particularly for socioeconomically disadvantaged students and those lacking
access to digital resources. A major contribution of their work lies in the systematic use
of international variation in school closure policies to estimate the educational impact of
the pandemic. Taken together, these studies demonstrate that methodologically robust
strategies exist to approximate pandemic-related learning losses within the constraints
of cross-sectional LSA data. Building on these advances, our study extends prior work

In summary, large-scale assessments provide extensive and internationally comparable
data, although their cross-sectional design poses challenges for the analysis of longitudinal developments. Recent methodological advances (e.g., Jakubowski et al., 2023, 2024;
Kennedy & Strietholt, 2023) have demonstrated how such data can be used to approximate temporal dynamics and pandemic-related learning losses. Building on these innovations, our study introduces two complementary methodological extensions. First, we
model an interaction effect between study cycles (2018 and 2022) to examine whether
the impact of socioeconomic status on scientific literacy shifted during the pandemic.
Second, we embed this approach into a meta-analytic framework, treating each participating country as a unit of analysis to strengthen explanatory power. This combined
strategy allows us to generate more nuanced insights into the educational impact of the
COVID-19 pandemic on international educational equity.

The present study
In this study, we aim to investigate the influence of socioeconomic status (SES) on science achievement and specifically, we aim to investigate how the COVID-19 pandemic
may have exacerbated disparities in science achievement, paralleling trends observed in
subjects like reading and mathematics. To achieve this, we employ a novel meta-analytic
approach encompassing all participating countries participating in the PISA studies of
2018 and 2022 as robust pre- and post-pandemic datasets. Our analysis initially examines to what extent SES influences scientific literacy across different countries. Furthermore, we explore whether an interaction effect exists between SES and the PISA cycle
(2018 and 2022) on scientific literacy, allowing us to infer about the COVID-19 impacts.
Furthermore, we include country-specific characteristics to explain variations between
countries. Hence, this study addresses the following research questions.
RQ1: To what extent do country-level associations between students’ socioeconomic
status (SES) and scientific literacy differ across the 69 countries participating in the
PISA 2018 and 2022 studies?
RQ 2: To what extent does the interaction between SES and PISA cycle (2018 vs. 2022)
vary across countries, indicating potential differences in how the SES–science relationship changed over time?
RQ3: To what extent do country-specific indices, such as the Human-DevelopmentIndex (HDI) and the Digital Readiness Index (DRI), explain possible variation across
countries regarding the tested model? (meta-analytic moderation by HDI and DRI)?

Method
Participants and design

The study involves a total of N = 1,060,311 participants, who are 15-year-old students
from the 2018 and 2022 cycles of PISA. PISA is an international educational monitoring
study that assesses students’ competencies in reading, mathematics, and science on a
cross-sectional basis, conducted in cycles. Participants were selected through a stratified

sampling process: schools were first chosen to participate in the PISA study, followed
by the selection of students within these schools (for more details on sampling, see
OECD, 2024a). The gender distribution of the participants is balanced across the n = 69
participating countries in the 2018 and 2022 PISA cycles. One country was excluded
from the analyses due to inconsistent data, which did not meet the criteria for reliable
interpretation.
Instruments

Scientific literacy is assessed through regular cross-sectional PISA cognitive-based
assessments. In these assessments, students participate in a competency test involving a series of units covering various science domains. Within these units, students
answer questions ranging from multiple-choice items to open-ended responses, which
are either automatically coded or manually scored by trained personnel, depending on
unit complexity (OECD, 2024a). In PISA, ten plausible values serve as estimates for proficiency for each participant based on their responses to the competency-based items.
Plausible values are multiple estimates of a student’s ability derived from an underlying
distribution of student proficiency (OECD, 2024a). Notably, because scientific literacy
was a minor domain in both PISA 2018 and PISA 2022, students responded to the same
science tasks in both years. The questions pertain to real-world phenomena in biology,
physics, and chemistry and cover topics such as nutrition, biohazards, environment, science, and technology, addressing the three competence areas of explaining phenomena
scientifically, evaluating and designing scientific inquiry, and interpreting data and evidence scientifically. Example items can be viewed here: ​h​t​t​p​s​:​​/​/​w​w​w​​.​o​e​c​d​.​​o​r​g​/​​e​n​/​a​b​​o​u​t​/​

Socioeconomic status (SES) is operationalized using the Index of Economic, Social, and
Cultural Status (ESCS), a validated measure for concluding individuals’ SES. The ESCS in
PISA is composed of three components: the highest SES of parents’ occupations (HISEI),
the educational attainment of the parents (PARED), and the possession of wealth-related
items (HOMEPOS). This index allows for the aggregated consideration of various background characteristics (for further information, see here: OECD, 2024a; Avvisati, 2020).
To account for country-specific characteristics, moderator analyses were conducted
using the Human Development Index (HDI) (UNDP, 2023) and the Digital Readiness
Index (DRI) (Cisco, 2024). These indices were chosen because they might explain variations in the tested model, especially considering the research questions (RQ1 and 2) and
the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. The HDI measures a country’s average achievements in areas such as education and standard of living. The DRI provides insights into a
country’s infrastructure and readiness to utilize digital technologies.

used to predict scientific literacy based on socioeconomic status and its interaction with

whereby:

• 	 Independent Variables (ESCS and PISA cycle):—ESCS: Socioeconomic status, which

• 	 Intercept (β0): This term represents the expected scientific literacy score when the

• 	 Coefficient (β1): β1 indicates the change in scientific literacy associated with a oneunit change in ESCS, holding the PISA cycle constant. It measures the main effect of
socioeconomic status on scientific literacy.
• 	 Interaction Term (β2): β2 reveals how the relationship between ESCS and scientific
literacy varies across PISA cycles (2018 vs. 2022). It quantifies whether the impact of
socioeconomic status on scientific literacy differs depending on the assessment year
of the PISA.
• 	 Error Term (ε): ε denotes the error term, accounting for unexplained variance in
scientific literacy.
Applying the linear models as presented above, to each of the combined (2018 & 2022)
PISA country data sets, we can identify the main effect of socioeconomic status on scientific literacy (RQ1) and quantify how socioeconomic status’s impact on scientific literacy differs across assessment cycles (2018 vs. 2022) through an interaction effect. The
linear models tested were analyzed meta-analytically across 69 participating countries
using summary effects (Brunner et al., 2023; Hofer et al., 2024). Each country was treated
as an independent study, and sampling weights and plausible values were applied in line
with OECD recommendations. this metanalytical approach captures between-country
variability while respecting the independent sampling procedure of students within each
participating country.The between-studies variance τ2 represents the variance of the
distribution of actual effects across the population, whereby lower values and nonsignificant values indicate a low between-study variation (Harrer et al., 2022). Cochran’s Q
describes the extent of deviation of the observed effects from the estimated summary
effect (Harrer et al., 2022). The I2 statistic, ranging from 0 to 1, is used to assess betweenstudy heterogeneity and represents the proportion of total variance attributable to the
variance between studies (Konstantopoulos & Hedges, 2019). To estimate scientific literacy scores, all ten plausible values were utilized (OECD, 2024a), and sampling weights
were incorporated into the analysis (Asparouhov, 2005). All analyses were conducted
using the statistical software R (R Core Team, 2024), using the packages BIFIE survey
(Robitzsch & Oberwimmer, 2024) and metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010). To further examine
contextual influences on cross-national variation, country-level indicators such as the
Human Development Index (HDI; UNDP, 2023) and the Digital Readiness Index (DRI;
Cisco, 2024) were incorporated as predictors in meta-regression models implemented

Discussion
A central focus of this study is the relationship between socioeconomic status (SES)
and scientific literacy, particularly in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. While
corresponding associations have been extensively examined in mathematics and reading, the evidence base for science education remains comparatively limited. Similarly,
pandemic-related learning losses in science are less systematically documented than
in other subject areas (Betthäuser et al., 2023). To address this research gap, a metaanalytic approach was applied to internationally data from 69 countries from the PISA
cycles 2018 and 2022 (see Brunner et al., 2023). Two assessment waves were considered,
enabling a comparison before and after the educational disruptions caused by the pandemic: 2018 (pre-pandemic) and 2022 (post-pandemic). The objective was to generate
robust and internationally comparable insights into the development of socially determined disparities in scientific literacy. In addition, a methodological approach was
implemented that models interaction effects within a linear regression framework. This
approach enables the analysis of changes between the two PISA cycles despite the crosssectional nature of the data. Potential shifts in educational inequality can be identified
and interpreted by explicitly modeling the interaction between assessment year and SES.
Our findings revealed a strong and statistically significant relationship between SES
and scientific literacy. This indicates that students with higher SES also show higher
achievements in scientific literacy. This finding is consistent with existing evidence from
the literature, which has documented similar SES-related performance advantages in
other academic domains, such as mathematics and reading (Votruba-Drzal et al., 2013).
The persistence of these advantages in scientific literacy underscores the pervasive role
of SES in shaping educational outcomes across various subjects.
Second, our findings identified a significant interaction effect between the PISA cycles
(2018 vs. 2022) and SES on scientific literacy. This interaction suggests that the influence
of SES on scientific literacy shifted notably between these assessment periods, highlighting how the COVID-19 pandemic may have deepened pre-existing inequities. Specifically, students from lower SES backgrounds appeared to be more adversely affected
regarding their scientific literacy during the 2022 cycle than their more privileged peers.
Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, students might have faced challenges accessing educational resources, learning in less structured environments, and engaging with complex
scientific concepts at home. So far, research suggests more significant learning setbacks
in mathematics than reading during the pandemic (Betthäuser et al., 2023). This may
be because reading deficits were comparatively easier to mitigate at home, as reading
is often more closely linked to everyday activities and readily accessible materials. By
contrast, subjects such as mathematics and science—particularly given the mathematical components often embedded in science—typically require more structured instruction and engagement with abstract concepts, which may make them harder to learn
independently. Consequently, it is plausible that students from lower socioeconomic

Page 12 of 16

Kastorff and Heine Large-scale Assessments in Education

(2025) 13:36

backgrounds, who may have had fewer resources and support systems during the
COVID-19 pandemic, faced greater difficulties in sustaining their scientific literacy.
However, this interpretation should be viewed cautiously, as direct evidence on these
mechanisms remains limited.
Beyond the immediate COVID-19 pandemic effects, it is essential to situate these findings also within the broader, longer-term trends in student performance and educational
inequality. Analyses of successive PISA cycles have shown a general stagnation or decline
in science performance in many education systems over the past two decades, accompanied by persistent or even widening socioeconomic disparities (OECD, 2023). These patterns might suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic did not necessarily only create new
forms of inequality but rather amplified structural disparities that have been observable
over time. In this context, the stronger SES gradient in 2022 may reflect an acceleration of pre-existing trends, emphasizing the importance of sustained policy attention to
equity in science education beyond short-term COVID-19 pandemic responses. To
investigate the relationship between socioeconomic status (SES) and scientific literacy—
particularly in light of the COVID-19 pandemic—a statistical model including an interaction term between SES and the study cycle (2018 and 2022) was implemented. This
approach allows for examining changes in the SES–SES-scientific literacy relationship
across the two PISA cycles. The method builds on previous approaches (Jakubowski et
al., 2023, 2024) used to assess learning deficits during the COVID-19 pandemic in the
context of large-scale assessments. Our model extends this framework in two important ways: 1) It enables the analysis of changes in the SES–outcome relationship between
two cross-sectional study cycles, and 2) With the integration of a meta-analytic perspective, the proposed method allows for country-specific analyses, thereby addressing a key
limitation of prior research, which often focused on individual countries in isolation.
By leveraging internationally comparable PISA data, this approach provides a broader,
cross-national perspective on educational inequalities exacerbated or altered by the
pandemic.
Limitations and further research

Although the PISA study is not a longitudinal design intended to identify causal effects,
using the PISA cycle as a variable can reveal nuanced patterns and shifts in educational
inequalities that may remain obscured in traditional cross-sectional analyses. This
approach is particularly relevant for this study because, in the two consecutive PISA
rounds of 2018 and 2022 focused on scientific literacy, it was ensured that (1) an identical set of science tasks was completed by representative student samples, (2) selected
according to the same sampling principles in both cycles (e.g., OECD, 2024a). This
method allows for deriving new insights from cross-sectional data by leveraging consistent assessment conditions to explore changes over time within the same assessment
framework. However, although data from two PISA cycles were pooled in this study
to examine changes over time, the analyses are based on independent cross-sectional
samples rather than longitudinal data, which limits the interpretation of country-level
change during the COVID-19 pandemic.
It was found that neither the Human Development Index (HDI) nor the Digital Readiness Index (DRI) significantly moderated the relationship between SES and scientific literacy nor the interaction effect for SES and the survey year. This suggests that having

Kastorff and Heine Large-scale Assessments in Education

(2025) 13:36

access to digital resources on a country level or a country’s level of human development
alone cannot explain the disparities in scientific literacy related to socioeconomic status
(SES), even in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. Further research should explore
other country-specific variables to understand how these factors might contribute to
differences in SES and scientific literacy, particularly in the context of the COVID-19
pandemic. However, it would be beneficial to expand the scope to include all countries
participating in PISA for future research. Furthermore, future studies could focus on
analyzing single aspects of the ESCS index as a composite measure of socioeconomic
status (SES), such as parental education, purely economic measures such as income, or
purely social measures of cultural and class affiliation. While composite measures provide a robust and comparable indicator of socioeconomic status in extensive international studies, delving into a more fine-grained analysis of the effect of single measures
could be central for a more comprehensive understanding of the detailed mechanisms
of social disadvantages and could provide an explanation for the observed relationship
between socioeconomic status and scientific literacy (see also Betancur et al., 2018). Furthermore, the present study did not include additional robustness or sensitivity analyses,
as our focus was on estimating cross-national patterns based on consistent PISA indicators. Future research could address this limitation by examining the stability of results
using alternative model specifications, subsets of countries, or additional PISA cycles.
Conclusion and implications

This study enhances our understanding of the discrepancies in scientific literacy associated with socioeconomic status (SES) and how the COVID-19 pandemic has influenced
these differences. Our research underscores the evolving impact of SES on educational
outcomes in various PISA cycles, emphasizing the need for inclusive and equitable educational policies to address the immediate learning setbacks due to the pandemic and
the longstanding inequalities in access to quality education. This highlights the urgent
requirement for targeted interventions to address the learning disparities in scientific literacy, particularly for students from lower SES backgrounds. While considerable efforts
have been directed toward mitigating learning setbacks, extending these recovery efforts
to science education is equally important. National initiatives such as the American Rescue Plan of 2021 and Germany’s “Startchancen” program aim to enhance educational
opportunities for underprivileged students. It will be crucial to evaluate the impact of
these programs on scientific literacy as we approach the PISA 2025 cycle, ensuring that
recovery strategies are comprehensive and practical across all educational domains.
