A B S T R A C T
It is commonly recognized that test performance is influenced by both cognitive ability and motivational factors. 
To explore this phenomenon, a random sample of 15-year-old Swedish students (n = 5504), whose PISA 2018 
results were linked to national registry data, was analyzed. Students' PISA performance was regressed on their 
self-reported test-taking effort in PISA, their national test scores in corresponding subject domains, and the 
interaction between these variables. Results reveal that test-taking effort had an independent influence on PISA 
scores after controlling for high-stakes test results (β = 0.15). More importantly, the relationship between the 
high-stakes national test and the low-stakes PISA was stronger at higher levels of effort (interaction coefficient; β 
= 0.05). Students who report low effort underperform in PISA by approximately one-third of a school year's 
learning gain, emphasizing the role of effort in obtaining an accurate assessment of ability. Implications for 
research and practice are discussed.
Educational relevance and implications statement
The findings of this study have implications for educational policy- 
making. Test-taking effort was found to have an independent influ­
ence on PISA test scores over and above ability, as measured by the high- 
stakes national test. Additionally, the relationship between students' 
PISA scores and their national test grades was stronger for students who 
reported higher test-taking effort in PISA. This finding implies that PISA 
test scores are more reflective of true ability for students with high test- 
taking motivation than those reporting lower motivation. Policymakers 
typically view results from low-stakes large-scale assessments as re­
flections of students' abilities. On the other hand, it is crucial to under­
stand that low-stakes test scores measure not just subject knowledge, but 
also non-cognitive factors like test-taking motivation. These test-taking 
behaviors can reflect essential personality traits that are important for 
handling real-life tasks and challenges, making them relevant to the 
PISA test. However, since test-taking motivation in international large- 
scale assessments varies across countries and student groups, future 
research should assess its impact on score comparability.
1. Introduction
International large-scale assessments (ILSAs), like the OECD's Pro­
gramme for International Student Assessment (PISA), have become 
increasingly important as instruments for informing education policies 
and reforms at both national and international levels (e.g., Grek, 2009; 
Hopfenbeck et al., 2017; Lindblad, Pettersson, & Popkewitz, 2018). 
Despite the high stakes for policymakers, PISA remains a low-stakes test 
for individual students, as their performance on the test has no conse­
quences at the individual student level. Students receive no feedback on 
their results, and the results are only reported at the national level long 
after the participating students have left compulsory school. In high- 
stakes assessments, motivation to take the test is primarily influenced 
by the associated stakes, with the assumption that most test-takers will 
exert their best effort throughout. In low-stakes assessments, motivation 
to take the test tends to be more variable due to the lack of personal 
consequences (Barry, Horst, Finney, Brown, & Kopp, 2010; Thelk, 
Sundre, Horst, & Finney, 2009; Wise & Smith, 2016). It is therefore 
reasonable to assume that not all students will be equally motivated to 
perform at their best in low-stakes assessments such as PISA.

programs susceptible to this risk (Ivanova, 2024; Pools & Monseur, 
2021; Wise & DeMars, 2005; Wise & Smith, 2016). In the same vein, 
several researchers have concluded that results from low-stakes assess­

complicating their interpretation (Borgonovi & Biecek, 2016; Buchholz, 
Cignetti, & Piacentini, 2022; Kyllonen & Kell, 2018; Lundgren, 2023; 
Segal, 2012; Silm, Must, & T¨aht, 2019). Consequently, test scores can be 
seen as a combination of skill (ability) and will (motivation) (Ekl¨of, 
2010).
Extensive research shows a positive correlation between students' 
test-taking motivation and their performance, highlighting that 
increased motivation is associated with higher test scores (Akhtar & 
Firdiyanti, 2023; Baumert & Demmrich, 2001; Cole, Bergin, & Whit­

Wise & DeMars, 2005). Silm et al. (2020) found that the average cor­
relation between self-reported effort and test performance was r = 0.27 
(0.22–0.30) for school students and r = 0.39 (0.35–0.43) for university 
students. Additionally, test takers generally display higher levels of both 
motivation and performance in high-stakes assessments as opposed to 
low-stakes ones (Cole & Osterlind, 2008; Ekl¨of & Knekta, 2017; Wise & 
DeMars, 2005, 2010; Wolf & Smith, 1995).
While previous studies have explored validity concerns regarding 
students' motivation and effort in PISA and other low-stakes assess­
ments, few, if any, studies have successfully untangled whether student 
motivation and effort or lack thereof actually biases scores from low- 
stakes tests. There have been studies in ILSA contexts suggesting that 
country rankings would change if student effort was taken into account 
(Borgonovi & Biecek, 2016; Gneezy et al., 2019), while others have 
reported that rankings are little affected by variations in student effort 
even if level of effort itself varies across groups (Butler & Adams, 2007; 
Guo & Ercikan, 2020; Michaelides, Ivanova, & Avraam, 2024; Rios & 
Guo, 2020; Wise, Soland, & Bo, 2020). Essentially, it remains unclear to 
what extent test-taking motivation explains variation in low-stakes test 
scores above and beyond students' cognitive ability (Reeve & Lam, 
2007). The present study attempts to fill this gap by examining this issue 
using data from one of the participating PISA countries: Sweden. Swe­
den was selected because it is one of the few countries in PISA where 
students' PISA scores can be linked to register data containing high- 
stakes national test results. This allows us to explore the role of test- 
taking motivation in the relationship between high-stakes national test 
scores and low-stakes PISA outcomes conducted in the same grade. Our 
investigation seeks to determine the combined influence of students' 
skill and will on PISA test scores.
2. Theory and previous research
2.1. Theoretical background
Test-taking motivation can be understood as a situation-specific 
motivational construct: it is a motivational state that is tied to a given 
task and can be defined as the extent to which test-takers feel motivated 
to give their “best effort to the test, with the goal being to accurately 
represent what one knows and can do” (Wise & DeMars, 2005, p. 2) 
within the tested content area. Consistent with this definition, effortful 
behavior is the outcome of a motivated state, and motivation plays a 
critical role in determining the level of effort exerted by test-takers 
during the specific testing situation.
The theoretical framework of the Expectancy-Value model of 
achievement motivation (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000) offers a valuable 
foundation for understanding inter-individual differences in motivation 
and is frequently used in previous research to elucidate the relationship 
between test-taking motivation and performance (e.g., Akhtar & Fir­
diyanti, 2023; Finn, 2015; Knekta & Ekl¨of, 2015). The expectancy-value 
models suggest that achievement motivation is a function of two 
primary factors: (a) students' expectancy of success, strongly linked to 
their cognitive abilities, and their self-beliefs, and (b) the value they 
attribute to the task, encompassing aspects like interest, importance, 
usefulness, and cost. The interaction of these factors plays a crucial role 
in determining achievement-related behaviors, such as effort, persis­
tence and performance. Studies that have attempted to model test-taking 
motivation within the expectancy-value framework provide support for 
using this theory (Akhtar & Firdiyanti, 2023;Knekta & Ekl¨of, 2015; Penk 
& Schipolowski, 2015) and have generally found that test-taking effort is 
best viewed as the outcome of expectancy and value (Knekta & Ekl¨of, 
2015; Penk & Schipolowski, 2015). In line with the propositions of the 
expectancy-value theory, students may not value a good performance if 
a good performance is not rewarded, hence may not be motivated to 
invest cognitive resources and spend the amount of effort needed in low- 
stakes assessment situations.
2.2. Test-taking motivation: impact on performance and measures used
The relationship between test-taking motivation and test perfor­
mance is a topic of ongoing investigation. While the research field is 
smaller compared to for example research on domain-specific motiva­
tion, a substantial body of research has evolved over the last couple of 
decades. Various methods exist to measure test-taking motivation and 
effort, with self-reported measures and behavioral measures serving as the 
primary approaches used as proxies (Buchholz et al., 2022). Self- 
reported measures involve questionnaire items, while behavioral mea­
sures analyze students' actions during assessments, such as response 
times and item-level engagement and response rates (e.g., Ivanova & 
Michaelides, 2023; Lundgren & Ekl¨of, 2020; Ulitzsch, von Davier, & 
Pohl, 2020).
Historically, the most common way of assessing test-taking motiva­
tion has been through post-test self-report surveys, asking students 
directly about the amount of effort they dedicated to their test responses 
or the seriousness of their answers. Studies using self-reports are mainly 
correlational and have consistently displayed positive but typically 
small to moderate associations between self-reported test-taking moti­
vation and test performance (cf. Cole et al., 2008; Ekl¨of & Knekta, 2017; 
Knekta & Ekl¨of, 2015; Penk, P¨ohlmann, & Roppelt, 2014). A meta- 
analytic review by Silm et al. (2020) revealed that the average corre­
lation between self-reported effort and test performance across the 
reviewed studies was r = 0.33. In the context of PISA, the “Effort 
Thermometer” is a self-report measure presented to students at the end 
of the test. It measures students' self-reported effort in the PISA test 
(PISA Effort) and contrasts it with the effort they would have invested 
had the PISA assessment counted towards their grades (School Mark 
Effort). Butler and Adams (2007) found that realistically reported effort 
(meaning students report spending equal or less effort on PISA compared 
to a test that counts towards their grades) was associated with PISA 
reading achievement with an effect size comparable to gender and SES, 
around r = 0.25.
With the digitalization of PISA and other achievement tests, there has 
been a growing interest in making more “objective, unobtrusive” 
behavioral assessments of test-taking motivation. In particular, quick 
response time, referred to as rapid guessing and measured using a 
response time effort (RTE) index (Wise, 2017; Wise & Kong, 2005; Wise, 
Pastor, & Kong, 2009), has been recognized as an indicator of disen­
gaged test-taking behavior (e.g., Goldhammer, Martens, Christoph, & 
Lüdtke, 2016; Michaelides & Ivanova, 2022; Rios & Soland, 2022; Silm, 
Must, & T¨aht, 2013; Wise, 2019). Within the PISA context, it has been 
found that the proportion of rapid guessing students varies across 
countries and student subgroups, such as gender and SES backgrounds 
(Goldhammer et al., 2016; Rios & Soland, 2022).
Another behavioral measure is test persistence, indicating to what 
extent students can sustain a consistent level of performance throughout 
the test (e.g., Borgonovi & Biecek, 2016; Debeer, Buchholz, Hartig, & 
Janssen, 2014; Zamarro, Hitt, & Mendez, 2019). Findings from PISA

When comparing self-reported and time-based measures, the latter

between self-reported and behavioral measures tends to be low, both at 
individual and country levels, suggesting they capture different aspects 
of test-taking motivation and/or suffer from different sources of bias or 
measurement error (Buchholz et al., 2022). Akhtar and Firdiyanti 
(2023) found the correlation between self-reported effort (SRE) and 
response-time effort (RTE) to be r = 0.28, while other studies indicate 
even lower correlations (Cs´anyi & Moln´ar, 2023; Hofverberg, Ekl¨of, & 
Lindfors, 2022; Silm et al., 2019). Silm et al. (2019) concluded that 
while SRE distinguished between higher levels of effort, RTE primarily 
differentiated between test-takers with low motivation. The specific 
validity of self-reported and behavioral measures of test-taking moti­
vation is subject to ongoing investigation.
Test-taking motivation has also been studied experimentally. Wise 
and DeMars (2005) reviewed 12 studies with experimental manipula­
tion of test-taker motivation in low-stakes conditions. The findings 
showed that motivated students achieved significantly higher scores, 
with results averaging over half a standard deviation higher than their 
unmotivated counterparts. Experimental studies have further suggested 
that test-taking motivation and/or score validity can potentially be 
improved by monitoring student attentiveness (Wise, Bhola, & Yang, 
2006), by providing external incentives (Gneezy et al., 2019), or by 
increasing perceived relevance (Rios, 2021).
There are however some contradicting findings when it comes to the 
effects of different incentives to increase motivation and thereby per­
formance (Baumert & Demmrich, 2001; Braun, Kirsch, & Yamamoto, 
2011; O'Neil, Abedi, Miyoshi, & Mastergeorge, 2005; Rios, 2021). It has 
furthermore been shown that students report high motivation to spend 
effort and do their best also on low-stakes tests (Ekl¨of, 2010; Hopfenbeck 
& Kjærnsli, 2016). Recently, the issue of how much motivation and 
effort that is “enough” to accurately show what one knows and can do 
has also been discussed. For example, a highly proficient student may be 
motivated to do well on a test but does not need to invest maximum 
effort but rather a sufficient amount (cf. Cs´anyi & Moln´ar, 2023; 
Lundgren, 2023). This again points to the intricate links between ability 
and motivation.
2.3. Differences in test-taking motivation in low- vs high-stakes contexts 
and across countries
Previous studies in the Swedish context using self-report measures of 
test-taking motivation (and the same effort scale we used in the present 
study) have shown significant differences in reported motivation and 
effort between low-stakes tests (PISA, TIMSS, national test field trials) 
and tests where the stakes for the students are higher (national tests, 
tests used for admission to higher education) (cf. Ekl¨of & Knekta, 2017; 
Knekta, 2017). For example, Ekl¨of and Knekta showed that while 88 % 
of Swedish Grade 9 students taking a national test in 2012 agreed they 
felt motivated to do their best on the test, and 89 % agreed they 
perceived the test as important, corresponding percentages for 15-year 
olds taking the PISA 2012 test was 63 % and 46 % (d = 0.8 and d =
1.2, respectively).
As regards cross-country differences in reported effort and motiva­
tion, there is evidence in the ILSA contexts that the level of effort varies 
across countries (cf. Debeer et al., 2014). The effort measure used in the 
present study was a national augmentation to the Swedish PISA student 
questionnaire which means comparisons with other countries cannot be 
made. However, in PISA 2018 the previously mentioned “Effort Ther­
mometer” was completed by students in all participating countries. 
Results show that overall, students report a reasonable level of effort on 
the PISA test, but also that they would have spent more effort on the test 
had it counted towards their grade (OECD, 2019). Further, Sweden 
ranks as one of the countries reporting lowest level of effort on PISA 
according to the thermometer, and largest difference between effort 
actually invested and hypothesized effort had the result counted towards 
their grade (OECD, 2019). On the other hand, on the time-based mea­
sure of effort that OECD calculates (based on a fixed rapid-guessing 
threshold), Sweden scored at the OECD mean. There are discrepancies 
between self-reported effort and time-based effort for most countries, 
which may be due to biases in the self-report, or misclassification in the 
time-based estimates. Just because a student does not respond exces­
sively rapid to an item does not necessarily mean the student was 
motivated to spend effort on the item (OECD, 2019).
2.4. The present study
The existing literature has highlighted performance differences in 
low vs. high-stakes testing environments, raising questions about the 
role of test-taking motivation and effort in influencing these differences. 
However, a crucial question persists: does low test-taking motivation 
bias test performance, or does it merely reflect initial differences in 
abilities? Reeve and Lam (2007) suggest that the correlation between 
test-taker reactions, such as test-taking motivation, and test perfor­
mance may be spurious due to the common antecedent of general 
cognitive ability. Based on the self-serving bias framework, and positive 
attribution bias theory (Heider, 1958; Nisbett & Ross, 1980; Whitley Jr. 
& Frieze, 1985), low-performing individuals might mitigate ego threat 
by reporting low motivation. In contrast, high-ability individuals engage 
in self-enhancement, evaluating the test more favorably and thus 
reporting higher motivation. A notable limitation of prior research is the 
lack of observation of student ability measured under high-stakes con­
ditions, leaving it ambiguous whether test-taking motivation is 
confounded by ability (see, however, Cole et al., 2008; Silm et al., 2019; 
Zilberberg, Finney, Marsh, & Anderson, 2014). Our study addresses this 
gap by combining low- (PISA) and high-stakes (national test) assessment 
outcomes for the same individuals. Specifically, we use the students' 
PISA reading score and their national test grade in reading, both as­
sessments conducted in grade 9. By including an external high-stakes 
assessment (national test grade) alongside self-reported effort in PISA, 
we can more effectively differentiate the roles of motivation and ability 
on test performance.
In addition to controlling for high-stakes test results, we investigate 
whether the relationship between students' PISA scores and their na­
tional test grades is moderated by their self-reported effort in the PISA 
test. Assuming that the result on the high-stakes national test approxi­
mates the best result a given student can achieve given his or her pro­
ficiency level, we hypothesize that the strength of the relationship 
between the low- and high-stakes tests is higher for students who report 
higher levels of effort in the low-stakes test. Students are typically more 
motivated to do their best in tests that have significant personal conse­
quences for them, such as counting towards their grades, compared to 
those without such stakes (Ekl¨of & Knekta, 2017; Knekta, 2017; Wise & 
DeMars, 2005). In the case of national tests in Sweden, the results are 
important for students as they play a role in their grades. Final grades, in 
turn, serve as a selection criterion for the next level in the school system, 
giving them a high-stakes character.
Our research questions are as follows: 
• Does self-reported test-taking effort predict low-stakes test perfor­
mance after controlling for high-stakes test scores (an indicator of 
general ability) in the same subject domain?

2.5. Contextual background
Before going into the study's methodology and analytical approach, a 
brief overview of Sweden's national assessment system is provided, 
along with key distinctions between the national tests and the PISA test.
In Sweden, teachers have full responsibility for assessment and 
grading, with grades awarded after a course or semester using a 
criterion-referenced grading system. For core subjects, centrally devel­
oped national tests and assessment materials are provided to ensure 
equitable grading practices. These national tests are administered in 
grades 3, 6, and 9, as well as in upper secondary school, covering various 
subjects and focusing on specific curriculum content. The national tests 
have high stakes for students as teachers are expected to use and, as per a 
2018 regulation, give special consideration to the results when assigning 
final grades (Swedish National Agency for Education, 2022). Further­
more, the final grades obtained in ninth grade serve as a selection cri­
terion for admission to upper secondary school, while grades from upper 
secondary school are used for university admissions, thereby intro­
ducing a high-stakes element into the grading system.
While the national tests and the PISA assessment have different 
purposes and designs, they share similarities in terms of content, 
cognitive processes, and constructs. Notably, there is a significant 
overlap between PISA tasks in all three subject areas—reading, mathe­
matics, and science—and the Swedish curriculum (Fr¨andberg & Hag­
man, 2017; Johansson, Klapp, & Ros´en, 2019; Sollerman, 2019), even 
though PISA is not directly aligned with national curricula. However, 
differences exist in administration format: PISA is computer-based, 
while the national tests were still paper-based in 2018. Additionally, 
PISA's reading comprehension component has employed an adaptive 
testing model since 2018, tailoring the test based on students' previous 
responses.
3. Data and method
3.1. Participants
We used a random sample of 5504 15-year-old Swedish students who 
took the PISA test in 2018 (50.2 % female) (for more information on 
sampling and non-response in PISA, see OECD, 2019). The majority of 
students (n = 5334) were enrolled in grade 9, with 108 students in grade 
8 and 62 students in upper secondary school (grade 10). During the 
administration of the Swedish PISA study in 2018, personal identifica­
tion numbers were collected from the participating students. This 
enabled linking the PISA student data with national registry data which 
included information about students' national test grades in grade 9, 
among other variables. Among the initial PISA sample, the large ma­
jority provided consent to connect their PISA data with registry data, 
leading to the availability of both PISA and registry data for 5432 stu­
dents (98.7 %). A data usage agreement was established with the 
Swedish National Agency for Education to utilize the combined PISA 
and national registry data, following approval from a regional ethical 
board in Sweden.
3.2. Measures and variables
3.2.1. Independent variable: national test results
The students' national test grade in reading in grade 9 (NT_Read), 
indicating a high-stakes assessment, and serving as an external measure 
of students' ability, was used as the independent variable. In grade 9, the 
national test in Swedish consists of three subtests: subtest A (speaking), 
subtest B (reading), and subtest C (writing). To achieve the best 
comparability to PISA reading, we used the results from the reading 
subtest only.
The reading subtest aims to assess four reading comprehension 
processes, which closely align with the reading processes outlined in the 
PISA framework (Tengberg & Skar, 2017). In the 2018 reading test, 
there were 23 items, both constructed-response (about 2/3) and 
multiple-choice (about 1/3). Raw scores are converted to the six-point 
grading scale employed in the Swedish school system. The grading 
scale ranges from A to F, with A to E representing passing grades and F 
signifying a fail grade. In this analysis, the grading scale was coded 0 (F) 
to 5 (A). Missingness is attributed to students who were absent on the 
day of testing and constituted 10.6 %.
3.2.2. Moderating variable: self-reported test-taking effort in PISA
As a measure tapping into all aspects of test-taking motivation was 
not possible to augment to the Swedish PISA student questionnaire, and 
as effort can be conceptualized as the outcome of a motivated state in 
relation to a given assessment, we used a four-item test-taking effort 
scale to measure students self-reported effort on the PISA test. The four 
items are listed in Table 1 (Descriptive statistics are shown in Table 2). A 
4-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly agree” to 4 = “Strongly disagree”) 
was employed as response format. All items were reverse-scored, asso­
ciating a low value with low test-taking effort. Missing responses ranged 
from 13.4 % to 14.1 %. The four-item scale showed high internal con­
sistency for the present sample, with Cronbach's alpha at 0.87. It can be 
noted that the Swedish students in PISA 2018 generally reported putting 
forth effort into the PISA test (M = 2.80), with some variability as ex­
pected (SD = 0.69). It is also essential to highlight that the scale provides 
a holistic measure, with students reporting their test-taking effort for the 
entire PISA test, rather than focusing on a specific subject domain. A 
latent factor (PISA TTE) representing the four items was constructed (see 
further information below), with higher scores on this factor denoting a 
higher degree of the test-taking effort construct exhibited by a student.
As regards gender differences, there was a minor difference in mean 
composite scores (d = 0.03); girls: 2.82 (SD = 0.64), and boys: 2.78 (SD 
= 0.75). The larger standard deviation for boys indicates more vari­
ability. Previous studies have noted gender differences in test-taking 
motivation with males exhibiting greater variability between high and 
low-stakes assessments compared to females, and females generally 
putting in more effort than males (Braun, Kirsch, & Yamamoto, 2011; 
Coffman & Klinowski, 2020; DeMars, Bashkov, & Socha, 2013; Ekl¨of, 
2007). However, considering the lack of significant differences in re­
ported test-taking effort between boys and girls in the Swedish PISA 
2018 sample, it was decided not to include gender as a covariate in the 
present analyses.

mathematics and science serving as minor domains. Students took a two-

3.3. Data analyses
To address our research questions, PISA test scores were regressed on 
self-reported test-taking effort in PISA, the national test grade 
(NT_Read), and the interaction between them. We use confirmatory 
factor analysis (CFA) to model PISA TTE as a latent variable (PISA TTE), 
using the four items from the PISA effort scale as indicators, and sub­
sequently apply latent moderated structural equations modeling (LMS) 
(Klein & Moosbrugger, 2000). This method outperforms traditional 
regression analysis with raw scores as it accounts for measurement error 
through the latent variable.
To set the scale for the latent variable, the mean was fixed to 0 and its 
variance to 1. The observed predictor, the national test grade, was also 
centered, which facilitates the interpretation of the main effects in the 
presence of a statistically significant interaction effect (Aguinis & 
Gottfredson, 2010). A two-step estimation approach was employed to 
assess latent variable interactions (Muth´en, 2012), estimating main ef­
fects before introducing the interaction term. Analysis of structural 
equation models (SEM) was performed using Mplus version 8.5 (Muth´en 
& Muth´en, 1998–2017), using the maximum likelihood estimator with 
robust standard errors (MLR). When incorporating the latent interac­
tion, the analysis command specified “TYPE = RANDOM” to estimate 
the random slopes, while “ALGORITHM = INTEGRATION” was used to 
enable numerical integration for estimating interaction effects.
3.3.1. Model fit
An acceptable fit for the CFA and SEM models was determined by a 
comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) of ≥0.95, a 
root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA) of ≤0.06, and 
standardized root mean squared residuals (SRMR) of ≤0.08 (Hu & 
Bentler, 1999). The χ2 test was also considered.
As the fit indices discussed above are not provided for LMS models in 
Mplus, we evaluated differences in model fit between models with and 
without interaction terms based on log-likelihood values. Additionally, 
we used Akaike information criteria (AIC) and Bayes information 
criteria (BIC) to compare the nested models, with lower AIC and BIC 
values indicating a better fit to the data (Kelava et al., 2011).
3.3.2. Hierarchical data, missing values and weights
The hierarchical nature of the data, including the clustering of stu­
dents within schools, was taken into account using the design-based 
correction of standard errors available in Mplus (TYPE = COMPLEX 
with the CLUSTER option; Asparouhov, 2005). Missing data were 
addressed through full information maximum likelihood (FIML). Stu­
dent final weights (W_FSTUWT) were applied to correct for unequal 
sampling probabilities of students and to account for non-response.
3.4. Robustness checks
Two robustness checks were conducted. First, we replicated the LMS 
analysis using science instead of reading as the subject domain. This 
involved replacing the national test grade in reading with a composite 
score of the national test grades in Biology, Chemistry, and Physics.3
In the Swedish school system, national tests in natural science sub­
jects in grade 9 are randomly allocated to schools, with each student 
undertaking the test in only one subject (Biology, Chemistry, or Physics). 
The national science tests comprise four subtests, consisting of three 
theoretical parts with a mix of multiple-choice and open-ended ques­
tions, and one laboratory test. Test scores from the subtests are aggre­
gated and converted to the six-step grading scale, as described 
previously. The aggregated national test results in the three science 
subjects were summed into a composite score (NT_Science; Descriptive 
statistics are given in Appendix B). We were unable to utilize the grade 9 
mathematics national test results as the mathematics test was illegally 
spread on the internet in 2018 (Swedish National Agency for Education, 
2022).

4. Results

An observed positive correlation of r = 0.66 between reading 
achievement in PISA and the National Test grade in reading (NT_Read) 
indicates a moderately strong relationship between students' perfor­
mance in the PISA assessment and their performance in the national test. 
Higher test-taking motivation in PISA, as measured by the questionnaire 
items assessing self-reported effort (Effort1 to Effort4), was also posi­
tively and significantly correlated with higher levels of PISA reading 
achievement, although the strength of these relationships was relatively 
weak (ranging from 0.13 to 0.19). Moreover, the correlation between 
the four PISA test-taking effort items and the high-stakes National test 
grade (NT_Read) was positive but very weak (ranging from 0.05 to 
0.09). This indicates that students with varying levels of reading ability, 
as assessed by the high-stakes national test, are likely to report both low 
and high effort in PISA. Additionally, the four effort items showed 
moderate to high positive inter-correlations, indicating that they 
captured a consistent construct.
4.1. Confirmatory factor analysis of test-taking effort items in PISA
Prior to the main analyses, confirmatory factor analysis was per­
formed to test the measurement model of PISA TTE. The model showed 
excellent fit, Satorra-Bentler corrected (SB)-χ2 [2] = 0.575; p = .607, 
RMSEA [90 % CI] = 0.000 [0.000, 0.023]; CFI = 1.000; TLI = 1.001, 
SRMR = 0.002), with standardized λ ranging from 0.76 to 0.82. There 
was a very weak positive relationship between PISA TTE and the Na­
tional Test grade in reading (NT_Read), r = 0.07.
4.2. Testing the motivation x ability interaction
Three models were specified in which PISA reading achievement was 
regressed on the latent variable PISA TTE and the high-stakes national 
test grade in reading in a stepwise manner. Unstandardized and stan­
dardized path coefficients are presented in Table 3. When entering the 
PISA TTE latent variable as a single predictor in the first step (Model 1, 
Table 3), a positive, moderate relation with PISA reading achievement 
was observed (b = 22.18, SE = 2.10, p < .001, β = 0.21), indicating that 
a one-standard deviation unit increase in TTE is associated with an in­
crease in 22 points on the PISA reading achievement scale. However, to 
consider the possibility that students with lower ability may downplay 
motivation to protect their ego, while high ability individuals engage in 
self-enhancement, viewing the test more positively and reporting higher 
motivation, the National test grade in reading (NT_Read) and PISA TTE 
were simultaneously included as predictors in Model 2 (Table 3).
While keeping the national test grade constant (our proxy for abil­
ity), PISA TTE maintained a significant positive, although somewhat 
weakened, relationship with PISA achievement (b = 16.25, SE = 1.85, p 
< .001, β = 0.15). This implies that when students' national test grades 
in reading are held at a comparable level, those who reported higher TTE 
in the PISA test still performed significantly better, corresponding to an 
expected increase of 16 PISA points for a one-standard deviation unit 
increase in TTE. Additionally, the National test grade in reading 
(NT_Read) demonstrated a significant predictive relationship with PISA 
achievement (b = 47.69, SE = 1.05, p < .001, β = 0.65). Model 2 fit the 
data well (χ2(9, N = 4919) = 29.526, RMSEA = 0.022, CFI = 0.997, TLI 
= 0.995, SRMR = 0.029) and accounted for 44.3 % of the variance in 
PISA achievement.
We hypothesized that students with higher reported levels of TTE in 
PISA would show a stronger association between their performance on 
the high-stakes national test and the low-stakes PISA achievement. This 
hypothesis was tested in Model 3, where the latent interaction term 
between PISA TTE and NT_Read was integrated. The interaction coeffi­
cient between PISA TTE and the national test grade was positive and 
statistically significant (b = 3.73, SE = 1.02, p < .001, β = 0.05), con­
firming our hypothesis. This finding implies that PISA test scores are 
more reflective of true ability for students reporting high test-taking 
effort than those reporting lower effort.
In moderation analysis, f2 is a standardized effect size that quantifies 
change in R2 as a function of the moderator (Hair, Hult, Ringle, Sarstedt, 
Danks, & Ray 2021; Memon et al., 2019). As the average effect size in 
tests of moderation has been found to be small (overall mean observed 
effect size was 0.009, and median 0.002, in Aguinis, Beaty, Boik, & 
Pierce, 2005), Kenny (2018) proposed f2 effect sizes of 0.005, 0.01, and 
0.025 as realistic standards of small, medium, and large effects. In the 
present study, we observed an f2 = 0.005, thus corresponding to a small 
effect.4

4.3. Robustness checks
Given that the TTE scale is a broad measure not specific to any 
particular subject domain, we conducted a first robustness check to 
assess if the interaction effect remained significant when replacing the 
outcome variable PISA reading scores with PISA science scores and using 
the composite measure of students' national test results in Biology, 
Physics, and Chemistry (NT_Science) as independent variable. The 
findings validate that the interaction effect is statistically significant also 
when PISA TTE and NT_Science were used as predictors together with 
the latent interaction term (b = 3.47, SE = 0.99, p < .001, β = 0.05). Path 
coefficients for Models 1, 2, and 3—with PISA science achievement used 
as the outcome—are presented in Table B.1 in Appendix B.
Second, we modeled linear relationships in the main analyses. While 
non-linear relationships can be examined, they introduce the risk of 
overparameterization, particularly in interaction analyses, which are 
already data-intensive (e.g., Nagengast et al., 2011). This challenge is 
further amplified when testing non-linear interactions. However, Gignac 
et al. (2019) suggested that the effect of TTE on low-stakes test perfor­
mance may diminish at higher levels—meaning that while low TTE has a 
strong impact, increasing TTE from moderate to high may yield no 
additional benefit. To explore this within our moderation framework, 
we squared the TTE variable, interacted it with national test scores, and 
included both in the model. The interaction effect (PISA TTE2 ×
NT_Read2) was small and not significantly different from zero (b = 0.43, 
SE = 0.51, p > .05), providing no evidence of a non-linear moderation 
effect. However, as noted earlier, non-linear interaction analyses further 
exacerbate the risk of overparameterization, making their interpretation 
challenging.
5. Discussion
This study explored the interplay between test-taking motivation and 
ability in predicting low-stakes performance using two achievement 
tests with differing stakes: national tests (high-stakes) and PISA (low- 
stakes) together with a self-report measure of test-taking effort. 
Assuming that the result on the high-stakes national test approximates 
the best result a given student can achieve given his or her proficiency 
level, we hypothesized that students who reported low test-taking effort 
in PISA would exhibit a weaker relationship between their PISA results 
and their national test grades, which in turn could be taken as an indi­
cation that lack of effort contributed to a less than optimal performance.
5.1. The combined influence of motivation and ability on low-stakes test 
results
Consistent with previous research, our results demonstrate a signif­
icant positive relationship between self-reported test-taking effort and 
test performance in the low-stakes test (b = 22.18; β = 0.21, p < .001). 
The positive influence of TTE on low-stakes performance was consistent 
when both PISA reading and science achievement were used as out­
comes. Similar associations of comparable magnitude have been re­
ported in previous studies examining the link between self-reported test- 
taking effort and performance in low-stakes assessments (e.g., r = 0.25 
in Butler & Adams, 2007; r = 0.27 in Ekl¨of & Knekta, 2017; r = 0.25 in 
Hopfenbeck & Kjærnsli, 2016; r = 0.33 in Silm et al., 2020).
One limitation of prior correlational research is the potential con­
founding of motivation by ability. Students with higher motivation 
might achieve better results not only because of their motivation but 
also because of their higher ability levels. Drawing on the self-serving 
bias hypothesis and positive attribution bias theory (Heider, 1958; 
Nisbett & Ross, 1980; Whitley Jr. & Frieze, 1985), low-performing in­
dividuals may reduce ego threat by reporting lower motivation. In 
contrast, high-ability individuals tend to engage in self-enhancement, 
perceiving the test more favorably and consequently reporting higher 
motivation. By including high-stakes national test results from grade 9 
alongside self-reported TTE in PISA, we were able to control for students' 
ability independently from test-taking effort. It was found that (1) PISA 
TTE showed a weak correlation with ability, as measured by the high- 
stakes national test (r = 0.07), indicating that ability does not 
confound this relationship, (2) the correlation between the low-stakes 
PISA test and the high-stakes national test was r = 0.66, reflecting a 
moderately strong relationship, and (3) the relationship between PISA 
TTE and PISA achievement weakened but remained significant (b =
16.25; β = 0.15, p < .001) when the high-stakes national test results 
were adjusted for. While some previous studies show that after con­
trolling for prior differences in general cognitive ability, test-taking 
motivation is unrelated to test performance (e.g., Reeve & Lam, 
2007), others have found that test-taking motivation indicators have an 
independent influence on low-stakes test performance beyond prior 
knowledge (Cole et al., 2008; Silm et al., 2019), and that test-taking 
motivation is weakly correlated with ability (Wise & Kong, 2005). 
This study similarly points to the role of test-taking effort making an 
individual contribution to low-stakes test performance above that of 
ability.
We hypothesized that students who reported low test-taking effort in 
PISA would exhibit a weaker relationship between their PISA scores and 
their national test results, thus underestimating their actual ability level. 
The interaction coefficient between PISA TTE and the national test grade 
was positive and statistically significant (b = 3.73; β = 0.05, p < .001), 
albeit with a small effect size. This finding implies that a more accurate 
assessment of PISA test performance occurs when test-taking effort is 
high. For more motivated individuals, the performance gap between 
PISA and the national test is thus smaller. These results highlight the 
practical impact of low TTE on the PISA scale, students with low TTE 
(−1 SD) underperform by approximately one-third of a school year's 
learning gain compared to those with high TTE (+1 SD), emphasizing 
the critical role of effort in obtaining an accurate assessment of student 
ability.
5.2. Implications for research and practice
The study's implications underscore the critical interplay between 
ability and motivation in low-stakes assessments, such as PISA. While 
policymakers often interpret results from low-stakes large-scale assess­
ments as indicative of students' abilities, it is equally important to 
recognize that low-stakes test scores go beyond measuring subject 
knowledge to encompass non-cognitive skills like motivation 
(Borgonovi & Biecek, 2016; Buchholz et al., 2022; Kyllonen & Kell, 
2018; Segal, 2012).
That scores on low-stakes tests may reflect both “skill and will”; both 
ability and test-taking motivation, does not necessarily imply the scores 
are invalid as such, only that both the skill and the will component needs 
to be acknowledged when interpreting results. Longitudinal studies 
following PISA participants have shown a positive association between 
PISA performance and significant life outcomes, such as high school 
completion and tertiary education attendance, indicating PISA's pre­
dictive capability (Borgonovi, Ferrara, & Piacentini, 2021; OECD, 
2010). Borgonovi and Biecek (2016) thus argue that low-stakes assess­
ments may offer valuable insights into students' abilities in tasks with 
limited external rewards, a type of task often encountered in real-life

situations (see also OECD, 2023). As pointed out by the OECD (2023), 
“PISA does not measure students' maximum potential but what students 
actually do in situations where their individual performance is moni­
tored only as part of their group's performance” (p. 298).
An additional layer of complexity arises from the varying levels of 
test-taking motivation in low-stakes tests such as PISA that have been 
observed across countries (e.g., Borgonovi & Biecek, 2016; Butler & 
Adams, 2007; Gneezy et al., 2019; OECD, 2023; Zamarro et al., 2019). 
For example, Zamarro et al. (2019) found that 32 to 38 % of the 
between-country variation in PISA scores can be explained by measures 
of effort. Consequently, the interpretation of test scores in PISA may 
have different meanings across countries, and as pointed out by Gneezy 
et al. (2019), “motivation may be an important confound in interna­
tional comparisons” (p. 305).
Given these considerations, it is important to continuously assess 
test-taking motivation and effort in low-stakes tests such as PISA, both 
within and across countries. Neglecting variations in test-taking moti­
vation could result in unreliable comparisons over time and between 
countries. If test-taking motivation remains consistent over time within 
a particular country, it is less likely to raise validity concerns. However, 
changes in test-taking motivation over time could suggest potential is­
sues related to the validity of test results, if not taken into account. 
Recently, OECD has reported that in the majority of countries, students 
in PISA 2022 reported less effort than they did in 2018. Further, for some 
countries with sharp performance declines between the two adminis­
trations, there was also a sharp decline in reported effort, and overall, 
there seems to be a strong association between differences in relative 
effort and performance differences in PISA (OECD, 2023). Also, previous 
research in the Swedish context has suggested that increase in reported 
effort could explain the performance increase observed between 2012 
and 2015 (Ekl¨of, Costa, & Knekta, 2017). It should however be noted 
that different modes of administration (change from paper- to computer- 
based testing between 2012 and 2015) and differences in major subject 
tested (reading in PISA 2018, mathematics in PISA 2022) could also 
have an impact on student ratings of effort.
5.3. Limitations and future directions
This study is not without limitations. Firstly, we cannot know the 
extent to which self-reported TTE reflects the real effort students 
expended on the test. While self-reporting is still a common way of 
assessing test-taking motivation (DeMars et al., 2013; Silm et al., 2020), 
and self-report methods offer valuable insights into the subjective 
experience of an individual, they have well-known disadvantages as 
they are sensitive to response biases (see Kankaraˇs, 2017 for a thorough 
review). With computer-based tests, more ‘objective’ and task specific 
measures based on student behavior during testing have become more 
common.
Yet, behavioral measures of TTE also come with challenges regarding 
complexity, interpretability, and the validity of conclusions. Straight­
forward time-based measures run the risk of confounding ability, task 
demand and effort (Lundgren, 2023), and although promising model- 
based approaches have been developed (Lundgren, 2023; Ulitzsch 
et al., 2020) such an approach was deemed unfeasible to apply in the 
current study, in particular considering the matrix sampling and adap­
tive test design applied in PISA 2018. Future studies could, however, 
benefit from combining self-report measures with behavioral measures 
(see, e.g., Borgonovi, Ferrara, & Piacentini, 2023; Hofverberg et al., 
2022) to obtain a broader picture of students' test-taking effort in the 
context of low- and high-stakes tests.
Secondly, the present study used a random sample of Swedish 15- 
year-old students, and the generalizability of the findings may be 
dependent on the specific educational context of Sweden, along with 
cultural factors. It is interesting to note that in the PISA 2018 effort 
thermometer, more than three out of four students in Sweden reported 
that they would have invested more effort in the PISA test had it counted 
towards their grades, which is higher than the OECD average of 68 % 
(OECD, 2019). Gneezy et al. (2019) argue that there may exist differ­
ences across countries and cultures in baseline levels of intrinsic moti­
vation to perform well on assessment tests, which holds particular 
relevance for low-stakes scenarios where external incentives are limited. 
Consequently, it can be hypothesized that in countries where the 
intrinsic motivation to perform well is lower, the moderating effect of 
TTE on the relationship between high- and low-stakes test outcomes is 
more pronounced compared to countries with higher initial intrinsic 
motivational levels. This warrants further exploration in future research 
where PISA results can be combined with high-stakes measures.
Similarly, other assessment emotions and how they relate to test- 
taking motivation is an area in need of further exploration. Just as 
level of intrinsic motivation may differ between countries, emotions 
such as pride or boredom may differ between samples depending on 
culture, how the assessment is presented or the overall assessment 
context. In the Swedish case, PISA is administered during the same part 
of the semester where students have their national tests, which could 
obviously have an impact on their feelings towards PISA. We have not 
encountered any research on assessment emotions in relation to test- 
taking motivation in PISA but recent research in other contexts have 
paid attention to this issue (cf. Finney, Satkus, & Perkins, 2020; Perkins, 
Pastor, & Finney, 2021) and may be worth considering in future studies 
in ILSA contexts as well.
Thirdly, due to the cross-sectional nature of the data, causal inter­
pretation with respect to test-taking effort and its moderating role on the 
relationship between the low-stakes PISA and the high-stakes national 
test cannot be made. A stronger design would involve experimentally 
manipulating test-taking motivation in a randomized setting, thereby 
eliminating potential confounding with other variables correlated with 
test-taking motivation. While our approach—using repeated observa­
tions of students in low- and high-stakes tests—addresses this issue to 
some extent, it does not fully eliminate the possibility of confounding, 
even though we do not identify clear confounders. However, a key 
strength of our approach is its high external validity, as it is based on 
large-scale, randomly drawn samples.
5.4. Conclusion
The aim of our study was to investigate whether test-taking moti­
vation seems to actually have an independent impact on low-stakes test 
scores or whether it is just a proxy for student ability. Our results suggest 
that ability is important for performance on PISA, but also that test- 
taking motivation is indeed a moderating factor. The relationship be­
tween students' national test results and their PISA achievement was 
found to be stronger for students who reported higher test-taking effort 
in PISA, meaning the PISA score for this group of students is a more 
accurate indicator of their true ability. The results demonstrate the 
practical impact of low test-taking motivation on PISA results, as stu­
dents with low TTE (−1 SD) were found to underperform by approxi­
mately one-third of a school year's learning gain compared to those with 
high TTE (+1 SD).
While ILSA test scores are often regarded as measures of students' 
abilities, policy makers and other stakeholders should also acknowledge 
that low-stakes test scores reflect not only subject knowledge but also 
non-cognitive factors like test-taking motivation. As the level of test- 
taking motivation in PISA has been found to vary across countries and 
student groups, further research is needed to investigate how these 
differences impact score comparability.
