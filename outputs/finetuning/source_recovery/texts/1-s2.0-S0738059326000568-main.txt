This study investigates how distinct cultural scripts of collectivism moderate the conversion of home possessions
into scientific literacy. Analyzing PISA 2022 data from 230,078 students across 37 countries, multilevel models
reveal three pathways: (1) In Latin America, relational collectivism amplifies wealth as a protective buffer in
high-inequality systems; (2) In Muslim-majority societies, deontological collectivism creates a cultural-metrical
misalignment, attenuating returns on PISA-defined cultural assets; (3) In East/Southeast Asia, achievementoriented collectivism fuels a paradox of resources, where wealth secures positional advantage but diminishes
returns on basic educational resources. Findings demonstrate that social reproduction mechanisms are culturally
contingent, urging a shift from universal best practices to context-sensitive equity policies.

2. Literature review and theoretical framework
2.1. Family capital and the development of scientific literacy
The family environment constitutes a primary determinant of
adolescent educational trajectories—a crucible wherein economic and
cultural resources converge with class-differentiated parental beliefs
and child-rearing practices to shape academic outcomes (Lareau, 2018;
Pinquart, 2016). To explain the persistent association between family
background and achievement, scholars have long invoked capital theory
(Bourdieu, 1986; Coleman, 1988). This framework posits that families
transmit advantage through a portfolio of convertible assets, a mechanism robustly validated in contemporary research (Jæger and Karlson,
2018; Tan et al., 2019). This lens proves particularly salient for examining scientific literacy, a critical 21st-century competency extending
beyond content mastery to encompass evidence-based reasoning and
civic engagement (OECD, 2023). Its development is not a mere classroom transaction but an ecological process, contingent on synergistic
learning environments (National Research Council, 2015). Within this
ecology, the family functions as the foundational site for mobilizing
"science capital" (Archer et al., 2015), rendering scientific literacy an
ideal outcome for assessing how multidimensional family capital converts into academic capability.
However, dominant models of capital conversion often carry an
implicit assumption of universality. This study challenges that assumption by examining four key types of home possessions, attending to both
their established mechanisms and their potential for cultural variation.
Family wealth (economic capital) is traditionally viewed as enabling
investment in enriched learning environments (Duncan and Murnane,
2011), yet its function is highly context-dependent. In highly unequal
systems, it may primarily serve as a protective buffer against systemic
deficiencies, while in hyper-competitive systems, it may be the key to an
educational "arms race" for positional goods like elite tutoring (Bray,
2022). Similarly, the value of cultural possessions (generalized cultural
capital), such as art and classic literature, is socially constructed. Their
"exchange rate" may be inflated in highly stratified societies or devalued
where they misalign with local cultural canons (Lamont and Lareau,
1988). Home educational resources (instrumental capital), such as a
desk or a quiet study space, are often seen as universal prerequisites for
learning, as they can minimize extraneous cognitive load (Sweller et al.,
2019). However, their potentially declining marginal utility in
resource-saturated environments and their subordination to other forms
of investment raise questions about their contemporary importance.
Finally, books in the home (specific cultural capital) serve as a classic
proxy for a home’s scholarly culture, strongly linked to achievement
(Evans et al., 2010). Yet, scholarly consensus suggests that sheer quantity is a crude proxy at best; the value of this objectified capital is
contingent on its activation through parental practices (Jæger and
Breen, 2016). This logic of activation and domain-specificity is critical.
For example, developmental psychology reveals that home environments for literacy and numeracy function as distinct systems
(Skwarchuk et al., 2014), and even within a single domain, specific
input (e.g., talk about a number’s concrete meaning) is far more predictive than general conversation (Gunderson and Levine, 2011). This
developmental logic applies directly to science education, where cultivating a scientific mindset requires targeted, domain-relevant resources
and interactions (Guler and Baydar, 2023). This underscores the
importance of not just the quantity but the quality and type of books. For
instance, dramatic cross-national variations in the returns on home

2.2. The cultural ecology of capital conversion
To transcend universalistic assumptions, we integrate capital theory
with ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner, 1979) and cultural
psychology. Here, the family microsystem—where capital conversion
occurs—is embedded within a macrosystem of cultural values, beliefs,
and structures (Bornstein, 2017). This macrosystem acts as a filter,
shaping what is considered a valuable asset and which parental practices
are deemed effective, and ultimately, setting the exchange rate for
converting family capital into educational success (Gelfand et al., 2017;
Markus and Kitayama, 2010). Consequently, while the family drives
capital conversion, the cultural macrosystem dictates its operational
pathways.
While broad collectivist dimensions can support learning via an
interdependent self-concept (Markus and Kitayama, 1991), their effect
on capital conversion is dynamically configured by specific cultural
meaning systems (Adams and Markus, 2003). The simplistic
individualism-collectivism dichotomy has proven inadequate for
explaining cross-cultural variance in educational processes (Oyserman
et al., 2002). Therefore, moving beyond this coarse-grained distinction
is essential. Drawing on Brewer and Chen's (2007) seminal clarification,
this study deconstructs the general label of collectivism. They argue that
the term masks at least two distinct forms: one centered on interpersonal
relationships and obligations (Relational Collectivism) and another
focused on loyalty to a broader group identity (Group Collectivism).
Building on this logic, we conceptualize culture as a repertoire of
"patterned practices" (Adams and Markus, 2003, p. 346) that steers how
different cultural clusters mobilize resources. We adapt this framework
to propose three such repertoires relevant to education, each hypothesized to constitute a unique pathway of capital conversion. These
pathways illuminate how cultural groups activate their own scripts to
navigate the border crossings between indigenous knowledge systems
and the domain of science (Aikenhead and Jegede, 1999), ultimately
charting distinct ecological routes of capital conversion.
In the context of Latin America, a region marked by deep-seated
wealth disparities (Gandelman and Lluberas, 2023), what Brewer and
Chen (2007) term Relational Collectivism manifests powerfully. This is
channeled through the cultural script of familismo, which frames the
family and community not merely as a passive buffer, but as a unit for
active, collective struggle and support (lucha comunal) against institutional fragility (Rusoja, 2024; Suárez-Orozco and Suárez-Orozco, 2009).
A recent meta-analysis of 163 studies confirmed that familism values,
particularly the sense of family obligation, are consistently linked to
positive adjustment among Hispanic/Latino individuals, acting as a
crucial psychosocial resource (Cahill et al., 2021). This cultural script
operates within a structural context marked by entrenched class stratification (Portes and Hoffman, 2003) and educational systems that, far
from being engines of mobility, often serve to perpetuate these very
inequalities (Carnoy and Marteleto, 2021; Stromquist, 2004). In this
ecology, family capital is theorized to assume a profoundly protective
function. The "exchange rate" for family wealth is amplified not merely

A second distinct script, termed Deontological Collectivism, is
prevalent in many Muslim-majority societies. This cultural logic, interwoven with Islamic values, frames the pursuit of knowledge as both an
act of worship (‘ibadah) and a communal obligation (fard kifayah) (see
Al-Attas, 1980; Berkey, 2003). This framework reveals a potential
cultural-metrical misalignment between indigenous intellectual capital
and PISA’s secular, Western-centric metrics. Critically, this misalignment reflects a global epistemic hierarchy (Connell, 2020), where
Northern assessment tools often fail to capture Southern realities. For
instance, homes rich in revered religious texts constitute significant local
capital but remain invisible to PISA.
This potential misalignment is not merely a technical issue of measurement but reflects a deeper global epistemic hierarchy (Connell,
2020; Said, 1977), where Northern theories and metrics often fail to
capture Southern realities. PISA's standardized assets, such as classic
European literature, may not hold the same cultural currency as revered
religious or local literary traditions (Ridge, 2014). This tension has
historical roots; as Eickelman (1992)argued, the expansion of modern
mass education in Muslim societies did not erase traditional modes of
learning but created a complex co-existence where different knowledge
systems compete for legitimacy. A home rich in revered religious texts,
therefore, constitutes immense local cultural capital but is likely to
remain invisible to PISA's metrics.
Based on this logic, we advance our Cultural-Metrical Misalignment
Hypothesis: We hypothesize that the observed effect of PISA-defined
cultural capital will be significantly attenuated in the Muslim-majority
cluster. This attenuation, we argue, may not reflect a weaker underlying process of cultural transmission, but rather a measurement artifact
inherent to the way international assessments like PISA construct a
simplified and standardized reality, leading to an epistemic disconnect
with local contexts (Gorur, 2016).
A third pathway is shaped by what can be termed AchievementOriented Collectivism, a script prominent in East and Southeast Asia
that, while also relational, distinctively channels collective effort toward
educational attainment as the principal path to family honor and social
mobility (Chao and Tseng, 2002; Cheng and Kaplowitz, 2016). This
cultural imperative often manifests through specific parenting strategies
that channel family resources into the shadow education system (Zhang,
2020). As empirically demonstrated in the Korean context (Park et al.,
2011), this investment is not merely supplementary but is a central
mechanism through which parental involvement and resources translate
into cognitive outcomes. This reliance on purchased educational services sets the stage for our hypothesized paradox. This cultural imperative, coupled with high-stakes examination systems, has fueled a
hyper-competitive educational arms race, giving rise to a massive
"shadow education" industry of private tutoring that functions as a
parallel, market-driven system (Bray, 2022). This intense focus on exam
performance creates a unique resource ecology. In this high-pressure
environment, we theorize the emergence of a "paradox of resources."
On one hand, the instrumental value of family wealth is expected to be
amplified, as it is repurposed from acquiring basic goods to purchasing
decisive positional advantages—namely, elite tutoring and specialized
test-preparation courses, which represent a hyper-commodified form of
concerted cultivation (Choi and Park, 2016; Lareau, 2018). On the other
hand, we posit that this intense focus on external, specialized instruction
diminishes the perceived value and relative impact of common,
home-based assets. This leads to our hypothesis of a bifurcated moderation pattern: a significantly amplified effect for family wealth, coupled

with an attenuated or even negative effect for home educational
resources.
2.3. The present study
The preceding review reveals a critical tension: while capital theory
provides a powerful lens for understanding educational inequality, its
core mechanisms are often assumed to be culturally universal. This
assumption overlooks the profound ways in which the cultural macrosystem calibrates the meaning, value, and function of home possessions.
It prompts the question: Is the “exchange rate” for these assets a global
constant, or is it a local variable shaped by distinct cultural scripts? To
address this gap, our study moves beyond a monolithic view of collectivism to provide a more culturally calibrated understanding of social
reproduction. This is guided by two central research questions:
RQ1: To what extent are four key types of home possessions (family
wealth, cultural possessions, home educational resources, and books in
the home) associated with adolescent scientific literacy across a diverse
sample of countries?
RQ2: How is this global relationship systematically moderated by
distinct cultural ecologies? Drawing on our theoretical framework, we
test three divergent hypotheses, each corresponding to a unique cultural
pathway:
(a) The protective buffer hypothesis: Are the returns on home possessions, particularly wealth, amplified in the high-inequality contexts
of Latin America?
(b) The cultural-metrical misalignment hypothesis: Are the returns
on PISA-defined cultural assets attenuated in Muslim-majority societies?
(c) The paradox of resources hypothesis: In the hyper-competitive
systems of East and Southeast Asia, is the return on wealth amplified
while the return on basic instrumental resources is simultaneously
attenuated?
3. Data and method
3.1. Data source and sample
This study utilizes the 2022 cycle of the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA), a triennial international survey organized by
the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD).
PISA assesses the competencies of 15-year-old students in reading,
mathematics, and science and collects extensive background data. The
2022 cycle, with its focus on scientific literacy, provides an ideal dataset
for this research.
The analytical sample was constructed based on theoretical framework of differentiated collectivism. Moving beyond broad cultural labels, we classified participating countries and economies into three
theoretically meaningful, mutually exclusive clusters, with the remaining countries constituting a reference group. The classification was
informed by established frameworks in cross-cultural research (e.g.,
Hofstede, 2001; House et al., 2004) and refined to align with the study's
focus on educational processes, considering shared historical, linguistic,
and structural-economic features relevant to education. We acknowledge that any such clustering is a simplification of complex realities, we
employ this approach as a necessary heuristic to systematically test our
culturally-grounded hypotheses. The four clusters are defined as follows:
The Latin American Cluster (N = 6): This group includes Spanish and
Portuguese-speaking nations in the Americas, unified by shared Iberian
colonial histories, the prevalence of Catholicism, and a distinct cultural
script of familismo, which emphasizes collective well-being and
communal resilience (Rusoja, 2024).They are also often characterized
by high levels of socioeconomic inequality, a critical structural factor
influencing education (Portes and Hoffman, 2003).
The Muslim-majority Cluster (N = 10): This cluster brings together
countries from diverse geographical regions where Islam provides a
shared macro-level cultural framework. While acknowledging immense

employed a series of two-level hierarchical linear models (HLM) using
HLM 6.08 software. To test our hypotheses about divergent cultural
pathways, we estimated four separate random-intercept, random-slope
models, one for each family asset variable. This approach allows for a
clear examination of how cultural context moderates each specific
family asset-scientific literacy relationship.

3.2.2. Independent variables: home possessions
Four standardized indices (M=0, SD=1) from PISA were used to
capture the multifaceted nature of home possessions. To mitigate multicollinearity and isolate the unique moderation effect on each asset, we
estimated their impacts in separate models: (1) Family wealth (economic capital): A composite index of 13 items measuring material and
digital resources (e.g., cars, laptops).; (2) Cultural possessions (objectified cultural capital): An index of 8 items assessing the presence of
culturally significant goods (e.g., works of art, classic literature).; (3)
Home educational resources (instrumental capital): A composite of 5
items measuring proximal learning supports (e.g., a computer for
schoolwork, educational software).; (4) Number of books in the home
(specific cultural capital): A single-item (PISA item: ST255Q01JA)
measure serving as a classic proxy for the home's "scholarly culture,"
included for its established role in fostering a literate environment.

5.1. The global baseline and the insufficiency of instrumental capital
Our analysis first establishes a critical global baseline (RQ1). Family
wealth, cultural possessions, and books robustly predict scientific literacy, reaffirming capital’s foundational role (Jæger and Karlson, 2018;
OECD, 2023). Strikingly, however, home educational resources
(instrumental capital) exhibited weak and inconsistent associations with
achievement, with effects significantly attenuated across all collectivistic clusters.
This counter-intuitive finding challenges the presumption that
providing basic study tools (e.g., desks, computers) constitutes a universally effective equity intervention. Instead, it supports a more sophisticated interpretation of capital theory: the mere presence of
instrumental capital is insufficient. Its value is only unlocked when
activated by embodied cultural capital—the specific parental practices
and skills needed to guide its use (Jæger and Karlson, 2018; Lareau,
2018). In complex learning environments, these resources are necessary
but insufficient; they fail to address stratified capacities to leverage
them. This aligns with research on digital divides, where inequality
increasingly stems from skills (Helsper, 2021) rather than access (Van
Dijk, 2020). As UN reports note, Latin America exemplifies this trend,
with profound disparities in digital competency development despite
rising device access (Herrera et al., 2025). Our results empirically substantiate that the global educational advantage now resides less in
possessing resources than in cultivating capacities to deploy them.

5.2. Divergent pathways of capital conversion
Our central contribution lies in demonstrating how the capital conversion process follows three divergent pathways, each recalibrated by a
distinct cultural macrosystem.
(a) The Protective Buffer Pathway in Latin America: Amplified
5

M. Wen et al.

International Journal of Educational Development 122 (2026) 103546

returns amidst systemic inequality. Our findings for Latin America
strongly support the Protective Buffer Hypothesis (RQ2a). In this
pathway, both family wealth and cultural possessions yield significantly
amplified returns. Against a backdrop of entrenched class structures
(Portes and Hoffman, 2003) and an educational landscape that perpetuates inequality (Carnoy and Marteleto, 2021), family capital assumes a
critical protective function. This is not merely about protecting children
from physical insecurity, but from educational obsolescence. It is often
actualized through parental networks that shape wage expectations and
mobility pathways (Damdinsuren et al., 2024). This process is culturally
channeled through the script of familismo, which mobilizes family and
community resources as a form of collective organizing for survival and
advancement (Cahill et al., 2021; Rusoja, 2024). As documented by
CEPAL, public education systems in Latin America struggle to equip
students with the necessary digital competences for the modern world
(Herrera, Huepe, and Trucco, 2025). Consequently, family wealth becomes the primary tool to purchase access to private institutions or
enrichment programs that can bridge this critical skills gap. The
amplified return on wealth is, the price of securing a future-proof education in a region where the public system is lagging behind. Wealth is
leveraged not merely for a marginal advantage, but to purchase foundational educational security—safer schools and better-resourced private institutions—thereby insulating children from systemic precarity
(Carnoy and Marteleto, 2021). The simultaneous negative moderation
for home educational resources is particularly revealing. It may indicate
a pattern of misaligned investment, whereby less-advantaged families
prioritize tangible but less effective goods (e.g., a computer), while
wealthier families leverage wealth for access to what truly confers
advantage in this context: exclusive schooling and elite networks.
(b) The misalignment pathway in the Muslim world: A question of
epistemic recognition. In the Muslim-majority cluster, our findings
validate the Cultural-Metrical Misalignment Hypothesis (RQ2b). The
significantly attenuated returns on PISA-defined cultural assets (books,
cultural possessions), juxtaposed with the stable effect of family wealth,
indicate that the issue lies not in a failed cultural transmission, but in
what is being measured. Concretely, PISA’s standardized indices overlook locally esteemed capital (e.g., religious texts), thereby suppressing
observed effects. The empirically illustrated that the link between PISA's
cultural asset index and student achievement is notably weak. Our
framework compellingly reinterprets this phenomenon: the issue is not
that local cultural capital is problematic or deficient, but rather that it is
rendered invisible by an assessment framework that fails to recognize
revered local assets as valid forms of capital. Our results resonate
powerfully with critiques of PISA’s performative power to construct a
singular, globally legible reality (Gorur, 2016). As we theorized using
the concept of Deontological Collectivism, the pursuit of knowledge in
many of these societies is fostered through assets like religious literacy
and revered texts that PISA simply cannot see (Al-Attas, 1980; Berkey,
2003). This phenomenon is not abstract; recent scholarship on the
Arabian Gulf, for instance, provides concrete, regional evidence of how
local educational cultures create learning realities that diverge sharply
from the premises of international assessments (Dickson, 2025). Similarly, specific national-level studies, for instance in Turkey, highlight the
influence of unique local factors on literacy skills that standardized
models may not fully capture (Özdemir, 2024), lending further empirical weight to our misalignment interpretation.
Therefore, the attenuated effect we observe is interpreted as an
artifact of epistemic injustice (Connell, 2020). This injustice is rooted in
a fundamental methodological problem well-known in cross-cultural
research: the lack of measurement invariance. The assumption that an
instrument measures the same construct in the same way across cultures
is fragile. Indeed, advanced methods have demonstrated that even
seemingly universal constructs require sophisticated statistical adjustments to ensure comparability (Lomazzi, 2018). Our findings powerfully
suggest that PISA's cultural capital index is a prime example of such
non-invariance, its measured effect suppressed by a failure to achieve

cultural and epistemic equivalence.
(c) The Paradox of Resources Pathway in East & Southeast Asia: An
educational arms race. This third pathway, identified in the East and
Southeast Asian cluster, confirms our Paradox of Resources Hypothesis
(RQ2c). It is characterized by two powerful, opposing forces: an
amplified return on family wealth and a diminished return on home
educational resources. This paradoxical pattern vividly illustrates the
dynamics of a hyper-competitive educational arms race fueled by
Achievement-Oriented Collectivism (Bray, 2022). In these
achievement-oriented societies, basic resources like a desk or a family
computer have become near-ubiquitous, thus losing their power to
confer a competitive edge (a saturation effect). Meanwhile, wealth becomes even more consequential, strategically deployed to purchase
decisive positional advantages. This process—whereby family wealth is
directly channeled into elite private tutoring to boost cognitive outcomes—is precisely the mechanism documented by Park, Byun, and Kim
(2011) in South Korea. Our findings thus provide macro-level, cross-national evidence for this dynamic of hyper-commodified "concerted
cultivation" (Kim, 2016;Lareau, 2018), where the critical learning support is outsourced and purchased, not merely provided within the home.
The non-significant interaction for generalized cultural assets in this
context is also telling; their signaling value may be dwarfed by the
overwhelming importance of targeted, exam-oriented academic investments. This process is powerfully explained by research on Tiger
Parenting, which shows middle-class families strategically use shadow
education as a primary service to be purchased (Zhang, 2020). This
perspective illuminates both sides of our paradoxical finding: the effect
of family wealth is amplified because it is the direct currency for purchasing these decisive tutoring services, while the impact of standard
home resources diminishes precisely because the most critical learning
activities have been strategically outsourced. This dynamic is so intense
that even major reforms aimed at reducing student burden, such as
China’s ‘double reduction’ policy, have been critiqued as potentially
'counter-effective', as they may simply drive the competition for
educational resources underground, further amplifying the role of private wealth (Zhang, 2023).
5.3. Theory implications
These findings yield two significant theoretical contributions. First,
our study moves beyond critique to empirically deconstruct collectivism
as a monolithic analytical category. By demonstrating that distinct
cultural scripts generate divergent pathways of capital conversion,
answer the call for more fine-grained cultural analysis (Santos et al.,
2017) and show precisely how lumping diverse societies together can
mask critical mechanisms of social stratification. Second, our research
enriches and contextualizes capital theory. We provide robust,
cross-national evidence that an asset's exchange rate is not intrinsic but
is contingent on the cultural macrosystem (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). The
function of wealth, for example, dynamically shifts from a tool for
competitive advantage to a protective buffer or a key to an arms race,
contingent upon the prevailing cultural script. In doing so, this study
offers a tangible, mechanistic account of the mutual constitution of
culture and psychology (Markus and Kitayama, 2010) in the domain of
education. By bridging sociology and cultural psychology to explain
how societal-level forces shape educational inequality, we contribute a
culturally-calibrated perspective to the broader project of Southern
Theory (Connell, 2020).
5.4. Policy implications: forging context-sensitive pathways to educational
equity
The findings from this study underscore that there is no single path to
educational equity. They compel a shift from adopting universal best
practices to designing context-calibrated interventions that align with
local mechanisms of social reproduction.

Equity in Latin America: Mitigating the need for a “Protective

resource distribution but also but also about recognizing diverse forms
of knowledge. Policy should move beyond simply measuring inputs.
International bodies like the OECD and national ministries must
collaborate with local scholars to co-develop assessment frameworks
that recognition and validate locally esteemed forms of cultural and
intellectual capital. This is a critical step toward the more inclusive social contract for education envisioned by UNESCO (2021).

beyond the “Paradox of Resources.” The paradox of resources indicates
that simple resource-provisioning policies are likely to be ineffective.
Interventions must be more sophisticated, addressing the systemic
drivers of the hyper-competitive arms race. Given that outright bans on
tutoring can be counter-effective (Zhang, 2023), a more strategic,
two-pronged approach is required: (1) reforming high-stakes examination systems to reduce their outsized influence, and (2) regulating the
shadow education market while simultaneously promoting more holistic
definitions of success within schools to rebalance the educational
ecosystem (Zhang and Bray, 2020).

6. Limitations and future directions

Informed consent statement

This study's conclusions should be interpreted in light of several
limitations, each of which opens avenues for future research. First, the
cross-sectional nature of PISA data precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies are needed to trace the dynamic conversion of capital over
time. Second, our use of country-level cultural clusters, while a necessary heuristic, masks significant intra-national heterogeneity. Future
research could explore this variation using multi-level models that
incorporate regional or individual-level cultural measures. Third, and
most central to our argument, our reliance on PISA's standardized asset
indicators warrants caution. Our misalignment hypothesis posits that
these measures may lack validity in certain cultural contexts. We
strongly advocate for future research to move beyond critique and
directly test this proposition. For instance, quantitative studies
employing advanced psychometric techniques like alignment optimization (cf. Lomazzi, 2018) could formally test the measurement invariance of PISA’s capital indices across our proposed clusters. Such an
analysis would not only provide a empirical test of our hypothesis but
could also pave the way for developing more culturally-equitable
assessment tools. Finally, our theoretical model could be expanded by
incorporating other cultural dimensions, such as power distance, which
may also moderate these processes (e.g., Bornstein and Lansford, 2019).
Building on this study's foundation, pursuing these more complex
models will be crucial for refining our understanding of how educational
equity is made—and unmade—across the world’s diverse cultural
ecologies.
