Competition and cooperation are multilevel, culturally constructed concepts with significant yet
contentious roles in education. Despite their importance, their multilevel associations with academic performance across cultures are underexplored. This study used multilevel modeling with
PISA 2018 data to examine how student and school-level competition and cooperation relate to
academic performance in reading, math, and science across eight cultures and 76 economies.
Results from the pooled sample indicated that cooperation generally demonstrated a stronger
positive association with academic outcomes than competition, with the associations being more
pronounced at the school level than at the individual level. Cultural and country-specific analyses
revealed nuanced findings: while most countries showed no significant associations, where significant relationships were found, cooperation tended to positively correlate with academic
outcomes. Conversely, competition tended to be negatively linked to performance, except in
African & Middle Eastern and Confucian cultures, where school-level competition exhibited
strong positive links to performance. Importantly, the findings highlight that the relationship
patterns vary by analytical level and cultural context, with significant implications for educational policy and practice across diverse cultures.

1. Introduction
Competition and cooperation are fundamental forms of human interaction, deeply embedded in everyday life and extensively
studied by researchers (Johnson, 2003; Johnson & Johnson, 1989). In education, as global competition intensifies, competitive school
environments are increasingly seen as a means to enhance students’ academic performance (Ho & Lu, 2019). Advocates argue that
competition serves as a motivational force, significantly influencing student behavior and performance (Baumann & Harvey, 2018;
Worrell et al., 2016). However, the impact of competition on academic outcomes remains contentious. Several studies have
demonstrated that within-school competitive climate and inter-school competition are associated with higher academic achievement
(Harrison & Rouse, 2014; Heller-Sahlgren, 2018; Meng et al., 2023; Ponzo, 2011; Rudolf & Lee, 2023). Yet, other research warns that
excessive competition can lead to adverse effects such as heightened anxiety and unethical behavior, which may result in disengagement, dropout, and decreased academic performance (Canning et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022).
Akin to competition, cooperation also presents both benefits and challenges. While some research links cooperation to enhanced
achievement and increased effort (Johnson & Johnson, 2005; Johnson et al., 2014), others highlight issues like social loafing among

students (Gillies, 2016). Social interdependence theory suggests that individuals achieve higher performance in cooperative settings
than in competitive ones (Johnson & Johnson, 1989, 2009; Johnson et al., 2010, 2014). However, a recent study by Rudolf and Lee
(2023) conducted in Korea suggested that a competitive school atmosphere may be more conducive to students’ academic performance
than a cooperative one. In fact, the impacts of competition and cooperation may vary depending on contextual factors.
Understanding these varying impacts requires considering the broader context in which competition and cooperation occur. Recent
studies have revealed that competition and cooperation are multilevel and culturally bound constructs (Fülöp, 2004; Park, 2024; Swab
& Johnson, 2019). They manifest at both individual and group levels and are shaped by diverse contexts and cultures. As multifaceted
constructs, competition and cooperation influence learning behavior and performance individually, through students’ perceptions,
and collectively, through the school’s climate (Park, 2024). As culturally constructed concepts, perceptions and attitudes towards
competition and cooperation vary significantly across cultures (Fülöp, 2004; Gonzalez-Cabrera, 2018). Consequently, the influence of
competition and cooperation on outcomes may vary across cultures.
Although extensive research has examined competition and cooperation in education, the multifaceted, multilevel interactions
between these factors and student performance remain understudied. Previous work often focuses on competition or cooperation in
isolation, overlooking their potential interplay (e.g., Li et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2023; Park, 2024). Moreover, most studies are
confined to single-country contexts and thus fail to address cultural variations (e.g., Meng et al., 2023; Rudolf & Lee, 2023). Park’s
(2024) study notably accounted for the multilevel nature of competition within specific cultural contexts but did not investigate
cooperation at individual and collective levels, focusing solely on mathematics. This narrow scope may limit the broader applicability
of its findings, given that different academic domains may respond differently to competition and cooperation. Cooperation itself may
also shape students’ experiences in unique ways, influencing outcomes through mechanisms distinct from those of competition.
Indeed, competition and cooperation are often interrelated and can be complementary, contingent on various contextual factors
(Gonzalez-Cabrera, 2018). Neglecting to examine both in tandem may therefore restrict our understanding of their interplay within
academic settings.
To address these gaps, this study utilized cross-country PISA 2018 data to examine how students’ perceptions of competition and
cooperation, alongside school-level climate, relate to academic performance in reading, mathematics, and science across diverse
cultural contexts. Specifically, the study aimed to: (1) determine whether competition or cooperation shows a stronger relationship
with student performance, (2) assess whether these relationships are more pronounced at the school or student level, and (3) examine
whether these patterns vary across cultures and countries. The specific research questions are:
(1) To what extent do student perceived competition and cooperation relate to students’ academic performance in reading,
mathematics, and science across different cultures and countries?
(2) To what extent do school-level competition and cooperation climates relate to students’ academic performance in reading,
mathematics, and science across different cultures and countries?
(3) Do the association patterns between student perceived and school-level competition/cooperation with academic performance
differ or align across cultures?
By examining these questions, this study aimed to clarify the roles of competition and cooperation in education, taking into account
individual perceptions, school climates, and cultural factors. The findings will offer important insights for educational policy and
practice, suggesting ways to leverage both competition and cooperation to enhance student performance across diverse contexts.
2. Literature review
2.1. Social interdependence theory
Social interdependence theory focuses on the action efficiency, internal psychological process, interaction modes, and outcomes of
individuals interacting with each other in cooperation and competition (Deutsch, 1949; Johnson, 2003; Johnson & Johnson, 1989,
2005). The basic premise of the theory is that: “how social interdependence is structured determines how individuals interact within
the situation, which, in turn, affects outcomes” (Johnson & Johnson, 1989, p. 5). Therefore, the theory provides a framework for
understanding how cooperation and competition affect students’ academic performance.
According to social interdependence theory, social interdependence exists when individuals’ outcomes are affected by others’
actions (Johnson & Johnson, 1989). When positive interdependence is structured, cooperation exists, which leads to individuals
interacting in ways that support each other’s success. When negative interdependence is structured, competition exists, which leads to
individuals blocking each other’s success. In other words, in positive interdependence (i.e., cooperation), individuals’ goal attainments
are positively correlated, so they have no conflict of interest, whereas the opposite holds true for negative interdependence (i.e.,
competition). Theoretically, both contexts can increase an individual’s effort to achieve and their connections with others, leading to
higher performance. Social interdependence theory posits that competitive conditions can, to an extent, increase efforts to achieve, and
improve task performance and productivity, but cooperation promotes higher levels of all these outcomes than the competition
(Johnson & Johnson, 1989). Moreover, in educational settings, cooperative environments confer greater advantages to students than
competitive ones (Johnson & Johnson, 2009).

2.2. Students’ academic performance in cooperative and competitive environments
The prior literature exploring students’ academic performance in cooperative and competitive environments has sought to
determine whether competition or cooperation offers greater benefits to student learning. Most research and practice on cooperative
learning have found that students tend to achieve higher in cooperation than they would if they were working competitively or
individually (Hsiung, 2012; Johnson & Johnson, 2009; Johnson et al., 2010; Slavin, 1985, 1996). However, some researchers have
pointed out that cooperation also comes with potential drawbacks, such as poor group dynamics and the free-rider tendency (Çelik
et al., 2013; Echazarra, 2020; Gillies, 2016).
Several meta-analyses have found that, relative to competitive contexts, cooperative contexts result in greater motivation and
higher academic achievement (Johnson & Johnson, 2005, 2009; Johnson et al., 2014; Qin et al., 1995; Roseth et al., 2008). The reason
why students tend to achieve less in competition than they would if they were working cooperatively is perhaps that, in competitive
contexts, individuals tend to engage in self-protective strategies (Johnson & Johnson, 2009). When in competitive situations, students
may withhold effort, create imagined impediments, and unrealistically lower expectations for succeeding and the importance they
attribute to the task, so as to make excuses for their failure and minimize anxiety about succeeding (Johnson & Johnson, 2009).
In contrast, some researchers have found that competition can be constructive and beneficial for performance in some circumstances (Johnson & Johnson, 1974, 1989; Sheridan & Williams, 2011; Sherif, 1978; Tjosvold et al., 2006). For example, Sheridan and
Williams (2011) study found that at least six dimensions (such as being neither winners nor losers and social comparison of competencies) constituted constructive competition, which can motivate students to learn and help them develop competencies. Furthermore, at the school level, some studies found that competition between schools tended to encourage better academic performance (Ho
& Lu, 2019; Harrison & Rouse, 2014; Heller-Sahlgren, 2018). Evidence from South Korea suggested that a competitive learning
environment positively predicted students’ reading, mathematics, and science scores, while a cooperative school climate significantly
predicted only science scores (Rudolf & Lee, 2023).
In summary, although free-riding behavior may occur in cooperative settings, social interdependence theory and related research
suggest that cooperative environments can enhance academic performance. In contrast, the effects of competition on academic outcomes remain inconclusive and appear to vary based on contextual factors.
2.3. Multilevel nature of competition and cooperation and the cross-cultural differences
Competition and cooperation are multifaceted constructs that operate at multiple levels within educational contexts and are
profoundly shaped by cultures (Fülöp, 2004; Park, 2024; Swab & Johnson, 2019). Their multilevel nature is evident at both individual
and institutional levels, in which competitive/cooperative school climate or students’ perceptions of these constructs shape motivation, engagement, and academic outcomes (Lee & Seo, 2022; Meng et al., 2023; Murayama & Elliot, 2012; Park, 2024). Notably,
individual perceptions of competition and cooperation can reflect reversed causality: higher academic performance may lead students
to view their environment as more cooperative or competitive, rather than these perceptions directly driving performance. Thus, such
findings alone provide insufficient guidance for policy, given the complexity of the underlying mechanisms. Despite the challenges in
interpreting these individual-level findings, a multilevel approach remains essential. Such an approach helps avoid the ecological
fallacy, in which group-level observations are inaccurately attributed to individuals, and vice versa (Mikk et al., 2016; Snijders &
Bosker, 2011). Examining competition and cooperation at each level offers theoretical insights into how they interact with contextual
factors and enables policymakers and educators to design interventions that target the most relevant level, thereby fostering more
effective academic outcomes.
The cultural dimension adds another layer of complexity to this analysis. Although studies on social interdependence in regions like
the Americas, Europe, the Middle East, Africa, Asia, and the Pacific Rim have produced consistent core findings (Johnson & Johnson,
2005), interpretations and manifestations of competition and cooperation differ significantly across cultures. Given cultural differences such as economic systems and values, different cultures have different definitions of cooperation and competition, as well as
their respective suitable conditions. As Fülöp (2004) argued, competition is a culturally constructed concept, and there are cultural
differences in people’s attitudes and concepts on competition. His study among Japanese and Hungarian university students found that
young people in the two countries had different concepts of competition and competitors (Fülöp, 2004). Moreover, research has found
that students from collectivist societies are more likely to perceive competition as positive, contributing to progress for both themselves and society. In contrast, students from individualistic countries tend to perceive competition as negative, often viewing it as a
zero-sum situation (Fülöp, 1999, 2004; Watkins, 2010). Park (2024) found that the positive relationship between student competitiveness and mathematics performance was enhanced when the student was from a culturally competitive country.
In summary, competition and cooperation are each associated with students’ academic performance at both the individual and
school levels. Cross-cultural psychology research underscores notable cultural differences in how these constructs are conceptualized,
as well as their inconsistent associations with student outcomes. However, to our knowledge, no study has yet examined—across
different cultures—the associations of both student perceived and school-level cooperation and competition with academic performance simultaneously. Consequently, whether competition or cooperation is more strongly linked to academic performance, at which
level these relationships are most pronounced, and whether these patterns remain consistent or vary across cultural contexts all remain
unclear.
To address this research gap, this study drew on nested data from multiple PISA 2018 countries to examine how student perceived
cooperation and competition, as well as school-level climates, relate to students’ academic achievement across eight distinct cultures.
The research is informed by Schwartz’s (2009) cultural values theory, which identifies seven orientations and maps national cultures

3. Method
3.1. Data
The present study used secondary data from the 2018 PISA conducted by the Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and
Development (OECD). PISA 2018 assessed 15-year-old students from 79 countries and economies in mathematics, science, and reading
literacy, with a particular focus on reading (OECD, 2019). In addition to academic performance, PISA collected extensive background
information on students, parents, teachers, principals, and schools.
To ensure representativeness, PISA employed a two-stage stratified sampling procedure (OECD, 2019). In the first stage, schools
serving 15-year-olds were selected based on explicit and implicit stratification variables. In the second stage, a sample of 42 students
was randomly selected from each school; if a school had fewer than 42 eligible students, all were included, but a minimum of 20
students per school was maintained.
This study used data from up to 576,142 students (49.8 % female) from 20,631 schools in 76 countries and economies. Canada,
Cyprus, and Vietnam were excluded due to missing or invalid information for the outcome or primary independent variables. The
remaining countries and economies were grouped into eight cultures based on Schwartz’s (2009) framework and a previous study
(Wang et al., 2021). Table 1 provides the included countries and economies within each culture, sample sizes, the percentage of female
students, and mean values of student- and school-level competition and cooperation.

3.2.2. Independent variables
The study focused on four key independent variables: student perceived competition, student perceived cooperation, school
competition, and school cooperation.
PISA 2018 used two multi-item questions to measure students’ perceptions of cooperation and competition at school, scaled with
item response theory to form two indices: PERCOOP (student perceived cooperation) and PERCOMP (student perceived competition).
Students rated how true each statement was for their school on a scale from “not at all true” to “extremely true.” Three items—“Students seem to value competition,” “It seems that students are competing with each other,” and “Students seem to share the
feeling that competing with each other is important”—comprised the PERCOMP index. Likewise, three items—“Students seem to value
cooperation,” “It seems that students are cooperating with each other,” and “Students seem to share the feeling that cooperating with
each other is important”—comprised the PERCOOP index.
School-level measures of competition and cooperation were derived using a two-step procedure. First, the “asrol” command in Stata
was used to calculate the mean perceived competition and cooperation of each student’s peers while excluding that student’s own
response (Rudolf & Lee, 2023). These peer averages were then aggregated at the school level, producing two school-level indices that
remain the same for all students within each school but vary across schools. Excluding the focal student’s own responses in the first step
helps mitigate reverse-causality bias and provides a more objective representation of the school climate.
In recognition of the risk that individual-level perceptions may suffer from reverse causality, the supplementary analysis restricts
the independent variables to the aggregated school-level competition and cooperation only. By removing the student perceived
competition and cooperation measures, this supplementary approach aims to more cleanly identify how a collectively perceived
competitive or cooperative climate within schools relates to academic outcomes.
3.2.3. Covariates
Previous research has identified individual demographic and personality traits, family background, and school characteristics as
significant influences on students’ academic performance. To assess the impact of competition and cooperation on academic outcomes
and mitigate the risk of omitted variable bias, this study incorporates these essential covariates, following Park (2024) and Rudolf and
Lee (2023).
In detail, individual characteristics include student gender, grade, a dummy variable for grade repetition, immigrant status, and a
comprehensive set of personality-related traits, such as attitudes toward competition, self-efficacy, learning ambition, and attitudes
toward learning activities.
Household characteristics comprise parents’ years of schooling, parents’ occupational status, household educational resources,
family wealth, number of books, and parents’ emotional support.
School characteristics encompass school size, a public-school dummy, student-to-teacher ratio, the proportion of teachers with a
master’s degree, indices of shortages in educational materials and staff, an index of poorer student behavior, the number of outside
competitors, and the school’s economic, social, and cultural status.
Detailed information and descriptive statistics on all variables are provided in Appendix Table S1.
3.3. Analytical strategy
To take into account the nested structure of PISA data, the present study applied two-level hierarchical regression models to assess
the associations of student-level and school-level cooperation and competition with academic performance. First, a null model without
any predictors was constructed to examine the overall percentage of variation in students’ academic performance between schools for
the pooled sample. The equations of the null model can be found below:
The equation for the null model is:

4. Results
4.1. Descriptive statistics, measurement reliability, validity, and invariance
Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations for the core variables in the pooled sample are presented in Table 2. The results
indicate that all four predictor variables were significantly and positively related to reading, mathematics, and science achievement,
although the correlation coefficients were generally modest. Notably, cooperation at both the student and school levels showed
stronger correlations with academic achievement than competition did. Moreover, the associations at the school level were slightly
stronger than those at the student level.
The correlation between student and school-level competition/cooperation was moderate (around 0.4), suggesting that these
constructs are distinct, thereby presenting a low risk of multicollinearity. To further assess multicollinearity, tolerance and Variance
Inflation Factor (VIF) statistics were examined for all variables. All tolerance values ranged between 0.391 and 0.957, exceeding the
threshold of 0.2, and all VIF values ranged from 1.04 to 2.56, remaining far below the critical 10, indicating no signs of collinearity (Yu
et al., 2015).
To assess the reliability and validity of student perceived competition and student perceived cooperation, I calculated Cronbach’s
alpha (α), McDonald’s omega (ω), composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE) for each economy. In line with
previous studies (Panesi et al., 2020; Viladrich et al., 2017), α, ω, and CR values above 0.70 indicate acceptable reliability, whereas
AVE values exceeding 0.70 are considered excellent and those above 0.50 are deemed acceptable. As shown in Table S2, all α values
exceeded 0.70—except in Lebanon, where α for student perceived competition was 0.689—implying acceptable reliability in most
instances. In addition, both McDonald’s ω and CR were above 0.70 for each construct across all countries and economies, suggesting
satisfactory internal consistency. Most AVE values also exceeded 0.50, except for student perceived competition in Lebanon, Moldova,
and Colombia, where AVE fell slightly below 0.50. Overall, these findings indicate that measures of student perceived competition and
student perceived cooperation exhibited acceptable convergent validity and reliability.
Furthermore, the measurement invariance of student-level perceived competition and cooperation across the eight cultural groups
was tested using a 2-factor multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model. This model conceptualized the two constructs as
correlated factors, each underpinned by their respective items. Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), TuckerLewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). A CFI and TLI greater than 0.90, and an RMSEA <0.08,
were considered acceptable fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999). For evaluating invariance, cutoff values of ΔCFI ≤ 0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, and
ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 were employed for configural invariance, and ΔCFI ≤ 0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.010 for metric and
scalar invariance, in line with Wang et al. (2021).

4.2. Multi-level analysis results based on the pooled sample and across eight cultures1
Table 4 presents the results of both the null and full models for the pooled sample. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was
calculated based on the null model to assess the proportion of variance in students’ academic performance attributable to the school
level across three academic subjects. The ICC values ranged from 0.491 to 0.518, indicating that approximately 50 % of the variance
exists between schools. This substantial between-school variation justifies the use of Hierarchical Linear Modeling (HLM) to examine
the relations of student-level and school-level competition and cooperation to students’ academic performance in the present study.
The full multilevel model results indicated that, after controlling for all covariates, imputation dummies, and country dummies,
students’ perceived competition was negatively associated with their performance in reading (β = − 1.588, p < .01), mathematics (β =
− 0.648, p < .001), and science (β = − 0.940, p < .001). In contrast, students’ perceived cooperation positively predicted performance
across these academic domains, with β ranging from 0.971 to 1.812 (ps < 0.001). At the school level, both competition and cooperation, were positively correlated with academic performance in all three domains. Notably, school-level cooperation had a particularly strong positive association, with β values ranging from 19.321 to 21.312 (ps < 0.001). For school-level competition, β values
ranged from 3.299 to 4.962 (ps < 0.001). Overall, at the pooled level, higher student perceived competition was linked to lower
academic performance, whereas higher student perceived cooperation, as well as school-level cooperation and competition—particularly cooperation—was associated with improved academic performance. Moreover, school-level competition and
cooperation were more strongly related to academic outcomes than their student-level counterparts.

showed a mixed pattern for the relationship of student-level and school-level competition and cooperation with academic performance

5. Discussion
Competition and cooperation are multilevel, culturally constructed concepts that permeate various aspects of human life. These
topics are both significant and contentious within the fields of teaching, learning, and educational research. Surprisingly, limited
studies have examined the relationship between these elements and educational outcomes across diverse cultural and national contexts
from a comprehensive and multilevel perspective. This study addresses these gaps by utilizing data from PISA 2018 to explore how
competition and cooperation at both the student and school levels relate to academic performance in reading, mathematics, and
science across eight cultures and 76 countries/economies.
The cross-national analysis of the pooled sample in this study showed that both school-level competition and cooperation, especially cooperation, were positively associated with academic performance across all three subjects. At the student level, perceived
competition was negatively related to academic performance, while perceived cooperation was positively associated, although these
associations were weaker than those observed at the school level. Cultural and country-specific analyses yielded more nuanced results,
highlighting the need for a multifaceted perspective encompassing various levels and cultures. In most countries, neither competition
nor cooperation at both levels was significantly related to academic performance. However, in areas where significant associations
were identified, school-level cooperation consistently had positive links to academic success. School competition was strongly positively correlated with performance in African & Middle Eastern and Confucian cultures but was less evident or even reversed
elsewhere.
This research sought to: (1) evaluate whether competition or cooperation is more beneficial for student academic achievement; (2)
identify if these associations are stronger at the student or school level; (3) examine potential differences across countries and cultures.
Regarding whether competition or cooperation is more beneficial for student achievement, this study demonstrated that cooperation was generally more positively associated with student achievement than competition. This aligns with social interdependence
theory and previous research on cooperative learning, which suggest that students achieve better academic results in collaborative
environments compared to competitive ones (Johnson & Johnson, 1989, 2009; Johnson et al., 2014; Slavin, 1985). This study not only
supports this conclusion at the pooled sample level, but also reveals differences in student-level and school-level, as well as across
cultures and countries, extending and complementing the findings. Notably, the results indicated that the positive correlation between
cooperation and academic achievement was more evident and significant at the school level. Conversely, the negative relationship
between competition and academic performance was more frequently observed at the student level but was stronger at the school
level.
Second, this study demonstrates that both cooperation and competition at the student and school levels are related to academic
success, with notable variations across cultures and countries. This supports the previously established multilevel model of competition (Park, 2024; Swab & Johnson, 2019) while extending it to incorporate cooperation. Park’s recent study (2024) showed that
competition at multiple levels is linked to math performance, with cultural factors moderating these associations. The present findings
build on that work by showing that cooperation at multiple levels is also associated with academic achievement, while revealing
12

International Journal of Educational Research 133 (2025) 102707

Y. Li

distinct cultural patterns. Notably, school-level competition and cooperation generally exhibit stronger relationships with academic
performance than student-level perceived competition and cooperation. This contrasts with Park’s finding that individual competitiveness was more closely related to math scores than the school’s competitive climate. The discrepancy may be explained by differences in how student-level competition was measured, as Park (2024) assessed students’ attitudes toward competition rather than
their perceptions of competition.
Third, this study revealed variations in the association patterns of competition and cooperation to academic performance across
cultures and countries, with some noteworthy cultural patterns. Notably, a strong positive relationship was observed between schoollevel competition and academic performance in African & Middle Eastern, and Confucian cultures, with the latter exhibiting particularly notable results. Conversely, this relationship was significantly negative in Eastern and Balkan Europe, as well as in Western
Europe. Additionally, student perceived cooperation typically exhibited a positive correlation with academic performance in many
regions, especially in Central and Baltic Europe, Eastern and Balkan Europe, and Western Europe. However, in African & Middle
Eastern, Confucian, and Latin American cultures, cooperation was negatively associated with reading performance. These findings
underscore the necessity of considering cultural factors in educational research and practice.
These cultural differences may stem from the distinct values intrinsic to each culture. Confucian cultures highlight hierarchy and
mastery, whereas African & Middle Eastern cultures emphasize embeddedness, both indicative of a collectivist orientation (Schwartz,
2009). In collectivistic societies, individuals are acutely aware of group competition (Wu & Talhelm, 2024). Consequently, students in
these cultures may be more driven to excel academically in competitive environments and less so in cooperative settings. Conversely,
in Eastern Central and Baltic Europe, Eastern and Balkan Europe, and Western Europe, mastery, hierarchy and embeddedness are less
emphasized (Schwartz, 2009). Therefore, students in these regions may feel more comfortable and motivated to achieve higher academic performance in a cooperative environment, but may feel too anxious in a competitive environment. Additionally, the generally
low competition climate in these cultures suggests that competition may not be highly valued. Consequently, high competition might
be a disadvantage for students’ academic performance in these cultures, yet high student cooperation could be an advantage. Park’s
study (2024) supported this notion, indicating that students from countries with a more competitive culture experience a more
pronounced positive association between students’ competitiveness and their mathematics performance.
Despite overall cultural coherence, notable cross-country differences exist within cultural groups concerning the relations of
competition and cooperation to academic performance. For instance, within Confucian cultures, school-level competition generally
exhibited a positive relationship with academic performance in mathematics, reading, and science, while student perceived cooperation showed a negative association. However, country-specific analyses revealed that the negative association between student
cooperation and academic performance is prominent in Korea, whereas in Singapore, cooperation positively correlates with reading
and science performance, and the positive relationship of school competition with academic success was not observed. These discrepancies likely result from institutional differences and cultural specificities within broad cultural categories, warranting further
investigation.
Finally, note that student perceived competition and cooperation may be subject to reverse causality: students with different academic achievements may perceive their environment in varied ways. This subjective variation could bias model estimates and
warrants caution when drawing policy implications. To address potential biases and better represent school climate, a supplementary
analysis examining only school-level competition and cooperation was conducted. Although this strategy does not fully address causal
concerns, it provides clearer insight into how broader school climate relates to academic outcomes. The results align with the main
findings: a strong cooperative school climate generally correlates with higher achievement across cultures, whereas school-level
competition shows more nuanced effects. In some cultural contexts and countries (e.g., Ukraine and Sweden), competition is negatively associated with student performance, whereas in African & Middle Eastern countries and especially in Confucian contexts, it
retains a strong positive association. These convergent findings underscore the benefit of a cooperative school atmosphere while
highlighting the context-dependent nature of competition. Notably, while school-level measures help mitigate reverse causality, they
do not completely address issues like endogenous school selection or non-random student sorting. Future research should incorporate
more robust causal designs (e.g., longitudinal or quasi-experimental methods) to clarify these processes and account for selection
effects.
6. Conclusion and implications
This comprehensive study, utilizing PISA 2018 data from 76 countries/economies across eight cultures, provides valuable insights
into the complex relationships between competition, cooperation, and academic performance. The results indicated that, generally,
cooperation demonstrated a stronger positive association with academic outcomes than competition, with these effects being more
pronounced at the school level compared to the individual level. Notably, the relationships between competition, cooperation, and
academic performance exhibited variability depending on the analytical level and cultural context examined. This underscores the
necessity of considering both individual and institutional factors, alongside cultural contexts, when assessing the influence of
competition and cooperation on academic achievement. These findings carry substantial theoretical and practical implications for
educational research and policy.
From a theoretical perspective, the results contribute to the existing body of literature on social interdependence theory and
cooperative learning (Johnson & Johnson, 2009; Slavin, 1985). The pooled sample results generally support social interdependence
theory (Johnson & Johnson, 2005, 2009), revealing that higher levels of cooperation are positively correlated with improved academic
performance, while increased competition is associated with lower performance. However, the study also identifies significant variations at the student level, school level, and across different cultural and national contexts. These insights offer a valuable extension to

cross-cultural research on social interdependence theory and cooperative learning, emphasizing the need for context-specific interpretations of these theories.
Moreover, the study challenges the assumption that competition universally benefits academic outcomes, highlighting the complex
and context-dependent nature of its effects across diverse educational settings. While school-level cooperation tends to show a positive
association with academic performance, the impact of competition is more nuanced. These findings underscore the importance of
considering both the level (student or school) and the cultural context when evaluating educational strategies.
From a practical standpoint, these findings offer important guidance for educators and school administrators seeking to improve
student achievement. Encouraging cooperative environments at the school level, which are generally linked to better academic
outcomes, should be a priority. Fostering collaborative projects, group activities, and peer support mechanisms can create an environment conducive to higher performance (Ghaith, 2002; Qureshi et al., 2023). Additionally, the study suggests that the role of
competition is culturally specific. In contexts where competition is associated with positive outcomes, such as in African & Middle
Eastern and Confucian cultures, educators might consider integrating competitive elements to complement cooperative practices.
Conversely, in settings where competition appears to have a neutral or negative association, emphasizing cooperation is crucial.
At the policy level, the implications are significant. Policymakers should prioritize the creation of cooperative environments within
curricula and school programs. According to existing literature, cooperative pedagogy and cooperative learning foster a collaborative
atmosphere and enhance academic performance (Agwu & Nmadu, 2023; Johnson & Johnson, 2009). Therefore, policies should be
implemented to promote collaborative learning and provide teacher training in cooperative pedagogy. Additionally, acknowledging
contextual differences in competition and cooperation is crucial; policies should be flexible enough to adapt strategies based on each
school’s unique context. For instance, research by Bratti et al. (2011) suggests that competition may have a more positive impact on
students in larger classes, indicating that policymakers should carefully consider the influence of class size on motivation and performance. By adopting a culturally and contextually sensitive approach, policies can support both academic success and holistic
student development, ensuring effectiveness and equity across diverse educational settings.
7. Limitations and future directions
This study possesses several strengths, including a large sample size, use of a cross-cultural and national dataset, and a comprehensive and multilevel perspective that simultaneously examines competition and collaboration at both student and school levels.
Nevertheless, several limitations warrant attention in future research.
First, the cross-sectional design precludes firm causal conclusions and overlooks potential long-term impacts. Moreover, studentlevel perceptions of competition and cooperation may vary by achievement level, introducing possible endogeneity that complicates
both interpretation and policy implications. To address these issues, future research should therefore employ longitudinal or experimental approaches and robust analytic techniques—such as instrumental variables—or integrate data from principals and teachers.
Second, although multilevel models and relevant covariates were employed to explore cross-cultural patterns, the mechanisms
driving these relationships remain unclear due to the secondary, aggregated nature of PISA data. Qualitative or mixed-methods studies
may clarify how cultural norms shape perceptions of competition and cooperation and lead to differing outcomes across contexts.
Lastly, given the global decline in student well-being and the rise in mental health issues (Bruckauf, 2017; Marquez & Long, 2021;
Twenge et al., 2018), future research should also consider well-being outcomes, including emotional, psychological, and relational
well-being, alongside academic outcomes, to provide a more holistic understanding of how competition and cooperation affect students’ overall development.
