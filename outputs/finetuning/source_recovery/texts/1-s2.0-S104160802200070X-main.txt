In addition to enabling students to gain knowledge, education aims to make them competent, confident, and
enthusiastic learners. Using TIMSS 2019 data from eighth graders in mathematics classrooms in Hong Kong (N =
1146) and England (N = 1981), this study explored the relevance of classroom management and instructional
clarity to students' mathematics self-concept and value, academic emotions (i.e., enjoyment and boredom) and
mathematics achievement by using a latent multi-group SEM analysis. The findings corroborated the controlvalue theory and indicated that classroom management was not always beneficial to the increase of students'
enjoyment and achievement in Hong Kong, but the favorable effects of classroom management on academic
emotions and achievement were observed in England. Additionally, instructional clarity has been demonstrated
to improve students' enjoyment and achievement in mathematics while reducing boredom in both groups, with
the advantage being greater in Hong Kong classrooms. The limitations and implications are then discussed.

1. Introduction
As educational research is undergoing an affective turn, a growing
number of scholars acknowledge the pervasiveness of emotions in the
classroom. Discoveries indicated that learning-related emotions are
closely linked to students' motivation, learning strategies, psychological
well-being, and career choices (Liu et al., 2018; Pekrun et al., 2017;
Schukajlow et al., 2017). Consequently, emotions are no longer
considered an epiphenomenon with little educational relevance but
instead have a significant impact on the productivity of school education. Therefore, it is essential to have a deeper understanding of
emotional antecedents.
Emotions in academic situations refer to academic emotions, which
are directly associated with learning and academic success/failure
(Pekrun, 2006). The control-value theory (CVT; Pekrun, 2006) is one of
the theoretical paradigms guiding the study of academic emotions. The
CVT framework assumes that emotional reaction is initiated by cognitive appraisals, and not just by the circumstance itself. Informed by the
CVT, we know that context matters in academic emotions since it provides information regarding students' academic controllability and
value. Frequently, control assessment is operationalized as academic

self-concept, which refers to students' evaluations of their own academic
competence (Shavelson et al., 1976). Typically, value evaluation refers
to students' assessments of the significance and utility of educational
activities (Pekrun, 2006).
Teachers are undoubtedly the most important stakeholders in all
educational settings, as they are specialists in a range of teaching approaches, strategies, and course design (Kunter et al., 2013). Moreover,
instructional clarity and classroom management guarantee instructional
engagement (Mitchell et al., 2017), and they appear to be the two most
influential teaching characteristics for students' affective performance
(see Maulana et al., 2016). Nevertheless, the mediation assumption of
the CVT from teaching-related factors has only been studied by a limited
number of studies, with the primary focus being on supportive teaching
(e.g., Burić, 2015; Forsblom et al., 2021; Goetz et al., 2020). In this regard, the purpose of the present study is to examine the relationship
between classroom management and instructional clarity, cognitive
appraisals, and academic emotions. This study focuses on the mathematics classroom context due to the domain-specificity of academic
emotions, academic self-concept, and academic value (see Goetz et al.,
2007; Guo et al., 2018).

The CVT is a theoretically sound framework for investigating students' academic emotions, positing that proximal cognitive appraisals
and distal social environments are key drivers of learning-related emotions (Pekrun, 2006). It also assumes that academic emotions can further
affect students' subsequent learning behaviors and outcomes. The CVT
integrates the plethora of developing results on academic emotions and
serves as the foundation for the great majority of existing academic
emotion research based on these two assumptions (e.g., Burić, 2015;
Forsblom et al., 2021; Goetz et al., 2020; Gong and Bergey, 2020).
A combination of control and value appraisals arouses diverse academic emotions, such as enjoyment, anxiety, boredom, and anger. Since
emotions are multifaceted psychological processes with affective,
motivational, cognitive, and physiological components (Damasio,
2004), academic emotions can be further classified along three dimensions in terms of valence (positive versus negative), activating level
(activating versus deactivating), and object emphasis (activity versus
outcome; Pekrun, 2006). Due to the frequency with which students in
classroom settings report mathematics-related enjoyment and boredom,
as well as the differences in their valence (Cui et al., 2017), the focus of
this study was on these two academic emotions. Furthermore, longitudinal evidence revealed that enjoyment/boredom and academic
achievement had a reciprocal relationship over time (Pekrun et al.,
2017; Putwain et al., 2018), highlighting the significance of enjoyment
and boredom in academic contexts. Enjoyment, an activity-focused
emotion with high academic control and value, has been shown to
have many benefits in promoting students' interest, self-regulation behaviors, deep learning strategies, and achievement (Goetz et al., 2012;
Pekrun, 2006), corresponding to the Broaden-and-Build theory, which
describes that enjoyment could broaden students' thought, attention and
problem-focused coping (Fredrickson, 2001). In contrast, boredom has
garnered significant attention in recent years due to its negative effects
on learning. If students are over- or under-challenged, they will grow
bored (Pekrun, 2006). Boredom, as a negative and deactivating emotion,
leads students to lose concentration, study superficially, and get disengaged, all of which may impair their academic performance (Pekrun
et al., 2017; Tze et al., 2021).

classroom management, each with its focus. Collectively, classroom
management is conceptualized by misbehavior management (Ding
et al., 2008; Lewis et al., 2005), discipline and rules (Emmer et al., 1980;
Kunter et al., 2007), constructs of guidance (Evertson and Weinstein,

taken to create and maintain a learning environment conducive to successful
instruction (pp.17-43).” In this sense, classroom management is a catchall word for instructors' attempts to monitor classroom activities such as
student conduct, student engagement, and learning. Following this
definition, classroom management in this study refers to teachers
implementing a series of measures to deal with misconduct in the
classroom, as well as establishing an orderly and supportive environment that encourages students to actively participate in classroom
activities.
Even though research has consistently demonstrated the importance
of classroom management to students' academic performance (e.g.,
Kunter et al., 2013), few studies have looked into the connections between classroom management and academic emotions, as well as the
process that underpins these relationships. An earlier meta-analysis by
Seidel and Shavelson (2007) identified a comparatively high effect size
between regulation and affective outcomes (e.g., interest, attitude, and
belief) as compared to its associations with students' learning processes
and cognitive results, implying the key role of management in students'
affective performance. However, this beneficial effect of classroom
management on students' academic emotions is not universal. In an
earlier study, Frenzel et al. (2007) discovered that classroom management, which focused on direct behavior control, had no relationship
with enjoyment, anxiety, or anger but was negatively related to
boredom. Similarly, a recent study by Westphal et al. (2018) found that
classroom management decreased boredom but had no impact on
mathematical enjoyment, corroborating the results of a longitudinal
study by Lazarides and Buchholz (2019). The empirical evidence presented previously suggested that classroom management is effective in
reducing student boredom. However, additional research is required to
establish the connection between classroom management and positive
emotional experience.
Only a few studies in the educational context have relied on the CVT
mediation assumption to investigate the relationship between teaching
characteristics and academic emotions (e.g., Burić, 2015; Cui et al.,
2017; Forsblom et al., 2021; Goetz et al., 2020). These studies identified
the mediating roles of cognitive appraisals in the teaching-emotions
association. Supportive and enthusiastic teaching, for example, might
improve students' mathematics self-concept and value, resulting in
greater mathematical enjoyment and less boredom (Cui et al., 2017;
Goetz et al., 2020). Although few studies have directly investigated the
links between classroom management and cognitive appraisals and academic emotions, such potential correlations do exist. Classroom management is expected to follow the CVT and self-determination theory
(SDT; Ryan and Deci, 2000), highlighting the importance of the classroom environment in developing students' academic self-concept and
motivating them to participate in classroom activities. That is, students
will have a high level of academic control and value if the learning
environment meets their expectations, such as efficient learning time
and autonomy support, by incorporating competence beliefs, motivations, and meaning into their engagement. As uncovered in the study of
Maulana et al. (2016), positive relationships between classroom management, academic self-concept, and intrinsic value existed. Overall,
empirical evidence is vastly missing in existing literature, and more
research is needed to explore the underlying mechanism of classroom
management and academic emotions.

classroom management strategies may change based on how different
instructors define inappropriate behavior and its causes.
In contrast, research in British schools revealed that teachers
considered student discipline one of the most significant concerns, with
the most common complaints being disobedience, hyperactivity, and

and Merrett, 1988). Drawing upon the report of a two-year investigation
by the Children's Society in England, 29 % of junior high school students
in urban areas said their classmates disrupted their classes daily, and
nearly half of the students said that their peers' noise made it difficult to
concentrate in class regularly (Layard and Dunn, 2009). Teachers in
British schools reported that they often spent more than half of the
lesson time conducting classroom management, and they considered
talking out of turn and hindering others as most troublesome (Houghton
et al., 1988; Merrett and Wheldall, 1984). This is quite different from the
view of Hong Kong teachers. Thus, it can be further inferred that the
classroom context influence how appropriate classroom behavior is
defined and, as a result, how classroom management is implemented. In
addition, the importance of a positive teacher-student relationship in
Western classroom management cannot be overstated. Through
comparative studies, Wubbels (2011) recognized that Western classrooms, such as those in Germany, America, and England, are shifting
from behavior management to curricular involvement and socialemotional learning with a focus on relationships. Setting consistent
behavioral norms, rewarding positive behavioral performance, and

1.4. Instructional clarity and academic emotions
Instructional clarity is one of the most important prerequisites for
instructors to engage in teaching activities, and it is a critical component
of effective teaching (Maulana and Helms-Lorenz, 2016). Instructional
clarity was defined as a teacher's ability to explain or otherwise assist
students in thoroughly understanding the material (Metcalf and Kim,
1992). Likewise, Maulana et al. (2016) defined instructional clarity as a
teacher's capacity to deliver classroom instruction clearly and concisely.
In line with this definition, instructional clarity in this study refers to the
teaching that is easy to understand in terms of clear responses to students' queries, linking new lessons to past knowledge, and attempting to
integrate what is taught with students' daily experiences in mathematics
class. In this sense, instructional clarity in the current study also contains
teachers' activation of students' thinking.
Despite that instructional clarity has been linked to academic
accomplishment (e.g., Bardach et al., 2018; Hines et al., 1985), little
research has been done on its associations with academic emotions or
the probable mechanism underlying this association. To our best
knowledge, one study has explicitly examined the relationship between
instructional clarity, termed quality of teaching including clarity and
structure, and academic emotions. The study was notable because
instructional clarity was found to be positively related to individuallevel enjoyment and negatively related to boredom, but had no associations with classroom-level enjoyment or boredom (Frenzel et al.,
2007), implying that instructional clarity was more predictive at the
student level. Besides, limited evidence demonstrated the influential
effects of instructional clarity on academic self-concept and value and
further showed the decisive role of clarity in affecting students' cognitive
appraisals compared with other teaching characteristics (Maulana et al.,
2016). Theoretically, instructors' professional values and attitudes are
communicated via well-structured and clear instruction; students can
perceive and assimilate desired values for themselves except for
knowledge information and then demonstrate these values and competence beliefs in their subsequent behavior and performance (Arthur
et al., 2005). In line with the CVT, high academic control and value
perception may arouse students' enjoyment and lower their boredom
(Pekrun, 2006). However, past studies solely conducted preliminary
exploration on the relationship between instructional clarity and academic self-concept/value/emotions in isolation. Whether the effect of
instructional clarity on students' emotions is mediated by academic selfconcept and value remains an open question. Take as an example, when
students have higher competence beliefs, high-quality teaching may
trigger their positive emotions and undermine their negative ones; on
the contrary, such effects may not be equally effective when students
have a lower mathematics self-concept (Lazarides and Buchholz, 2019).
Taken together, the connection between instructional clarity and academic emotions is highly inadequate, and the lack of an impact mechanism requires more investigation.

From the standpoint of the CVT and empirical evidence mentioned
above, this study attempts to identify the mediating roles of cognitive
appraisals (i.e., mathematics self-concept and value) in classroom
management and instructional clarity and academic emotions, focusing
on eighth-graders in mathematics classrooms. Furthermore, data from
Hong Kong and England were used to validate the findings' applicability
in diverse educational contexts. The mathematics classroom context is
selected for the following two considerations. First, students are expected to acquire mathematics in all educational systems worldwide and
the popularity of STEM education has made mathematics-related abilities necessary and strongly addressed (see Tze et al., 2021). Second,
previous research has shown that students' emotions in mathematics
classrooms are more intense than in other domains (e.g., Latin and English; Goetz et al., 2006) and has confirmed the domain-specificity of
academic emotions (Goetz et al., 2007). Positive emotions have been
observed to decrease during secondary school (e.g., Vierhaus et al.,
2016). Combined with the fact that teenage brain development in terms
of cognition, emotion, and creativity, is at a critical period (Blakemore
and Choudhury, 2006), the current study focuses on domain-specific
emotions and appraisals in secondary mathematics classrooms. The
following three questions guided this study.

2.2.1. Two teaching characteristics
Five items in the Instructional Clarity in Mathematics Lessons (ICML)
scale in TIMSS 2019 student questionnaire were used to examine
instructional clarity, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly
agree). For example, “My teacher is good at explaining mathematics.”
Classroom management was assessed by four items in the Disorderly
Behavior during Mathematics Lessons (DBML) scale, ranging from 1
(every or almost every lesson) to 4 (never). It is operationalized by the
degree to which the class is perceived to be disturbed (see Lazarides and
Buchholz, 2019). The example item was “My teacher has to keep telling
us to follow the classroom rules.” Considering that items with a 4-point
scale are treated as ordered categorical variables, McDonald's omega
coefficient might be a more appropriate indication of reliability than
Cronbach's alpha (Flora, 2020). The omega coefficients for instructional
clarity in the two groups were assessed as having good internal reliability (ωHK = 0.911 and ωUK = 0.901); meanwhile, the omega coefficients for classroom management were ωHK = 0.913 and ωUK =
0.902.

1. Do students' mathematics self-concept and value mediate the relationship between the two teaching characteristics and academic
emotions? In line with the CVT (Pekrun, 2006), we hypothesize that

2. Is there a difference in the impact of classroom management on
students' academic emotions in Hong Kong and England? Given the

2. Method
2.1. Sample
This study selected students from grade 8 in Hong Kong and England
mathematics classrooms from the TIMSS 2019 database for analysis.
3265 students from Hong Kong (45.6 % girl) and 3365 students from
England (53.5 % girl) participated in the survey. As suggested by the
findings of Veiga et al. (2021), students with native and immigrant
parents had differences in cognitive, affective, and behavioral engagement. Therefore, considering the complexity of students' backgrounds
and to ensure the purity of samples, this study selected students who
have native-born parents. There were valid samples of 1146 students
from 142 classes in Hong Kong and 1981 students from 158 classes in
England. In the sample of Hong Kong, 526 girls and 620 boys were
included in the final analysis, with an average age of 13.99 years
(ranging from 10.08 to 17.92; SD = 0.48), and there were 1056 girls and
925 boys in the sample of England, with an average age of 14.04 years
(ranging from 9.50 to 17.75; SD = 0.38).

This study conducted a latent multi-group structural equation
modeling to examine and compare the underlying relations between
classroom characteristics and mathematics achievement and two emotions in two groups. Measurement invariance (MI) is a requirement for
multi-group comparison since it allows for valid comparisons across
groups (Shahzad et al., 2020). Configurable, metric, and scalar measurement invariance was investigated in this study to determine if the
multi-group analysis was appropriate for the two groups. Because the fit
of metric and scalar invariance models is commonly assessed by
comparing the fit of two nested models, the model fit criteria used for
each test of invariance were included changes in the chi-square (Δχ2),
the Comparative Fit Index (ΔCFI ≥ − 0.010; Chen, 2007), the Tucker–Lewis index (ΔTLI ≤0.050; Little, 1997), Root Mean Square Error of
Approximation (ΔRMSEA ≤0.015; Chen, 2007) and Standardized Root
Mean Square Residual (ΔSRMR ≤0.030 for metric invariance and
ΔSRMR ≤0.010 for scalar invariance; Chen, 2007; Putnick and Bornstein, 2016).

2.2.4. Mathematics achievement
Mathematics achievement was employed from the standardized test
in TIMSS 2019, which measures students' knowledge in algebra, geometry, data and probability, and number. There are a vast number of
assessment items in TIMSS; however, each student is only given a fraction of the problems to measure their mathematical ability to finish the
test in a specified amount of time. Accordingly, five plausible values are
provided to measure the range of abilities that a student could have. It is
recommended that when involving statistical analysis, each plausible
value should be used separately instead of simply averaging these values
(Martin and Mullis, 2019).
2.2.5. Background variables
Previous studies consistently showed girls with less positive emotions but more negative emotions than boys (e.g., Frenzel et al., 2007;
Lazarides and Buchholz, 2019). Gender was controlled in this study, and
it was dummy coded girl = 0 and boy = 1. Another important factor
influencing students' cognitive and affective performance is familyrelated resources, which are commonly operationalized as home
educational resources or socioeconomic status (e.g., Chen et al., 2012;
Hu et al., 2018). In this study, book ownership was used to assess the
individual's home resources, and the example item is “How many books
are there in your home?” with a range of 1 (none or very few) to 5
(many) (Enough to fill three or more bookcases). Lastly, age might be an
important demographic factor resulting in differences in enjoyment and
boredom, especially for middle-school students (Lei et al., 2018). As a
result, gender, home resources, and age were controlled in this study.

2.3. Data analysis

To examine whether students from Hong Kong and England have
similar perceptions of teaching characteristics, mathematics selfconcept and value, and mathematics-related emotions, a measurement
invariance test was conducted to see if students' ratings of involved
variables are comparable. Table 4 presents the results of the measurement invariance tests of the configural, metric, and scalar models. The fit
indices showed a good fit of the configural model (χ2 (568) = 2570.299,
CFI = 0.963, TLI = 0.957, RMSEA = 0.048, SRMR = 0.041), supporting
the configural invariance. With regard to the metric model, as Table 3

4. Discussion
This study examined the relationship between classroom management and instructional clarity and students' academic emotions in Hong
Kong versus England, expanding the empirical evidence on the aforementioned correlations in diverse educational settings. Findings
revealed the mediating roles of mathematics self-concept and value in
the two teaching characteristics and academic emotions (except for the
path from classroom management to mathematics value in the English
group), confirming Pekrun's (2006) CVT and emphasizing the need of
considering multiple appraisals when studying the teaching-emotions
relationship.
Within our expectations, findings on classroom management
revealed some inconsistencies in England and Hong Kong. First, in line
with previous findings (e.g., Lazarides and Buchholz, 2019; Maulana
et al., 2016), results in the English group demonstrated that classroom
management had no direct association with students' enjoyment but
boredom and mathematics achievement. Furthermore, the analysis of
mediation revealed that classroom management had an indirect

Otherwise, teachers will provide verbal instructions or criticisms, and

class, surreptitiously conversing. As described by Sun (2015), teachers in
Hong Kong played an authoritarian role inside the classroom but a
caring role outside the classroom. In this context, discipline problems,
especially non-attentiveness may become the focal concern of a teacher's
classroom management in most Chinese classrooms (see Ding et al.,
2008). Accordingly, direct behavior control, such as rules setting, hinting, and directive statements will be used more frequently, which is
often considered efficient in Chinese classroom contexts, allowing
classes to return to orderly quickly and thus reducing wasted teaching
time (Ding et al., 2008; Sun, 2015). This is consistent with our findings,
which reveal that teachers in Hong Kong mathematics classrooms
frequently use rules. However, such external control may have longterm negative learning consequences for students, particularly in the
face of teacher criticism or denial. Instead of acquiring skills and
knowledge for their development, students may try to please their
teachers to avoid punishment; students may also have a negative selfidentification, believing that they are lousy students and losing their
confidence as a result (Nelsen, 2011). By contrast, in European countries, the relationship between parents/teachers and children tends to be
more equalitarian (e.g., Garcia et al., 2020; Li and Du, 2013) and based
on warmth, mutual respect, and involvement (Gimenez-Serrano et al.,
2022; Nash et al., 2016). Combined with the features of Western education that emphasize cultivating interests rather than pursuing high
scores, the pressure on parents, teachers, and students to achieve school
success may be lower than in collectivistic cultural contexts (Chao,
2001), which is advantageous for the development of students' academic
self-concept. Thus, the primary objective of classroom management
differs between the two educational systems, resulting in distinct
classroom management philosophies, techniques, and outcomes.
Our results revealed the significant and favorable effects of instructional clarity on students' academic emotions and achievement in Hong
Kong and England, confirming our hypothesis. In addition, findings
suggested that in Hong Kong, instructional clarity had a greater impact
on students' mathematics achievement and enjoyment than in England.
One possible explanation is that influenced by the listening culture, an
essential component of effective discourse in the Chinese cultural
tradition (Gao and Ting-Toomey, 1998), the listening-centered classroom atmosphere places a high value on instruction clarity and coherence, which is one of the most important factors in students' evaluations
of teaching quality and thus has a greater impact on their positive
learning experiences and academic performance. The limited effect of
instructional clarity on student achievement in the English group warrants additional consideration. As demonstrated by our findings, the
promotion of instructional clarity on students' mathematical performance was primarily through mathematics self-concept, reflecting the
Western schools' emphasis on fostering students' self-esteem or selfconcept (Chao, 1996; Wentzel and Caldwell, 1997). Nonetheless, the
direct negative effect of teaching clarity on mathematics achievement
undermined this overall positive effect. This unexpected finding calls for
additional discussion. In Western classrooms, learning-related theories
are typically grounded in the active nature of mental processes and
inquiry-based activities (see Li, 2003). Learners are usually encouraged
to become active, independent problem-solvers and explorers. During
this process, however, merely exposing students to content that is
explicitly conveyed is not sufficient for learning. As stated by Lujan and
DiCarlo (2006), too much instruction leads to insufficient learning. In

5. Limitations, conclusions, and implications
There are some limitations of the current study that should be
mentioned when interpreting findings. Constrained by the items of
TIMSS, this study only examined two teaching characteristics, namely,
classroom management and instructional clarity. Indeed, other
teaching-related variables, such as cognitive activation (Lazarides and
Buchholz, 2019) and teacher support, including academic and
emotional support (Liu et al., 2018), have been clarified their significant
associations with academic emotions. Thus, more aspects of quality of
teaching should be examined simultaneously in future studies to know
more about the specific contribution of each factor. In addition, items
were selected to operationalize target variables such as mathematics
self-concept, enjoyment, and boredom in the TIMSS questionnaire
rather than specialized and frequently used scales. Inconsistent application of measurement scales may result in some bias regarding the
relationship between variables. A related issue is that this research is
unable to investigate the qualities of teaching in more depth. For
instance, according to Martin and Sass (2010), classroom management
encompasses both behavior management and instructional management, which are contradictory management approaches. Different
management strategies and their relevance to academic emotions
cannot be addressed comprehensively in this regard. For future research,
we suggest that researchers should implement fine-grained concepts and
assessment instruments tailored to the specific features of constructs to
test the robustness of the findings in this study.
Despite the limitations mentioned above, this study contributing to
the existing literature is threefold. First, it supported the CVT by identifying the multiple mediating roles of cognitive appraisals (i.e., mathematics self-concept and value) between two teaching characteristics
and academic emotions. Second, the data revealed differences in the
mediating effects of control-value evaluations on classroom management and academic emotion in Hong Kong and England, with English
classrooms outperforming Hong Kong classrooms. Third, findings
confirmed the powerful role of instructional clarity in promoting students' positive learning experiences in both educational systems, especially in Hong Kong. Notably, our findings also imply that instructional
clarity is beneficial for students' achievement, but the effectiveness may
suffer from ceiling effects.
Detailed knowledge of how the classroom environment affects students' emotions may help educators better understand the significance
of classroom teaching and guide attempts to create engaging classroom
settings in specific academic contexts. The findings provide some practical implications. First, the importance of instructional clarity cannot be
overstated. Instructional clarity extends beyond repetition. Understanding of concepts, relationships, and procedures should be constructed and developed by students. Teachers can encourage this process
by carefully evaluating the type and organization of information and
teaching strategies, reducing the amount of material students are expected to memorize, and devoting much more effort to helping students
become active learners and knowledge constructors, thereby contributing to students' competence beliefs and their perceptions of the value
of mathematics learning. Second, Hong Kong educators should reevaluate their roles as classroom managers. Successful classroom supervisors
prioritize learning over maintaining a quiet environment, demonstrate
personal acceptance of students, and offer helpful suggestions
frequently. Teachers could look into the root of a student's misbehavior
and devote more time to listening to the student's inner voice. Instructors can also improve communication and the teacher-student
