This article examines a multidimensional conceptualization of engagement and how it is related to achievement
outcomes and teacher classroom practices in mathematics (including structure, support, and challenge). It draws
insights from existing literature on engagement and other related research areas, including motivation and
teaching effectiveness, and conducts empirical investigations using international large-scale survey and assessment data in four education systems (i.e., Singapore, Finland, Australia, and Romania) that have distinct cultural
and social backgrounds and achieve at varying levels on international assessments. Confirmatory factor analysis
results validate engagement as a multidimensional construct across contexts. Path analysis results reveal
different patterns in the relations among engagement, achievement, and teacher classroom practices. In some
contexts, relations between teacher practices and engagement or achievement vary as a function of student or
teacher report. Implications and future directions are discussed.

1. Introduction
Existing literature has demonstrated evidence that teachers exert
large and accumulative influence on students’ learning outcomes (e.g.,
Konstantopoulos & Chung, 2011; Rivkin, Hanushek, & Kain, 2005).
Multiple lenses have been used for examining teacher effects with support for models that focus on the learning process rather than student
outcomes. As Kelly (2012) argues, while outcome-based accountability
may provide extrinsic motivation for teachers to perform better, it fails
to support teachers in reflecting on and refining their instructional
practices that generate and sustain student engagement. In the dynamic
model put forth by Creemers and Kyriakides (2006) for evaluating
educational effectiveness, teacher classroom practices are a central focus
among factors at multiple levels (e.g., student, classroom, and school)
that are assumed to have direct or indirect relations with student outcomes. The model also takes into account multiple goals of education
incorporating outcome measures that expand the focus beyond only
cognitive outcomes. This consideration is echoed in the findings of
Kyriakides, Christoforou, and Charalambous (2013) meta-analysis of
studies on teaching effectiveness, which suggest that in comparison to
cognitive outcomes, affective, psychological, or behavioral outcomes
are less examined. The present study focuses on student engagement and

further explores the relations among engagement, achievement, and
teacher classroom practices.
Engagement is a critical component of students’ learning experience
(Klem & Connell, 2004; Marks, 2000). It can mediate the impact of
teachers’ instructional practices on student achievement outcomes (e.g.,
Guthrie, Wigfield, & You, 2012). It also is associated with desirable
social and emotional learning outcomes (Klem & Connell, 2004).
Moreover, student engagement in secondary education has been found
to be related to health outcomes (National Research Council, Institute of
Medicine of the National Academies, 2004), postsecondary outcomes
(Finn & Owings, 2006; Janosz, 2012) and even more distal outcomes
such as employment (Janosz, 2012) and productive citizenship (Davis &
McPartland, 2012), and adult criminal behavior (Ou, Mersky, Reynolds,
& Kohler, 2007).
Another rationale for studying engagement is that engagement is not
an attribute, such as gender and race, which remains relatively stable,
but an alterable state of being subject to multiple contextual factors,
including family, school, and community (Reschly & Christenson,
2006). Emerging evidence from existing literature suggests that
engagement is responsive to teacher classroom practices (e.g., Dolezal,
Welsh, Pressley, & Vincent, 2003; Skinner & Belmont, 1993). In their
meta-analysis of middle school students’ science engagement, Aker and

Although engagement has become an increasingly important
construct in the educational field, evidence from large-scale studies,
especially those conducted in an international context, is sparse. As
Lubienski (2017) points out, comparisons in an international context can
shed light on phenomena or practices that are often viewed as “normal”
or “inevitable” within a country but in fact may not be. For instance,
students’ socioeconomic status (SES) has been found to be an important
predictor of student outcomes in the U.S. context (e.g., Sirin, 2005).
Using international data for comparisons, researchers have further
identified that student SES is a larger predictor of students’ access to
qualified teachers (Han, Son, & Kang, 2017) and achievement outcomes
(Chiu, 2017; Han et al., 2017) in the U.S. than in other Asian countries
studied. In contrast, urban vs. rural educational inequality is especially
salient in some developing countries in Asia, Latin America, and Europe,
such as Thailand, Mexico, and Bulgaria (OECD, 2013). These findings
can assist researchers, policy-makers, and other key stakeholders in
education systems in better understanding why certain educational
phenomena exist in a given society and in identifying best practices
happening elsewhere that might help improve their own education
system—the major goals of comparative studies as identified by Postlethwaite (1988). With a stronger theoretical and empirical knowledge
base about engagement in various social and cultural contexts, effective
interventions could be designed and delivered to create an engaging
learning experience for students that ultimately leads to desirable
educational outcomes, including immediate ones (e.g., academic performance, social and emotional learning outcomes) and distal ones (e.g.,

Finn’s (1989) participation-identification model explains how
participation in classroom and school activities (i.e., the behavioral
dimension) interacts with identification, where students feel themselves
to be significant members of the school community (i.e., the affective
dimension), to impact the likelihood of academic success. Other models
of engagement that have been developed in more recent years propose
three, four, or even more dimensions (e.g., Appleton, Christenson, Kim,
& Reschly, 2006; Fredricks et al., 2004; Luckner, Englund, Coffey, &
Nuno, 2006; Reschly & Christenson, 2006; Skinner et al., 2009; Wigfield
et al., 2008). Although researchers use different terminology and characterize engagement in various ways, three key dimensions emerge from
these studies: (1) a behavioral dimension, which includes compliance
with classroom norms, preparation for class, and concentration (e.g.,
Finn, Pannozzo, & Voelkl, 1995; Finn & Rock, 1997; Fredricks &
McColskey, 2012); (2) an affective/emotional dimension, which includes expressing interest and enjoyment, and being happy and in a
good relationship with teachers (e.g., Appleton et al., 2006; Fredricks &
McColskey, 2012); and (3) a cognitive dimension, which includes sense
of control, beliefs about the value of academic work, future aspiration
and goals, and willingness to exert effort and adopt learning strategies to
achieve certain learning goals (e.g., Appleton et al., 2006; Corno &
Mandinach, 1983; Fredricks & McColskey, 2012; Fredricks et al., 2004).
It is important to note that researchers vary in the way each
dimension is defined and sometimes their definition of a certain
dimension overlaps with others’ definitions of other dimension(s)
(Ainley, 2012). For example, effort has been considered as behavioral
engagement in some studies but referred to as cognitive engagement in
others; students’ valuing of school has been framed as part of both
emotional and cognitive engagement (Fredricks & McColskey, 2012).
Since the three-dimension framework including emotional, cognitive,
and behavioral engagement is supported by strong empirical and theoretical evidence (Johnson & Dean, 2011; Fredricks & McColskey, 2012)
and delineates relatively clear boundaries among the various dimensions (while still acknowledging the connections among them), this
study follows the three-dimension framework and synthesizes current
literature on some overlapping definitions (e.g., categorizing willingness
to invest in learning to achieve certain goals as cognitive dimension
rather than emotional dimension) to conceptualize engagement.
Using measures available in two international large-scale survey and
assessment datasets, this study focuses on emotional and cognitive
engagement constructs in particular, including interest, self-concept,
and future utility beliefs. These constructs may be instantiated to refer
to internalized and presumably relatively stable engagement states, or
alternately, to more temporally specific reactions to instruction. Previous research on engagement has treated interest in particular as a
moment-in-time construct highly sensitive to aspects of the instructional
task at hand (e.g. Shernoff et al., 2016). The present study uses measures
that implicitly refer to long-run, internalized engagement states.

2. Literature review
2.1. Conceptualization and measures of engagement
Compared to achievement, the construct of engagement has emerged
relatively more recently in education research but has become increasingly prevalent (Appleton, Christenson, & Furlong, 2008). Broadly, it
has been defined as a construct for understanding student involvement
in education across education levels, highlighting variation in the experiences of individual students in their educational activities (Ainley,
2012). More specifically, it has been described as an action that incorporates emotions, attention, goals, and persistent effort (Skinner,
Kindermann, & Furrer, 2009).
Researchers adopt different approaches to conceptualizing engagement. Some studies have conceptualized engagement as a process (e.g.,
Pianta, Hamre, & Allen, 2012), while others consider it as an outcome
(e.g., Appleton et al., 2008; Skinner, Furrer, Marchand, & Kindermann,
2008). Some scholars consider engagement as both a learning process
that mediates educational outcomes and an outcome itself (Reschly &
Christenson, 2012).

toward science (Kanter & Konstantopoulos, 2010), and between teacher

enjoyment and interest, efficacy and identification, and future utility
beliefs (Kelly & Zhang, 2016), suggesting teacher classroom practices
may impact students’ attitudes and beliefs.
Models of effective teaching range from a focus on generic teaching
practices found across all subjects, to subject-specific teaching frameworks, to models emphasizing specific learning domains and skills (e.g.
argumentation). Examples of generic practices include effective monitoring of student learning, and the provision of emotional support (see e.
g. The Danielson Group, 2013; Hamre et al., 2013). Subject-specific
frameworks include the Mathematical Quality of Instruction framework (Hill et al., 2008) and the Protocol for Language Arts Teaching
Observation (Grossman, Cohen, Ronfeldt, & Brown, 2014), while other
models emphasize more narrow sets of skills that cut across multiple
subjects such as written argumentation (Reznitskaya, Anderson, & Kuo,
2007). In addition, models of effective instruction vary in the degree to
which they emphasize educational processes and outcomes at the student, classroom, school, or system level (Muijs et al., 2014). In the
present study, the focus is on generic classroom teaching practices, with
outcomes of interest at the student level.
Given this focus for the current study, Brophy (1988) provides a
theoretical framework for examining effective classroom practices,
including providing meaningful learning objectives, a supportive environment, appropriate level of challenge, and optimizing/balancing the
use of the three general strategies. These features have been reflected in
research examining specific teacher practices to varying degrees. For
example, measures of rigorous instruction examined in Matsumura et al.
(2008) include “clarity and detail of expectations for student learning”
and “teacher presses for evidence or for students to explain thinking.”
These measures correspond to providing meaningful learning objectives
and appropriate level of challenge, respectively, in Brophy’s framework.
Measures of engaging instructional practices examined in Skinner and
Belmont (1993) and in Dolezal et al. (2003) include “teacher shows
affection, understanding, and sympathy” and “teacher gives encouragement/praise”, respectively. Both practices reflect providing a supportive environment in Brophy’s framework.
This study builds upon Brophy’s (1988) framework, synthesizes
common themes that emerge from studies examining specific teacher
classroom practices, and classifies engaging teacher classroom practices

It is important to note that while effective classroom practices may
share common features, they can be examined through different perspectives. Classroom observations provide detailed information on
teacher classroom practices, but this approach could become timeconsuming and labor-intensive. Survey studies using questionnaires to
collect teacher-reported or student-reported instructional practices are
relatively cost-effective and easier to scale up. This approach also allows
for capturing multiple perspectives, although it is acknowledged that
data collected from the teacher perspective and student perspective may
not always be in alignment with each other (van Beek, de Jong, Wubbels, & Minnaert, 2014). For instance, looking at the responses of middle
school students and their teachers to the same questions about mathematics instruction administered in the National Assessment of Education
Progress (NAEP) surveys, Desimone, Smith, and Frisvold (2010) found
that students and teachers tend to reach greater level of agreement in
reporting the frequency of instructional practices that involve use of
objects (e.g., calculators and textbooks) but greater discrepancies were
identified when respondents reported on practices that involve more
subjectivity (e.g., writing and discussion about mathematics). These
findings suggest that different perspectives could provide additional
insight in studying teacher classroom practices.

3.2. Measures
3.2.1. Measures of engagement
On the student questionnaire in both data sources, students were
asked to report the extent to which they agreed with a series of statements that tap into the construct of engagement in mathematics. Examples from TIMSS 2011 include “I enjoy learning mathematics”
(interest); “I usually do well in mathematics” (self-concept); and “I need
to do well in mathematics to get the job I want” (future utility beliefs).
Examples from PISA 2012 include “I enjoy reading about mathematics”
(interest); “I get good grades in mathematics” (self-concept); and
“Making an effort in mathematics is worth it because it will help me in
the work that I want to do later on” (future utility beliefs).
The full set of measures of engagement can be found in Table 1
(TIMSS 2011) and Table 2 (PISA 2012) in the Results section. All items
presented response options on a Likert scale with 1 indicating strong
agreement and 4 strong disagreement. Responses were recoded (some
are reverse coded as well) to 0–3 scale2 where higher value indicates
greater level of engagement.

3. Data and method
3.1. Data sources
This study uses data from the Trends in International Mathematics
and Science Study 2011 (TIMSS 2011) and the Program for International
Student Assessment 2012 (PISA 2012). International studies, such as
TIMSS and PISA, often use self-reports on Likert-scale items to measure
respondents’ traits and attitudes, including engagement measures.
Existing research has demonstrated that respondents can use different
references in their evaluation of statements when responding to such
survey items (e.g., Heine, Lehman, Peng, & Greenholtz, 2002). For
example, people from East Asian cultures tend to avoid the extreme
values on the Likert scale more often than people from Western cultures
because the former group may use a cultural reference that promotes
modesty to a larger extent (Lie & Turmo, 2005). For another example,
students in higher-performing education systems tend to exhibit lower
self-concept, which may result, in part, from more demanding standards
and higher pressure to achieve (Van de gaer, Grisay, Schulz, & Gebhardt,
2012). In light of this reference group effect, the current study selected
the following four countries: Australia, Finland, Romania, and
Singapore as focal education systems. These four countries have distinct
cultural and social background and perform at varying levels on the
international assessments, such as TIMSS and PISA. In addition, similarities and differences between the TIMSS 2011 and PISA 2012 study
design make them complementary for examining engagement across
countries.
First of all, both studies employ a two-stage sampling design with a
sample of schools selected at the first stage. TIMSS 2011 selected one or
more intact fourth-grade or eighth-grade classes of students from each
sampled school at the second stage while PISA 2012 selected about
thirty-five 15-year-old students from each sampled school. Although
sampling procedures at the second stage is not identical in the two
studies, the target student populations (i.e., PISA-eligible students in
2012 and eighth graders in TIMSS 2011) are similar in terms of age and
grade level. This study focuses on eighth graders in TIMSS 2011 to
maximize comparability of findings with PISA 2012 participants.
Second, both studies include mathematics through assessments and
survey questionnaires.1 However, while TIMSS 2011 assessed the extent
to which students at eighth grade have mastered a specific curriculum,
PISA 2012 evaluated 15-year-old students’ ability to apply what they
have learned at school to solve real-life problems (OECD, 2014). This
distinction provides opportunities for examining different types of
mathematics achievement outcomes (i.e., content vs. application) and
their relations with engagement and instruction.
Third, both studies administered background questionnaires to students, parents, and school administrators, providing multiple perspectives on students’ educational experiences across different education
systems. While TIMSS 2011 also included teacher questionnaires, PISA
2012 did not administer teacher questionnaires. As a result, measures of
teacher classroom practices come from different reporting sources in

3.2.2. Measures of teacher classroom practices
As mentioned earlier, measures of teacher classroom practices are
reported by teachers in TIMSS 2011 and by students in PISA 2012. On
the TIMSS 2011 mathematics teacher questionnaire, teachers reported
how frequently they practiced certain instructional strategies. For
example, how often do teachers “relate the lessons to students’ daily
lives” (structure), “praise students for good effort” (support) and ask
students to “decide on their own procedure for solving complex problems” (challenge). On the PISA 2012 student questionnaire, students
were asked to report how frequently their mathematics teachers practiced certain instructional strategies. For example, how often does the
teacher set clear goal for learning (structure), give extra help when
students need it (support), and assign projects that require at least one
week to complete (challenge).
The full set of measures of teacher classroom practices are reported
in Table 1 (TIMSS 2011) and Table 2 (PISA 2012) in the Results section.
All items presented response options on a Likert scale with 1 indicating
the highest frequency and 4 the lowest frequency. Responses were
reverse coded to 0–3 scale where higher value indicates greater frequency of certain teacher classroom practices. These items were used to
create three composite variables indicating teacher classroom practices
(i.e., structure, support, and challenge). Each composite variable takes
the mean averaged over the items measuring the corresponding type of
teacher classroom practices. As discussed in more details below, these
composite variables were included in the path analysis as key independent variables.

3.3. Analytic approach
This study uses structural equation modeling (SEM) techniques to
answer the research questions. The SEM models hypothesize how sets of
observed variables define latent variables and how these variables are
related to each other. As implied by their literal meaning, observed
variables refer to items providing information on specific measures that
can be directly observed. Latent variables or factors refer to constructs
that could not be directly measured but serve as the underlying driving
force of the observed variables.
One important advantage of SEM techniques is that the models
explicitly take measurement error into account in statistical analyses, for
both observed and latent variables (Schumacker & Lomax, 2016). In
other words, both observed and latent variables with their associated
measurement error terms could be included in SEM models, as needed.
In terms of modeling, SEM incorporates measurement models (i.e.,
confirmatory factor analysis [CFA]) with structural models (i.e., path
analysis), combining the measurement benefits of CFA and regression
techniques (Schumacker & Lomax, 2016). For this study, CFA and path
analysis are utilized, respectively, in examining: (1) multiple dimensions
of the construct of engagement, and (2) the relations among engagement, achievement, and teacher classroom practices. The CFA measurement model helps achieve the goal of parsimony by combining
multiple observed variables that measure a common underlying
dimension of engagement (i.e., interest, self-concept, and future utility
beliefs). Next, path analysis examines the structural relations among the
multiple dimensions of engagement, achievement, and teacher classroom practices.

4. Results
4.1. Descriptive statistics
Table 1 and Table 2 report the descriptive statistics on all variables
included in the analysis from TIMSS 2011 and PISA 2012, respectively.
Overall, students across the four countries report around average level of
engagement in mathematics with the means close to 1.5, the midpoint
value on the 0–3 scale. At the same time, sizeable variation in engagement level exists within each country with the standard deviation
ranging from 0.60 to 1.17 in TIMSS 2011 and from 0.68 to 1.00 in PISA
2012. All measures of engagement reported in Table 1 and Table 2 were
included in the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the multidimensional framework of engagement discussed earlier.
According to teacher reports of teacher classroom practices in TIMSS
2011, teachers tend to provide more support in their instruction (e.g.,
encouraging students to improve and praising students for good effort)
than structure and challenge across the four focal countries with the
means of support items generally higher than those of structure and
challenge items. According to student reports of teacher classroom
practices in PISA 2012, teachers in Singapore tend to provide more
structure, support, as well as challenge than teachers in all the other
three countries with the means of teachers classroom practices items in
Singapore generally higher than those in the other three countries
except for a few practices.

4.3. Path analysis results
Table 5 and Table 6 report the path analysis results for each country
using TIMSS 2011 and PISA 2012, respectively, including standardized
structural path coefficients, correlations, R2 values for selected endogenous variables, and data-model fit indices. For parsimony, factor
loadings in the measurement model are not discussed in this section
since they are covered in detail in the previous section. For each country,
the final model includes the three latent engagement factors (i.e., interest, self-concept, and future utility beliefs), three key independent
composite variables on teacher classroom practices, mathematics
achievement level outcome, and two demographic and home background variables (i.e., gender and socioeconomic status) as control
variables. The model achieves adequate data-model fit for all the four
countries in TIMSS 2011 and PISA 2012, with RMSEA less than 0.08 and
CFI greater than 0.90 in general.
Looking at TIMSS 2011 results (Table 5), limited evidence is found
that teacher-reported classroom practices have impact on student
engagement while the results suggest some practices are associated with
achievement outcomes. In comparison, demographic and home background explain more variance in both student engagement and
achievement outcomes.
Specifically, little to no association between teacher-reported classroom practices and interest in mathematics is found across countries.
This pattern applies to the relationship between teacher classroom
practices and future utility beliefs as well. For self-concept, only teacherreported challenging practices are found to be significantly and positively associated with it in Australia (β = 0.11), but not in the other
three countries. While structure and support are not found to be closely
related to mathematics achievement outcomes either, challenge is
significantly and positively associated with achievement outcomes in
Finland (β = 0.12), Australia (β = 0.15), and Romania (β = 0.12), but
not in Singapore.
On the other hand, demographic and home background is found to
be more strongly related to student engagement, especially self-concept.
Both gender and socioeconomic status (SES) have statistically significant
and positive associations with self-concept across the four countries. In
Australia, both gender and SES are significantly associated with interest
and future utility beliefs as well, but the association is less strong than
that with self-concept. In other countries, limited associations are found
between gender and interest or future utility beliefs, but SES remains a
consistent predictor of interest and future utility beliefs, with the
exception of Romania. Consistent with previous research (e.g., Kalaycioğlu, 2015; Takashiro, 2017), SES is found to be a strong predictor of
mathematics achievement outcomes across the four countries (β ranging
from 0.32 to 0.50).

Considering the contradictory findings on challenge in particular, one
explanation for the negative association observed in student reports is
reverse causation, with lower achieving students perceiving higher
levels of challenge (rather than challenging classroom content actually
reducing achievement outcomes). While in general student reports of
instructional practices tend to have more robust signal in these data than
teacher reports, simple cross-sectional measures of challenge reported
by students may not be very useful for making comparisons across
classrooms. Overall, the findings indicate the need for more reliable
measures of challenging instruction in future research. From the
perspective of flow theories of engagement, providing an optimal
amount of challenging instruction is the key in creating an engaging
learning environment (Shernoff et al., 2014). In other words, students
can experience increased engagement when they are appropriately
challenged with tasks that require reasonable cognitive demand and are
provided necessary support to complete the tasks. Only measuring the
frequency or difficulty level in the tasks reflects an oversimplified view
of “the more challenging the better” while the emphasis should be
placed on the appropriateness of challenge over the degree of challenge
in the measures. Alternatively, the current measures may just be
inherently indexed to the students’ relative achievement level (such that
higher achieving students report lower challenge) and might instead
refer to specific cognitive demands and activities.
In all, the different patterns in findings suggest implications for
studying teacher classroom practices. First, the association between
student-reported teacher classroom practices and engagement and
achievement is stronger than that between teacher-reported classroom
practices and engagement and achievement, highlighting the importance of different perspectives in the learning process. While it is
possible that larger measurement error exists in teacher reports of their
classroom practices due to social desirability bias, among other potential
sources of inaccurate reporting (e.g., recollection of classroom practices
in retrospective), it should be noted that teacher classroom practices are
not operationalized in exactly the same way across the two datasets. In
particular, TIMSS items measuring teacher classroom support focus
more on the emotional aspect of support while PISA items emphasize
academic support and autonomy supportive practices. This discrepancy
between the two datasets could be confounding the different patterns in
findings to some extent. Alternatively, perhaps, teacher reports of instruction need to be differently conceptualized around more specific and
discrete curricular and pedagogical content, with more exploratory
research goals for the time being.

In addition to teacher reports and student reports, a third-party
perspective on teachers’ classroom practices has been used in a
growing body of literature on teaching and classroom practices. This
perspective sheds additional light on understanding the influence of
teachers’ classroom practices on student outcomes given the stark differences found in results depending on the reporter. Findings from
Seidel and Shavelson’s (2007) meta-analysis of teaching effectiveness
research suggest that the effect size magnitudes tend to be the largest
when teaching practices data come from classroom observations, followed by when data are collected from student questionnaires and,
weakest of all, teacher questionnaires. Further, studies that used classroom observation data from videotape recordings suggest that certain
instructional approaches appear to be closely associated with desirable
learning outcomes in diverse settings (e.g., Leung, 2005;
Näslund-Hadley, Loera Varela, & Hepworth, 2014). At the same time,
counterevidence exists that null to modest relationships are found between teacher classroom practices and student outcomes (Garrett &
Steinberg, 2015). On the one hand, the mixed evidence suggests that
instruction is highly context-specific (Hansen, 1981; Harris, 2011);
certain effective teaching strategies in one setting may not be as effective
in another context. It is encouraging to see increasing evidence of
effective teaching in the literature based on classroom observations,
including international studies such as the TIMSS 1999 Video Study
(Hiebert et al., 2003) and the more recent TALIS Video Study (OECD,
2020). With technology advancements and the fast-developing automated analysis methods, it is hopeful that more fine-grained observational tools can soon become a cost-effective approach to be utilized in
large-scale studies (Kelly et al., 2020).
In Romania, it is curious that regardless of the source of reporting on
teacher classroom practices, little to no association is found between
teacher practices, structure and support in particular, and fewer
engagement outcomes. The exception is the positive association between support and self-concept, which is consistent with other countries. One possible explanation of the limited association between
teacher classroom practices and student outcomes is that the pattern (e.
g., frequency and intensity) of exposure to instruction may be differently
institutionalized in Romania. For instance, the number of hours per year
spent on mathematics instruction was considerably higher in Romania
(145) compared to that in Finland (105), according to the report TIMSS
2011 international results in mathematics (Mullis, Martin, Foy, & Arora,
2012). While it is counterintuitive that larger instructional effects were
found in Finland where less instructional time was provided, it would be
interesting to examine other factors that may help explain the “less is
more” phenomenon, such as the frequency and intensity of mathematics
classes. Specifically, future studies can investigate what pattern(s) of
exposure to instruction (e.g., more frequent classes with shorter time
periods vs. fewer classes a week but each with a longer time period) can
maximize the positive impact of instruction on student outcomes.
Another possible explanation is that in countries where there is
greater coherence in teachers’ practices, which may be achieved
through a standard curriculum or teacher training program, the stronger
the association between teacher practices, as perceived by students, and
student outcomes in the later grades. It is possible that students’ exposure to instructional practices that are similar to and build on what they
have experienced in earlier grades helps create a learning experience
with less variation and further strengthens the association between
teacher classroom practices and student outcomes. Compared to
Romania, results for Singapore, Finland, and Australia exhibit more
consistent and stronger relationships between teacher classroom practices, as reported by students, and student outcomes. This pattern, in
part, may be attributed to the national curriculum or teacher training
programs established in these countries that ensure greater coherence in
teachers’ practices across grades. Examples include the national School
Mathematics Curriculum in Singapore (Kaur, 2014), increased support
for teacher professional development programs at the national level in

Finland (Sahlberg, 2011), and Australia’s major federal government
policies on the “Quality Teacher Program” (Hardy, 2008).
Another unique finding about Romania is that challenging classroom
practices, as reported by students, are found to be positively related to
student engagement while the relation is either negative or limited in
other countries. Such paradoxical relationship may be partially
explained by the reference group effect, in which members of different
groups use different standards or references to evaluate given statements
(Heine et al., 2002). To illustrate, in responding to questions about how
frequently certain challenging instructional practice took place, Romanian student respondents may have used a reference that is different
from what students in other countries had in mind. In other words, what
a student in Romania perceives as challenging might be different than a
student in another country. It is also possible that Romanian students
used a different reference for evaluating statements about their
engagement. Take self-concept for example, evidence from existing
literature suggests that countries with higher achievement levels may
have more demanding curricula and higher academic standards, which
may result in lower levels of self-concept reported by students (Van de
gaer et al., 2012).
In addition, how students spend their time outside school hours and
parental involvement could also be a source diluting or enhancing
teacher’s influence on student outcomes in unique ways in different
contexts. Future research using available measures of these constructs
can inform and enrich the discussion about cross-country comparisons.
5.3. Significance of the study and future directions
This study uses international large-scale survey and assessment data
to examine a multidimensional construct, engagement, and its relations
with teacher classroom practices and achievement outcomes, with a
focus on mathematics. The findings are generalizable to large student
populations across four countries with diverse economic and social
backgrounds. Building upon previous research studying engagement in
smaller-scale settings, this study accumulates further evidence supporting the multi-dimensional conceptualization of engagement across
multiple education systems.
In addition, an important feature of this study was the use of both
teacher and student reports to simultaneously assess the same instructional constructs. An overall implication from comparing those data
sources is that while teachers’ perspectives remain important, teacher
reports alone are insufficient to provide a robust portrait of teaching
quality since they do not always identify the practices most strongly
related to student outcomes. This study corroborates existing literature
(e.g., Fauth et al., 2014) that examines student reports of teacher
classroom practices and provides international evidence that students’
perspectives can provide a valuable lens for studying teaching quality.
At the same time, it is recognized that no single instrument should be
used alone for high-stake purposes, such as teacher evaluation (Fauth
et al., 2014; Ferguson, 2012).
Future studies can further explore potential factors that moderate the
association between teacher classroom practices and student engagement and achievement across education systems. As suggested by recent
evidence in the German context, students from higher socio-economic
backgrounds tend to benefit more from certain types of teacher classroom practices compared to their peers from less advantaged backgrounds (Atlay, Tieben, Hillmert, & Fauth, 2019). More evidence on the
differential impact certain teacher practices may have on different
groups of students can better inform the development of more targeted
instructional interventions.
Due to limited data availability, this study only tested two of the
three dimensions in the theoretical framework of engagement (i.e.,
emotional and cognitive dimensions). Although some measures tapping
into the behavioral dimension are available in PISA 2012, this study only
focused on measures of the emotional and cognitive dimensions that are
available in both TIMSS 2011 and PISA 2012 for consistency and

Another limitation of this study relates to the cross-sectional design
of the data. While international large-scale survey and assessment
studies, such as TIMSS and PISA, provide valuable information for
investigating the relations between student outcomes and various factors at multiple levels and conducting cross-country comparisons, information about students’ prior achievement and their experiences in
earlier grades is often limited. As a consequence, the inferences drawn
from such secondary analyses, including the current study, are correlational in nature. Future research using longitudinal data to examine
the impact of teacher classroom practices from earlier grades on student
outcomes over time could provide additional insights into effective
classroom practices and teacher training.
It is also noted that this study looks at engagement in mathematics as
a general attitudinal orientation towards the subject. This is different
from measuring engagement at the moment of instruction, capturing
students’ real-time response to teacher classroom practices. More
research efforts that collect data on student engagement at the moment
of instruction could provide further evidence on how this type of
engagement is related to instruction and achievement outcomes and
inform interventions that facilitate engagement and improve achievement. Moreover, examining engagement in other subjects and how it is
related to teacher classroom practices and achievement outcomes that
are specific to other subjects may yield interesting cross-subject comparisons. Insights from multiple subjects could inform effective and
targeted interventions that ultimately make the overall learning experience both engaging and rewarding beyond the mathematics
classrooms.
