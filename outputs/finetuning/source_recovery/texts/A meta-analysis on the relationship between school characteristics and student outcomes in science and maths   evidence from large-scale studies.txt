ABSTRACT
In the present meta-analysis, we examine how secondary school
characteristics – such as schools’ academic press, school climate,
material resources, personnel resources, classroom climate, instruc-
tional practices, out-of-school activities, and socioeconomic status
(SES) composition – provide opportunities for students to engage
in science and maths, and how these matter with regard to students’
cognitive and motivational-aﬀective outcomes. The meta-analysis
includes 71 (international) articles from large-scale studies with
a total of 3,960,281 students, 260,390 schools, and 285 eﬀect sizes
that were transformed to correlation coeﬃcients. Multilevel meta-
analyses were performed. Results identiﬁed a number of school
variables that can be regarded as relevant for making a diﬀerence
in student outcomes and at the same time be inﬂuenced by educa-
tion. These refer to school variables such schools’ academic press,
classroom climate, instructional practices, and out-of-school activ-
ities. Moreover, SES composition was signiﬁcantly related to student
outcomes. Material and personnel resources as well as school climate
yielded a close to zero eﬀect. No diﬀerences were found between
cognitive and motivational-aﬀective outcome variables or between
science and maths. The results point to the most promising school
characteristics for promoting students’ outcomes and emphasise
schools’ potential for students’ engagement in science and maths.

Introduction
From economic, societal, and political perspectives, students’ skills in science and mathe-
matics are undeniably important (Bosworth et al., 2013; Mullis & Martin, 2016). However,
students still lack proﬁciency as well as engagement with science (Krapp & Prenzel, 2011;
National Research Council, 2011a, 2013; OECD, 2016). Therefore, many stakeholders in
education question how to promote students’ outcomes in science and education, and
research is concerned with determining the variables that predict these outcomes. While
’internal’ factors such as students’ personal backgrounds (e.g. family characteristics, socio-
economic status [SES], and cultural capital) have repeatedly and consistently been shown

to predict student science outcomes (Fouad et al., 2010; Archer, DeWitt, Osborne, & Dillon
et al., 2012; Nugent et al., 2015; George & Kaplan, 1997; Taskinen et al., 2013), these
personal background characteristics cannot easily be inﬂuenced or changed. Therefore,
when searching for factors that can be actively manipulated and developed, schools are
regarded as valuable resource. Science education research on school-related opportu-
nities and their relationships with student outcomes has been expanding (e.g. Belland,
Walker & Kim, 2017; Belland, Walker, Kim et al., 2017; Basl, 2011; D’Agostino, 2000;
Hogrebe & Tate, 2010; X. Ma & Klinger, 2000). Basl (2011), for example, found that students
attending schools that oﬀer preparation for science-related careers showed higher career
orientations; Hogrebe and Tate (2010) investigated the inﬂuence of SES composition, and
D’Agostino (2000), Belland, Walker & Kim (2017) and Belland, Walker, Kim et al. (2017)
analysed whether instructional characteristics predicted students’ achievement (in STEM;
science, technology, engineering, and maths). Although schools characteristics are rather
distal with regard to the concrete learning activities necessary for acquiring knowledge
and reaching certain learning developments, schools do promote student outcomes by
oﬀering and expanding learning opportunities and by providing a speciﬁc learning
environment than can enable necessary learning actitivities (Seidel & Shavelson, 2007).
Therefore, school characteristics interact with classroom variables and aﬀect teaching (e.g.
Maag Merki et al., 2015; Reynolds et al., 2014). The learning opportunities schools provide
are manifold and a large variety of school variables is under research. Multiple frameworks
intend to integrate these various school variables into one model. Although these frame-
works do not have a speciﬁc focus on science or maths, they are useful for categorising
the multitude of school variables that are under investigation. Opdenakker and Van
Damme (2006), for example, distinguish between school composition, school context,
and school practice, which, in turn, aﬀect the classroom as well as students’ achievement.
While school composition (e.g. student population, teaching staﬀ, school leader) and
school context (e.g. location, resources, sizes) are – at least at the school level – diﬃcult to
change, school practice (e.g. organisation and management, work and learning climate)
can be developed. Scheerens (2015) also provides a framework of school variables and
diﬀerentiates between school antecedents (e.g. higher level policies, school neighbour-
hood), school ecology (e.g. student composition, resources, extracurricular activities), and
school leadership, policies, and organisations (e.g. curriculum, achievement orientation,
cooperation among staﬀ). Although the two models diﬀer in terminology as well as in
grouping diﬀerent variables into categories, both assume that these school characteristics
form the environment for actual learning opportunities in the classroom with relation-
ships to students’ outcomes, particularly, to students’ achievement.
Previous meta-analyses and reviews in the ﬁeld of school characteristics examine single
variables only, such as school climate (e.g. Thapa & Cohen, 2013), school resources
(Greenwald et al., 1996; Hanushek, 1997; Hedges, & Pigott et al., 2016), school SES
composition (e.g. Mickelson et al., 2013; mathematics outcomes), extracurricular activities
(e.g. Holland & Andre, 1987), teacher-student relationship (e.g. Quin, 2017), or teaching
methods (e.g. Furtak et al., 2012; science education) and their relationship with student
outcomes. These meta-analyses have three open challenges: First, the meta-analyses do
not focus on a speciﬁc discipline (e.g. are not speciﬁcally restricted to science and maths
education) but rather include student outcomes in various disciplines. Scheerens and
Blömeke (2016) noted, however, that it is not yet clear how school characteristics are

related to subject-speciﬁc outcomes. Second, the comparison of multiple school learning
opportunities at the same time has not yet been considered. Meta-analyses on single
school variables are worthwhile with respect to the question how one variable is related
to student outcomes. However, without comparing these absolute eﬀect sizes to other
school characteristics, these absolute values are diﬃcult to interpret. They do not allow
inferences on the relevance of the variable conglomerate. Therefore, it is not yet clear
which of the school’s learning opportunities are most relevant. Finally, most of the present
literature reviews focus on cognitive outcomes. However, a narrow view on students’
achievement as a cognitive outcome has been criticised and it has been claimed that non-
cognitive student outcomes should also be considered (e.g. Grossman, Cohen, & Ronfeldt
et al., 2014; Reynolds et al., 2014; Seidel & Shavelson, 2007), particularly, in the ﬁeld of
science (Fredericks et al., 2017; Greenﬁeld, 1997; Lamb et al., 2012; Logan & Skamp, 2013).
Non-cognitive outcomes encompass, for example, motivational-aﬀective outcomes. In
their review of motivational constructs, Murphy and Alexander (2000) diﬀerentiated
between goals, interest, (intrinsic and extrinsic) motivation, and self-schema, such as self-
eﬃcacy or self-concept. In addition to motivational orientations, aﬀective outcomes
deﬁned as emotions (e.g. enjoyment, anxiety) can be examined as student outcomes
(Shuell, 1996). Motivational-aﬀective constructs are usually conceptualised as context-
and discipline-speciﬁc, and this, in turn, should be reﬂected in their measurement. Also,
with the multitude of school variables, diﬀerent processes may apply in predicting
students’ cognitive and motivational-aﬀective variables. While schools’ extracurricular
activities may rather be related with motivational-aﬀective variables, students’ cognitive
outcomes may be predicted by high learning expectations.
The present study
In light of previous meta-analyses, the present meta-analysis aims to summarise interna-
tional research on the relationship between school variables (as learning opportunities)
and student cognitive and non-cognitive outcomes in science and maths in secondary
schools, and examines whether diﬀerent processes on the school-level apply (Eisenhart
et al., 2015). Therewith, the present study advances previous research by (a) including
multiple school variables concurrently, (b) investigating diﬀerences in cognitive and non-
cognitive outcome variables, and (c) addressing subject-speciﬁc relationships. In particu-
lar, we examined the following research question: How does the eﬀect size for the
relationship between school variables and student outcomes diﬀer for (a) diﬀerent groups
for school characteristics (e.g. material resources, school climate), (b) cognitive and
motivational-aﬀective outcomes, and (c) science and maths?
Therewith, we aim to identify those school-related variables that seem to be most
promising (a) when promoting students’ outcomes in science and maths. Contrary to
previous meta-analyses that focused on single school variables, the present study
expands the knowledge about the speciﬁc relevance of diﬀerent school variables and
enables recommendations about initiatives on the promotion of science and maths
outcomes based on the relative importance of school characteristics. By comparing
cognitive and motivational-aﬀective outcome variables (b), we gain information about
whether, for instance, school variables are more strongly related to motivational-aﬀective
variables than to cognitive variables. Testing the generalisability of eﬀects across science

With our goal to compare multiple school characteristics and multiple student outcome
variables in the meta-analysis, we face the challenge of a meta-analysis that needs to
guarantee the comparability of included studies. In order to have similar operationalisation
of the variables under investigation, we analysed the instruments of large-scales studies and
found comparable measures across studies. Therefore, we decided to focus on large-scale
studies only. Moreover, large-scale studies are characterised by surveying a large sample with
a wide range of participants (e.g. students, teachers, principals) and measures (e.g. standar-
dised achievement tests, background questionnaire) with a high degree of standardisation.
With their wide range of instruments and our goal to compare multiple school variables
concurrently, large-scale studies were particularly appropriate for the present study.
Method
Literature search
To answer our research questions, we conducted a meta-analysis. Our literature search
process involved two steps. First, we searched subject-speciﬁc databases ERIC (Education)
and Web of Science (social sciences – general/interdisciplinary, pedagogy, philosophy,
political science, psychology, law, sociology, economic sciences). The search in both
databases yielded mostly distinct results with only few duplicates (see below). We used
keyword combinations concerning (1) school variables, (2) student outcomes (cognitive
and motivational-aﬀective), and (3) the disciplines science and maths. Detailed search
terms are presented in Table 1. As we intended to cover a broad range of variables
characterised as school variables, studies that claimed to investigate school variables were
eligible. Our search included all appropriate synonyms. The Boolean terms ’AND’ and
’NEAR’ separated the search terms, resulting in the following search construction: school
variables AND (student outcomes [two separate searches for cognitive and motivational-
aﬀective variables] NEAR/1 science and maths).1 As outlined above, we limited our search

Inclusion criteria and study selection
In addition to search criteria such as publication period, we established further criteria for
inclusion: studies were eligible if (a) they addressed a relationship between school
variables and student outcomes (thematic ﬁt), (b) the dependent variable under investi-
gation was related to at least science or maths, (c) the sample was located at the
secondary education level,2 (d) they were empirical quantitative studies, (e) they reported
all relevant parameters to calculate or transform the eﬀect size necessary for the meta-
analysis (see section ’Eﬀect sizes’ for further details), (f) they were published in the English
language, (g) they were published in peer-reviewed journals, and (h) they were part of
a large-scale project3 either on a national or an international level (i.e., Trends in
International Mathematics and Science Study [TIMSS; n = 23]; Programme for
International Student Assessment [PISA; n = 31]; National Assessment of Educational
Progress [NAEP; n = 1]; National Longitudinal Survey of Youth [NLSY; n = 2]; National
Longitudinal Study [NLS; n = 2]; National Education Longitudinal Study [NELS; n = 8];
Longitudinal Study of American Youth [LSAY; n = 5]; High-school and Beyond Study [HS:B;
n = 2]; Educational Longitudinal Study [ELS; n = 1]; Early Childhood Longitudinal Study
[ECLS; n = 1]). We used large-scale studies as inclusion criteria in order to have comparable
research designs and instruments assessing the variables under investigation.
During an initial screening in which we reviewed titles and abstracts, we categorised
the articles as eligible, non-eligible, or indecisive with regard to the inclusion criteria. In
this step, 685 articles were directly excluded. For example, excluded articles solely
assessed general (not subject-speciﬁc) student outcomes (e.g. Dettweiler et al., 2015),
only elementary schools (e.g. Adams, 2014), were not based on a large-scale study (e.g.
Bae, 2007), or did not examine school variables (e.g. Gregory & Weinstein, 2004).
Full texts were acquired for the articles in the eligible and indecisive categories. We
contacted authors to retrieve those articles for which full text was not accessible. Articles
(n = 19) for which the full text still could not be retrieved were excluded from the analysis.
The most frequent reasons for exclusion were that the articles did not address the
relationship between school variables and students’ outcomes in science or maths, did
not provide suﬃcient details for eﬀect sizes, or addressed a sample of elementary
students. As we decided to include large-scale studies only (e.g. PISA, TIMSS), we checked
whether the studies used the same database (i.e., study year, countries, and variables
under investigation) to exclude that the same eﬀect size was aggregated twice. The study
pairs Alacacı and Erbaş (2010) and Demir et al. (2010), Akyüz (2014) and Ker (2015), as well
as Lam and Lau (2014) and Sun et al. (2012), used the same database and some of their
variables were similar; however, their models were quite diﬀerent so that we decided not
to exclude the studies. The coding of the full texts with respect to the inclusion criteria
yielded a total of 71 articles, 285 reported eﬀect sizes, 3,960,281 students, and 260,390
schools.4 The reference list contains all included studies, which are denoted with an
asterisk.

School characteristics
As we speciﬁed relatively broad search terms for school variables (e.g. school factor,
school context, see Table 1), we sought to include studies that investigate multiple school
characteristics concurrently. We used the school and classroom levels of Scheerens (2015)
Integrated Multilevel Model of School Eﬀectiveness as a framework to categorise the
school characteristics (the original models additionally comprised a system and a student
level which exceed the focus of this meta-analysis). By using this framework as
a theoretical base for our coding scheme, we aimed at capturing the following main
categories of school characteristics: school ecology; school leadership, policies, and
organisation; classroom ecology and climate; and teaching. The following section
describes the main categories and the indicators for the main category (see also Figure 2).
The category school ecology consisted of schools’ composition regarding students’ socio-
economic background and school climate. We used several variables as indicators for school
SES composition, namely parental educational and occupational background, SES index
(e.g. International Socio-Economic Index of Occupational Status [ISEI], Index of Economic,

Social and Cultural Status [ESCS]), free or reduced lunch eligibility, and ethnic minority (e.g.
Alacacı & Erbaş, 2010). For this code, it was necessary that socioeconomic background was
not measured exclusively at the student level, but that a measure at the school level was
analysed (e.g. school mean). School climate can be deﬁned as the quality and character of
school life (J. Cohen, & McCabe et al., 2009). Studies examining school climate had to
explicitly address the school environment (as opposed to classroom climate). Indicators
were problem behaviours (e.g. attendance, crime, violence, suspension; Perry & Morris,
2014), quality of relationships (e.g. student-teacher, teacher-teacher; Shin et al., 2009),
school learning climate (e.g. Grubb, 2008), and safety (e.g. Parcel & Dufur, 2001).
The category school policies and organisation is characterised by curriculum, schools’
academic press, material resources, and personnel resources. Curriculum is deﬁned by diﬀer-
entiation and focus of courses. Speciﬁcally, this includes ability grouping (e.g. Park, 2005).
Schools’ academic press refers to the school environment (Bryk & Thum, 1989) and was
deﬁned by how strongly the school environment pushes students to academic success and
conforms to speciﬁc standards of achievement. Indicators were academic achievement
expectations (e.g. Ker, 2015) and emphasis on homework (Matsuoka, 2014). Material resources
refer to a school’s material infrastructure at the school – in particular, the availability or
shortage of adequate resources (e.g. Akyüz, 2014) and information and communication
technology (ICT) resources (e.g. D. Zhang & Liu, 2016). Personnel resources refer to the
characteristics of a school’s personnel. Indicators were: the degree to which teachers were
certiﬁed (e.g. Southworth, 2010), whether they had a teaching degree or a Master’s in
education (e.g. Parcel & Dufur, 2001), teacher experience (e.g. Mohammadpour, 2012b), the
number of teachers (i.e., percentage of female teachers, percentage of science teachers,
teacher-student ratio, teacher shortage; e.g. Mohammadpour, 2012b), and teachers’ readiness
to teach (e.g. Ker, 2015).
As indicators for the category classroom ecology we used classroom climate and
examined how learning climate in class (e.g. student-teacher or student-student relation-
ships, and disciplinary climate; Shin et al., 2009) or limitations on instruction (e.g. aca-
demic abilities, backgrounds, or needs too diverse; disruptive or uninterested students;
Mohammadpour, 2012b) were related to student outcomes.
Finally, the category teaching was assessed by teachers’ instructional practices, pre-
paration for the future, and out-of-school activities. With regard to instructional practices,
we contrasted student-centred techniques (e.g. hands-on activities, investigation, inquiry-
based teaching, or application to real-world context; Mere et al., 2006) with teacher-
centred techniques (e.g. traditional teaching approaches; Papanastasiou, 2008). In addi-
tion to teachers’ instructional practices, schools may oﬀer supplementary learning oppor-
tunities for experiences in science and maths, such as preparation for the future and out-
of-school activities. The studies deﬁned preparation for the future as the degree to which
schools prepare their students for science/academic careers in general or for a STEM
career in college (e.g. Basl, 2011). Experiences and learning opportunities in science and
maths that do not take place in the regular classroom were coded as out-of-school
activities. Indicators included oﬀering school activities (e.g. science clubs, excursions,
competitions) or programmes in science and maths (e.g. Taskinen et al., 2013).
Due to the small number of studies that investigated preparation (n = 3) and curricu-
lum (n = 2) these variables were excluded from the analyses, as the statistical power for
these variables would be rather low (Valentine et al., 2010).

Student outcome variables
In the large-scale study set, students’ cognitive outcomes were operationalised by using
test scores, grades, or GPA. The motivational-aﬀective outcomes comprised motivation,
enjoyment, interest, attitudes, self-eﬃcacy, or self-concept. All variables were used as
equivalent indicators for cognitive or motivational-aﬀective outcomes.
Discipline variables
Finally, the discipline under investigation was coded. We coded articles according to
whether they examined maths or science.
Eﬀect sizes
The primary studies included in the meta-analysis reported multiple types of eﬀect sizes
(diﬀerences in mean, log odds, correlation coeﬃcients, β, etc.). Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, &
Rothstein et al. (2009) point out that ‘the fact that they used diﬀerent metrics should not be an
impediment’ (p. 45), in case the studies are judged to be comparable. To summarise the eﬀect
sizes, it is essential that all eﬀect sizes have the same metric (Morris & DeShon, 2002).
Therefore, we ﬁrst transformed all eﬀect sizes to correlation coeﬃcients and their standard
errors using the Comprehensive Meta-Analyses software (Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, &
Rothstein et al., 2014). We used Peterson and Brown (2005) formula to convert standardised
regression coeﬃcients into correlation coeﬃcients. The articles reported regression coeﬃcients
as standardised or unstandardised eﬀect sizes. To avoid an immense loss of information by
excluding these articles, we converted the unstandardised regression coeﬃcients into stan-
dardised coeﬃcients by using the (pooled) standard deviations of the dependent and
independent variables (Becker & Wu, 2007; Slagt et al., 2016).5 In cases where no standard
deviation for the independent and/or dependent variables was reported, or where the
manually standardised values were invalid (e.g. values larger than 1), the eﬀect sizes were
excluded from the analysis.
Finally, we recoded negatively directed variables (e.g. percentage of students eligible
for free lunch) and their corresponding eﬀect sizes so that all eﬀects were positively
directed. If several regression or multilevel models were reported in the article, we used
the coeﬃcients from the most comprehensive model in this study, except when
a mediator or moderator was added to the ﬁnal model.
Data analysis
On average, 4.01 eﬀect sizes were reported per study. Studies investigated (a) multiple
outcomes (e.g. science and maths achievement), (b) multiple indicators for one outcome
variable (e.g. maths grade and standardised maths achievement test), (c) multiple indepen-
dent variables (e.g. SES composition and climate variables), (d) multiple indicators for one
independent variable (e.g. percentage of free lunch, or Economic Social Cultural Status [ESCS],
as indicators for SES), or (e) independent subsamples/subgroups within the study (e.g. data
from PISA 2006 and PISA 2009, diﬀerent countries, diﬀerent ages/grades, diﬀerent projects).
When studies analysed multiple indicators for either the independent or the dependent
variable (b and c), we averaged these eﬀect sizes (Donker et al., 2014; Hunter & Schmidt, 2004;

Malti & Krettenauer, 2013) using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (Borenstein,
Hedges, Higgins, & Rothstein et al., 2013), yielding a combined measure of eﬀect sizes.
With the exception of independent samples/subgroups within studies (e), eﬀect sizes
within studies were interdependent. Consequently, we conducted multilevel meta-analyses
to address the nested data structure using the metaSEM package (Cheung, 2015) in the
R statistical environment (R Development Core Team, 2016). Since 26.76% of the articles
examined multiple independent samples, we used the sample instead of the articles/studies
as cluster variables. We performed a three-level meta-analysis, with the sampling variance of
eﬀect sizes on level one, the eﬀect sizes within each study’s speciﬁc sample on level two, and
the study samples on level three (cf. Gajda et al., 2016; Machts et al., 2016; Scammacca et al.,
2014). Finally, we used the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010) to examine publication bias
via the funnel plot and the Egger’s regression test (Egger et al., 1997). The raw data and

Before answering the research questions, we ﬁrst examined the within- and between-
sample variances of the overall model. In order to examine diﬀerences in eﬀect sizes across
school characteristics (research question 1a), cognitive and motivational-aﬀective variables
(research question 1b) and disciplines (research question 1c), we used two approaches: First,
we calculated separate analyses for each variables to gain a descriptive overview over the
data. Second, we calculated moderator analyses in a multilevel meta-analysis model. These
moderator analyses enabled us to account for dependencies among eﬀect sizes, examine
statistical signiﬁcance of the diﬀerences, and explain the variance of the baseline model.
Sensitivity analysis and publication bias
To identify whether outliers might bias the analyses, we ﬁrst examined the distribution of
eﬀect sizes in a forest plot, and then identiﬁed eﬀect sizes that were larger than three
standard deviations above or below the overall eﬀect. Further, to test whether the results
were inﬂuenced by a publication bias, we used funnel plots and Egger’s regression test
(Egger et al., 1997) which are both available in the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010)
using trim and ﬁll (Duval & Tweedie, 2000). Finally, we included a robustness check with

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias
In the present analyses, two eﬀect sizes were above or below three standard devia-
tions of the overall mean and can thus be regarded as outliers (i.e., Horn et al., 2006,
SES composition; Papanastasiou, 2008, instructional practices). We eliminated the
eﬀect sizes and re-ran the overall meta-analysis. The results did not meaningfully
diﬀer (r = .14; 95% CI [.12, .17]). Thus, we decided to use the full study sample as the
data basis.
We created a funnel plot (see Figure 11; standard error on the y-axis, eﬀect sizes on
the x-axis) and used Duval and Tweedie’s trim and ﬁll method to assess how the point
estimates are aﬀected. The imputed eﬀect sizes (white dots) indicate that rather positive
eﬀect sizes necessary for a symmetric plot are missing in the study set. Similarly, the
Egger’s regression test revealed indication of publication bias on the eﬀect size level
(z = −2.58, p < .01). On the study level, the Egger’s regression did not indicate publication
bias (z = 0.08, p < .93). In sum, it seems that signiﬁcant positive eﬀects are missing in our
sample.

Discussion
In the present paper, we attempted to investigate how school variables diﬀer in their
relationship with student outcomes in science and maths (research question 1a). Contrary
to meta-analyses and reviews that examine single variables only, the present meta-
analysis provided insights into the most promising school-related variables by investigat-
ing multiple school variables concurrently in a multi-level moderator analysis. The ﬁnd-
ings revealed that the eﬀect sizes diﬀer signiﬁcantly across school variables and that
including the school variables in the model (instead of having an overall model only)
enabled us to explain 54% of the within-sample variation in the eﬀect sizes. In particular,
we gained knowledge about the speciﬁc relevance of the most relevant school character-
istics (Wood et al., 2006).
We found the largest relationship for schools’ SES composition. While the individual
socioeconomic status of students is often related to students’ cognitive achievement (e.g.
Berkowitz et al., 2017), in the present analyses we focused on the schools’ SES

with van Ewijk and Sleegers (2010) who also did not ﬁnd signiﬁcant diﬀerences between
science and maths for the eﬀect of SES on students’ achievement.
Limitations
The ﬁndings from this meta-analysis need to be interpreted in light of the focus of the
study and the inclusion criteria imposed for study selection. In the present study, we
aimed at synthesising international research on general school characteristics in science
and maths. Therefore, we used broad search terms with synonyms of school character-
istics as one relevant keyword and, in a second step, we coded the variables under
investigation more speciﬁcally (i.e., generic as well as subject-speciﬁc variables). We
deliberately applied this approach in order to gain knowledge about the speciﬁc rele-
vance of various school variables. However, studies that investigated a speciﬁc variable
not labelled as school characteristics, for example, the relationship between school
climate and students’ outcome in science, may not have been included in the present
meta-analysis. Therefore, the estimated eﬀect sizes are limited to those studies who
embed their analyses in the context of general school characteristics.
Moreover, as this study constitutes a ﬁrst step towards demonstrating schools’ potential
to promote student outcomes in science and maths, we restricted our analysis to school
characteristics as an independent variable even though internal factors such as students’
personal backgrounds are highly relevant for predicting student outcomes in science and
maths. Nevertheless, a large number of the included studies analysed both individual and
school characteristics in their regression models. Consequently, the summarised overall
eﬀect can be interpreted as the relationship between school variables and student out-
comes in science and maths over and above individual student diﬀerences.
As the primary studies included in the meta-analysis possessed a multitude of indica-
tors for school characteristics, we limited our search to large-scale studies. Through this
restriction as well as through careful coding and analysis of studies we included only
comparable eﬀect sizes in the aggregation (Cooper et al., 2009). Otherwise, measure-
ments and research designs would have been too diverse and the requirement for a meta-
analysis to include comparable studies only would not have been met. Without the
restriction to large-scale studies, 115 eﬀect sizes stemmed from non-large-scale studies

as compared to 307 eﬀect sizes from large-scale studies. With the focus on large-scale
studies, the present study takes up nicely J. A. Middleton et al.’s (2015) work on large-
scales studies in mathematics education. While they present exemplary studies on the
topics of curriculum, teaching, and learning, with a focus on intervention studies in
mathematics education, the present meta-analysis adopted a more comprehensive
approach on school-level predictors of learning, included science as well, and aggregated
the existing ﬁndings statistically. Nevertheless, the restriction to large-scale studies also
inheres some limitations. Depicting the fact that large-scale studies are dominated by
correlational designs, we transformed all eﬀect sizes to correlation coeﬃcients in order to
be able to compare and synthesise eﬀect sizes. Thus, we are unable to draw causal
inferences from the results. Experimental studies, applying controlled designs, have
a higher potential regarding causal conclusions; however, with the broad focus on school
characteristics, these operationalisation would have not been comparable anymore.
Finally, the study selection was limited to studies from secondary education as well as
English and peer-reviewed journals. As DeWitt and Archer (2015) revealed that diﬀerences
in science career orientations already exist in elementary school, the question remains
whether our ﬁndings on the potential of school characteristics for student outcomes can
be transferred to elementary education as well. The restriction to publications in the
English language might not reﬂect the international state of research. However, 73 % of
the studies have included countries other than the US, covering countries such as
Singapore (11%) or Turkey (7%), so we can conclude that international data has been
synthesised in the present meta-analysis. Country-speciﬁc eﬀects cannot be excluded,
however, due to the high degree of standardisation and high quality standards in large-
scale studies, between-country diﬀerences should not result from diﬀerences in the
methodological procedure.The restriction to peer-reviewed journals may have caused
publication bias in favour of signiﬁcant results (Matt & Cook, 2009). The analysis of
publication bias revealed that signiﬁcant positive eﬀects are missing in the present
study. This pattern is rather uncommon for publication bias as a consequence of focusing
on peer-reviewed journals, for which missing non-signiﬁcant eﬀects must be expected.
Therefore, we suggest that with our focus on large-scale studies, the restriction on peer-
reviewed journals was the method of choice.
Implications for future research
The present study yields implications for future research and practice. First, in addition to
the aggregation of eﬀect sizes, this meta-analysis also provides a literature overview of
studies focused on school characteristics and student outcomes in science and maths. The
review and the coding of the included studies demonstrate that the majority examines
cognitive student outcomes (256 eﬀect sizes as opposed to 29 from motivational-
aﬀective). To increase our knowledge about how school variables are related to students’
motivational-aﬀective variables, more research is needed. In particular, future research
should rely on theoretical considerations of diﬀerential eﬀects between school character-
istics and students’ cognitive and motivational-aﬀective variables, respectively. While it
may be assumed that out-of-school activities, for example, may rather be related to
motivational-aﬀective variables, research has not yet compared cognitive and motiva-
tional-aﬀective outcome variables systematically. Second, Scheerens and Blömeke (2016)

claimed to examine subject-speciﬁc relationships. In the future, it will be interesting to
contrast disciplines that conceptually may be more diﬀerent from science and maths.
Although some of the included studies in the present meta-analysis also examined out-
comes in other ﬁelds, such as reading, history, or life sciences, with our search terms we
did not intend to compare these disciplines systematically. Third, focusing on science and
maths, one additional outcome variable may be derived, namely students’ career orienta-
tions (Osborne et al., 2003; Means et al., 2016; National Research Council, 2011a; Reinhold
et al., 2018). In an overview of 28 studies investigating school eﬀects on students’ career
orientations, Reinhold et al. (2018) found various conceptualisations of the construct of
career orientation in STEM (e.g. interest in engineering or science-related careers, inten-
tion to declare a STEM major in college, career aspirations in science, completion of
a STEM major). Due to this conceptual heterogeneity and the small number of studies
they have identiﬁed (only eight were large-scale studies), we decided to focus, as a ﬁrst
step, on the comparison of cognitive and motivational-aﬀective outcomes. Fourth, the
literature review revealed further gaps in the literature with regard to curriculum (n = 2)
and preparation programs (n = 3). We have excluded these variables from our analyses
due to reasons of statistical power. Hence, this points to the need for future research to
learn more about their relevance. Finally, although theoretically assumed, hardly any
study included in the present meta-analysis examined mediating or moderating eﬀects.
For instance, it could be reasonable that eﬀect sizes for instructional practices might diﬀer
depending on the availability of material resources and thus do not show a linear, but
rather a nonlinear eﬀect. Therefore, future research should not only focus on underlying
mechanisms to promote students’ engagement in science and maths but also address
various conditions for a potential inﬂuence (i.e., moderator analyses).
Practical implications
By including studies that investigate school characteristics in general and by including
multiple school variables concurrently, the present meta-analysis analysed the relative
importance of diﬀerent school variables and identiﬁed which school variables matter the
most for student cognitive and motivational-aﬀective outcomes in science and maths.
Previous meta-analyses have focused on single school variables and thus prevent com-
paring eﬀect sizes across studies and across school variables. In the present study, the
largest eﬀect sizes were found for schools’ SES composition. However, our ﬁndings also
underline the importance of those learning opportunities that can be shaped and mod-
iﬁed by schools (e.g. school’s academic press, classroom climate, instructional practices,
and out-of-school activities). Certainly, these learning opportunities take place within
a particular context of a school (i.e., school’s SES composition or material or personnel
resources), which cannot easily be altered. Likewise, student outcomes in science and
maths are – in a large part – determined by internal factors, such as the student’s
educational background. The fact that signiﬁcant eﬀects were found over and above
the contextual variables reveals that secondary schools do have the potential to promote
students’ engagement in science and maths. Thus, our ﬁndings support that individual
schools are not only accountable for but also capable of promoting students’ outcomes in
science and maths. Based on the results of the present meta-analysis, initiatives on the
promotion of science and maths (from administrators or stakeholdes) should focus on
