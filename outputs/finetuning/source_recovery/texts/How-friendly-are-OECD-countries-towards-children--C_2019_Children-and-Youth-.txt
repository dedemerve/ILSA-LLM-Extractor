A B S T R A C T
The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC; United Nations, 1989) has brought upon a new reali-
zation of children's rights as including both nurturance and care rights and the newly adapted self-determination
rights. However, efforts to measure countries' commitment to children have neglected self-determination rights.
This study builds upon a previous study that took a holistic approach to studying African countries' commitment
towards children using a “child friendliness index”. This composite index was adjusted to fit the challenges faced
by developed OECD countries and utilized sets of indicators on child self-determination and nurturance and care
in order to create friendliness score for each country. Results show that Nordic countries are the most child-
friendly, with high scores on both sets of indicators, while Korea, Spain, Turkey and the Czech Republic are the
least. Results also show that there is no association between the two sets of indicators, and while Gini coefficient
and GDP per capita were correlated with nurturance and care, they were insignificantly linked with child self-
determination. These findings could indicate that child friendliness requires a commitment not only to securing
the nurturance and care of children, but also to assist children in promoting their self-determination, an over-
looked aspect in child welfare research and policy. This kind of action could enable children to participate as
active agents in society.
1. Introduction
The apparent gap between children's rights as a formality and their
implementation in practice calls for new measurements set to asses
countries' commitment to children - as the realization of children's
rights and well-being in practice (Mekonen, 2010). This view is based
on a notion of children's rights as including new participation rights
side by side with the classic nurturance and care rights, a branch of
rights that has yet to receive enough attention in that literature, par-
ticularly in measurement efforts (Ben-Arieh, 2008; Ben-Arieh &
Tarshish, 2016).
Hence, This study seeks to utilize the framework of the United
Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC; United Nations,
1989) and the theoretical school of the “new” sociology of childhood as
tools for creating a child-friendliness index for OECD countries that will
be sensitive to both rights of nurturance and care and rights of self-
determination as provided for by the convention on the rights of the
child (Rogers & Wrightsman, 1978; Ruck & Horn, 2008). This attempt
builds and expands on work by Mekonen (2010), who created a similar
index using indicators of children's rights in African countries.
1.1. The normative framework: UNCRC
The UNCRC has been regarded by many as the single most im-
portant milestone in determining countries' commitment towards chil-
dren (Ruck & Horn, 2008). The UNCRC is a comprehensive statement
that outlines different types of children's rights: economic, political,
civil, and social. Unlike previous declarations, it goes far beyond the
mere protection of children by addressing their right to have a voice
and to achieve their full potential (Kosher, Jiang, Ben-Arieh, &
Huebner, 2014).
The rights stipulated in the UNCRC are often grouped into clusters.
One accepted typology name three distinct types of rights mentioned in
the UNCRC, or “the three Ps”: provision, protection, and participation
(Troope, 1996). Provision concerns the provision of appropriate ser-
vices, goods, and resources, so that children are able to develop their
abilities fully; Protection concerns the right to be safe from various risk
factors and harmful living conditions; and Participation relates to the
right of the child to be respected as an active member of the family,
community and society (Kosher et al., 2014).
Others classify children's rights by dividing the articles of the
UNCRC into rights of nurturance and care, which are paternalistic by

In any case, the rights granted in the UNCRC are to be seen as both
“present” and “future” oriented (Ben-Arieh & Tarshish, 2016; Qvortrup,
2002); the child as a “becoming” citizen in need of nurturance and care,
and a “being” present actor that has the right of self-determination. This
view is well complemented with the “new” sociology of childhood.
1.2. The theoretical framework: The “new” sociology of childhood
The “new” sociology of childhood views childhood as a stage in
itself, with its defining characteristics – a permanent but ever-changing
social structure, similar to gender, class, and race (Qvortrup, 1994,
2002, 2009). According to this view, the childhood structure interacts
with other structures of society, and thus children are seen as active
social actors in the present with a role in shaping their world (Ben-
Arieh, 2000; Prout, 1997).
As a society, we are still accustomed, however, to view children as
members of adult-dominated families or schools, as subunits that can be
understood and quantified in terms of income, education and child
protection (Qvortrup, 2002). In that respect, the story of children is
similar to the story of other marginalized groups in social policy,
women in particular. Feminist criticism of this androcentric view of
social policy spurred reconsideration of the place of women in welfare
states. The scholars leading that trend created a parallel route of social
policy theory and research, one that looked at the effects of social
policy on women and vice versa (Lewis, 1992; Williams, 1995), and
developed new variables and typologies (Gal & Ajzenstadt, 2010).
The sociology of childhood calls for a similar effort to be directed to
children. If childhood is present and not only future-oriented, a social
structure like gender, then children must be allowed an active role in
changing policy, research, and services (Ben-Arieh, 2005, 2008), and
the effects of social policy on children must be better understood. It also
stresses the need to look beyond traditional indicators and to new ter-
ritories of children's active, present role as social actors: child partici-
pation in the personal and public domain in order to fully encompass
the commitment of countries to children. As Olk (2010) stated, such a
new paradigm “can only be established by implementing new ideas and
values concerning the role of childhood and children in society” (p. 6).
1.3. Child-friendly governance
Comparative research on children's living conditions is a relatively
new development (Ben-Arieh, 2005, 2008). Since the United Nations
Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) was ratified, we wit-
nessed growing efforts aimed at comparatively measuring children's
life, their wellbeing (Ben-Arieh, 2006; Bradshaw, 2015; Schulz, Ainley,
Fraillon, Kerr, & Losito, 2009) and state generosity towards children.
Notable comparative efforts include a comparison of child benefit
packages in 22 countries (Bradshaw & Finch, 2002), ISCWeb children's
worlds survey (Rees & Main, 2015), an index of child well-being in
Europe (Bradshaw & Richardson, 2009), the Health Behavior in School-
aged Children survey (Currie et al., 2012), UNICEF Innocenti report
cards (see for example Chzhen, Gromada, Rees, Cuesta, & Bruckauf,
2018; UNICEF, 2017) and OECD reports and working papers such as
“how is life for children”, “doing better for children” and “doing better
for families (OECD, 2009, 2011, 2015). However, these studies did not
attempt to measure the newer, more comprehensive notion of child
“friendliness” towards children.
The concept of child-friendly government was coined by Mekonen
(2010), who created an index for measuring government performance
in realizing children's rights and wellbeing in Africa. He defined a child-
friendly government as “one which is making the maximum effort to
meet its obligations to respect, protect and fulfill child rights and ensure
child well-being” (Mekonen, 2010, p. 207). Mekonen's approach was
based on the abovementioned three Ps. However, since there is very
little international comparative data on participation, especially in
African countries, he only used indicators from the Provision and Pro-
tection domains. Based on such indicators, Mekonen (2010) ranked
African countries from the most (Mauritius) to the least child-friendly
(Guinea- Bissau).
Mekonen's research has not been replicated in other countries. This
is perhaps due to the lack of data on the Participation domain and the
fact that the Protection domain indicators are based mostly on ratifi-
cation of international and regional legal documents, national laws and
policies, justice system structure, the existence of child protection en-
tities, national action plans and organizations dedicated to the im-
plementation of children's rights (Mekonen, 2010). These groups of
protection indicators served their purpose in detecting variance be-
tween African countries.
However, since challenges faced by children in advanced, high-in-
come countries nowadays stretch far beyond ensuring their mere sur-
vival (Ben-Arieh, 2008), measuring and monitoring children's terms of
life in these countries using protection indicators will probably not
yield conclusive results. Hence, there is a need to better fit the concept
of child friendliness to the developed world context. This can be done
using the broader notion of nurturance and care (Ruck, Abramovitch, &
Keating, 1998) that pertains to different aspects of child welfare in
society (Lahat, Helwig, Yang, Tan, & Liu, 2009; Ruck and Horn, 2008),
protecting children and shaping their development (Cherney & Shing,
2008), thus encompassing both protection and provision indicators
(Kosher et al., 2016). This typology, in turn, allows the researcher to
better fit specific indicators to the context of high-income countries.
The other concept of this typology, Self-determination, can replace the
participation domain as the other key concept. Both concepts derive
from the same articles of the CRC (Ben-Arieh, 2005; Kosher & Ben-
Arieh, 2017; Ruck, Keating, Saewyc, Earls, & Ben-Arieh, 2016) and
pertain to children's right to exercise participation in various arenas of
their lives (Kosher et al., 2016) and to some extent even exercise control
over some life aspects (Cherney & Shing, 2008; Rogers & Wrightsman,
1978). However, since this typology predates the UNCRC, it is im-
portant to add that self-determination in the spirit of the UNCRC should
mean that children's voice is to be respected and heard, meaning chil-
dren's view are to be taken into account in decisions (Kosher & Ben-
Arieh, 2017), but not necessarily acted upon. Moreover, self-determi-
nation could stretch beyond personal participation and into domains of
participation in policy and decision making in the public sphere (Ben-
Arieh, 2005; Ben-Arieh & Tarshish, 2016; Tisdall, 2008).
An important question arising from the adaptation of this typology
to real-world measurement is what indicators should be used to mea-
sure children self-determination and nurturance and care. While the
research field is still new and lacking important groups of indicators,
and the fact that any choice of indicators is to some extent subjective, it
is important to discuss possible indicators and a rationale for mea-
surement.
1.4. Indicators aimed at measuring child-friendly governance in OECD
countries
The Child Friendliness Index will be composed of two groups of
indicators: nurturance and care and self-determination. This view is
supported by previous research and the UNCRC, which address the two
as equally important to child development (Hart, 1991; Peterson-Badali
& Ruck, 2008; United Nations, 1989). Micklewright and Stewart
(1999), for instance, advocate measuring children's “good life” as the
sum of their material wellbeing, health and survival, education and
personal development, and participation. National reports also support
this perspective, such as The National Children's Agenda in Canada,
which includes four goals that correspond with both groups of

outcomes in key domains addressed in the UNCRC: health (Article 24),
material conditions (27), education (28) and protection (19); and
budgetary commitment dedicated to supplying children's needs (4, 26).
This two-fold thesis pertains to both government input and actual
policy outcomes, as successfully applied by Mekonen (2010)

1.4.2. The self-determination sub-index
The self-determination sub-index comprises indicators on child
participation in the personal domain and participation in the public
domain. Children's voice is a key aspect in the UNCRC (Article 12). Note
that the right to self-determination is not limited to the freedom to
express one's views, but includes access to information, a degree of
independence, liberty and privacy (Kosher et al., 2014), and extends to
child participation in policy and decision making (Ben-Arieh, 2005).
1.4.2.1. Child participation in the personal domain. The notion that
children should have a voice and take part in decisions regarding
their life is embedded in the aforementioned emphasis on being. Shier's
(2001) model for enhancing children participation in decision-making
claims the first step for participation is for the child to be listened to by
an attentive adult. In addition, freedom of choice has been regarded as
an important contributor to satisfaction and well-being (Helliwell,
Layard, & Sachs, 2015). Of particular relevance to the present study,
Lee and Yoo (2015) found that freedom to choose was a dominant
factor in country variance in children's subjective wellbeing.

children have a policy role as young citizens (Ben Arieh, 2005;

The review presented illustrated the need for a ‘child friendliness
index’ for developed countries, which draws on the principals of the
UNCRC and the “new” sociology of childhood. In that sense, this
measure should include indicators on both types of rights mentioned in
the UNCRC and view children as an active social structure, which is
entitled to participate in various arenas.
2. Research goal and questions
The study reported here aims to contribute to research on child-
friendly government and to suggest a new child-oriented approach to
comparing how countries care for their young citizens. An index for
measuring child-friendly government was developed, adapting previous
work by Mekonen (2010) to high-income countries, operationalized
here as OECD members. This adaptation required a reevaluation of
indicators, looking beyond indicators of protection and provision to
those of wellbeing and participation (Aber, 1997; Ben-Arieh et al.,
2001). Accordingly, the research questions are as follows:
1. How well do different groups of indicators in the sub-indices of
nurturance and care and self-determination perform in explaining
country variations in child friendliness?
2. Are indicators in the two different sub-indices correlated?
3. What is the child friendliness level of each country?
3. Method
3.1. Data
The data used in this research was collected from secondary sources
for the 35 OECD member countries. OECD countries were selected as
representative of developed economies for several reasons. First, OECD
datasets on family and child wellbeing are amongst the most compre-
hensive and updated comparative datasets available. Second, utilizing
the OECD dataset creates standardization and helps to deal with data
comparison issues. Finally, and most importantly, OECD countries all
have advanced economies, allowing a bigger and similar degree of in-
vestment in children, and thus are able to tend to children's self-de-
termination and nurturance and care beyond their protection and sur-
vival.
Indicators in the nurturance and care sub-index were gleaned
mostly from the OECD How's life? Study (2015) and the OECD family
database (OECD, 2019). This comprehensive dataset includes a broad
range of indicators: the structure of families (SF); the labor market
position of families (LMF); public policies for families and children
(PF); and child outcomes (CO). Two more complementary data sources
were employed: UNICEF data on child deprivation were used to de-
termine child poverty rates and Know Violence in Childhood data on
corporal punishment assessed child maltreatment rates (Adamson,
2012; Akobirshoev & Nandakumar, 2017).
Data on self-determination were gleaned from two different sources.
The first was the 38-country International Civic and Citizenship
Education Study (ICCS; Schulz et al., 2009). The second was the IS-
CIWeb Children's Worlds 2nd wave data on subjective wellbeing
(ISCIWeb, 2019).

The selected indicators chosen were checked for internal reliability
and grouped into clusters, as seen in Table 1. The analysis method was
similar to Bradshaw, Hoelscher and Richardson's Index of Child Well-

Following Mekonen's (2010) decision not to use any weighting;
every indicator in this study had the same weight in the analysis. This
decision is supported by Nardo et al. (2005), who argue that when the
field of study is new, and there is limited knowledge of indicators, equal
weighting is considered a good alternative. However, since this re-
search addresses child friendliness, which is a positive notion, the Z-
scores of negative indicators (such as poverty or bullying rates) were
multiplied by (−1).
4. Findings
4.1. Nurturance and care
The final Nurturance and Care Index is a scale composed of the
mean score of 17 indicators (α = 0.796) that reflect two notions of
child nurturance and care: child outcomes, which is comprised of 13
indicators in health, material conditions, protection and school (coun-
tries with more than three missing indicators were dropped from the
analysis); and budgetary commitment, which is comprised of four
indicators on public spending on basic child programs (countries with
more than one missing indicator were dropped from the analysis). The
full scores and ranks for each of the factors by country are shown in
Table 2.
Table 2 shows the 31 countries (out of 35) that scored in both
groups of indicators. The data on the table is color-coded: top third
results (green), middle third (yellow), and bottom third (red). The
Nordic countries, Denmark, Sweden, Finland and Norway, are at the
top of the league table. The first three scored in the top third at both
groups of indicators, the same as Luxemburg. All countries in the top
third are European, except for Korea, that came first in child outcomes,
but close to the bottom third in budgetary commitment. In the middle
third, Germany and Slovenia follow the same pattern as Korea, while
Japan and The Netherlands both score high on child outcomes and very
low on budgetary commitment. New Zealand is the only country to
score in the bottom third of child outcomes, but top on budgetary
commitment. In the bottom third, the US and Italy score almost alike,
with third middle scores on child outcomes and bottom third scores on
budgetary commitment, same as Austria. Poland, Slovak Republic,
Spain, Chile, and Turkey are last with third bottom scores in the two
groups of indicators. Turkey performs considerably worse than the
other 30 countries, with a negative index score.

score exceptionally high in all indices. This finding is consistent with
results from other studies, ranking Nordic countries in the top tier of
child well-being, subjective well-being, material conditions and overall
satisfaction with life (Bradshaw, Keung, Rees, & Goswami, 2011;
Bradshaw & Richardson, 2009; EUROSTAT, 2015). Nordic countries are
considered exemplary cases of social-democratic welfare regimes,
viewing welfare as a social right, promoting equality and solidarity
between groups and providing generous social subsidies (Esping-
Andersen, 1990; Rostgaard, 2014). This model emphasizes social ser-
vices for families and children as a key component (Sipilä, 1997), but
more importantly, Nordic countries promote child participation as a
manifestation of the right of citizenship in legislation, national projects
and initiatives (Council of Europe, 2011; Hennum, 2014) and regard it
as an inherent part of child early education (Bae, 2009).
On the other hand, some countries were ranked differently when
comparing results with previous research. For example, Bradshaw et al.
(2013) ranked Germany and Spain in the top two-thirds of child well-
being and another comparative well-being study ranked the UK in the
bottom third and Germany again in the top third (Bradshaw &
Richardson, 2009). Some discrepancies in country rankings were also
evident in the OECD doing better for children comparative study
(2009). It should be noted that the fluctuations of country rankings in
different indices could be artifacts of the indicators chosen for com-
parison, missing values and differences in measurement (see discussion
in Rees & Main, 2015).
Another Important finding resulted from the nurturance and care
and self-determination typology. It appears that when comparing how
specific countries rank in each sub-index, some countries show similar
or close ranking, which can be high (eg., the Nordic countries), med-
iocre (eg., Estonia, Slovenia) or low (eg., Spain) (see Fig. 1). However,
results from other countries show that countries can be ranked very
differently in each sub-index. Turkey, for example, is ranked high in
self-determination, but especially low in nurturance and care, while
Luxembourg and United Kingdom are a clear mirror picture, ranking
high on nurturance and care and low on self-determination. Naturally,
an exceptionally high or low score in one sub-index effects the total
country ranking (see Table 4). This finding, alongside the lack of cor-
relation across the two sub-indices and their groups of indicators, can
contribute to the understanding that when comparing country friend-
liness, it is important to look beyond a total index to performance in
either sub-index. This could be of help to countries that seek to improve
their commitment to the full spectrum of children's rights.
Measuring country performance in the two sub-indices also high-
lighted the fact that while the nurturance and care sub-index is posi-
tively associated with GDP per capita, the self-determination sub-index
is not significantly associated with either GDP per capita or budgetary
commitment to invest in children. This could prove to be a significant
since it is possible that the universally acceptable connection between
financial investment and nurturing and caring for children in different
arenas (Huebner et al., 2016; Pereznieto, Golemac Powell, & Avdagic,
2011) may not apply to children's self-determination. It could be that
investing in children's self-determination means to tend more to public
views, perceptions, and norms since research suggests that adults are
more inclined to support rights of nurturance and care over rights of
self-determination (Peterson-Badali & Ruck, 2008). In this respect,
Lansdown (2009) claims that while western societies place adult civil
and political liberties on a pedestal, children are excluded because of
their socioeconomic dependency in adults. This, in turn, hinders chil-
dren's ability to develop autonomy and serves as a justification to limit
child participation in decision making.
This study was the first to employ a new Child Friendliness Index in
OECD countries. While the Nordic countries score high in both sub-
indices, other countries struggle to achieve high rankings in both nur-
turance and care and self-determination. This finding highlights the
need to address both aspects as integral to children's lives. Despite
limitations such as indicators chosen and missing data, findings from
this study show that country ranking in child self-determination can be
entirely different from country ranking in nurturance and care. This, in
turn, sheds light on the importance of measuring self-determination
indicators when addressing countries commitment to children's rights.
There are simply not enough indicators measuring child self-determi-
nation and not enough international commitment to perform such
measurements. This study represents a first step in narrowing that gap
in measurement, as well as addressing the importance of that aspect for
child well-being.
5.1. Limitations
This study has several limitations that could affect the general-
izability of its findings. First, this index was created using a secondary
analysis of data from various sources. When utilizing different sources
of data collected by diverse entities, especially in a comparative per-
spective, data is only available for specific years and ages and can vary
from country to country. Also, since sampling is unique in each study or
database, some data is available only for a specific region (e.g., data
collected only for Istanbul represents Turkey) or ethnic group (e.g.,
Flemish-only sampling in Belgium). Also, the fit of the chosen

kind, and thus can serve as a starting point for other studies to follow.
5.2. Directions for future research and practice
This exploratory field will benefit from more research on child
friendliness. First, there is an immediate need to define better and re-
fine the concept of child-friendliness. Many questions unanswered are
still unanswered in this new field: What is a child-friendly country?
How do we measure child friendliness? Using which indicators? Is
child-friendliness about outcomes or inputs? Do nurturance and care
and self-determination capture the concept of child friendliness
worldwide, and in OECD countries in particular? What is the appro-
priate weighting of the two indices in a composite index?
Empirically, this field will benefit from studies with larger country
samples in order to see how the Child Friendliness Index applies in
different settings and cultures. It is also important to study the effects of
child friendliness in practice, especially how well child friendliness
correlates with children's subjective well-being in order to see whether
a child-friendly country indeed makes for a happier childhood. Also,
since there were many outliers in this study, it is important to study
cases of countries with low GDP and budgetary commitment that rank
high on the index, to stimulate comparative learning of “what works”.
The low number of countries with a self-determination score highlights
the need to create more international-level indicators that address this
aspect, in order to complement the relatively stable measurements of
nurturance and care. If self-determination nowadays is seen as an in-
herent part of children's rights, then comparative data on this domain in
children's lives can help monitor children's terms of life. This also calls
to further explore the lack of correlation found in this study, between
self-determination, GDP per capita and budgetary commitment, to de-
termine what impacts child self-determination. Another important as-
pect that should be employed in future research is the inclusion of
children as more than passive participants. Children should be seen as
partners in research, from the design of the study through data col-
lection to utilizing the data (Ben-Arieh, 2005).
Practically, the concept of child friendliness can prove beneficial in
many aspects of children's lives. First, after an index of child friendli-
ness with a more robust dataset is proven reliable and valid, it could be
used as a tool to measure implementation of and improvements in
children's rights in practice, since the full spectrum of rights guaranteed
in the UNCRC is the framework for the index. Second, it can serve
countries in an effort to better focus policy efforts on domains of chil-
dren in which they are comparatively lacking. Finally, if a child-friendly
country is indeed better for children, the Child Friendliness Index could
be used to create a mandatory threshold of friendliness, one that will
actively improve the lives of children worldwide.
