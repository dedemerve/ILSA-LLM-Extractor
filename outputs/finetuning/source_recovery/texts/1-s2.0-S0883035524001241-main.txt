This study joins an intense research debate regarding whether competition is beneficial for student outcomes, focusing on the relationships between student-level and school-level competitiveness and student academic achievement. I also examine the impact of country-specific
competitiveness context on the relationships between competitiveness and student achievement.
This study’s assumptions and hypotheses were guided by theoretical tenets of Swab & Johnson’s
multilevel model of competition and competitiveness and the person-culture fit model. Using
Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 data and World Values Survey (WVS)
data from 57 countries, I analyzed three-level hierarchical linear models (HLM) with cross-level
interactions to test these hypotheses. The results showed that student competitiveness was
positively related to student achievement in mathematics within schools. Between schools, a
competitive climate was positively correlated to student mathematics achievement. Cross-level
interactions revealed country context positively moderated the relationship between student
competitiveness and achievement. These findings emphasize the importance of considering
broader cultural competitiveness contexts when examining the association between student
competitiveness and achievement.

1. Introduction
Competition is a ubiquitous and pervasive aspect of human life (Elliot, 2020; Hayward & Kemmelmeier, 2007) and has been
extensively examined in educational research (Abril & Trinidad, 2022). Several studies have shown that student achievement is linked
to a country’s competitiveness and, in turn, its economic performance (Baumann & Winzar, 2016; Baumann et al., 2019; Hanushek &
Woesmann, 2012). Others have shown competition can be a motivating factor in education and can have significant consequences for
student behavior and achievement (Baumann & Hamin, 2011; Baumann & Harvey, 2018). Studies have demonstrated that student
achievement can be impacted by the degree of student competitiveness and the level of competitiveness in a school (Korn & Elliot,
2016; Nebel et al., 2016). Nonetheless, the effects of competition on student learning remain a hotly debated topic in the educational
and social psychology literature (Elliot, 2020).
Several studies have suggested that competition has positive effects that increase motivation and learning, thereby promoting
academic achievement (Baumman & Harvey, 2021; Carter & Yeo, 2016; Swab & Johnson, 2019; Worrell et al., 2016). In particular,
some studies claim that moderate competition may be beneficial (Nelson & Dawson, 2017), especially in certain cultures, such as in

Asia (King et al., 2012; Sommet et al., 2019). However, other scholars have claimed that competition has negative effects that can lead
to increased stress, depression, and maladaptive behavior like school avoidance, which lead to further detrimental consequences
(Baumann & Harvey, 2021; Canning et al., 2020; Harris & Houston, 2010). Too much competition and competitiveness can encourage
cheating or unethical behavior (Kohn, 1992; Senko & Dawson, 2017; Van Yperen & Orehek, 2013), which can lead to low engagement,

Previous studies on the effects of competition are largely based on small case studies or studies carried out in highly controlled
laboratory settings where participants were asked to complete some assigned tasks (Bratti et al., 2011; Kistruck et al., 2016; Murayama
& Elliot, 2012). These experimental and field research studies have high internal validity but not always high external validity, so
many have limited generalizability (Lam et al., 2004). Research on competition and competitiveness in natural environments is not
commonly found (Swab & Johnson, 2019). This is a significant gap; an individual’s environment can shape their attitudes and
strengthen or weaken their intentions (Bratti et al., 2011). Moreover, recent research has shown that competition is a culture-bound
construct (Gonzalez-Cabrera, 2018; Richard et al., 2002); different meanings and divergent perspectives on competition could result in
differences in how an individual’s competitiveness impacts various outcomes (King et al., 2012).
Emerging studies on person-culture fit, also known as person-environment fit, have emphasized the significance of congruence
between an individual and the larger societal context in achieving optimal outcomes (Fulmer et al., 2010; Jokela et al., 2015; Van
Vianen, 2018). This theory proposes that students achieve better academic outcomes when their own perceptions align with those of
others in the broader society in which they are situated (King et al., 2021). In light of these findings, I can expect the impact of
competitiveness to be influenced by the broader culture’s view of competitiveness.
Given the importance of environmental context to individuals’ competitive attitudes and benefits from competition, there are
surprisingly few studies using generalizable data examining the influence of environmental context such as community and countrylevel attitudes towards competition (Bratti et al., 2011; Elliot et al., 2018). To bridge this gap, this study conceptualizes the relationship
between competitiveness and student achievement as having a multilevel structure (Swab & Johnson, 2019) and analyzes the
interaction of competitiveness at both student and school levels with a country-level culture of competitiveness using data from the
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development’s (OECD) 2018 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA),
which collected comparable information on students who enrolled in schools in different countries. This approach has the advantage of
using generalizable data based on a standardized performance measurement that allows consideration of the influence of environmental variables.
Research Question 1. Do student competitiveness and school competitiveness climate influence academic achievement?
Research Question 2. Do both the relationships between student competitiveness and academic achievement and between school
competitiveness climate and academic achievement vary based on the culture of competitiveness?
2. Background
2.1. Competitiveness
The definition of competitiveness is “the enjoyment of interpersonal competition and the desire to win and to be better than others”
(Spence & Helmreich, 1983, p.41). Competition and competitiveness can be distinguished in that competition refers to a circumstance
where an individual or group of individuals strives to accomplish a zero-sum outcome, whereas competitiveness refers to a preference
for competition that varies between people (Swab & Johnson, 2019). Competitiveness is viewed as an individual’s position of success
relative to others, implying the ability and willingness to outperform others in performance situations (Murayama & Elliot, 2012;
OECD, 2019).
Competitiveness can be conceptualized into three aspects: trait competitiveness as a dispositional characteristic of the person
(Murayama & Elliot, 2012; Spence & Helmreich, 1983); perceived environmental competitiveness as a subjective perception or
characteristics of the performance situation (Elliot et al., 2018); and structural competition as an objective characteristic of an actual
setting (Elliot, 2020; Swab & Johnson, 2019). Individually or together, the three aspects have been examined by researchers analyzing
the impact of competitiveness on a variety of educational outcomes, including self-reported competence, learning strategies, and test
performance (Murayama & Elliott, 2012; Elliot et al., 2018; Swab & Johnson, 2019). In that sense, student (trait) competitiveness
refers to an attitude where students generally enjoy, value, and engage in competition, and may be an important foundation for
educational achievement (Weissman et al., 2022).
Competitiveness can also be a feature of the environment (Fletcher et al., 2008). School competitiveness can be conceptualized as a
characteristic of the perceived academic environment at the school level (perceived competitiveness) (Sommet et al., 2023). Perceived
competitiveness represents a subjective perception of the competitive nature of the environment at the school (Ames & Archer, 1988;
Karabenick, 1994). School competitiveness as a construct can be defined as the competitive disposition shared by a school, resulting in
a preference for competition (Swab & Johnson, 2019). In this study, I consider student competitiveness (individual perceptions) and
school competitiveness climate (perceptions shared by students in a school).
2.2. Competitiveness and achievement
The prior literature exploring the relationship between competition and performance has examined whether competitiveness inhibits or promotes achievement (Baumann & Harvey, 2018; Richard et al., 2002). Some studies have viewed competitiveness as a

negative force, leading to cheating, sabotage, and bullying (Butera et al., 2021). Others, however, have viewed competitiveness as a
positive force because it allows students to express their natural tendency to improve their performance (Hayward & Kemmelmeier,
2007; Lam et al., 2004). In accordance with social comparison theory, individuals engage in comparing themselves to others as a means
to validate their own competence (Suls & Wheeler, 2000). Competitiveness can direct attention to key tasks and result in improved
performance (Fletcher et al., 2008). Highly competitive individuals are likely to avoid doing poorly relative to others (Baranik et al.,
2010), especially in sports (Crowley, 2004; Jury et al., 2015) and in work situations (Houston et al., 2015; Sauers & Bass, 1990).
In education settings, a meta-analytic study found a positive and statistically significant association between trait competitiveness
and exam performance (Murayama & Elliot, 2012). Prior research also highlights an association between individual trait competitiveness and better performance goals, which are in turn associated with better affective and cognitive outcomes, which are correlated
with positive student achievement outcomes (Diseth, 2015; Senko & Dawson, 2017; Van Yperen et al., 2014). King et al. (2012)
showed that individuals tend to perceive competition as a positive trait that leads to personal and social improvement and that
competitiveness is positively related to both mastery and performance goals, suggesting that trait competitiveness, learning strategies,
and achievement goals are interrelated.
A few economic studies have attempted to systematically review and empirically measure the influence of school competition on
school and student performance in several countries (Agasisti, 2013; Belfield & Levin, 2002; Gibbons et al., 2008). Belfield and Levin
(2002)) reviewed more than 41 empirical articles demonstrating the beneficial effects of competition between schools on educational
outcomes in the United States. Agasisti (2013) examined the statistical association of competition between schools and improved
school performance in Italy, suggesting that increased competition may play a potential role in improving educational outcomes.
Gibbons et al. (2008) also found evidence of a positive causal relationship between competition and school performance, as well as
student achievement in the UK, indicating that schools with more competition have better academic outcomes than those with less.
Competitiveness can be a key factor in understanding what affects student performance. However, much of the prior research is
based on case studies from different countries and, therefore, different cultures. For instance, the findings of King et al. (2012) are valid
in the Asian context, but may or may not apply in other contexts. Previous empirical research studying the relationship between
competitiveness and performance suffers from an overreliance on laboratory settings (e.g., Baer et al., 2010; Mulvey & Ribbens, 1999).
Murayama and Elliot (2012) conducted an experimental study to test whether competition predicts performance on anagrams among
U.S. college students. Experimental tasks like dyadic games, puzzles, or resource-allocation measures might not be associated with
students’ day-to-day behaviors or with students’ authentic learning in class and school (Lam et al., 2004; Richard et al., 2002). Despite
these limitations being acknowledged since the initial stages of theorizing, there is little research that addresses the resulting gap (Baer
et al., 2010). Therefore, I investigate whether these associations generalize across cultures in a large-scale analysis founded on a
standardized performance measure, allowing for effective observation of education activities (Bratti et al., 2011).
Competition manifests in a variety of ways, including among individuals, within groups, or between groups (Worrell et al., 2016).
In addition, competition can take place in a complex process, depending on situational and contextual factors (Gonzalez-Cabrera,
2018). Given that competition takes place in a multilevel structure that differentiates individuals and groups (Swab & Johnson, 2019)
and is based on the goals and interactions of participants in social circumstances (Richard et al., 2002), it is surprising that most prior
literature on the association between competition and performance has paid less attention to structure and context (Fülöp, 2004). I
address this issue by examining competition’s structural and contextual mechanisms, including its multilevel structure and the
interaction between people and contexts, which can have a significant impact on the association between competition and performance. For example, consider the likely impact of country-level competitiveness culture on student and school competitiveness.
Indeed, scholars have long highlighted the importance of context; previous research has demonstrated the need to regard contextual
factors as moderating variables rather than merely controlling for them in quantitative studies (Sebastian & Allensworth, 2019).
2.3. Culture of competitiveness
There are distinct differences in cultural views of competition (Wu & Talhelm, 2023). North Americans generally view competition
as essential to individual growth and societal advancement (Watkins, 2006). East Asians show less enjoyment of competition compared
to North Americans (Houston et al., 2005). This implies different countries have different perceptions and may help explain the
conclusions of a 2012 report that international best practices in education have not been found (The Economist Intelligence Unit,
2012). This report refers to how country-specific differences in the learning process act as an elusive "black box," making it difficult to
consistently predict or quantify how inputs are transformed into outputs (Thiemann, 2017a; 2017b). One possible explanation for
these findings is the different teaching and learning cultures in different countries (Hofstede, 1986). Prior studies have explored the
correlation between personality traits and student academic performance through the complex and multidimensional lens of culture
(Baumann & Harvey, 2018; Zhou, 2015).
Students who come from different countries are influenced by different social and cultural backgrounds and thus have distinct
characteristics (Hanushek, 2011; Qin, 2021). Specifically, students’ learning behaviors vary widely based on cultural distinctions in
cognitive patterns and thought processes. Culture may influence many other aspects of education, including student self-control,
student interactions, and student-teacher relationships (Thiemann, 2017a). For example, students from competitive and
performance-oriented cultures tend to compare themselves with higher-achieving peers and motivate themselves to outperform their
peers in pursuit of success. Moreover, policies that group students by ability can benefit competitive cultures but may adversely affect
non-competitive cultures (Thiemann, 2017b). Within competitive cultures, students may be motivated through social comparison by
the opportunity to be elevated to a higher-track group, which leads to improved performance. Students from competitive cultures
actively seek out more competitive environments, while students from non-competitive cultures avoid competitive environments

In this study, the culture of competitiveness is defined as a measure of country-level competitive preference based on a question
from the World Values Survey (WVS) (Inglehart et al., 2022). This measure has been utilized to represent cultural differences in the
domain of competitiveness in cross-country value surveys (Hofstede et al., 2010; House et al., 2004). Cultural differences in
competitiveness have been studied in connection with the teaching and learning behavior of students, with research indicating
different perceptions between students from competitive and non-competitive cultures with regard to the best students and average
students and thoughts of failures (Hofstede, 1986). The theoretical modeling is based on these classifications of aspects in competitive
and non-competitive cultures (Thiemann, 2017a). In addition, cross-level interaction takes place when higher-level variables influence
the nature and intensity of the association between lower-level characteristics and their outcomes (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002).
Considering the aspects of the methodology, the national context of competitiveness culture may influence students’ achievement
indirectly through student competitiveness and school competitiveness climates (Ruiter & Van Tubergen, 2009). This study centers on
the country-specific cultures of competitiveness that may influence the relationship between competitiveness and student
achievement.
3. Guiding framework and hypotheses
The guiding framework for this study combines insights from a multilevel model of competition and competitiveness (Swab &
Johnson, 2019) and the person-culture fit perspective (King et al., 2021). Based on the framework, this study hypothesizes that student
competitiveness and school competitiveness climate positively influence student achievement and that national competitiveness
culture positively moderates both relationships—student-level competitiveness and student achievement, and school-level competitiveness and student achievement—based on a high degree of congruence between an individual’s personal perceptions and the values
upheld by his or her culture (Du et al., 2019). Prior literature proposed the multilevel framework of competition and competitiveness
on the basis of individual and team properties and the situational context (Swab & Johnson, 2019). Competition commonly occurs not
only between individuals but also between groups, including teams, organizations, institutions, states, or nations, which generates
complexity. Moreover, the outcomes of competitive action result from the interaction between the situation, creating further
complexity. To illustrate these complex processes clearly, Swab and Johnson (2019) conceptualized this model (Fig. 1):
According to the theory of person-culture fit, the more closely an individual’s traits align with those of the broader environment in
which the individual is embedded, the more optimal results will occur (Fulmer et al., 2010). Person-culture fit, or value congruence, is
demonstrated by the accordance between an individual’s personal values and the cultural values prevalent in their society (i.e., the
values of most people in a culture) (Sortheix & Lonnqvist, 2015). Research has focused on congruence in various psychological
constructs, including emotions (match between an individual’s emotional profile and the dominant emotional experiences of others in
society; for reviews, see De Leersnyder et al., 2014, 2015), personality (match between an individual’s personality traits and the
prevailing personality traits in one’s community; for a review, see Bleidorn et al., 2016), and values (match between an individual’s
social values and the dominant social values of one’s society; for a review, see Du et al., 2019). While different types of fit have been
studied, all have supported the core tenet: better fit leads to better outcomes (King et al., 2021). Using cities, regions within a country,
and nations as the units of analysis, person-culture fit has been studied in a variety of contexts (King et al., 2021). In cross-cultural
research, countries or nations are often used as proxies for culture (Hofstede, 1980; Oyserman, 2017; Schwartz, 1997). This study

4. Methods
4.1. Data and sample
This study used the most recent cross-national data from the OECD’s PISA 2018, which consisted of data from approximately
710,000 15-year-old students from the 79 participating countries and economies (OECD, 2019), allowing for a large-scale analysis to
explore the research questions. Along with assessing students’ academic proficiency, PISA collects student background information as
well as school characteristics information and uses a two-stage stratified sampling to ensure an adequate representation of student
populations in the sample (OECD, 2019; Yamamoto et al., 2019). For the first stage of school sampling, PISA used explicit and implicit
stratification variables to sort schools that enrolled 15-year-old students (for reviews, see OECD, 2019). Next, for the second stage of
student sampling, a sample of 42 students (35 for paper-based countries) were randomly selected with equal probability (OECD, 2019).
This study used student surveys, which consisted of 436,343 student responses from 16,312 schools in 57 of the 79 PISA countries. This
is because mathematics achievement (i.e., PV1MATH-PV10MATH: dependent variable) from PISA 2018, and competitiveness climate
survey items (i.e., ST205: independent variable) from PISA 2018, and cultural data (i.e., culture of competitiveness: independent
variable) from the World Values Survey (WVS) are not available for all countries.b
4.2. Measures
4.2.1. Dependent variable
PISA 2018 student mathematics achievement scores were utilized as the dependent variable. PISA 2018 assessed the core school
subjects of reading, mathematics, and science (OECD, 2019); this study focused on mathematics because, in contrast to other subjects,
mathematics answers are either right or wrong and thus may better predict actual rank, which can reflect more explicitly in attitudes
towards competition (Niederle & Vesterlund, 2010). PISA presents student performance data using ten plausible values, considered
random draws of individual performance estimates to compute precise population estimates (Rutkowski et al., 2010). For this analysis,
the ten plausible values were appropriately combined (Muthén & Muthén, 1988–2017; Rutkowski et al., 2010; see for e.g., Sebastian &
Huang, 2016). Across OECD countries, the plausible values are shown with a mean of 500 and a standard deviation of 100, with 25 to
30 PISA points (1/4 or 1/3 of a standard deviation) approximately equivalent to one year of student learning (Woesmann, 2016).
4.2.2. Student competitiveness
The competitiveness variable at the student level measured trait competitiveness by referring to Spence and Helmreich’s (1983)
five-item competitiveness subscale (e.g., “I enjoy working in situations involving competition with others”). In this study, student
competitiveness measures consisted of three indicators (e.g., “I enjoy working in situations involving competition with others”) using a
four-point Likert scale, which was extracted from PISA 2018 Student Questionnaire Data (see Appendix A).
4.2.3. Competitiveness climate
The competitiveness variable at the school level was extracted from the PISA 2018 Student Questionnaire Data, which includes
variables of perceived environmental competitiveness at the school. Perceived environmental competitiveness refers to an individual’s
cognitive perception of competitive nature, meaning that people vary in the extent to which they perceive situations and the individuals involved in them as competitive (Elliot et al., 2018; Murayama & Elliot, 2012). This type of competitiveness is often evaluated with Murayama and Elliot’s (2012) measurement tool (e.g., “In this class, it seems that students are competing with each other”).
In this study, four indicators were used to measure the perceived environmental competitiveness (e.g., “Students seem to value

4.2.4. Culture of competitiveness
The competitiveness variable at the country level was constructed in three stages (see for e.g., Thiemann, 2017b). The first stage
was to extract a measure of country-level competition preferences from the WVS (Inglehart et al., 2022). The WVS is a comprehensive
global comparative social survey project dedicated to examining the social, political, economic, religious, and cultural values of people
worldwide every 5 years. Beginning in 1981, the survey involved conducting national surveys in nearly 100 countries utilizing a
common questionnaire. To obtain representative national samples, random sampling is used (Inglehart et al., 2022). From the WVS,
responses to the given statement were extracted: “Competition is good. It stimulates people to work hard and develop new ideas”
versus “Competition is harmful. It brings out the worst in people.” Participants answered their level of agreement with the statement on
a scale from 1 to 10, where 1 indicates “competition is good” and 10 indicates “competition is harmful.” Between 1989 and 2018, 365,
635 people participated, and the data from all waves were aggregated at the country level, and a simple aggregation was computed for
each country. After that, I calculated a culture value index per wave and ran a regression panel on the culture value index of country
and time variable dummies to confirm the time persistence of the cultural values (see for e.g., Thiemann, 2017b). Finally, I created the
culture of competitiveness index by normalizing the data to range from 1 to 10 and reversed coding it in order that 10 represented the
most competitive country, while 1 indicated the least competitive one. According to the resulting index, in general, countries in Eastern
Europe, the Balkans, Oceania, the United States, and China are considered competitive. Other Asian countries are moderately
competitive. Non-competitive countries are those in Western Europe and South America. The contents of the culture of competitiveness survey and the full ranking of countries are provided in Appendix C and D.
4.2.5. Control variables
In this study, I included a series of control variables at the student, school, and country levels that are found in many recent
publications using PISA data (see for e.g., Flannery et al., 2023; Hanushek et al., 2013; Sebastian & Huang, 2016; Thiemann, 2017b). In
addition, I added some contextual variables that might be correlated with the competitiveness variables. Ten student background
variables were used for control purposes: gender, immigration status, student’s home language (to see whether the student spoke the
same language as the test language), school year/grade, grade repetition, the PISA index of economic, socioeconomic status (SES), the
squared term of ESCS, student attitudes toward learning activities, student perceptions of teacher-directed instruction, and teacher
support (OECD, 2013; Wang & Degol, 2016).
Thirteen school background variables were used for control purposes: the school average ESCS, student-staff ratio, school size, the
squared term of school size, percentage of students with special educational needs (SEN), principal perceptions of staff shortages,
principal perceptions of the extent to which the quality of teaching materials hinders instruction, school location, principal perceptions
of student behavior, principal perceptions of teacher behavior, the proportion of teaching staff who attended professional development, principal perceptions of parental involvement in local school governance, and the share of government funding (Flannery et al.,
2023; OECD, 2013). Country-level control variables included log gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and the GINI index for
income inequality, obtained from the World Bank database (see http://data.worldbank.org/). Table 1 provides detailed information
on the variables used in this study.

4.3. Analytic approach
The analysis procedures involved two major stages. The first stage examined the construct validity of the measures by conducting a
multilevel confirmatory factor analysis (MCFA). After performing the MCFA and creating survey-based measures of student
competitiveness and competitiveness climate, to answer the research questions, I used three-level hierarchical linear models (HLM).
This approach is appropriate for this study because of the nested data structure; students are clustered within schools, which are
clustered within countries, so hierarchical linear modeling can provide a more accurate estimate of standard errors when using nested
or structured data (Raudenbush & Byrk, 2002).
Data preparation was performed with SAS 9.4 statistical software (Statistical Analysis Software Institute, 2013). The multilevel
(two-level) Maximum Likelihood (ML) data imputation procedure was conducted using Mplus 8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017) to
account for missing data. PISA 2018 achievement data uses ten plausible values to account for the uncertainty in estimating student
proficiency. Using student mathematics proficiency data, the imputation procedure in Mplus 8.10 was configured to generate ten
different values for each missing data point to account for the uncertainty associated with estimating a missing value founded on
observed information. The data from the multiple imputations (MI) procedure was combined with the ten plausible mathematics
proficiency values and the culture of competitiveness value to get ten different datasets for analysis (see for e.g., Sebastian & Huang,
2016). I utilized both student-level and school-level weights presented by PISA in running the multilevel regression, assigning weights
to each student and each school to consider unequal probabilities of selection and nonresponse.

6. Discussion and implications
Competition and competitiveness have always been controversial but important topics in teaching, learning, and educational
research. Surprisingly, little research has investigated the relationship between competitiveness and educational outcomes in a largescale analysis or across different countries. This study aimed to address these gaps in previous studies by examining how student-level
and school-level competitiveness might be related to student mathematics achievement, reflecting the multilevel model of competition
and competitiveness (Swab & Johnson, 2019). In addition, I examined how social contextual variation, specifically cultural differences
at the country level, moderates the relationship between competitiveness and student achievement, considering the person-culture fit
perspective. Using advanced empirical analysis, this study supports previous findings highlighting the positive role of student- and
school-level competitiveness on student learning outcomes. Furthermore, in societies where people generally value competition,
student competitiveness was associated with better mathematics achievement outcomes.

Regarding the question of whether competitiveness is beneficial or detrimental to achievement outcomes, this study suggests that
student competitiveness positively influences student mathematics achievement. This finding is consistent with prior literature presenting that competition is an important predictor of social and learning outcomes (Baumman & Harvey, 2021; Carter & Yeo, 2016;
King et al., 2012; Sommet et al., 2023; Swab & Johnson, 2019; Worrell et al., 2016) and is empirically positive for educational
improvement (Bratti et al., 2011; Diseth, 2015; Murayama & Elliot, 2012; Senko & Dawson, 2017; Van Yperen et al., 2014). Moreover,
beyond examining the effects of competitiveness, which have been studied primarily in the laboratory or in small case studies (Baer
et al., 2010; Kohn, 1992; Mulvey & Ribbens, 1999; Murayama & Elliot, 2012), this study provides additional evidence of the significant
positive relationships between student competitiveness and student achievement when examined in a large-scale dataset.
Second, this study indicates the importance of school competitiveness climate for student performance. Previous research in the
area of competition has predominantly centered on the main effects on competitiveness at the individual level. Lewin (1936)
emphasized that psychologists face the challenge of integrating findings from different levels of analysis (psychological, sociological,
and cultural). This study seeks to address this challenge through various levels of analysis, ranging from the student to the school
levels, taking into account the multilevel structure of competition and competitiveness (Swab & Johnson, 2019). Although some
teachers and principals are wary of encouraging competition, in this study, the school competitiveness climate was aggregated from
students’ perceptions of environmental competitiveness was related to higher test scores in mathematics. In contrast to Marks’s (2009)
small case study of Australian students, which found a highly competitive school environment had a detrimental effect on student
performance, this large-scale analysis indicates a competitive school climate is likely to predict improved student achievement
outcomes.
Third, with a focus on contextual effects, this study examined how the relationship between student- and school-level competitiveness and student performance may be impacted by cultural context through cross-level interaction analysis. To my knowledge, this
is the first study of its kind to examine the contextual effects on the relationship between competitiveness and achievement. These
findings show that competitiveness varies as a function of social context, advancing beyond prior literature that has primarily
examined competitiveness as an individual difference variable. Furthermore, this study demonstrated that competitiveness among
students living in nations with a more competitive culture had a stronger positive effect on student mathematics achievement. The
findings of this study corroborate existing evidence on the significance of person-culture fit in achieving optimal results (Fulmer et al.,
2010). While prior research has primarily concentrated on personality traits and socio-demographic features (Bleidorn et al., 2016)
and the domain of utility value (King et al., 2021), this study extends these results to the culture of competitiveness.
Consistent with the expectations of this study, more competitive students in countries with a higher culture of competitiveness
show increases in mathematics achievement. Students’ sense of fit with their environment may lead them to believe that their values or
traits are acceptable, thus strengthening the effect of those values on their performance. Students whose competitiveness matches the
competitiveness of his or her peers in the broader societal context might also experience lower levels of uncertainty and feel greater
validation. These ideas align with psychological theories that underscore the significance of interpersonal connections and belongingness (Baumeister & Leary, 1995).
This study holds multiple theoretical and practical implications. As mentioned, it is possible that previous research on competition
has underestimated the effects of competitiveness on student performance by considering a limited structure, situation, and environment. Education’s effectiveness and success depend largely on our understanding of the mechanics of teaching and learning and
about the role that environmental context plays in the application of this understanding (Kasik, 2015). The findings of this study
indicate teaching and learning practices should consider socio-cultural contexts and learning environments and offer approaches for
this consideration.
The research has practical implications for educators who want to improve student achievement; namely, that fostering healthy
competition can be helpful. Some scholars have advocated “co-opetition” or a “moderated” form of competition for enhancing student
learning outcomes (Nelson & Dawson, 2017; Swab & Johnson, 2019). In co-opetition, where individuals or teams are nested (e.g.,
students in schools), students can adapt their competitive dispositions to compete in pursuit of higher-order goals or to compete against
their own prior outcomes for a better collective goal (Abril & Trinidad, 2022). According to Berson and Oreg (2016), schools play a
critical role in shaping the values and traits of children. Relevant studies have shown that competitive values, goals, and behaviors are
effectively transmitted from school leaders (teachers, coaches) to followers (students, pupils) over time (Butera et al., 2021; Sommet
et al., 2017). Moreover, principals’ perspectives are manifested in the overall school climate, which in turn is represented in students
over time (Berson & Oreg, 2016). Given the evidence on the essential role of principals in cultivating school culture (Deal and Peterson,
1990), principals need to recognize their responsibility in creating student values and behaviors. Teachers, coaches, and principals
need to recognize their role and influence in incorporating "healthy" and "moderated" forms of competition into teaching and learning
in their schools.
Lastly, this study has policy implications. According to previous literature, Bratti et al. (2011) demonstrated that students’
competitive attitudes tend to be associated with higher gains in math test scores as class size increases, suggesting that incentives for
student competitiveness are stronger in larger groups, where powerful dynamics are more likely to occur. In addition, Thiemann
(2017b) revealed that a culture of competitiveness increases the effect of ability grouping, in which students are grouped based on their
ability or past performance, on students’ achievement. The study found evidence that, in competitive cultures, students were more
motivated when surrounded by peers of similar abilities than when surrounded by those of heterogeneous abilities. I believe that this
study highlights and contributes to the importance of considering student traits and the culture of a given country when designing or
implementing educational policies, including class size adjustment and ability grouping within the school system.

7. Limitations and future directions
This study has several strong points, such as its substantial sample size, cross-national data set, and the utilization of sophisticated
statistical techniques (multilevel hierarchical linear modeling with random slopes). Though, in spite of its strengths, there are four
main limitations that future research should consider and address. First, given the cross-sectional design of this study, the results need
to be understood as correlations rather than causal inferences. Longitudinal studies designed to explore a possible causal effect of
competitiveness as well as the contextual effects on nations are warranted. Second, the PISA 2018 dataset did not include information
on individual trait cooperativeness in students; it only included information on students’ perceived environmental cooperativeness,
which can be aggregated into a school cooperativeness climate variable. Acknowledging that competition and cooperation may be
interrelated and complementary depending on a variety of factors (Gonzalez-Cabrera, 2018), studying competitiveness and cooperativeness together at every level may provide a better understanding of the likely more comprehensive and sophisticated relationships
between competitiveness and cooperativeness in different cultural contexts. Third, it is worth noting that in this study, the independent
variable was competitiveness, and the outcome was math test scores. Prior literature explored the importance of various dimensions
such as school discipline, school uniforms, and work ethic that might play a role in explaining the relationship between competitiveness and performance (Baumann & Krskova, 2016; Baumann et al., 2016; Krskova & Baumann, 2017). In addition, Flannery et al.
(2023) presented that researchers and policymakers were increasingly placing a high degree of importance on a broader range of
outcomes. Therefore, future research is encouraged to consider various dimensions when measuring the effect of competitiveness on
educational outcomes, as well as a broader range of non-cognitive outcomes, including a sense of belonging, educational aspirations,
and well-being. Lastly, even though the indicators used to construct the latent variables were shown to be reliable in previous studies
(Murayama & Elliot, 2012; Sommet et al., 2023; Spence & Helmreich, 1983), I acknowledge that the indicators in this study are not
perfect measures to capture all of the complicated aspects of the constructs. Specifically, the measures of competitiveness in this study
were constituted of three manifest variables among the five items in the original subscales. Moreover, the measures of school
competitiveness climate relied on student surveys in this study. There is a need for future research to replicate and compare the results
of this study using other measures, such as surveys of principals and teachers, that provide meaningful, robust, and comprehensive
information about school competitiveness climate.
8. Conclusion
This study, by using the PISA 2018 survey data for a large-scale analysis based on a standardized measure of achievement, fills a gap
in educational research that was mostly based on small case studies or experiments in laboratory settings. The research adds empirical
evidence to the emerging debate on the socio-cultural processes involved in teaching and learning and contributes to the literature on
educational policy studies. Moreover, the findings reveal considerable complexity regarding feasible implications and underscore the
significance of considering level-specific factors (classroom/school/nation) in educational policy development. Given the complex
relationships demonstrated by the results, educators and policymakers should consider socio-cultural contexts in designing and
implementing educational policy and teaching and learning strategies. The research is a first attempt to investigate empirically the
effect of this aspect of cultural context on competitiveness. The evidence obtained from the compositional and contextual effects
procedures offers insight for future work considering cooperativeness together with more sophisticated methods such as Latent Profile
Analysis.
