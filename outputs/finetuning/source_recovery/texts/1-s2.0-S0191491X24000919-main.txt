Yimei Zhang , Maria Cutumisu *
Department of Educational and Counselling Psychology, Faculty of Education, McGill University, Montreal, QC, Canada

A B S T R A C T

Mathematics is a crucial yet challenging subject for all students. Therefore, it is important to understand the role
of academic resilience in mathematics, which enables students to overcome academic challenges. This study
applied two machine learning algorithms, Lasso Regression (LR) and Random Forest (RF), to predict the
mathematics literacy of resilient students from high-performing economies across cultures in PISA 2022. The
findings indicated both RF and LR performed better in Western cultures than in Eastern cultures. Furthermore, in
Eastern cultures, mathematics self-efficacy for 21st-century skills played an important role in predicting resilient
students’ mathematics literacy, followed by self-efficacy towards mathematics, and mathematics anxiety. In
Western cultures, self-efficacy towards mathematics was the predominant predictor, followed by mathematics
self-efficacy for 21st-century skills. Theoretically, this study identifies key factors in predicting resilient students’
mathematics literacy across cultures. Methodologically, it is the first to apply ML in exploring resilient students’
mathematics literacy. Practically, it guides educators interested in developing interventions to improve resilient
students’ mathematics literacy.

2.2. Machine Learning and Mathematics
Over the past several years, ML has been increasingly employed to
examine students’ mathematics performance. For instance, Gabriel et al.
(2018) built two boosted regression trees to investigate the impacts of
mathematics dispositions on Australian students’ mathematics literacy.
Their findings demonstrated that mathematics self-efficacy was the top
variable predicting their mathematics literacy. Another study conducted
by Wang et al. (2023), used RF to predict East Asian students’ mathematics performance. They investigated why students in East Asia achieved high mathematics performance. Their findings indicated that
students’ confidence in mathematics, socioeconomic status, and school
emphasis were the top three predictors of students’ high-performing
mathematics outcomes. A similar study was conducted by Bernardo
et al. (2022). This study employed RF to determine the most important
features that could be used to identify poor-performing mathematics
students in the Philippines. The results indicated that lower expectations
of completing a postsecondary vocational degree, and lower expected
occupations were important features in this context. In addition, Sokkhey and Okazaki (2019) conducted a study to identify the most efficient
ML models to predict students’ mathematics performance. They identified that RF outperformed the other ML models (i.e., Multilayer Perceptions, Decision Trees, Support Vector Machine, and Logistic
Regression). The results were echoed by a study conducted by Espinosa-Pinos et al. (2022), which used Decision Tress, Naïve Bayes, and RF to
identify students’ behavioral patterns in mathematics – passing or
failing mathematics. They found that RF had the best performance
among all applied ML techniques.
The related literature reveals that most researchers have considered
using ML techniques to investigate students’ mathematics performance,
whereas limited research has focused on examining resilient students’
mathematics performance. To fill this gap, this study employs ML to
predict resilient students’ mathematics performance across different
cultures.

2. Literature Review
2.1. Machine Learning and Academic Resilience
In the past few years, researchers have conducted several studies to
examine students’ academic resilience through ML techniques. For
instance, Zheng et al. (2024) applied Support Vector Machine to identify
key predictors of resilient students’ digital reading within the context of
high-performing Asian countries. Their findings indicated that the use of
metacognitive strategies in reading and reading enjoyment were salient
predictors. A study conducted by Wang et al. (2022) echoed these results
and found that metacognitive strategies and the joy of reading were the
top variables predicting Hong Kong (HK) students’ academic resilience.
A similar study was also conducted in HK, which used RF to predict HK
resilient students’ science performance (Wang et al., 2023). The results
demonstrated that confidence in science was the most important feature
of resilient HK students’ science success. Furthermore, Choi and Sung
(2024) incorporated cultural differences in predicting students’ academic resilience through RF. They found that self-efficacy and students’
expectation of higher education were important features for Korean
students’ academic resilience, whereas fixed mindset and meaning in
life were specific features for US students’ academic resilience. In
addition, Jin et al. (2022) applied Classification and Regression Trees
and Multilevel Logistic Regression to classify whether a disadvantaged
student was resilient or not resilient in B-S-J-G China data from PISA
2015. They found that the proportion of teachers in school with master’s
degree was the most important feature to identify whether the student
was resilient or not.
As summarized above, researchers have already noted the efficiency
of applying ML to examine students’ academic resilience. However,

3.1. Dataset
This study used the dataset from the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA), which is a triennial survey of 15-year-old
students around the world (OECD, 2023). It was first conducted in
2000 and is repeated every three years. Therefore, PISA 2022 is the
eighth and most recent iteration of the assessment (OECD, 2023).

Resilient students (n = 4654) from high-performing mathematics

United Kingdom (UK), Poland, Austria, Australia, The Czech Republic,
Slovenia, Finland, Latvia, Sweden, New Zealand (OECD, 2023). The

economies is that these high-performing regions in mathematics typically have well-established educational systems (OECD, 2023). By

Furthermore, in this study, resilient students are defined as “students

Therefore, the OECD guidelines were followed to select resilient
students (OECD, 2023). Firstly, Economic, Social, and Cultural Status
(ESCS)-disadvantaged students were screened. To be specific, students
in the bottom quarter of ESCS within their economy were selected.
Secondly, from the ESCS-disadvantaged group, students whose mathematics literacy was in the top quarter within their economy were further
selected. Moreover, to determine the top quarter of students in mathematics literacy, several steps were taken to ensure that the analysis accounts for the complexity of the survey design and the uncertainty in the
test scores. Firstly, an average of 10 plausible values (PV) of mathematics performance was computed to represent each sample’s overall
mathematics performance. Secondly, students were ranked based on the
average of 10 PVs, and samples from the top 25 % scores were selected.
Finally, students were chosen as participants according to two criteria:
they belonged to (1) the bottom quarter of ESCS and (2) the top quarter
of mathematics literacy.
Following this, the data related to Western culture (n = 3591) or
Eastern culture (n = 1063) were combined for further analysis (Triandis,
1995). Although the sample sizes for each group are relatively small,
they are still sufficient for applying ML algorithms. Firstly, previous
studies have shown that ML can effectively analyze small datasets, as ML
algorithms can identify complex patterns and interactions even with
limited data (e.g., Jin & Cutumisu, 2023). For instance, Jin and Cutumisu (2023) applied four ML algorithms, namely Logistic Regression,
Naïve Bayes, K-Nearest Neighbors, and Decision Tree to predict 93
pre-service teachers’ computational thinking performance. They found
that ML algorithms could effectively predict their computational
thinking performance, while successfully identifying the key factors
with high accuracy. Secondly, conducting targeted ML analysis which
predicted resilient students’ mathematics literacy enables a deeper
investigation into this specific subgroup, thus providing insights into the
areas of academic resilience and mathematics literacy. Thirdly, final

training and validation processes, which helped to minimize potential

3.4. Machine Learning Algorithms
This study employed two ML algorithms: LR and RF. Specifically, LR
is a type of linear regression that assumes a linear relationship between
features and response variables (Ranstam & Cook, 2018). It is beneficial
for feature reduction and interpretability. In contrast, RF is a type of
non-linear model that can model complex interactions between features
(Biau & Scornet, 2016). It is used for handling complex interactions and
non-linear relationships (Rigatti, 2017).
There are two main reasons why these two ML algorithms were
selected for this study. Firstly, these two ML algorithms have complementary strengths. Specifically, LR simplifies the model by reducing
features, making it more interpretable (Emmert-Streib & Dehmer, 2019;
Ranstam & Cook, 2018), whereas RF captures complex interactions and
non-linear patterns that LR might neglect (Biau & Scornet, 2016).
Applying both models can provide a more holistic understanding of our
data. Secondly, both models are robust and accurate: RF can provide
more accurate predictions in complex scenarios with many input variables (Biau & Scornet, 2016), whereas LR can create a simpler and more
interpretable model that may perform better when the underlying relationships are linear (Ranstam & Cook, 2018).
Additionally, it should be acknowledged that the primary goal of the
study was to build predictive ML models (i.e., RF and LR) to understand
the relationships between the features and the resilient students’
mathematics literacy (i.e., response variable), rather than to explore
causal pathways among predictors themselves. Specifically, RF was
designed to identify patterns and relationships directly from the data
without assuming any form of relationships between features (Biau &
Scornet, 2016). Therefore, it does not explicitly model mediation effects
and provide direct estimates of mediation effects in the way traditional
mediation analysis does. LR focuses on estimating the direct effects of
each predictor on resilient students’ outcomes, not the pathways
through which each predictor affects others (Ranstam & Cook, 2018).
Therefore, given the objectives of our study, the chosen two ML models
(i.e., RF and LR) were appropriate for identifying key predictors of
mathematics literacy among resilient students. By using both LR and RF,
the study leverages the strengths of each algorithm to achieve a balance
between model interpretability and predictive accuracy. Fig. 1 presents
the model structure for RF, while Fig. 2 shows the model structure for
LR.

3.8. Hyperparameter Tuning
Hyperparameter tuning is a process that aims to determine the values
of the hyperparameters that define the model’s optimal performance.
Grid search is one of the widely used methods for hyperparameter
tuning (Belete & Huchaiah, 2022). In this study, a grid of hyperparameter values was created following a series of iterations over
possible combinations of specific hyperparameters. Specifically, a grid
of hyperparameter values was carefully constructed based on prior
domain knowledge (e.g., Belete & Huchaiah, 2022; Pan & Cutumisu,
2024) and preliminary experiments. There were two steps in how the
range of hyperparameters was decided. First, a broader search using
wider intervals for each hyperparameter was conducted to identify the
promising areas within the hyperparameter space. Second, the search
was refined with narrower intervals, focusing on these regions to
fine-tune the hyperparameters. This two-step approach makes it possible
to efficiently locate the optimal space without exhaustively searching
the entire hyperparameter space. Moreover, the grid search enabled the
investigation of all potentially optimal hyperparameter combinations
that could lead to optimal model performance (Belete & Huchaiah,
2022). The details of the hyperparameters included in the grid search

3.7. Performance metrics
The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), coefficient of determination
(R2), and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) were used as the performance
metrics of this study.
RMSE measures the average magnitude of errors between the ML
model’s predicted values and actual values (Chai & Draxler, 2014;
Chicco et al., 2021). RMSE is sensitive to large errors because it squares
the error term, thus making it a good indicator of the ML model’s performance (Chai & Draxler, 2014; Chicco et al., 2021). When comparing
the performance of two ML models, a lower RMSE indicates that the ML
model has fewer large errors. For instance, if one ML model has an RMSE
value of 40, while another ML model has an RMSE value of 50, the first
ML model is better at minimizing large errors and performs better than
the second ML model. The formula for RMSE is as follows:

3.9. Feature Importance
For the RF algorithm, every node in a decision tree represents a
condition that tests a particular feature, with the goal of splitting the
dataset into two groups with comparable response variable values (Biau
& Scornet, 2016). Impurity is the criterion by which the optimal split is
determined (Scornet, 2023). The more a feature reduces the impurity at
each split, the more important it is. By averaging how much each feature
reduces impurity across all trees in the forest, the RF algorithm ranks the
features by importance. In other words, features that contribute more to
improving the model’s accuracy are ranked higher in importance.

There were several steps to apply nested cross-validation for developing and validating the ML models. First, the datasets were split into
five equally sized outer folds, ensuring that each fold maintained the
same proportional representation of data from each economy. For each
of the outer folds, one of these outer folds was set aside as the test set,
and the remaining four folds were combined to create a new dataset for
training and validation.
Within this combined dataset, an inner loop of cross-validation was
conducted. Specifically, the combined four outer folds were partitioned
into five equally sized inner folds, ensuring that each inner fold maintained the same proportional representation of data from each economy.
In each iteration of the inner loop, one of these inner folds was set as the
validation set, while the remaining four inner folds were used as the
training set. The inner loop cross-validation was repeated five times,
with each inner loop being used as the validation set once. The average
performance across these five iterations served as a performance indicator to select the best-performing hyperparameters.
Following this, the model was trained with the selected hyperparameters on the entire combined training set (i.e., four outer folds).
Finally, the trained model was tested on the outer test fold that was set
aside initially. As mentioned above, the final student weights (i.e.,
W_FSTUWT) were incorporated during the ML model training and
validation to ensure that the analysis accurately represented the underlying population structure. These weights represent the probability
that a student was sampled from a population (OECD, 2009). Incorporating the weights helps adjust for any sampling biases and ensures that
the results are generalizable to the broader population, leading to more
accurate and equitable model performance across regions within the
same culture group (OECD, 2009). The process was repeated for each of
the five outer folds, therefore each fold served as the test set once. The
final model’s performance was the average performance across all five
outer folds.

5.1. How well can lasso regression and random forest predict resilient
students’ mathematics literacy in high-performing regions?
This study adopted two supervised ML algorithms to predict resilient
students’ mathematics literacy. The findings showed that both RF and
LR algorithms performed better in Western cultures than in Eastern
cultures. The results are aligned with previous ML studies in the PISA

Western cultures than in Eastern cultures (Pan & Cutumisu, 2024).

may serve as a possible reason, which is consistent with previous ML
studies (Choi & Sung, 2024; Chung et al., 2021; Pan & Cutumisu, 2024).

strongly influenced by collective norms and behaviors (Triandis, 1995).

shape students’ experiences and interactions similarly (Chang et al.,
2011). A study conducted by Wang et al. (2023) supported the statement, finding that students from East Asia had constantly performed
well in mathematics compared to their international peers. Therefore,
uniformity and homogeneity lead to less variability in the data and
fewer distinct patterns that ML algorithms could uncover. In contrast,
Western cultures are characterized by a high degree of individualism,
leading to a wide range of behaviors, attitudes, and outcomes among
students (Basabe & Ros, 2005; Triandis, 1989). Therefore, the students
within Western cultures may tend to focus more on their unique

Furthermore, for Western cultures, self-efficacy towards mathematics plays the predominant role in predicting their mathematics literacy. It aligns with the Western emphasis on individualism and

perspective and highlighted that self-efficacy was a motivational predictor in mathematics achievement (Holenstein et al., 2022).
Additionally, it has been noted that mathematics efficacy towards
21st-century skills is emphasized in both cultures. Specifically, 21st-century skills are those skills that prepare students for the modern world,
including critical thinking, computational thinking, creativity, etc.
(OECD, n.d). This phenomenon reflects a broader educational goal of
equipping students with the ability necessary to navigate complex and
evolving challenges. For instance, computational thinking, a key
component of 21st-century skills, was part of mathematics competencies
in PISA 2022 (OECD, n.d; OECD, 2023). Computational thinking involves problem solving, designing systems, and understanding human
behaviors by drawing concepts from computer science (Wing, 2006). It
has the potential to encourage students to transfer what they have
learned, thus better preparing students for the 21st-century (e.g., Jin &
Cutumisu, 2024; Montuori et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2022). Both cultures are
increasingly focusing on computational thinking education (Grover &
Pea, 2013). Singapore, as an example from Eastern cultures, has promoted computational thinking in its educational systems (Kong &
Abelson, 2019). Similarly, Canada, an example from Western cultures,
has promoted computational thinking education (e.g., Hennessey et al.,
2017; Jin & Cutumisu, 2023). This illustrates that regardless of whether
an economy is from Eastern or Western cultures, preparing students with
21st-century skills is crucial for their success in a rapidly changing
world.
In summary, the findings highlight cultural differences in how these
factors influence resilient students’ mathematics literacy. In Eastern
cultures, anxiety plays a significant role, with support from collective
resources such as family and school being crucial. In contrast, Western
cultures place a greater emphasis on self-efficacy, reflecting individualistic values. Additionally, the results from both cultures emphasize the
importance of mathematics self-efficacy towards 21st-century skills. The

global shift towards 21st-century skills emphasizes the universal
importance of preparing students for modern challenges. The focus on
critical thinking, creativity, and computational thinking is essential
across all cultures, as these skills equip students to navigate complex and
evolving challenges effectively. Therefore, researchers must tailor
educational approaches to accommodate cultural contexts while
considering these global skills in achieving academic resilience and
success in mathematics.
5.3. Limitations and Future Research
This study has several limitations. Firstly, this study only includes the
predictors at individual levels, and this limited scope might not capture
the full complexity of factors influencing resilient students’ mathematics
literacy. Future research should consider including a broad range of
predictors from teachers’, schools’, and national levels, such as school
financial support for resilient students. This could provide a more
comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing resilient students’ mathematics literacy. Secondly, the participants included in this
study were from high-performing-mathematics economies and might
not represent the broad population of secondary adolescents across the
world. The samples in this study might be biased towards certain demographic or financial backgrounds. It potentially limits the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, future research could include diverse
populations across different levels of mathematics-performing countries. Thirdly, the study grouped countries into broad cultural categories, namely Eastern cultures and Western cultures, which may have
the potential to overlook the cultural differences that exist within each
group. Specifically, economies like Macao and Japan, both categorized
as Eastern cultures, have different cultural, educational, and social
contexts that might uniquely influence students’ academic resilience
and mathematics performance. This broad categorization might mask
such differences and potentially limit the understanding of how specific
cultural nuances impact resilience. In addition, even within Japan, there
are differences among subgroups, such as immigration populations, who
may face unique challenges and experiences that affect their academic
resilience differently than native-born students. Future research could
focus on examining individual regions or even intra-country differences
in great detail to capture unique cultural contexts and provide more
precise insights into the factors that promote academic resilience across
diverse student populations. Fourthly, this ML study concentrates on

6. Conclusion
This study applied two ML algorithms, RF and LR, to predict resilient
students’ mathematics literacy across cultures. The study indicates that
both ML algorithms performed better in Western cultures than in Eastern
cultures. This suggests that cultural differences, feature importance, and
sample size may influence the effectiveness of these two ML models.
The key predictors of resilient students’ mathematics literacy in both
cultural contexts were identified. In Eastern cultures, mathematics selfefficacy towards 21st-century skills are important skills, followed by
mathematics self-efficacy and anxiety towards mathematics. Different
from Western cultures where mathematics anxiety is not highly ranked,
anxiety towards mathematics plays an important role in Eastern cultures. It emphasizes the need to address emotional factors and reduce
anxiety to enhance resilient students’ mathematics literacy from Eastern
cultures. In Western cultures, mathematics self-efficacy emerged as a
predominant predictor, highlighting the importance of resilient students’ confidence in fostering their mathematics literacy. This finding
suggests that future interventions could focus on boosting resilient students’ self-efficacy toward mathematics, thus helping them to overcome
challenges and improve their mathematics literacy. Moreover, this study
also identified that the mathematics self-efficacy towards 21st-century
skills is underscored by both cultures. It indicates that fostering 21stcentury skills, such as computational thinking and creativity, is an
important strategy that can help students develop academic resilience
and mathematics literacy.
This study contributes to a deeper understanding of the factors
influencing students’ academic resilience and mathematics literacy
across different cultural contexts. By identifying the distinct and overlapping predictors in Eastern and Western cultures, this study can provide practical guidance for educators and policymakers.
In conclusion, this ML study not only highlights the importance of
selecting the suitable ML model for cultural contexts but also sheds light
on key factors from “I am”, “I have”, and “I can” dimensions that
contribute to academic resilience and success in mathematics across
cultures. Future studies could further explore these findings by examining how these factors or performance of ML models vary within the
same cultural area (e.g., Eastern culture) across different economies (e.
g., Macao and Japan). Additionally, investigating regional differences
within the same economy could also provide deeper insights into how
localized cultural, socioeconomic, and educational contexts influence
students’ academic resilience and mathematics literacy. By doing so,
educators can better support all students, thus helping them to develop
academic resilience needed to succeed in mathematics and even beyond.

5.4. Implications and Contributions
This study contributes to mathematics-related research from theoretical, methodological, and practical perspectives.
Theoretically, this study expands the resilience theory framework (i.
e., I have, I am, and I can) developed by Grotberg (1995) by identifying
related features and incorporating ML techniques in the data analysis.
By incorporating the related features into different dimensions of the
resilient theory framework, this study potentially broadens the framework’s scope and applicability within the context of secondary mathematics and academic resilience. In addition, by applying ML in
predicting resilient students’ literacy across cultures, this study fills the
literature gap by providing a nuanced view of the complex features that
may influence both Eastern and Western resilient students’ mathematics
literacy. Moreover, this study bridges the gaps between data analysis
research and mathematics related research by showing how ML can
display patterns and relationships that traditional statistics analysis
might neglect. It provides a more comprehensive theoretical understanding of factors affecting resilient students’ mathematics literacy
across different cultures. Furthermore, this study fills a gap in the
existing literature by explicitly comparing the distinct features of resilient students’ mathematics performance across different cultures. While
previous studies have examined factors such as math anxiety and
self-efficacy within specific cultural contexts, there is a paucity of
studies that directly contrast these influences across Eastern and Western cultures. Therefore, the findings of this study provide valuable insights that not only advance our understanding of cultural differences in
educational psychology but also inform the development of culturally
different educational strategies which can specifically be used for
improving the mathematics literacy of resilient students.
Methodologically, this is the first study applying ML to predict
resilient students’ mathematics performance. This innovative methodological approach illustrates the potential of ML to enhance predictive
accuracy and uncover the importance of various predictors. This study
can serve as a benchmark for future research that aims to predict special
participants’ mathematics literacy. Specifically, this study emphasized
the effectiveness of ML models and showed how different features could
be aggregated to predict resilient students’ mathematics literacy.
Therefore, it provides a foundation for future research to build upon,
including exploring more complex ML models or including more features to classify or predict special participants’ mathematics literacy.
Practically, this study provides a valuable reference for multiple
communities, such as educators, policymakers, and schools on how to
develop resilient students’ mathematics literacy. Specifically, this study
identified several key factors at individual levels (i.e., for Eastern cultures, the distinct feature was mathematics anxiety, whereas for Western
cultures, the most important predictor was self-efficacy towards mathematics) of resilient students’ mathematics literacy across cultures. For
instance, the researchers intending to develop resilient students’ mathematics literacy in Eastern cultures, especially in East Asia, could
consider creating interventions that focus on reducing their mathematics anxiety.
