The moderating role of parental support in the relationship between latent
profiles of bullying victimization and sense of school belonging: A
cross-national comparison
Yoonsun Han *, Hae Rin Kang , Ji Won Choe , Hyein Kim
Seoul National University, Republic of Korea

A R T I C L E I N F O

A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Cross-national research
Parental support
PISA data
Profiles of bully victimization
Sense of school belonging

Bullying victimization has a negative relationship with adolescents’ sense of school belonging, which is an
important contributor to their adaptive development. Based on the ecological systems perspective—which
suggests that multiple systems in adolescence including the family and school are interconnected—experiences at
home are linked with experiences at school. Thus the current study examined the relationship among bullying
victimization, sense of school belonging, and parental support. Self-reported data of students from five Northeast
Asian regions (Beijing-Shanghai-Jiangsu-Guangdong China, Hong Kong, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan) and the
United States from the 2015 Programme for International Student Assessment, an international cross-sectional
survey, was used. First, latent profile analysis was conducted to identify subgroups of adolescents who differ
in their experiences of bullying victimization. Second, multivariate regression analysis was used to examine the
association between victimization profiles and sense of school belonging, and to investigate the moderating role
of parental support in this relationship. There were more similarities in subgroups of victimization than differences across the six regions. The association between latent risk profiles and sense of school belonging varied
across the different regions. The buffering effect of parental support on the relationship between victimization
profiles and sense of school belonging was supported only by the data from Taiwanese adolescents. Despite
regional similarities and differences, a common finding emerged: membership to any subgroup of victimization
lowered adolescents’ sense of school belonging, when compared to membership to the very low group. Implications for intervention design for victimized adolescents are discussed.

1. Introduction
Bullying victimization is a serious international problem that has
detrimental effects for children and adolescents (Rigby & Smith, 2011).
Numerous studies have shown that bullying victimization is a risk factor
for negative psychological outcomes such as anxiety (Iffland et al., 2014;
Lee, 2020), depression (Davis et al., 2019; Lee, 2020), anger (Kochenderfer-Ladd, 2004), fear (Kochenderfer-Ladd, 2004), and aggression
(Sullivan et al., 2006). The bullying literature is limited, however, in the
following areas.
First, although bullying victimization is possibly a heterogeneous
experience, many analytic methods focus on the average individual and
may overlook outliers or marginalized individuals who are often in
greatest need of social services. Second, emotional and psychological
outcomes—such as anxiety, depression, aggression, anger, and

fear—have mostly been considered in bullying victimization research.
Given that bullying victimization often occurs within school grounds or
involves interaction with peers from school, the examination of how
negative interaction with peers may diminish feelings of relatedness and
connectedness to school (Wang & Eccles, 2012) is also of critical
importance, particularly during this developmental period. Third,
although non-school factors, such as parental support, have been discussed as a protective factor against negative school experiences and a
facilitator for sense of school belonging (Slaten et al., 2016), the literature has given greater attention to utilizing resources within the school,
such as teacher support, to resolve school-related issues. Fourth, the vast
majority of the literature on bullying victimization and sense of school
belonging have relied on English-speaking western samples (e.g., Allen
et al., 2018) and the few cross-national studies are mostly descriptive in
nature (e.g., Chester et al., 2015; Craig et al., 2009). Cross-national

victimization research is the ability to identify latent groups of individuals with similar bullying victimization experiences, including
small groups that may otherwise not be detected by variable-centered
methods that focus on the mean. Findings may play a crucial role in
the development of targeted interventions that are not only culturally
sensitive but also attuned to similarities and differences in bullying
victimization experiences of adolescents. However, no past study has
examined cross-national similarities and differences in bullying victimization experiences, especially in relation to sense of school belonging.

2. Literature review
2.1. Cross-national perspectives on bullying victimization
Although international findings suggest that there has been an
overall decline in bullying perpetration and victimization from the
1990s to mid-2010s (Finkelhor, 2013), peer bullying remains salient
among children and adolescents worldwide. The rates of bullying
victimization vary widely across and within regions. For example, the
rates of bullying victimization range from 2% to 61.7% in the United
States (Eisenberg et al., 2019; Jetelina et al., 2019; Lebrun-Harris et al.,
2018; Lessne & Yanez, 2016; Musu et al., 2019), 2.4–64.4% in Korea
(Foundation, 2020; Hong, Kim, & Hunter, 2019; Kwon et al., 2012;
Republic of Korea Ministry of Education., 2019), 7.5–35.8% in Japan
(Hamada et al., 2018; Mizuta et al., 2018; Osuka et al., 2019; Tochigi
et al., 2012), 2–66% in B-S-J-G (China) (Chan & Wong, 2015; Han et al.,
2017; Qiao et al., 2009), 8–62% in Hong Kong (Chan & Wong, 2015;
Fung, 2019; Wong, 2004; Wong et al., 2008), and 10.7–50% in Taiwan
(Chan & Wong, 2015; Chen & Cheng, 2013; Children’s Bureau, 2011;
Wang et al., 2019).
The prevalence and patterns of bullying victimization vary across
countries because bullying is a complex phenomenon with multiple
causes and varied outcomes (Espelage & Swearer, 2010; Koo et al.,
2008). Furthermore, translations of the English term bullying vary
across languages as they contain different cultural representations of the
bullying phenomenon **(Cheng, Chen, Ho, & Cheng, 2011; Smith,
2016; Smith et al., 2002). In collectivistic cultures, which emphasize
group harmony and social relationships, bullying perpetration may
occur by groups rather than by individuals (Hilton et al., 2010; Kanetsuna et al., 2006; Lee et al., 2016). Koo et al. (2008) examined 11- to
16-year-old Korean students’ experiences of “wang-ta” (Korean argot for
bullying) and found that most victims had been bullied by a group of
pupils from the same class. Lee et al. (2016) also found that participant
responses emphasized social exclusion when interpreting the meaning of
wang-ta. Similarly, the **Japanese Ministry of Education and Science
(2004; as cited in Akiba, 2004) reported that indirect bullying (e.g.,
exclusion from peer groups, being ignored) is the most frequently
experienced type of “ijime.” One study examined five types of bullying
across different ten Asia-Pacific regions and found that being “made fun
of or being called names,” was most commonly reported with the
exception of Korea and New Zealand (Lai et al., 2008). In another study,
verbal bullying emerged as the most frequently experienced type of
bullying in both Austria and Japan, but the rate of experience was higher
in Japan (Greimel & Kodama, 2011).

2.3. Bullying victimization and sense of school belonging
Generally, sense of belonging refers to the feeling that one is attached
and connected to others (Baumeister & Leary, 1995) and is a valued
member of the group (Newman et al., 2007). The desire to belong is a
fundamental and innate human need, and a low sense of belonging results in negative psychological consequences such as anxiety, depression, and loneliness (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). Sense of school
belonging is a psychological construct that is defined as one’s feeling of
acceptance, respect, inclusion, and support within the school environment (Goodenow & Grady, 1993). Rejecting and hostile exchange with
peers in the school setting may undermine one’s feeling of being
accepted and recognized as a valuable member of the school
community.
Sense of school belonging is an important factor that contributes to
students’ academic success and psychological functioning (Allen et al.,
2018). In particular, adolescents with a lower sense of belonging may
not reap the benefits of school connectedness (Eisenberg et al., 2003).
Diminished sense of school belonging may predict low academic
achievement, negative mental health outcomes, and greater maladaptive behaviors (Slaten et al., 2016). As for long-term consequences, a low
sense of school belonging is associated with a higher probability of
engaging in substance use in later years (Bond et al., 2007), as well as
improved academic adjustment, stronger work orientation, higher selfworth, and low internalizing and externalizing problems in college
(Pittman & Richmond, 2007).
Schools provide a primary setting for social development in adolescence. Any conflict-driven or unsupportive interaction with peers—such
as bullying victimization—can reduce relatedness and satisfaction in
school, which may further contribute to other psychological and
behavioral outcomes (Wang & Eccles, 2012). Empirical research has
confirmed the association between bullying victimization and low sense
of school belonging (Li et al., 2020). School belonging has been identified to mediate the path between bullying victimization and depression

2.4. Protective role of parental support
Although bullying victimization can lower one’s sense of school
belonging, protective factors outside the school may effectively buffer
the adverse effects of problems that adolescents encounter within their
schools. This idea can be supported by the ecological systems perspective, which suggests that multiple systems in adolescence including the
family and school are interconnected and that experience in one system
strongly influences the other (Bronfenbrenner, 1986). That is, experiences at the home can affect experiences at school; parents can become a
major contributor in preventing negative school experiences. Robust ties
between school and parents have been argued for to enhance positive
school climate and youth outcomes (Gage et al., 2014). Given the
interlinkage between the family and school systems, for adolescents who
share insecure peer relationships in school settings, parent’s secure and
effective support may contribute to improving adolescents’ coping skills
and resources, thus reducing the potentially detrimental effect of bully
victimization on the feeling of belonging to the school social environment. It is plausible that this protective effect of the family in adverse
school situations may be more prominent in collectivist cultures that
strongly value family goals and values than in individualist societies.
To our knowledge, no existing study examines the relationship
among parental support, bullying victimization, and sense of school
belonging across multiple countries; however, some empirical studies
provide a basis for the current study. Strong ties between parents and
adolescents can protect youth against negative school-related outcomes,
such as bullying victimization (Doty et al., 2017; **Kretschmer, Veenstra, Deković, & Oldehinkel, 2017; Lereya, et al., 2013). Similarly, in
Hong Kong (Chan & Wong, 2015) parent-adolescent attachment relationships were associated with lower bullying victimization experience and in South Korea, adolescent’s negative relationships with
parents were linked with increased likelihood of bullying victimization
(**Han, Ma, Bang, & Song, 2019). Parental support also plays an
important role in facilitating a greater sense of school belonging and
connectedness to school (Slaten et al., 2016).

3.2. Measures
According to PISA technical reports, the survey was constructed
under the direction of the PISA Governing board. For validity, the
questionnaire items were chosen and formatted with feedback from each
participating countries’ national project group and linguistic experts.
For reliability, PISA examined the magnitude and consistency of itemtotal score correlations for each scale by country, as well as, the
magnitude and the consistency of scale reliability by country. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis and item response theory analyses were used for checking each scale’s validity and reliability.
3.2.1. Dependent variable
The PISA survey questionnaire assessed students’ sense of school
belonging using six items (e.g., “I feel awkward and out of place in my
school”; “I feel lonely at school”). The respondents indicated their
agreement with each statement on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly
agree; 4 = strongly disagree). Responses to positively worded items (e.g.,
“I feel like I belong at school”) were reverse-coded; therefore, higher
scores were indicative of a greater sense of belonging. The scores of the
six items were averaged to yield a composite score (Cronbach’s αB-S-J-G
(China) = 0.803, αHong Kong = 0.682, αJapan = 0.809, αSouth Korea = 0.795,
αTaiwan = 0.868, αUS = 0.857).
3.2.2. Moderator
Parental support was defined as the degree to which parents were
engaged in and supportive of adolescents’ school activities. Students
responded to four statements (e.g., “My parents support me when I am
facing difficulties at school”; “My parents encourage me to be confident”). Responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly
disagree; 4 = strongly disagree), and the scores of the four items were
averaged to compute the composite score. Higher scores were indicative
of greater parental support (Cronbach’s α B-S-J-G (China) = 0.810, αHong
Kong = 0.805, αJapan = 0.855, αSouth Korea = 0.888, αTaiwan = 0.853, αUS =
0.869).

2.5. Study objectives and hypotheses
This study has three objectives: (1) to identify latent groups of adolescents who differ in their experiences of bullying victimization across
six regions, (2) to examine the association between victimization profiles and sense of school belonging, and (3) to investigate the moderating
role of parental support in this relationship.
With regard to the first objective, we expected to identify several
latent groups that differ in bullying victimization experiences. With
regard to group differences, we expected profiles characterized by more
severe victimization to report a lower sense of school belonging.
Parental support was expected to buffer the relationship between
different profiles of bullying victimization and sense of school
belonging. Finally, we expected the results of all the aforementioned
analyses to differ across the six regions due to their cultural and social
differences.

3.2.3. Independent variable
Victimization experiences were measured using six items that
required students to indicate how often they had experienced the
following events during the past 12 months: “Other students left me out
of things on purpose”; “Other students made fun of me”; “I was threatened by other students”; “Other students took away or destroyed things
that belonged to me”; “I got hit or pushed around by other students”; and
“Other students spread nasty rumors about me.” Responses were
recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never or almost never; 2 = a few
times a year; 3 = a few times a month; 4 = once a week or more). Higher
scores were indicative of a greater frequency of bullying victimization.
Responses to each question were used to identify victimization profiles
(Cronbach’s α B-S-J-G (China) = 0.840, αHong Kong = 0.861, αJapan = 0.802,
αSouth Korea = 0.711, αTaiwan = 0.799, αUS = 0.849).

3.2.4. Control variables
The demographic, socioeconomic, and relational covariates that
were entered in the multilevel regression model were carefully selected
from the literature on bullying victimization and sense of school
belonging (Allen et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2014). Grade was included as
a covariate; this was to control for variation in the grade level across
study participants, although the PISA study was conducted on 15-yearolds. Students were required to select their grade from a list of all
possible grades ranging from grade 7 to 13. The respondents were also
required to indicate their gender (i.e., 0 = female, 1 = male) because prior
studies have shown that girls are likely to have a higher sense of school
belonging than boys (Allen et al, 2018; Uslu & Gizir, 2017).
Adolescent’s socioeconomic background including parental education and family’s economic condition have been found to be positively
related to their sense of school belonging (Marksteiner & Kruger, 2016;
Ostrove & Long, 2007). Parental education was quantified as the mean of
a student’s report for the highest level of completed maternal and
paternal schooling. There were slight variations in how educational
level was measured across the six regions (for details, see the PISA 2015
Student Questionnaires). Nevertheless, the measures listed five International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) levels from 0 (preprimary education) to 3A (upper secondary education). The original
scores were reverse-coded, and higher scores were indicative of a higher
educational level (Cronbach’s α B-S-J-G (China) = 0.683, αHong Kong = 0.663,
αJapan = 0.467, αSouth Korea = 0.471, αTaiwan = 0.562, αUS = 0.755). When
only one parent’s information was available, a single metric was used as
a measure of the respective student’s parental educational level. To
assess material status, students were required to indicate how many of
the following eight material possessions were available at their homes:
televisions, cars, rooms with a bath or shower, cell phones with internet
access (e.g., smartphones), computers (desktop, laptop, or notebook),
tablets (e.g., iPad, BlackBerry, PlayBook), ebook readers (e.g., Kindle,
Kobo, Bookeen), and musical instruments (e.g., guitar, piano). The
response options were as follows: 1 = none; 2 = one; 3 = two; and 4 =
three or more. A composite score was computed by averaging the responses of eight items; higher scores were indicative of a higher material
status (Cronbach’s α B-S-J-G (China) = 0.768, αHong Kong = 0.683, αJapan =
0.547, αSouth Korea = 0.588, αTaiwan = 0.627, αUS = 0.666).
Adolescent’s immigrant status is known to be correlated with many
barriers to adjustment in a new school such as cultural and language
deficiencies, low expectation from teachers, lack of sociocultural resources, and lack of parental involvement and instruction information
(DeNicolo et al., 2017; Van Caudenberg et al., 2020). Those barriers may
negatively influence the sense of school belonging among adolescents.
Student’s immigrant status was measured by their reported country of
birth. Students whose country of birth was the same as the country in
which they were surveyed were assigned “0” and their counterparts
were assigned “1.”
Lastly, the quality of teacher-student interaction is crucial for
determining students’ school belonging (Slaten et al, 2016). As an indicator of perceived relationship between the teacher and student, this
study included teacher discrimination as a covariate. Teacher discrimination was measured using six items (e.g. “Teachers gave me the
impression that they think I am less smart than I really am”; “Teachers
ridiculed me in front of others”). Students indicated how often they had
experienced teacher discrimination during the past 12 months on a 4point Likert scale (1 = never or almost never; 4 = once a week or more).
A composite score was computed by averaging the six item scores
(Cronbach’s α B-S-J-G (China) = 0.805, αHong Kong = 0.784, αJapan = 0.755,
αSouth Korea = 0.780, αTaiwan = 0.721, αUS = 0.791). Higher scores were
indicative of more frequent teacher discrimination.

victimization experiences into groups. LPA is a person-centered
approach that allocates a person to a group based on their score on a
latent variable (Pastor et al., 2007). In other words, LPA focuses on the
relationships among individuals rather than describing the relationships
among variables (Jung & Wickrama, 2008). From a practical or policy
perspective, LPA can identify the heterogeneous groups that constitute a
sample. This approach allows researchers to understand the nuanced
aspects of an identified population (Howard & Hoffman, 2018), which
often offer crucial information that can facilitate the development of
targeted interventions.
The ideal number of latent classes can be ascertained by comparing
various model fit indices. The best-fitting model is selected based on
several evaluation criteria: Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian
information criteria (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (SSABIC); lower
values are indicative of a better fit (Tein et al., 2013). The entropy value
represents the quality of grouping in the LPA model (range = 0–1). An
entropy value that approximates 1 (i.e., greater than 0.80) is indicative
of high discrimination among the latent classes (Muthén, 2004). The
adjusted Lo-Mendell-Rubin test (Adj. LMR) and bootstrap likelihood
ratio test (BLRT) compare the k-latent profile model to that of k-1 profiles. A significant p-value indicates that the model with k profiles is a
better fit of the data than the model with k-1 profiles (Geiser, 2012).
After the number and characteristics of the latent groups are determined, individuals can be classified into the most corresponding profiles
based on their posterior probabilities. In addition to fit indices, considerations of parsimony, the research question, theoretical justifications,
and interpretability form the basis of the determination of the number of
classes (Jung & Wickrama, 2008). In this study, missing values were
addressed using the full information maximum likelihood method.
In the second step, a series of multilevel regression analyses were
conducted to test the hypotheses regarding the associations between
victimization profiles and sense of school belonging. As mentioned
earlier, in the PISA data, students were clustered within schools as a
result of systematic PPS sampling. Therefore, we examined the possibility of employing multilevel analysis using intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) in the basic model. Higher ICC values (range = 0–1)
indicate that the variances are largely attributable to the group-level
factor, such that students of a given group may have provided correlated responses to the assessments. In this study, the emergent ICCs (ICC
B-S-J-G (China) = 0.04, ICCHong Kong = 0.02, ICCJapan = 0.04, ICCSouth Korea =
0.03, ICCTaiwan = 0.01, ICCUS = 0.01) were too low to justify the use of
multilevel analysis (Koo & Li, 2016); therefore, a multivariate regression
model was used. All the models controlled for demographic (e.g., grades,
gender, immigration status), socioeconomic (e.g., parental education,
material status), and school-related (e.g., teacher discrimination) variables. Model 1, which included all the control variables, analyzed the
relationship between victimization profiles and sense of belonging.
Model 2 examined the moderating effect of parental support on this
relationship. Missing values were addressed using listwise deletion for
key variables.

4.2. Latent profile analysis
Table 3 presents the fit indices for the LPA models with two to six
profiles. AIC, BIC, and SSABIC values declined as the number of profiles
increased. The rate of decline was considered when the number of
profiles was determined. All the models had an entropy value that
approximated 1 and was greater than 0.80; therefore, the classification
of the students into their respective profiles was considered to entail a
high level of discrimination (Muthén, 2004). The p-value of the BLRT
was statistically significant for all the groups across all the six regions;
instead, the Adj. LMR was used to determine the optimal number of
profiles. Based on several model fit criteria and the interpretability of
results, the five-profile model was chosen for Japan, and the four-profile
model was chosen for the remaining five regions.
The labels of the profiles for each region, distribution of students
across profiles, and average item scores across profiles are presented in
Table 4 and is depicted in Fig. 1. For all six regions, three common
profiles emerged. The very low, low, and medium profiles were represented by three ring-shaped radial graphs that progressively expanded
from the center. That is, the radial graph of the very low profile was
nested within that of the low profile, and these two graphs were nested
within the graph of the medium profile. Specifically, adolescents in the
very low profile scored average “1” on all six items indicating that they
hardly experienced any bullying victimization; those in the low profile
scored on average “2” (=a few times a year); finally, those in the medium
profile reported an average score of “3”(=a few times a month).
Some variation in the latent profiles of bullying victimization
emerged with regards to the “made fun of me” item. In Hong Kong,
Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan, adolescents solely scored much higher
in the “made fun of me” item than other items, and thus was named the
made fun profile. In Japan, one latent profile scored high in both “made
fun of me” and “got hit or pushed around,” and was called the fun and hit
profile. In China, adolescents in the fun and destroy profile coexperienced “made fun of me” and “took away or destroyed things.”
Finally, in the United States, “made fun of me” experience did not stand
out, and was rather experienced together with “threatened,” “left me out
of things,” and “spread nasty rumours,” and was named the verbal and
relational profile.

psychological concept that bears significance to the study of bullying.
Past research findings on the link between bullying victimization and
sense of belonging are limited despite its importance. Furthermore,
there is a dearth of cross-national comparisons of this relationship even
though the findings may facilitate the development of culturally sensitive intervention models (**Espelage & Swearer, 2003).
In this study, bullying victimization experiences were classified into
several latent profiles using a person-centered approach across six regions. Both common and region-specific victimization profiles were
identified. As predicted, victimization profiles were differentially associated with sense of school belonging. Parental support moderated this

relationship among adolescents with specific victimization profiles
across the different regions. A detailed discussion of the study findings is
presented in the following sections.
5.1. Latent profiles of bullying victimization across regions
LPA revealed that the study findings were more similar than different
across the six regions. First, as represented in the made fun profile (e.g.,
Hong Kong, South Korea, Japan, and Taiwan), fun and destroy profile (e.
g., B-S-J-G China), and fun and hit profile (e.g., Japan), high scores
emerged for the “made fun of me” item across all the six regions even
when other bullying victimization items scored low. This finding is
consistent with many past findings, which suggest that making fun of

peers, teasing, or name-calling is a common form of bullying across
many countries (Collins et al., 2004; Hinduja & Patchin, 2010; Lai et al.,
2008). The emergence of Japan’s fun and hit profile was surprising as
ijime—a Japanese term that is most equivalent to the English term bullying—primarily describes a relational rather than a physical form of
bullying (Smith et al., 2002). A non-negligible proportion of adolescents
were classified into the fun and hit group, implying the possibility that
Japanese group-oriented bullying may occur in multiple forms.
Although most commonly reported, the ambiguous nature of funmaking behavior may confound its relationship with sense of school
belonging. For example, making fun of others may be presented in either
a “friendly” or an “upsetting” way (Smith et al., 2002). Depending on the
motivation behind the actor, as well as how the action is perceived by
the receiver, the experience of being made fun of may or may not lead to
negative adolescent outcomes. Further research through in depth interviews or more detailed survey questions is needed to explain why a
subgroup of adolescents are characterized primarily by past experiences
of having been made fun of by their peers, and whether there exist
similarities in the motivation for such behavior across regions.
Second, the large presence of adolescents with little experience of
bullying victimization in the past 12 months for all six regions is
consistent with the previously reported decline in the frequency of
school bullying across multiple countries (Rigby & Smith, 2011).
However, this does not imply that researchers should pay less attention
to the study of school bullying. Although the rates of bullying victimization vary considerably depending on the samples used, there is evidence to suggest that more than 60% of adolescents are victims of
bullying (Chan & Wong, 2015; Eisenberg et al., 2019; Kwon et al., 2012;
Wong et al., 2008). Despite the low rates of school bullying, bullying
victimization experiences have a nonnegligible impact on adolescent
development in the domain of school adjustment; thus, public interest in
this issue should persist.
Third, the very low, low, and medium profiles differed in the severity
of victimization experiences, and not by specific types of victimization.
This finding is resonant with previously identified ordered latent groups
of bullying victimization experiences (Bradshaw et al., 2013; Nylund,
Bellmore, Nishina, & Graham, 2007; Wang, Iannotti, Luk, & Nansel,
2010). Specifically, a study examined the victimization experiences of
middle and high school adolescents in the United States and found that
the high verbal, physical, and relational and low victimization/normative
classes obtained high and low scores on measures of all 10 types of
victimization, respectively (Bradshaw et al., 2013). Another longitudinal study that was conducted in the United States followed students
across three years of middle school (i.e., 6th to 8th grade; Nylund et al.,
2007) and identified three ordered classes: victimized, sometimes victimized, and nonvictimized. Similar results were reported by Wang et al.
(2010), whereby a nonvictim profile was nested within an all-types victims
profile. In sum, the present results, which concur with past findings,
suggest that latent profiles are ordered based on the severity of victimization experiences. In other words, adolescents who are identified as
victims of a certain subtype of bullying may be at a high risk of exposure
to other subtypes of bullying as well. Such findings may have important
implications that can inform the development of anti-bullying interventions for adolescents, especially because adolescents are more
likely to report direct rather than indirect forms of bullying to adults
(Rivers & Smith, 1994).

perception that one is an integral part of the school (Demanet & Van
Houtte, 2012b; D’hondt et al., 2015). The strong link between bullying
victimization and adolescents’ sense of school belonging is an important
issue that interventions should address because a poorer sense of school
belonging is likely to lead to risk behaviors such as misconduct
(Demanet & Van Houtte, 2012a), violence (Duggins et al., 2016), substance use (Napoli et al., 2003), or maladaptive outcomes such as suicide
(Fisher et al., 2015).
5.3. Parental support as a buffer
The final objective of this study was to examine if parental support
protects the negative relationship between bullying victimization
experience and a low sense of school belonging. Results for the interaction effects between the latent profiles of bullying victimization and
parental support were largely mixed across the six regions. Specifically,
the hypothesized buffering effect of parental support emerged only for
the Taiwanese subsample. Although adolescents in the made fun profile
reported a lower sense of school belonging than adolescents in the very
low profile, the corresponding difference between adolescents in the very
low and made fun profiles attenuated as parental support increased.
These results indicated that parental support may act as a protective
factor that buffers the adverse effects of victimization on adolescents’
sense of school belonging. That is, greater family support may offer the
resources that moderate the relationship between negative bullying
victimization experience and sense of belonging within the context of
the school. It is unclear, however, why such protective effects of parental
support were found only in the Taiwanese sample, particularly when
common values prevail across other Northeast Asian regions. The
meaning of bullying or specific behaviors that constitute bullying have
been found to be different across cultures (Smith et al., 2002), resulting
in different experiences of bullying victimization across different regions
(Greimel & Kodama, 2011; Hilton et al., 2010; Lai et al., 2008; Lee et al.,
2016). However, our results concerning the relationship among multiple
ecological systems indicate that rather than macro-level categorizations
of collectivist and individualist cultures, an in-depth investigation—possibly through qualitative methods—of the more immediate
micro processes that are experienced by adolescents in their everyday
lives is warranted in future research.
In contrast, among adolescents from B-S-J-G (China) the interaction
between parental support and the fun and destroy profile was statistically
significant, but negative in direction. In South Korea, the interaction
between parental support and the made fun profile was also negative,
indicating that greater parental support was associated with greater
difference in sense of school belonging between adolescents in the very
low and made fun profiles. In other words, greater parental support was
associated with larger gaps in the sense of school belonging of adolescents with very low and other risk profiles. Similarly, among adolescents
from the United States, group differences in the sense of school
belonging of adolescents with the very low and medium profiles increased
as parental support increased. In these regions, the need for parental
support in the daily school experiences of adolescents may have
heightened due to the greater detrimental role of bullying victimization
on sense of belonging. However, with cross-sectional data, it is difficult

6. Conclusion and implication
Despite the few aforementioned limitations, this study makes
important contributions to the literature. First, this study examined
similarities and differences in latent victimization profiles and their
associations with a sense of school belonging and parental support
across different regions. The findings of cross-national studies can
inform the development of culturally sensitive interventions. Interventions that are developed based on the findings of studies that have
been conducted in other regions may be less effective due to possible
region-level sociocultural differences. Identifying such cultural similarities and differences in bullying phenomena can inform the development
of effective interventions.
Second, since study results imply that the bullying phenomena can
be understood within the organic relationships among multiple
ecological systems including the school and family, it is possible to
consider parental support as a point of intervention in preventing
bullying behaviors and reducing the negative outcomes associated with
bullying. Parental support may be encouraged by establishing a school
committee made up of school staff, administrators, and parents to
discuss anti-bullying strategies (e.g., Olweus Bullying Prevention Program; Olweus, 2004), regularly providing information to parents about
the school’s methods to address bullying (e.g., Steps to Respect program
in USA; Frey et al., 2005), facilitating parent-teacher workshops and
consultation (e.g., Restorative Whole-School Approach in Hong Kong;
Wong et al, 2011), and offering parental education on understanding
signals of risk and responding adequately to adolescent’s experience of
bullying victimization (e.g., intervention program by the Blue Tree
Foundation in South Korea; The Blue Tree Foundation, 2019).

5.4. Limitations
The present findings, which were derived using nationally representative data, delineate results that can be generalized to the larger
population of adolescents. However, some limitations should be borne in
mind when the analytic results are interpreted. First, it is difficult to
draw conclusive statements about the causality of the emergent relationships because cross-sectional data were used to conduct the analyses. The present results suggest that sense of school belonging varies
across adolescents with different bullying victimization profiles. However, the exact direction or causal nature of these associations was not
examined. Therefore, follow-up studies should utilize longitudinal data
to examine the nature of the relationship between bullying victimization
experiences and sense of school belonging, as prior research using longitudinal data or meta-analysis methods have reported evidence for
reciprocal or bidirectional associations (Allen et al., 2018; Davis et al.,
2019).
The measures of key variables that were used in this study entail a
few limitations. For example, different types of victimization (e.g., online bullying victimization) were not assessed. Adolescents have easier
access to and are more familiar with digital devices than members of the
older generation; therefore, online bullying may be facilitated by
increased social interactions in cyberspaces with limited supervision
(Kowalski, Giumetti, Schroeder, & Lattanner, 2014). However, this
study focused solely on offline bullying victimization experiences
without considering online bullying victimization. Therefore, the present results may paint only a partial portrait of adolescents’ bullying
victimization experiences. Indeed, latent class analysis of nationally
representative data from 12 to 18-year-old adolescents revealed that
offline face-to-face victimization and cyberbullying do not necessarily
co-occur; instead, these experiences yield distinct latent groups (Barboza, 2015). Therefore, future research studies should assess both offline
and online bullying victimization experiences.
Several bullying victimization items were not normally distributed,
which is commonly reported in studies of bullying behavior. For
example, the vast majority of students in Taiwan and South Korea
responded never or almost never for the “I got hit or pushed around by
other students” item. Despite advantages to using normally distributed
indicators in latent profile analysis, normality does not seem to be a
necessary condition; however non-normality of items may affect the
performance of some of the model fit indices, and thus model selection
must also be strongly informed by guiding theory (Morgan et al., 2016).
Finally, another limitation of this study is that all measures are selfreported by adolescents. Although self-report measures are critical for
revealing one’s experiences, thoughts, and feelings, there is the risk of
response bias due to inability to fully understand the questions, to
correctly recall past experiences and feelings, or social desirability,
which can reduce the reliability and validity of the measure of interest
(Lucas, 2018). Self-reports may result in differential assessments,
particularly with regards to measures of the quality of relationships
(Han et al., 2012), such as bullying victimization and parental support.
Thus, future studies would use multiple forms of measures (i.e. parent
