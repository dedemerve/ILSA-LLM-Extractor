To address the lack of cross-national research on teacher outcomes of professional learning communities (PLCs),
this study investigates the relationships between PLCs and teacher outcomes using data from the Teaching and
Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018. With a sample of 127,339 teachers from 40 countries, we explore
how PLCs relate to job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and clarity of instruction. Results show a robust positive
relationship with job satisfaction, while relationships with self-efficacy and clarity of instruction are weaker and
vary by context. Our findings suggest that PLC effectiveness vary across educational and cultural contexts, calling
for further research.

1. Introduction
The professional development of teachers is considered an important
factor in the ongoing endeavour to improve student outcomes in schools
and in meeting the changing demands of education systems (Muijs et al.,
2014; Villegas-Reimers, 2003). Professional learning communities
(PLCs) are a popular approach to structuring such professional development through ongoing collaboration among teachers, who collectively examine and continuously seek to improve their instructional
practices, and thereby ultimately increase their students’ learning (Lee
et al., 2022; Stoll & Louis, 2007a).
Engaging teachers in PLCs has been associated with increased student learning and better teacher outcomes, including improved job
satisfaction, greater self-efficacy, and changes in instructional practices
(Lomos et al., 2011; Vescio et al., 2008; Yoo & Jang, 2022; Zhang et al.,
2020). While the number of studies into PLCs is growing, it remains
difficult to compare and accumulate findings due to differences in
measures and definitions of PLCs, as well as the associated concepts
(Christensen, 2022; Lee et al., 2022). Given the attention that PLCs have
received internationally, studies of teacher outcomes are sparse, limited
to a few national contexts, and have produced inconsistent results
(Zhang et al., 2020; Zhang & Yuan, 2020). The role of the context, in
terms of philosophies of education, pedagogical traditions and traditions
for teacher autonomy, seems to have been widely overlooked as PLCs
have been applied across the globe (Guo & Wang, 2021; OECD, 2020;

Vieluf et al., 2012). Teacher collaboration, which is a core element of
PLCs, has also been associated with negative outcomes for some teachers, such as loss of autonomy and conflicts about teaching beliefs
(Vangrieken et al., 2015). Furthermore, there are some indications that
collaboration produces different outcomes for teachers in different
contexts (Reeves et al., 2017) and for individuals with different characteristics, such as teaching experience and gender (Liang et al., 2022;
Valckx et al., 2020).
We argue that there is currently a gap in the research in terms of
cross-national comparisons using consistent measures of various outcomes associated with PLCs as a way to understand whether such relationships differ across national contexts, potentially providing an
explanation for the inconsistent results in the existing research. We seek
to help address this gap by exploring the associations between PLCs and
teacher job satisfaction, self-efficacy and clarity of instruction across 40
countries/economies. We analyse data from the Teaching and Learning
International Survey (TALIS) 2018, which enables us to apply the same
measure of the different concepts to representative samples of teachers
working in a wide range of contexts.

theory of situated learning, which posits that learning occurs through

practice, which were later developed by Wenger, particularly in their
emphasis on collaborative learning and the development of shared
practices among professionals (Wenger, 1998). However, the literature
on PLCs is more explicitly focused on increasing student learning, and
places a stronger emphasis on the role of the entire organisation and
leadership in creating and sustaining a collaborative culture in schools
(Blankenship & Ruona, 2007; Wenger, 1998).
Building on these theoretical developments and Hord’s seminal
work, the widespread implementation of PLCs, particularly in the United
States from the late 1990s onward, can largely be attributed to Richard
Dufour and colleagues. Their work provided popular and practical
guidelines for implementing and sustaining PLCs in schools, with a
strong focus on increasing student learning through professional
collaboration and reflective practice (DuFour et al., 1998, 2016). Since
then, PLCs have been widely implemented and studied internationally,
but the term itself is often used ambiguously, lacking a universally
accepted definition (DuFour, 2004; Stoll & Louis, 2007b).
The many different descriptions and definitions of PLCs are a source
of confusion and make it difficult to synthesise and compare findings
across studies. Many different measures of the concept of PLCs have
been developed and applied in empirical research internationally. Lee
et al. (2022) concluded that most applied measures cover at least the
following three components or measurement domains, while the number of dimensions, their content and labels differ substantially: ‘1)
Shared vision, values, and/or norms among school staff as a “community,” (2) focus on both improved student “learning” as an aim and
teachers’ continuous “learning” as an engine of PLCs, and (3) “collaboration” as a way to form and sustain PLCs’ (p. 277). Another distinction
in the definitions and measurements of PLCs is whether or not leadership
is considered an integrated aspect of PLCs, or if it should be considered
to be a crucial factor related to PLCs (Zhang et al., 2020).
Hudson (2023) proposed a conceptual framework for understanding
effective PLC operation in schools that describes how the PLC process of
enhancing student learning must take place through changes in teachers’ attitudes, beliefs and practices, and is these teacher related changes

2.2. Teacher job satisfaction
Job satisfaction is a popular and extensively researched concept
across disciplines and over time, and can briefly be defined as ‘a pleasurable or positive emotional state resulting from the appraisal of one’s
job or job experiences’ (Locke, 1976). Job satisfaction is one of the most
studied concepts in organisational psychology, and several models and
theories exist. A common distinction in the literature is that of intrinsic
and extrinsic factors, where extrinsic elements can for example be salary
or promotions, and intrinsic elements describe the satisfaction with the
work itself, including relationships with coworkers and personal or
professional growth (Judge et al., 2001). Teacher job satisfaction (TJS)
has also been studied intensively, and is considered important in relationship to for example student achievement, and has gained renewed
attention due to an international concern about mounting teacher
shortages, due to the relationship between TJS and retention (Hoque,
Wang, Qi, & Norzan, 2023; UNESCO, 2022).
While the ultimate aim of implementing PLCs is to increase student
learning, it has also been associated with increased intrinsic TJS theoretically: The collaboration between teachers in a PLC results in a shift:
rather than something that the individual teacher manages in isolation,
instructional practice becomes a collaborative endeavour where teachers learn with and from one another how to overcome challenges and
improve learning outcomes. The expectation is that this move towards
collective responsibility for student learning and mutual professional
support will improve teachers’ morale, enthusiasm and commitment to
the school’s goals and vision, leading to greater job satisfaction (Hord,
1997; Hord et al., 2008). Similarly, studies have shown that such close
collaboration with colleagues, including the opportunity to share their
doubts and the challenges and difficulties they encounter, is something
teachers associate with a greater sense of recognition for the work they
do and as enabling them to improve the quality of their instruction,
which leads to greater job satisfaction (Forte & Flores, 2014).
The results of empirical studies of the relationship between PLCs and
TJS have mainly shown a positive relationship between PLCs and/or
subdimensions of PLCs and teacher job satisfaction. Zhang and Yuan
(2020) investigated the relationship between both teacher-centric
characteristics and organisation-centric characteristics of PLCs and
teachers job satisfaction. They found that only teacher-centric characteristics of PLCs (‘collective inquiry and sharing’, and ‘shared purpose
and responsibility’) had a direct positive relationship with teacher job
satisfaction, while there was no significant direct relationship between
organisation-centric characteristics of PLCs (‘organisational structure’,
‘supportive leadership’ and ‘cultural barriers’) and job satisfaction.
Meanwhile, Zhang et al. (2020) found that both teacher- and
organisation-centric characteristics of PLCs were positively related to
job satisfaction. Finally, Yoo and Jang (2022) found that teachers who
participated in PLCs had higher job satisfaction on average.
As mentioned in the introduction, the broader field of research on
teacher collaboration has also linked collaboration to tension between
colleagues, a loss of autonomy and increased workload (Vangrieken
et al., 2015). We expect that such negative effects, should they exist,
would be reflected in teachers’ assessments of their job satisfaction.

2.3. Teacher self-efficacy
The concept of self-efficacy describes how individuals perceive their
ability to plan and execute specific actions. Specifically for teachers,
Bandura (1997) argues that teachers with high levels of instructional
efficacy operate under the belief that extra effort and appropriate
techniques can facilitate learning for students who are struggling,
whereas teachers with less instructional efficacy believe that there is
little they can do to facilitate learning for unmotivated or
under-performing students, which has a negative impact on the effort
they put into their instruction.

In addition to characteristics of the participating teachers, the success of PLCs may also be affected by contextual factors. Most research on
PLCs, as well as the development of the concept itself, has taken place in
relatively few contexts, especially the United States, although interest
has been growing internationally (Stoll & Louis, 2007b). While there is a
growing body of research concerning the outcomes of PLCs, it is difficult
to compare results across studies due to differences in the measures and
definitions used (Hudson, 2023). In a qualitative study comparing PLCs
in England and Finland, Webb et al. (2009) found that the context, in
terms of societal values and traditions, had a profound impact on the
possibilities for schools to function as PLCs, such as whether schools had
the freedom to focus on local concerns or were under pressure to meet
external performance targets. More generally, the outcomes of teacher
collaboration for both teachers and students have been shown to vary
between different countries (Reeves et al., 2017). To our knowledge,
there is no existing quantitative research examining PLC outcomes
across different national contexts. It therefore remains an open question
whether the inconsistent findings concerning teacher outcomes should
be attributed to differences in measures, contextual differences, or
something else entirely.

2.4. Instructional practices
The main reason for implementing PLCs in schools is to enhance the
instructional quality and thereby student learning. Despite this, research
into the ways that teachers alter their practice as a result of engaging in
PLCs is sparse. A review of the (primarily qualitative) existing research
on how PLCs affect instructional practice concluded that there was a
lack of detail specifying how teachers altered their instruction (Vescio
et al., 2008). A more recent review of the mainly qualitative or mixed
methods studies examining the effects of PLCs on teacher practice found
that, across the included studies, teachers reported that they had
changed their practice in various ways, such as using digital tools,
including formative assessment strategies and cooperative learning,
while the reasons for such changes were often vague or implicit (Doğan
& Adams, 2018). Doğan and Yurtseven (2018) found a positive and
significant relationship between PLCs and a composite measure based on
teachers’ own evaluations of three aspects of instructional quality.
Similarly Yoo and Jang (2022) found a higher average of ‘feedback
provision’ (which is treated as an indicator of instructional quality)
among teachers who participated in a PLC. In an analysis of teachers’
perceptions of changes in their competences and practice as a result of
engagement in PLCs, Vanblaere and Devos (2016) found that one of
three PLC dimensions was positively and significantly related to
‘changes in competences’, and that the same dimension was significantly
related to ‘changes in practice‘, although the latter relationship was no
longer significant after controlling for teacher and organisational characteristics. In a mixed methods study of science teachers engaged in
PLCs, the majority of teachers reported that their participation had
influenced their lesson planning and assessment strategies (Jones et al.,
2013).

2.6. Research questions
1) Are PLCs positively related to teachers’:
a) job satisfaction with their current school?
b) self-efficacy in instruction?
c) clarity of instruction?
2) Do the relationships differ between country contexts?
3) Do teaching experience and gender moderate these relationships?

The question capturing SVR was phrased as follows:

3.2. Professional learning community
We apply a measure of PLC developed in a secondary analysis since
there is no official measure included in TALIS 2018; Christensen, 2022).
This measure was developed based on an analysis of the most common
aspects of various definitions and applied measurements of PLCs, with
the purpose of including the most common aspects of PLCs, in an
internationally applicable measure, based on the relevant items in the
teacher questionnaire in TALIS 2018. The measures of PLC are modelled
as a shared cluster construct (Stapleton et al., 2016), using teacher responses in a multilevel confirmatory factor analysis to create a measure
of PLC at the school level. Three subdimensions make up a higher-order
factor of PLC: ‘Collaborative practice with focus on student learning’
(CPL) measures how often teachers engage with each other in collaborative activities that are related to PLCs, and, more specifically, the aspects of collaboration that relate to instruction and focus on the
individual student’s learning. ‘Shared vision and responsibilities’ (SVR)
measures the degree to which teachers agree that there is a collaborative
culture at their school, where teachers share the responsibility for
solving any problems that arise, and that they share a common set of
beliefs about teaching. ‘Supportive conditions’ (SC) measures the
teachers’ perceptions of barriers to their participation in professional
development, and hence to potential success of any PLC, such as not
having the necessary time and a lack of employer support. Specific
questions, items and response categories are presented below. As
mentioned in the introduction and the theory section, many different
definitions and measures of PLCs have been applied across studies, and
they vary significantly in terms of the content and number of dimensions
of factors included. The measure applied in this study does cover the
three common components identified by Lee et al. (2022), and aligns
with the group of studies that perceives leadership behaviour as a crucial
factor related to PLCs, rather than a part of PLCs (Zhang et al., 2020).
The intraclass correlations (ICC) for the items used in the measure are
included in Appendix A. For further information on the PLC measure,
and a discussion of the items and the subdimensions in relation to the
existing literature on PLCs and other applied measures, please see
(Christensen, 2022).

3.3. Job satisfaction
We measure job satisfaction using the official TALIS scale ‘Job
satisfaction with work environment’ (T3JSENV), because it measures
teachers’ satisfaction with the school/environment in which they are
currently working (example question: I would recommend this school as
a good place to work). There are other scales related to TJS in the TALIS
data that are concerned with the teaching profession in general and with
teaching autonomy. While working in a PLC could affect how teachers
perceive the profession, we believe that it would more directly affect
their satisfaction with the current working environment, which is
aligned with the theoretical expectation of increased intrinsic job
satisfaction as a result of engagement in PLCs.
3.4. Self-efficacy
For teacher efficacy, we use the TALIS scale ‘Self-efficacy in instruction’ (T3SEINS), which measures teachers’ beliefs that they can
vary their instructional strategies, craft good questions and use a variety
of assessment strategies. Teachers were asked “In your teaching, to what
extent can you do the following?” with one example item being “Provide
an alternative explanation, for example when students are confused”.
This scale is aligned with the PLC literature, which highlights the
importance of making sure that students actually learn what the teacher
intends.

3.2.1. Collaborative practice with focus on student learning (CPL)
The question capturing CPL was phrased as follows:
On average, how often do you engage in the following activities at
this school?
• Teach jointly as a team in the same class.
• Observe other teachers’ classes and provide feedback.
• Exchange teaching materials with colleagues.
• Engage in discussions about the learning development of specific
students.
• Work with other teachers in this school to ensure common standards
in evaluations for assessing student progress.
• Take part in collaborative professional learning.

3.9. Analytical approach
TALIS has a complex survey design and the data come with weights
to account for the unequal probability of the selection of participants
within each country. All our analyses are performed at the teacher level
and include the relevant weights in accordance with the TALIS 2018
user guide (OECD, 2019a). All preparation, analysis and visualisation of
data is done in R version 4.4.2, relying mainly on the packages ‘survey’
and ‘ggplot2’ (Lumley, 2004; R Core Team, 2022; Wickham, 2016). In
our pooled data analysis, we scaled the official teacher weights and
replicate weights into senate weights, which gives each country an equal
influence on the estimates. The estimates in the results section are from
generalised linear models that take into account the TALIS survey
design, using the ‘survey’ package. In the analyses comparing estimates
across countries, we apply Wald tests to determine whether the coefficients are significantly different (p < 0.05) from the median coefficient in each analysis. As a robustness check, we completed all analyses
with multiple imputation of missing data. The results based on multiple
imputation are presented in Appendix C and do not differ substantially
from the results presented below.
In line with most research on PLCs and recommendations by researchers (Lomos et al., 2011; Zheng et al., 2019), we analyse the relationship between each of the PLC subscales and the teacher outcomes
separately, as well as modelling the relationship between the overall
PLC measure and the three teacher outcomes. This allows us to assess
whether the strength of the relationship with the various outcomes
varies between different PLC subdimensions, thereby providing more
detailed insights.

4. Results

3.7. Data cleaning and preparation

4.1. Pooled data analysis

For some participating countries in TALIS 2018, all data were
missing for one or more variables that are included in our analysis. The
following countries were therefore excluded: Estonia, France, Hungary,
Mexico and Saudi Arabia did not have data on the PLC scales; Russia did
not have data on self-efficacy3; and Spain did not have data on teachers’
level of education. To ensure consistency across the pooled data analysis, countries with missing data for any key variables were excluded, as
including incomplete data would mean that the pooled analysis would
no longer represent the global perspective of the results presented in the
by-country analysis. As the variables of interest are all continuous, we
have standardised all these variables to have a mean of zero and a
standard deviation of one, allowing for interpretation of relationships as
standard deviations (SD). Our final dataset includes 127,339 teachers
from 40 countries, with an average of 188 schools per country and 17
teachers per school.

Using the pooled data to analyse the relationships between PLCs and
the three different outcomes, we find that there is a positive and statistically significant relationship between the PLC construct and each of
the three teacher outcomes (Fig. 1). The relationship is substantially
stronger between PLCs and job satisfaction (0.197 SD) than it is for selfefficacy (0.059–0.062 SD) and clarity of instruction (0.041–0.048 SD).
Looking at the three subdimensions of PLC separately, it is clear that
the strong and positive relationship between PLCs and job satisfaction is
primarily driven by the shared vision and responsibilities (SVR)
dimension (0.248 SD), and less so by the dimensions supportive conditions (SC) (0.131 SD) and collaborative practice with focus on student
learning (CPL) (0.076). We find that SC has a weak relationship with all
teacher outcomes, ranging from 0.022 to 0.131 SD. These results indicate that all dimensions of PLCs, as well as the overall measure, have

The relationship between PLCs and self-efficacy is positive and significant in 27 of the 40 countries included, while it is insignificant in the

5. Discussion
Our analysis shows some variations in the relationships between the
PLC constructs and the teacher outcomes across the countries included,
especially the relationships with self-efficacy and clarity of instruction.
In many cases, these relationships are very weak in magnitude and
statistically insignificant, while there are stronger and significant relationships in other countries. This suggests that the context may be an
important factor for the likelihood that PLCs will affect teaching practice
and self-efficacy beliefs. Differences in context may therefore, at least to
some extent, explain the inconsistent results in the existing literature on
the relationship between PLCs and self-efficacy. The relatively strong
and positive relationship between PLCs and job satisfaction is primarily
driven by the shared vision and responsibilities (SVR) And less so by the
other two subdimensions. This indicates that teachers’ perception of
sharing a vision and responsibilities with colleagues is strongly related
to their job satisfaction, while, on average, the frequency of collaborative activities as measured by CPL is less influential. The relationships
between CPL and our outcomes of interest are weak in all cases, but the
strongest relationship is with clarity of instruction. These findings align
with expectations, as both constructs - CPL and clarity of instruction –
are related to practical collaboration focused on instructional practices
and teachers’ evaluation of those practices. While the relationship between the CPL dimension and job satisfaction was rather weak in the
pooled data analysis, the same relationship is considerably stronger for
several countries when analysed separately (at or above 0.15 SD for
eight countries). This variance in results across countries potentially
points towards cultural differences in the appreciation and implementation of practical collaboration, as evidenced by the stronger
relationship between CPL and teacher job satisfaction in certain countries. Neither of the PLC constructs have a particularly strong relationship with self-efficacy, contrary to the theory-based expectation that
teachers who regularly cooperate on instructional matters and support
each other in solving issues will have a higher sense of self-efficacy. Our
results suggest that the expected positive outcomes of engagement in
PLCs for teachers may not be universal, with success possibly dependent
on contextual factors. However, we were not able to identify obvious
patterns that could explain the varying results of our countries, such
differences between western/non-western countries or OECD countries.
Another potential explanation for the disappointing results for the
relationship with self-efficacy and clarity of instruction is a superficial
implementation of PLCs, a phenomenon described as ‘PLC Lite’ by
DuFour and Reeves (2016), where schools claim to implement PLCs but
fail to embrace the full depth of the concept. If this should explain our
results however, it should be through the limitations of the measurement
of PLCs, or the possibility that the concept cannot live up to the expected
results. It is worth noting, however, that we found no statistically significant negative relationships in any of our analyses. which suggests
that PLCs, if anything, are positively related to the outcomes in our study
for the vast majority of teachers across contexts.
We find that the relationship between PLCs and job satisfaction is
considerably stronger and more stable across countries than for the relationships with the other two outcomes. While the ultimate aim of PLCs
is to increase student learning, and this ambition requires changes in
instructional practices, increasing teachers’ job satisfaction is important
in its own right, particularly in light of global teacher shortages and high
attrition rates (UNESCO, 2022). Multiple studies have established that
increasing teachers’ job satisfaction may reduce attrition (Madigan &
Kim, 2021), and engaging teachers in PLCs may therefore offer one
approach to attracting and keeping teachers in the profession. Given the
magnitude and stability of our results across countries and teacher
characteristics, it may be worthwhile to implement PLCs in schools. Our
results indicate that supporting a collaborative culture, sharing responsibility for school issues and providing staff with the opportunity to
participate in school decisions, as measured by the Shared vision and
responsibilities (SVR) construct, can be especially promising as ways of

4.3. Teacher characteristics
As a final step in our analysis, we investigate the degree to which the
positive relationships between PLCs and our teacher outcomes are
moderated by individual teacher characteristics, given that teacher
collaboration has been associated with negative outcomes for teachers
in some instances (Vangrieken et al., 2015). We answer this question by
segmenting the pooled data into subsets based on years of teaching
experience and gender, as well as combinations of the two. We assess the
relationships for teachers with less than three and more than twenty
years of teaching experience respectively. Our findings suggest that most
of the relationships we investigate are very stable across differences in
teaching experience and gender (Fig. 6). The most pronounced variation
is between male teachers at the extremes of teaching experience. It is for
these specific groups of teachers we find the weakest relationships and
also the only instances where we find insignificant results. Male teachers
with less than three years of experience have insignificant relationships
with Self-efficacy and Clarity of instruction in all cases. In sum, the relationships between PLCs and teacher outcomes are for the most part
very stable across differences in gender and experience, with only
certain groups of male teachers – especially those with less than three
years of experience – deviating somewhat from the norm.
Across these analyses, our main finding is that there is a quite strong
and robust relationship between PLCs and job satisfaction. For the
countries included, the overall measure, and especially the two subdimensions SVR and SC, has a strong and in almost all cases statistically
significant relationship with job satisfaction. On the other hand, we
generally do not find strong and convincing relationships between any
of the PLC constructs and the outcomes self-efficacy and clarity of instruction. For both of these outcomes, we found that, in many countries,
these relationships are not statistically significant and of negligible
magnitude, although there are exceptions in each case. While the relationship between the CPL dimension and job satisfaction had a coefficient of 0.077 in the pooled data analysis, the same relationship is
considerably stronger for a number of countries when analysed separately (at or above 0.15 SD for eight countries). Of the four PLC measures
applied, the frequency of collaborative activities as measured by CPL is
the strongest predictor of both self-efficacy and clarity of instruction,
although both relationships are considerably weaker than the relationship between our PLC constructs and teacher job satisfaction. Furthermore, these relationships are quite stable across teachers experience and
gender.

Comparing our findings to previous research, our results align with
the majority of studies investigating the relationship between PLCs and
teacher job satisfaction, such as Liang et al. (2022) and Yoo and Jang
(2022), who also found a positive relationship. When it comes to
self-efficacy, our results for the pooled international data show a weak
but positive and statistically significant relationship between each of our
PLC constructs and self-efficacy in instruction. For the countries individually, the results vary, both in terms of significance and magnitude,
depending on the PLC dimension and the country in question, thereby
further underlining that this relationship is more complex, as supported
by other studies such as Yada et al. (2023) and Zheng et al. (2021). Our
analysis of the relationship between PLCs and clarity of instruction
shows that, with a few exceptions, this relationship is small in magnitude and in many cases insignificant. The strongest relationships we find
are for the PLC dimension ‘Collaborative practice with a focus on student
learning’ (CPL), which makes sense as this dimension describes the
extent to which teachers collaborate concerning their instructional
practices, which gives them the opportunity to change/improve their
practice based on what happens in the classroom. Our results are quite
stable across teacher gender and experience, but we do find some
indication of weaker and insignificant relationships for less experienced
male teachers which connects to the findings of Valckx et al. (2020).
As we are using identical measures of PLCs across different countries
– an approach unique to this study – we can establish that the context
seems to be an important factor for the relationship between PLCs and
teachers’ self-efficacy and their instructional practices, suggesting that
the inconsistent results found in the existing literature are not exclusively due to differences in the applied measures. Furthermore, our
measures of PLC are developed as shared cluster constructs, which ensures that our measures reflect the teachers’ evaluation of the degree to
which the school operates as a PLC, as opposed to asking the principal if
the school is operating as a PLC, which may not align with the
perspective of the teachers. We suggest that future studies extend this
cross-national perspective on PLC outcomes to include student learning,
which is the main goal of implementing PLCs, or to include measures of
instructional quality that are less influenced by personal bias, such as
classroom observations or student evaluations. Policymakers may want
to pay close attention to evidence of the positive effects of PLCs in their
own country or similar contexts before investing in restructuring schools
into PLCs, especially when it comes to the aim of improving instructional
quality, as our findings show that the relationship between engagement
in PLCs and self-efficacy, as well as clarity of instruction, varies
considerably across countries.

Among the benefits of this design are that we have large and representative samples of teachers from a wide variety of countries, and that the
items we use are considered to have high validity and are statistically
comparable across the countries included. This enables us to assess these
relationships across a wide range of contexts using the same measures of
PLC, which overcomes the challenge of large differences in the measures
of PLCs applied in existing research.
Our results from analysing the pooled international data show a
positive relationship between PLCs and each of the three teacher outcomes included in our analysis, although the magnitude of these relationships varies considerably. Neither the overall measure of PLCs or
any of the three subdimensions individually have a particularly strong
relationship with self-efficacy or clarity of instruction in the pooled data
analysis (ranging from 0.022 to 0.068 SD), whereas the relationship
with job satisfaction is considerably stronger for the overall PLC measure (0.197 SD), for shared values and responsibilities (0.248 SD) and for
supportive conditions (0.131 SD). Although the magnitude of the relationships between our PLC constructs and self-efficacy and clarity of
instruction are weak in the pooled data analysis, we find examples of
stronger relationships for each of these outcomes when analysing
countries individually. For all relationships, we found cases where the
coefficients are significantly larger than the median, and other cases
where these corresponding relationships are non-existent and statistically insignificant. Our findings suggest that positive outcomes for
teachers engaging in PLCs are dependent on the context, and that the
inconsistent results in the existing literature may to some extent be
attributed to differences in the context in which they are applied. Our
results call for further studies into why these differences exist, and how
cultural and structural differences influence the functioning of PLCs
across the contexts in which they are applied. Factors such as pedagogical traditions, autonomy, and neoliberal pressures have yet to be
explored in relation to PLCs. This may be achieved either through
further analysis of the data applied here, by selecting cases to study in
detail and including detailed information about the contexts, or through
other qualitative or quantitative studies of PLCs and their expected
outcomes with a focus on contextual differences.

6. Conclusion
Our cross-country analysis of the relationships between PLCs and
different teacher outcomes has some limitations, mainly: 1) The data are
cross-sectional, which means that it is not possible to establish causal
relationships as it would be possible to using an experimental design. 2)
The PLC measures are schoolwide characteristics, which entails that the
sample size, especially in the country comparisons, has limited statistical
power due to the smaller number of schools included in each country. 3)
We do not know how recently the schools with high values on our PLC
constructs have begun functioning in this way. This could affect results,
as we would expect some lag between implementing PLC behaviours
and being able to observe potential effects on teacher outcomes. 4) We
