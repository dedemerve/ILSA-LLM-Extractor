ABSTRACT
As a key 21st-­century competence, creativity's assessment is gaining attention, especially with its inclusion in PISA tests. 
Understanding related social and educational factors is crucial. This study investigated how home and school creativity climates 
impact primary school children's creativity. Participants were 903 Spanish families attending 12 different schools. Parents/guard-
ians and teachers completed two questionnaires to determine the climate for creativity in the parent–child relationship and the 
environmental encouragement to creativity in schools. The creativity of 1052 children (6–13 years) was determined via a problem-­
finding and solving figural task. Results revealed mothers were more supportive of creative home environments; yet surprisingly, 
no significant correlation emerged between home creativity climate dimensions and children's creativity. Conversely, both cor-
relational and regression analyses demonstrated schools' crucial role in fostering creative achievements. Participation in educa-
tional innovation programs and using alternative methodologies significantly boosted student creativity. The study also found 
teachers generally held poor conceptualizations of creativity. These findings have important implications for promoting creativ-
ity in education.
1   |   Introduction
In the rapidly evolving landscape of the 21st century (marked 
by technological advancements, a shifting global economy, and 
uncertain environmental and health challenges), creativity is 
considered as one of the 4 Cs for developing successful global 
citizens (P21 2007). The other key skills include critical think-
ing, communication, and collaboration. As a result, fostering 
creativity in the educational system has become a priority, as 
developing creative thinking skills during the formative years is 
essential not only for children's personal growth but also for their 
future contributions to society. Research suggests that creativity 
in early and primary education can nurture problem-­solving 
abilities, encourage curiosity, and enhance cognitive flexibility, 
equipping children to adapt to and thrive in an unpredictable 
world (Runco 2003). However, the extent of these benefits may 
depend on factors such as instructional approaches, learning 
environments, and individual differences. As Beghetto  (2022) 
suggests, creativity should be unfrozen from rigid curriculum 
constraints and reframed as a fluid, central educational compe-
tency aligned with real-­world problem solving.
The debate on the importance of creativity in education has 
intensified in recent years. This is evident in the inclusion of

this research was conducted, the country's recent educational 
law aligns with this perspective, stating that “creativity will be 
worked on in all subjects” (LOMLOE 2020: 1222874).
The results of the PISA tests assessing 15-­year-­olds in Spain 
(OECD 2024) indicate a moderate level of creativity, with lower 
scores in certain geographical areas. The only identified expla-
nation for this pattern so far is the lower socio-­economic status 
of those regions. It is known that both individual factors (such as 
personality, intelligence or motivation) and environmental ones 
are key factors in children's creativity. Many studies have shown 
that creativity in childhood can be enhanced in a nurturing 
environment, but it can also be diminished in an unsupportive 
one. In this sense, both schools and families play a critical role in 
creativity by creating an intellectual climate that stimulates the 
creative potential of the individuals.
1.1   |   The Climate for Creativity at Home
Parental involvement in their children's education has been 
widely valued and encouraged in many societies, and different 
studies confirm the multiple benefits that parental educational 
commitment has on the development of their offspring: it in-
creases their academic performance (Boonk et al. 2018), favors 
academic adaptation, which in turn correlates with greater per-
formance, commitment, and motivation (Barger et  al.  2019), 
increases well-­being and mental health (Herke et  al.  2020), 
achieves higher self-­esteem (Batool  2020), or improves moti-
vation and establishes optimal emotional functioning (Cheung 
and Pomerantz 2012).
However, due to the difficulties in gathering the necessary 
data when working with families (Runco 2007), few empirical 
studies have been devoted to the family factors involved in cre-
ative development. Initially, these studies focused on the fam-
ily context of creativity, especially on celebrities, leaving aside 
the intentional and planned daily activities of parents to foster 
the creative skills and abilities of their offspring (Gralewski 
and Jankowska  2020; Kwaśniewska et  al.  2018). As pioneers, 
Harrington et  al.  (1987) conducted longitudinal studies with 
primary school students and their parents to corroborate Carl 
Rogers'  (1954) theory on the significant impact of parenting 
styles on creativity. Their findings indicated that supportive, 
autonomy-­encouraging parenting was positively associated 
with higher levels of creativity in children, whereas controlling 
or overly restrictive parenting tended to hinder creative devel-
opment. Increasing the creativity of boys and girls from child-
hood will impact their economic and social outcomes, but also 
their psychological well-­being (Conner et al. 2018; Forgeard and 
Eichner 2014).
Further research has highlighted the significant role of fam-
ily in shaping children's future creative potential. The creative 
competence of offspring could be predicted from parental be-
haviors, attitudes, and traits, through the so-­called “parenting 
styles” (Tang et al. 2022). Baumrind (1966) identified three par-
enting styles: authoritarian, authoritative or democratic, and 
permissive. Later, Maccoby and Martin  (1983) expanded this 
framework by dividing permissive parenting into two distinct 
styles: permissive (or indulgent) and neglectful (or indifferent). 
Each style is characterized by differences in setting limits and 
granting autonomy, reasoning norms and expectations, and 
controlling and providing emotional support (Yaffe 2014). The 
authoritarian parenting style is characterized by high demands 
and expectations, but little commitment and parental involve-
ment. The authoritative or democratic style balances sensitivity 
and warmth with clear limits and rules, using positive reinforce-
ment and reasoning to guide children. The permissive or indul-
gent style involves high responsiveness and emotional warmth 
but few rules or expectations, fostering strong parent–child con-
nections with minimal structure. At last, the negligent, indiffer-
ent, or uninvolved style is characterized by low responsiveness 
and engagement, with little or no demands or guidance. They 
tend to be negligent, indifferent, or careless parents, often preoc-
cupied with their own problems and unable to provide the emo-
tional and moral support their children need (Baumrind 1966; 
Maccoby and Martin 1983).
Most empirical studies reaffirm the negative impact of the au-
thoritarian parenting style on children's creativity, as it is re-
strictive and demanding while offering low responsiveness. 
Similarly, the negligent or indifferent style (characterized by low 
demands and low responsiveness) is also negatively related to 
creativity. In contrast, parenting styles that emphasize warmth 
and high responsiveness, such as permissive and authorita-
tive, show a positive correlation with creativity (Gralewski and 
Jankowska 2020; Jankowska and Karwowski 2019).
Recently, Kwaśniewska et al. (2018) introduced the concept of 
the “Climate for children's creativity in the family environment” 
to describe the consistent behavioral patterns parents adopt to 
foster their children's creativity. This involves creating a home 
environment that nurtures creativity through parental behav-
iors, attitudes, rules and routines. According to Kwaśniewska 
and Lebuda (2017), this creative climate integrates four catego-
ries of parental behaviors: encouraging experimentation with 
novelty and variety, encouraging nonconformity, inciting fan-
tasizing, and promoting persistence and dedication in creative 
efforts. However, recent meta-­analytic evidence shows that the 
relationship between parenting and creativity is highly context-­
dependent, with cultural and methodological factors affecting 
outcomes (Tang and Werner 2021).
1.2   |   The Climate for Creativity at School
While certain parenting styles positively influence children's 
creativity, some studies suggest that school environments can 
hinder creative development. The OECD's recent Teaching for 
Creativity report (2023) highlights how learning environments 
that encourage autonomy, risk-­taking, and collaboration are 
strongly associated with enhanced student creativity across di-
verse contexts. These findings parallel growing calls for schools 
to move beyond rigid structures and cultivate adaptive, student-­
centered pedagogies that prioritize learner agency and real-­
world problem-­solving (Lucas and Spencer 2023).
However, according to Runco et al. (2017), the structural organi-
zation of educational institutions tends to be restrictive, limiting 
the autonomy and independence essential for fostering students'

creativity. Abdulla Alabbasi et al. (2024) highlight discrepancies 
between the creativity students express inside and outside of 
school, with the latter being greater. This phenomenon, known 
as the “creativity gap,” reflects the difference between creativity 
at home and at school and is less pronounced in gifted students 
compared to their non-­gifted peers. Runco et al. (2017) raise the 
question of whether schools could take advantage of the poten-
tial resources that students use in contexts other than school to 
promote creativity within educational settings.
Numerous studies have examined the impact of schooling and 
different learning environments on creativity. Different ped-
agogical approaches and educational methods may influence 
creativity either directly or indirectly through motivation and 
well-­being. In a literature review, Thomas and Berk (1981) ex-
plored how different school environments affected children's 
creativity, but their findings were inconclusive. Their study 
highlighted the complex relationship between creativity devel-
opment and factors such as the type of educational system, gen-
der, and creativity type (verbal or figural). They concluded that 
an intermediate environment (one that is neither too structured 
nor too open or flexible) provides the most favorable conditions 
for fostering creativity. Recent research has highlighted the 
positive impact of alternative pedagogies that emphasize indi-
vidual initiative and action-­based learning, such as Montessori 
education at the primary level (Besançon and Lubart 2008) and 
Waldorf education at the secondary level (Besançon et al. 2015), 
on children's and adolescents' creativity. However, it is import-
ant to note that these studies did not explicitly control for other 
factors, such as socioeconomic status (SES) or neighborhood 
influences, which could also contribute to the development of 
creativity. Additionally, studies have shown that alternative 
schooling positively influences students' creative performance 
at the university entrance level (Gu et al. 2021); though similar 
concerns about the control of extraneous variables remain.
School climate, defined as all aspects of the school experience, 
including the quality of teaching and learning, school commu-
nity relationships, school organization, and the institutional 
and structural features of the school environment (Wang and 
Degol  2016) has a significant impact on the quality of stu-
dents' experiences and on their educational outcomes. Dudek 
et al. (1993) indicated that the school environment, particularly 
the classroom atmosphere, plays a far more crucial role in foster-
ing creative potential than previously recognized. More recent 
research has demonstrated, for instance, that school climate 
has a pivotal role in promoting creativity, mediated through 
teaching enthusiasm and teaching metacognition (Greenier 
et al. 2023). Additionally, studies have shown that the “coopera-
tion and intimacy” aspect of the classroom climate can directly 
predict student creativity (Chang et al. 2013), and that school 
climate has a direct and significant influence on students' trait 
creativity, mediated by both proactive personality and achieve-
ment motivation (Gao et al. 2020).
Teachers play a crucial role in either supporting, tolerating, or 
suppressing creativity in the classroom. By encouraging stu-
dents to take sensible risks, act independently, advocate for 
flexible thinking, and promote self-­confidence, teachers can 
significantly influence students' creative development (Gadja 
et  al.  2017). This support is particularly important, as many 
students tend to underestimate their own creative abilities 
(Beghetto et al. 2011). Without a responsive teaching style, char-
acterized by supportive and encouraging behaviors (Torrance 
and Myers 1970), students may struggle to develop their creativ-
ity. Zhang et al. (2020) found that teacher support was a signif-
icant predictor of key aspects of creativity such as fluency and 
originality. However, in classrooms where creative ideas and ex-
pressions could be killed softly (Beghetto 2013), it becomes dif-
ficult to develop and sustain students' creative behaviors. This 
challenge arises because teacher responsiveness can be linked 
to motivational aspects of creativity such as student motivation, 
confidence, and self-­efficacy (Zhang et al. 2021).
And lastly, the use of different teaching methods has also been 
shown to impact students' creativity. Problem-­based and project-­
based learning (Siew et  al.  2015; Albar and Southcott  2021) 
stimulate creative processes by fostering exploratory thinking, 
risk-­taking, experimentation, and resilience in students. For 
instance, combining possibility thinking and project-­based 
learning in a high school history course (Pan et al. 2023) was 
shown to significantly enhance students' creativity. Students 
in the experimental group outperformed those in the control 
group in all aspects of creativity assessed (fluency, flexibility, 
originality, and elaboration) and also in learning motivation 
(self-­efficacy). Inquiry-­based teaching, which includes explicit 
strategies to promote cognitive flexibility, is also believed to sup-
port creativity development (Dehaan 2009; Cremin et al. 2015). 
Jia et al. (2017) found that a mixed teaching approach, including 
both lecture-­based and inquiry-­based teaching, was more effec-
tive than using either method in isolation in students' creative 
problem finding performance. This mixed approach led to the 
most significant improvements in students' flexibility and origi-
nality in problem finding tasks, with significant interaction be-
tween teaching methods and instructional type (open vs. closed) 
in flexibility and originality.
1.3   |   The Present Study
Considering the significant roles of social environmental factors 
in children's creative development, this study investigates the 
impact of the climate for creativity within both the home and 
school contexts on the creative performance of primary educa-
tion children. A key objective of this research is to compare the 
influence of these two primary social environments. By exam-
ining the relationships between parental behaviors promoting 
creativity, school climate, and children's creative achievement, 
we aim to shed light on the relative importance of home and 
school in fostering creativity. We hypothesize that both positive 
home and school climates will be associated with higher cre-
ative performance.

mate for creativity at home; 967 responses were collected, from 
which 71.6% were from mothers, 19.1% from fathers, and 8.7%

A total of 1052 children, between 6 and 13 years of age, com-

textbooks, cooperative reading, dialogic literary gatherings, in-

Teachers working at all educational centers were asked to an-
swer a questionnaire to assess the support to creativity in their 
schools. 110 teachers participated in the study, from which 78.1% 
were female and 21.9% male, which corresponds to the popu-
lation of Primary Education teachers in Spain. The mean age 
of teachers was 44.63 years old, with a standard deviation of 
9.79 years.
2.2   |   Procedure
Before data collection began, we obtained consent from both 
the schools and parents. The school management teams, legal 
guardians, and participating students were thoroughly in-
formed about the research's scope and purpose, as well as the 
anonymization and data processing protocols. All procedures 
adhered to the guidelines set by the Ethics Committee of the 
University of Valencia.
The creativity questionnaires for children were administered on 
paper during a standard 45-­min class period in Spain. Both the 
classroom teacher and a researcher were present while students 
completed the task. Questionnaires for parents/legal guardians 
and teachers were also administered on paper, with no time 
limit for completion.
2.3   |   Instruments
Three previously validated questionnaires were utilized in this 
study: one to measure the climate for creativity at home, another 
to assess teachers' perceptions of support for creativity in educa-
tional centers, and a third to evaluate children's creativity.
2.3.1   |   Climate for Creativity at Home
The Climate for Creativity in Parent–Child Relationship 
Questionnaire (CCP-­CRQ) (Kwaśniewska et al. 2018) was used 
to assess the fostering of creativity at home. It is defined as “par-
ents overall relatively constant behavioral pattern that helps the 
child acquire a mindset, attitudes, personal qualities, and skills 
necessary for creativity” (Kwaśniewska et  al.  2018, 14). This 
24-­item instrument utilizes a seven-­point Likert scale to mea-
sure four dimensions of parental behaviors that promote a cre-
ative climate: encouragement to experience novelty and variety 
(EENV) (i.e., “I try to suggest to my child unconventional ways 
to solve problems”), encouragement of nonconformism (ENC) 
(i.e., “It is important to me that my child is always being polite”), 
support of perseverance in creative efforts (SPCE) (i.e., “I always 
value my child's ideas even if they are far from perfection”), and 
encouragement to fantasize (EF) (i.e., “I encourage my child to 
fantasize”). Some of the items were reverse-­coded. The values 
of internal consistency of the Spanish translation determined 
by Cronbach's alpha were acceptable for all dimensions of the 
questionnaire: encouragement to experience novelty and vari-
ety (α = 0.703), encouragement of nonconformism (α = 0.702), 
support of perseverance in creative efforts (α = 0.806), and en-
couragement to fantasize (α = 0.743). The questionnaire also in-
cluded demographic questions such as the respondent's identity 
(e.g., mother, father), age of respondent(s), and number of sib-
lings in the household.
2.3.2   |   Climate for Creativity at School
Teachers' perceptions regarding support for creativity in their 
schools were assessed using the Teaching for Creativity Scales 
(Rubenstein et al. 2013). This 36-­item questionnaire, employing 
a 5-­point Likert scale, comprises four sub-­dimensions: teacher 
self-­efficacy, environmental encouragement, societal value, 
and student potential. For the present study, only the six-­item 
Environmental Encouragement (EE) subscale was used. This 
subscale included both direct and reverse-­coded items, such as: 
“My administration encourages me to foster innovative think-
ing in my students” (D) or “My current school environment 
places little value on the development of student creativity” (R). 
The internal consistency of the Spanish translation for the envi-
ronmental encouragement dimension was good, as determined 
by Cronbach's alpha (α = 0.825).
2.3.3   |   Children's Creativity
To assess the creativity of the children, the Child Creativity Test 
(TCI) (Romo et  al.  2008) was used. This instrument is based 
on Getzels and Csikszentmihalyi's  (1976) problem discovery 
model, evaluating variables related to problem identification, 
formulation, and solution. Validated for a Spanish sample, the 
TCI boasts high validity, reliability, and robust psychometric 
properties (Romo et al. 2016). As a figurative task, the TCI is 
well-­suited for younger children who may not yet have mastered 
reading and writing but are comfortable with drawing. The test 
comprises two phases: “Problem formulation,” in which chil-
dren create a model using stickers with familiar figures, and 
“Problem solution,” in which children produce a drawing based 
on the initial model. A key advantage of the TCI is its presen-
tation as a recreational activity, which considers all phases of 
the creative process, not just the final outcome. Several variables 
are assessed: originality and atypical manipulation in the first 
phase, and material change, interaction, verbal elements, differ-
ences from the initial model, and invented added figures in the 
second phase. The total test score is the sum of these variables, 
providing a direct score. This direct score is then converted into

2.4   |   Statistical Analysis
The data analysis was carried out using SPSS v28 and a signifi-
cance level of p = 0.05.
First, descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, skew-
ness, and kurtosis) for the studied variables (EENV, ENC, 
SPCE, EF, EE and creativity score) were calculated. Variables 
with skewness less than 2 and kurtosis less than 3 were deemed 
normally distributed. According to the results, only SPCE was 
considered non-­normally distributed (skewness = −2.707; kurto-
sis = 13.586). Thus, parametric tests were applied, and the t-­test 
for independent samples was used to assess differences based 
on gender (Mann–Whitney U test for independent samples was 
used for SPCE). The Friedman test for paired samples was used 
to assess differences between variables. Levene's test showed 
that the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated 
for ENC [F(11, 1040) = 1.916, p = 0.034], EF [F(11, 1040) = 2.143, 
p = 0.015], and creativity score [F(11, 1040) = 1.923, p = 0.033]. 
So, a one-­way ANOVA test was used to assess differences based 
on educational center for EENV and EE; Welch's ANOVA was 
conducted for ENC, EF, and creativity score, and the Kruskal–
Wallis test was used for SPCE. Then, a post hoc analysis was 
performed with the Bonferroni adjustment to contrast the exis-
tence of significant differences through multiple comparisons. 
When significant differences were observed, effect sizes were 
calculated via Hedges' g. The correlation between variables was 
studied by Spearman's correlation coefficient. To examine the 
relative influence of the different variables on students' creativ-
ity, a theory-­driven approach with forced entry in a hierarchi-
cal multiple regression analysis was conducted. The analysis 
proceeded in two sequential blocks. In the first block, dummy 
variables representing the students' educational center and the 
environmental encouragement perceived by teachers were en-
tered to control for school-­level variance. The second block in-
cluded the four variables measuring the home creativity climate 
(EENV, ENC, SPCE, and EF). The change in R2 was examined 
at each step to determine the unique variance explained by each 
set of predictors. Standardized beta coefficients and their signifi-
cance were used to interpret the relationship between individual 
predictors and student creativity in the final model.

The relationship between the number of siblings and the differ-

negative correlation between SPCE and the number of siblings, 
r(1020) = −0.073, p = 0.019.
To check whether the climate for creativity at home was re-

different socio-­economic areas and Jankowska et al. (2024) in-

couragement to nonconformism F(11, 1051) = 2.767, p = 0.002, 
and encouragement to fantasize F(11, 1051) = 2.144, p = 0.015. 
However, post hoc analysis showed that these differences were 
only found between centers 2 and 9 (p = 0.005) and 2 and 12 
(p = 0.007) for ENC and between centers 5 and 9 (p = 0.032) for 
EF. Additionally, Spearman correlation coefficients between 
educational centers and all four dimensions of the CCP-­CRQ 
questionnaire varied between r = −0.042 and r = 0.045, with no 
significant correlation found in any of the cases.
The results for the climate for creativity at school, measured as 
the environmental encouragement perceived by teachers, are 
shown in Table 4. All centers were perceived as having a good 
environment for creativity development, with values above 3.5 
(of 5) in all cases except for school 2. However, statistically sig-
nificant differences were found between them; F(11, 98) = 1.500, 
p = 0.141. In fact, post hoc analysis showed differences between 
all of them, with p values < 0.001.

3.4   |   Regression Analysis
To further explore these results, a regression analysis for the re-
sults of the children's creativity was carried out. In the initial 
analytical plan, a hierarchical regression was considered to ex-
amine the unique contributions of educational center, teacher 
perception, and home creativity climate to students' creativity. 
However, preliminary analyses revealed that teacher percep-
tion of environmental encouragement (EE), when entered after 
the educational center variables, accounted for a negligible and 
non-­significant increase in explained variance (ΔR2 = 0.000, 
p = 0.978). This finding suggests that the variance explained by 
teachers' general perception of environmental encouragement 
substantially overlaps with or is largely absorbed by the variance 
associated with the specific educational centers themselves. 
To maintain parsimony and avoid redundancy in the model's 
structure, we therefore opted to include teacher perception (EE) 
in the first step of the regression model alongside the educational 
center dummy variables. School 10 was selected as the reference 
category for the educational center variable. This decision was 
based on its identification as the school with the smallest sample 
size, coupled with its relatively low mean creativity score (32.29) 
and the fact that it represents the modal characteristics in terms 
of type, location, and innovation projects (see Table 1).
The first block, comprising the educational center variables 
and teacher perception (EE), significantly predicted students' 
creativity, explaining 3.8% of the variance [R2 = 0.038, F(12, 
1039) = 3.375, p < 0.001]. The addition of the four home creativ-
ity climate variables (EENV, ENC, SPCE and EF) in the second 
block did not significantly improve the model [ΔR2 = 0.002, F 
change(4, 1034) = 0.658, p = 0.606], with the final model explaining 
4.0% of the variance [R2 = 0.040, F(16, 1035) = 2.679, p < 0.001].
The coefficients for both models are presented in Table 6. In Step 
1, several educational centers showed significant effects on stu-
dent creativity when compared to School 10. Specifically, school 
5 (B = 11.153, p = 0.044), school 6 (B = 9.712, p = 0.025), school 
7 (B = 10.135, p = 0.002), and school 11 (B = 13.093, p < 0.001) 
showed significantly higher creativity scores. Teacher percep-
tion of environmental encouragement did not emerge as a sta-
tistically significant predictor in this step (B = 0.124, p = 0.978). 
The variance inflation factor of 5.802 for EE indicates a mod-
erate level of collinearity with the school dummy variables. In 
Step 2, the four dimensions of parental behaviors (EENV, ENV, 
SPCE, and EF) were entered into the model. The inclusion of 
these variables did not lead to a statistically significant increase 
in the explained variance. None of these parental behavior di-
mensions were found to be statistically significant predictors of 
children's creativity (all p values > 0.350, with B values ranging 
from −0.321 to 1.433). The VIF values for these parental behav-
ior variables were well below 2.5 (ranging from 1.083 to 2.388), 
suggesting no problematic multicollinearity among them. The 
significant effects for the educational centers from Step 1 re-
mained consistent in Step 2.

personality and potential. Kwaśniewska et  al.  (2018) sug-
gested that parents may enhance their children's creativity by 
encouraging imaginative thinking and exploration of new and 
diverse experiences, which are essential for fostering a cre-
ative parent–child environment.
However, the present study's results do not support this the-
ory, as no significant correlation was found between chil-
dren's creative performance and any of the components of the 
home creativity climate (i.e., encouragement to experience 
novelty and variety, encouragement of nonconformism, sup-
port of perseverance in creative efforts, or encouragement to 
fantasize). Interestingly, significant positive correlations were 
observed among these different questionnaire dimensions, 
implying that parents who value creativity and cultivate a 
creative home environment are more inclined to support their 
children's creativity, consistent with research by Pugsley and 
Acar (2018). Consequently, a fundamental question arises: is 
there genuinely no correlation between the perceived home 
creativity climate and actual creative output, or is the rela-
tionship mediated primarily by broader parenting style or 
general parental support, or alternatively, does a correlation 
exist that is obscured by potential inaccuracies in parents' 
self-­perceptions regarding their home's creative climate? The 
social desirability of the responses (Rosenman et al. 2011) may 
have taken place with creativity currently in the spotlight, 
even if the introduction to the questionnaire clearly stated that 
there were no right or wrong answers, but different ways to 
understand the parent–child relationship. In addition, parents 
might present an idealized version of their parenting prac-
tices. They might report behaviors as they believe they should 
ideally occur, rather than how they consistently do occur in 
daily interactions. This could lead to an overestimation of the 
frequency or intensity of creativity-­promoting behaviors. Also, 
responses are based on recollection, which can be imperfect or 
influenced by recent events, potentially not reflecting long-­
term patterns of behavior.
Beyond the specific dimensions examined in this study, other 
unmeasured aspects of the parent–child relationship may also 
influence children's creative development. For instance, re-
search postulates that parental involvement characterized 
by high performance expectations and excessive external re-
straints could diminish children's creativity (Jankowska and 
Karwowski  2019). Conversely, parental involvement marked 
by a high acceptance of performance outcomes is thought to 
enhance it (Fan and Zhang 2014). Zheng et al. (2019) further 
explained that an optimal level of parental involvement in 
school-­related activities is beneficial for creativity development; 
yet both excessive and insufficient involvement in daily life af-
fairs may diminish it. Furthermore, the impact of parental in-
volvement on creative thinking appears contingent on student 
motivation: it may diminish creative thinking in students with 
low autonomous motivation, whereas facilitating it in those with 
high autonomous motivation (Liu et al. 2013).
Within the family unit, mothers and fathers often adopt distinct 
roles, leading to variations in parenting styles that can depend 
on both parent and child gender. Although empirical research 
on this topic remains somewhat scarce (Mastrotheodoros 
et al. 2019), a systematic review by Yaffe (2023) indicates that 
mothers tend to be characterized as more understanding, re-
sponsive, and supportive, as well as more supervisory, demand-
ing, and inclined to grant autonomy compared to fathers. This 
suggests that mothers may predominantly align with an au-
thoritative parenting style, whereas fathers might lean toward 
an authoritarian approach. Consistent with this, Kwaśniewska 
et al. (2018) assert that mothers frequently prioritize fostering 
a supportive climate for children's creative development. They 
achieve this through specific personality traits, including emo-
tional stability, support for perseverance, conscientiousness, 
agreeableness, and extroversion. These traits are linked to a 
mother's propensity to encourage a child's pursuit of innova-
tion and diversity. However, it is noted that highly meticu-
lous mothers may be less inclined to encourage questioning or 
rule-­breaking. In a similar vein, Mastrotheodoros et al. (2019) 
confirm that mothers typically provide a higher average level 
of support (encompassing love, warmth, and respect for their 
children's identity and idiosyncrasies) during childhood than fa-
thers. This level of support from both parents, however, tends to 
decrease from early to mid-­adolescence before stabilizing.
The findings of the present study corroborate these prior ob-
servations, as mothers scored significantly higher than fathers 
across all dimensions of the home creativity climate. Specifically, 
these observed differences were statistically significant for 
Encouragement to Experience Novelty and Variety (EENV) 
and Support of Perseverance in Creative Efforts (SPCE). This 
indicates that mothers in our sample demonstrated a greater 
willingness to expose their children to new situations, encour-
age unconventional thinking and action, and provide sustained 
support for creative activities, including appreciating their ideas 
and motivating them to persist even in the face of failure.
On the other hand, the influence of school on students' creativ-
ity was determinant. The climate for creativity, as perceived 
by teachers, demonstrated a statistically significant positive 
relationship with children's creative performance. Despite this 
significance, the correlation coefficient indicates a weak effect 
size, suggesting that while school climate does contribute to cre-
ativity, its isolated impact on children' creativity performance 
is limited, since in the regression model it explains 3.8% of the 
variance. This highlights the multifactorial nature of creativity, 
implying that schools seeking to foster creativity should shift 
focus to concrete, actionable pedagogical strategies. A partic-
ularly interesting finding from our study was a perceived par-
adox regarding innovative schools. Teachers from traditional 
schools reported higher levels of environmental encouragement 
for creativity compared to teachers in schools using innovative 
approaches. This counter-­intuitive result may indicate a dis-
crepancy between the theoretical aims of “innovative meth-
odologies” and their practical implementation or perceived 
effectiveness in fostering a creativity-­supportive environment. 
More critically, this disparity could suggest a varied or even 
limited conceptualization of creativity and its promotion among 
some primary education teachers, particularly when integrating 
new educational paradigms.

Our results indicate that the consistent implementation of inno-
vative methodologies within schools was a strong predictor of 
primary students' creativity. Specifically, schools participating 
in European educational innovation projects achieved superior 
creativity outcomes, demonstrating statistically significant differ-
ences in creative achievement when compared to more traditional 
centers. The adoption of approaches such as a reduced reliance 
on textbooks, a commitment to transdisciplinary learning, and 
the use of student-­centered techniques like project-­based learning 
and cooperative learning appear to have significantly impacted 
children's creativity. This positive effect might stem from an in-
creased focus on individual initiative and action-­based learning 
in these schools, or perhaps from the higher motivation these 
approaches elicit in students. Regardless of direct involvement 
in specific innovative projects, the school environment itself 
emerged as a clear predictor of children's creativity, as robustly 
demonstrated by our regression analysis. Several factors likely 
contribute to this influence, including distinct school identities 
and climate, the presence of particularly skilled teachers, and 
the school's ability to boost student motivation, confidence, or 
self-­efficacy in creative endeavors. The availability and variety of 
after-­school activities offered by a school could also be a contrib-
uting factor, given that Liang et al. (2021) showed participation 
in such activities plays a critical role in creativity development. 
Ultimately, student creativity is shaped by a complex interplay of 
various elements: the methodologies employed, the overarching 
school climate, classroom atmosphere, the pivotal role of teach-
ers, and even the specific type of creativity being fostered. Further 
research is essential to fully understand the relative weight and 
intricate relationships among these numerous factors.
5   |   Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations of this study should be considered when in-
terpreting our results and conclusions. First, while we collected 
data from a considerable sample of families and schools across 
various socio-­economic backgrounds, it was a convenience sam-
ple with a disproportionate representation of rural over urban 
schools. Consequently, our findings may not be representative 
of Spain as a whole, and caution should be exercised when gen-
eralizing these results to other regions of the country or different 
countries.
Second, we measured children's creative performance using a sin-
gle instrument: the Children's Creativity Test (Romo et al. 2008). 
This test, based on Getzels and Csikszentmihalyi's (1976) prob-
lem discovery model, possesses high validity, reliability, and 
robust psychometric properties for a Spanish sample. However, 
it is a figurative task specifically designed for younger children 
and does not assess other creative processes like verbal creativ-
ity or divergent thinking. Therefore, our results are exclusively 
applicable to creative achievement as measured by this particu-
lar instrument. Future studies would benefit from incorporat-
ing a broader range of creativity tests to capture diverse creative 
outcomes.
Third, the social environmental variables included in this study, 
specifically the climate for creativity at home and at school, are 
far from exhaustive. We did not examine other factors that could 
potentially contribute to students' creative outcomes, such as 
those related to motivation or broader parenting styles. Although 
not a primary focus of the current study, future research will 
aim for a more comprehensive analysis of the school climate and 
the specific methodologies employed in various educational cen-
ters to identify key aspects that influence student creativity. To 
achieve this, semi-­structured interviews and focus groups with 
parents and teachers, with particular attention given to those 
leading work in innovative schools, would be highly beneficial. 
Additionally, exploring how the school and home climates inter-
act, rather than treating them as separate factors, could provide 
valuable insights into their combined influence on creativity. 
This integrated approach could enrich our understanding of the 
dynamic relationship between these two critical environments 
and their collective impact on fostering creativity in students.
Fourth, both the climate for creativity at home and the climate 
for creativity at schools were assessed through self-­reported 
questionnaires completed by parents and teachers, respectively. 
Although these instruments offer numerous advantages, they 
are also susceptible to biases and limitations inherent in self-­
report data, such as issues related to honesty, introspective 
ability, and social desirability, all of which could influence the 
findings (Rosenman et al. 2011).

schools demonstrating robust positive effects. However, a gen-

development is not solely driven by isolated factors but by a 
complex interplay of specific institutional characteristics and 
potentially unmeasured or more nuanced aspects of home and 
school environments.
Given the significant role schools play in fostering children's cre-
ativity, our findings highlight two primary areas for interven-
tion. First, it is crucial to enhance teachers' understanding and 
awareness of creativity. These programs should aim to broaden 
teachers' understanding of creativity, emphasizing its transdis-
ciplinary nature and relevance across all subjects (e.g., in math-
ematics, science, and social studies). They should also equip 
teachers with concrete, actionable pedagogical strategies to 
cultivate creativity in their classrooms, moving beyond general 
encouragement and providing examples of effective transdisci-
plinary practices that actively stimulate creativity. These train-
ing initiatives should also be integrated into Teacher Training 
Faculties at the university level. There is a timely opportunity 
in Spain to include creativity in the curriculum, as a syllabus 
revision is underway, and creativity is currently not a dedicated 
subject, unlike reading comprehension or scientific reasoning.
Second, our study underscores the importance of enhancing the 
overall school climate for creativity. Although our regression 
analysis showed that a general measure of teacher perception of 
environmental encouragement didn't uniquely predict creativity 
beyond specific school effects, the significant and unique con-
tributions of several individual schools demonstrate that certain 
school environments are indeed yielding positive results for cre-
ative achievement. This indicates that it's not simply “innovative 
approaches” in general, but rather specific, effective institutional 
cultures, leadership, or programs within these high-­performing 
schools that warrant further investigation. Therefore, we rec-
ommend promoting a comprehensive Creative School Plan as a 
framework document for creativity in schools, mirroring Spain's 
existing Reading School Plan for reading comprehension. Such 
a plan would serve to strategically plan and harmonize efforts to 
foster creativity across the entire school. It would also support 
the design of highly divergent, interdisciplinary projects such as 
combining mathematics and literature, or music and natural sci-
ences (Botella Nicolás et al. 2025) and promote the integration of 
diverse artistic expressions and encourage initiatives like ideas/
inventions contests. Implementing a Creative School Plan re-
quires greater awareness of creativity among both teachers and 
school leaders. Fortunately, the current educational discourse 
on creativity, partly driven by PISA tests, presents a favorable 
context for its inclusion and promotion in Spanish educational 
policy and practice.
Numerous factors within the school environment can influence 
creativity, including methodologies, school climate, teacher 
practices, and student motivation. This highlights the impor-
tance of identifying effective combinations of these elements to 
maximize creative development in primary education children. 
This calls for a shift towards more descriptive and transversal 
research, moving beyond the prevalent focus on case studies in-
volving special education students (especially those with high 
abilities). Although reading comprehension is widely recognized 
as an indicator of academic, professional, and socio-­personal 
success, it is equally crucial to recognize that the ability to solve 
diverse problems creatively is a talent that demands attention 
and cultivation, extending far beyond the domains of art or cre-
ative genius. Empirical studies are, therefore, essential not only 
for evaluating the current state of creativity in educational sys-
tems but also for validating the instructional changes needed by 
21st-­century competencies.
