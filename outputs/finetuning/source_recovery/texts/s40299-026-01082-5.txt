Abstract
Teachers’ beliefs regarding AI-integrated teaching influence the intelligent application of education, yet there remains a 
gap in understanding the roles of leadership support and psychological factors. Guided by the Conservation of Resources 
Theory, this study examines how distributed leadership affects the AI teaching beliefs of junior high school teachers in 
Vietnam. Using data from TALIS 2024, a chain mediation model was established to investigate the mediating effects 
of teacher stress and teaching enjoyment. The results show that distributed leadership not only directly and positively 
predicts teachers’ AI teaching beliefs but also exerts an indirect influence through the pathway of stress reduction and 
enhanced teaching enjoyment. A key finding is that teacher stress itself is not significantly directly associated with AI 
teaching beliefs, whereas teaching enjoyment plays a central mediating role. This reveals that, in the context of educa­
tional intelligence transformation, distributed leadership primarily motivates teachers to embrace AI teaching innovation 
by cultivating their positive emotional capital, rather than merely reducing their workload. The findings underscore the 
importance of establishing distributed leadership structures and supportive emotional environments in schools, providing 
empirical evidence for educational policymakers and school administrators to promote sustainable intelligent education 
transformation through leadership development and emotional support.

refer to the set of cognitions, attitudes, and evaluations that 
teachers hold regarding the application of AI technology in

instructional practices (Prestridge et al., 2025). Positive 
pedagogical beliefs can motivate teachers to proactively 
explore and integrate AI tools, such as utilizing AI to design 
personalized learning materials tailored to students’ needs, 
thereby effectively enhancing students’ learning interest and

In the process of digital teaching transformation, teach­
ers’ pedagogical beliefs are not only influenced by their 
individual digital competence as an intrinsic resource but 
are also closely related to the resource support provided 
by their organizational environment (Chen et al., 2025). 
Among the many organizational factors affecting teachers’ 
pedagogical beliefs, distributed leadership theory offers 
a highly explanatory perspective. Distributed leadership 
emphasizes that leadership functions are no longer confined 
to principals alone but are distributed through dynamic 
interactions among organizational members, tools, and con­
texts (Hartley, 2007). By fostering power-sharing, collective 
decision-making, and teacher empowerment, it cultivates a 
collaborative, trusting, and supportive organizational cul­
ture (Bellibaş et al., 2021). Within such a collective envi­
ronment, distributed leadership can effectively alleviate the 
uncertainty and anxiety teachers experience when confront­
ing new technologies. School administrators, by providing 
teachers with autonomy and collective support, directly or 
indirectly enhance their professional capital and strengthen 
their confidence in adopting technology for teaching (Teng 
et al., 2024).
According to Hazzan-Bishara et al., the influence of 
distributed leadership on teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for 
AI is not a simple direct causal relationship but a decision-
making process shaped by both internal and external factors 
(Hazzan-Bishara et al., 2025). Among these, teacher stress 
arising from the work environment negatively impacts 
teachers’ physical and psychological well-being (Reinke 
et al., 2025). When teachers are consistently confronted 
with heavy workloads, limited resources, and ambiguous 
role expectations, their cognitive resources and emotional 
energy become depleted (Wang, 2025). In such a state, even 
if schools provide professional development opportuni­
ties and technical support, teachers may avoid engaging in 
AI teaching that requires additional resources due to self-
protective motivations (Funa & Gabay, 2025). In contrast, 
enjoyment of teaching is one of the most frequent and sig­
nificant emotions experienced by teachers, serving as a fun­
damental psychological drive that motivates them to engage 
in educational reforms and explore new knowledge and 
technologies (Trigwell, 2012). The empowering environ­
ment created by distributed leadership can effectively stim­
ulate teachers’ curiosity and desire to explore AI teaching. 
This intrinsic interest, in turn, helps transform external lead­
ership support into positive personal beliefs (Wang & Bai, 
2025). However, few studies have examined the impact of 
teacher stress and enjoyment of teaching on teachers’ peda­
gogical beliefs, particularly teachers’ pedagogical beliefs 
for AI—from the perspective of distributed leadership.
Vietnam, as an emerging economy actively embracing 
digital transformation among developing countries, has 
incorporated information technology and artificial intel­
ligence education into the core agenda of its educational 
modernization strategy in recent years, driven by national 
policies (Phuong et al., 2025). However, a notable gap per­
sists: while research on teachers’ AI pedagogical beliefs and 
professional development is gradually increasing globally, 
non-Western contexts, especially Vietnam, have rarely been 
included within the scope of empirical studies focusing on 
AI pedagogical beliefs (Hoang, 2025). Therefore, focusing 
on Vietnamese teachers will not only help fill the regional 
gap in AI teaching research, but also reveal how distributed 
leadership influences teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI 
through relevant variables such as teacher stress and enjoy­
ment of teaching within a centralized educational culture. 
This study aims to address the following two research 
questions:
Q1	 Can distributed leadership enhance teachers’ pedagogi­
cal beliefs for AI?
Q2	 What impacts do teacher stress and enjoyment of teach­
ing have on teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI?
Literature Review and Research Hypotheses
Theoretical Basis
For the sustainable development of schools, teachers are the 
core human capital and invaluable assets for achieving high-
quality education. Conservation of Resources (COR) theory 
posits that individuals have a fundamental motivation to 
acquire, retain, and protect their important resources (Hob­
foll, 1989). This theory particularly emphasizes three core 
elements: loss priority, resource investment, and resource 
contextuality (Hou & Tao, 2023). Loss priority suggests 
that the psychological impact of resource loss is far greater 
than that of resource gain, which is especially evident when 
teachers face pressures from change, making resource pres­
ervation, particularly alleviating teacher stress—a primary 
measure (Zhai, 2025). Resource investment indicates that 
teachers need to invest existing resources to acquire new

ones, providing a rationale for introducing organizational 
resources such as distributed leadership to support teach­
ers in coping with change (Ding et al., 2025). Resource 
contextuality explains that the acquisition and preserva­
tion of resources are influenced by the work environment, 
highlighting the critical role of the school’s organizational 
setting in teachers’ resource management (Zhang & Zheng, 
2020). Therefore, for schools to achieve sustainable devel­
opment, they must value teachers’ professional growth. At 
the institutional level, it is essential to prioritize investment 
in and optimize the organizational support system within 
the resource context, thereby safeguarding teachers’ profes­
sional needs and inspiring their initiative and creativity in 
responding to change.
Based on the COR theory, distributed leadership can 
be regarded as a crucial resource at the organizational 
level (Dongxian & Batool, 2024). Through mechanisms of 
empowerment and collaboration, it alleviates teacher stress 
and protects their existing resources on one hand, while 
enhancing their professional autonomy and teaching effi­
cacy to promote the accumulation of positive resources on 
the other (Yao & Ma, 2024). This dual pathway of resource 
preservation and enhancement creates favorable condi­
tions for teachers to explore and adopt emerging teaching 
tools such as AI (Ding et al., 2025). In other words, from 
a resource conservation perspective, distributed leadership 
provides vital external support for teachers to maintain and 
expand their psychological resources in the context of AI-
driven educational transformation (Wang & Bai, 2025). 
This implies that schools, by fostering a resource-rich envi­
ronment through distributed leadership, can effectively 
mitigate anxiety and resistance among teachers during the 
digital transformation of education, shifting them from pas­
sive adaptation to proactive exploration, thereby shaping 
their pedagogical beliefs for AI.
AI Teaching among Vietnamese Educators
Vietnam’s teacher training system emphasizes the develop­
ment of teaching skills and pedagogical practical knowledge 
(Nguyen et al., 2025). Through continuous professional 
development programs, the Ministry of Education and Train­
ing encourages teachers to engage in self-directed learning 
and reflection—an initiative that has effectively promoted 
the growth of their teaching competence and reflective 
awareness (Nguyen et al., 2020). Within a collective culture 
that values reflective practice, a potential foundation is laid 
for teachers to adopt and integrate AI teaching technologies.
Currently, supported by the government’s firm com­
mitment to education and steadily increasing investments, 
Vietnam is witnessing three emerging trends: technology 
integration, pedagogical innovation, and lifelong learning. 
Together, these forces are driving the transformation of the 
educational landscape (Le et al., 2024). In 2022, the Min­
istry of Education and Training of Vietnam set clear digi­
tal transformation goals for the education sector, including: 
developing interactive smart classroom systems and mod­
ern laboratories, establishing simulation labs, and organi­
cally integrating cutting-edge technologies such as virtual 
reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), machine learning, big 
data analytics, and artificial intelligence in both research 
and practice (Tuong et al., 2023). These goals impose new 
demands on teachers’ roles and beliefs. Teachers are no lon­
ger merely knowledge transmitters but must also become 
flexible users of technology and lifelong learners (Chen et 
al., 2025).
However, existing research indicates that Vietnamese 
teachers’ belief systems exhibit contradictions when con­
fronting AI technology. While they generally recognize the 
educational potential of AI, targeted interventions in infra­
structure development and organizational support remain 
insufficient (Hoang, 2025). Therefore, delving into the 
external organizational factors and internal mechanisms 
that shape Vietnamese teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI 
is crucial for advancing the success of Vietnam’s digital 
transformation in education.
Distributed Leadership and Teachers’ Pedagogical 
Beliefs for Technology
At the heart of distributed leadership lies empowerment 
and shared responsibility, challenging traditional hierar­
chical models by emphasizing collective accountability 
and collaborative decision-making (Zaghmout & Harrison, 
2025). Unlike the top–down approach to technology imple­
mentation common in traditional bureaucratic leadership, 
distributed leadership represents the art of involving both 
formal and informal school leaders alongside teachers in the 
school decision-making process. As the primary educational 
professionals within the school organization, school lead­
ers should proactively establish collaborative partnerships 
with IT specialists and other colleagues, seeking necessary 
support and professional guidance to promote the effective 
integration of teaching technologies (Theodorio, 2024). 
This participatory process itself constitutes a journey of co-
constructing shared beliefs (Spillane, 2005). Research by 
Zhao et al. indicates that distributed leadership, by build­
ing a shared and internalized vision for technology appli­
cation, effectively translates organizational objectives into 
teachers’ personal values and beliefs, thereby strengthening 
their intrinsic motivation to proactively explore and utilize 
technology (Zhao et al., 2025). Consequently, this study

The Mediating Role of Enjoyment of Teaching
Enjoyment of teaching refers to the positive emotional 
experiences—such as pleasure, satisfaction, and intrinsic 
fulfillment—that teachers derive from their instructional 
activities (Russo et al., 2020). Enjoying teaching can posi­
tively influence students’ learning engagement, inspire 
teachers’ desire to enhance instructional quality, and pro­
tect them from burnout and attrition (Frenzel et al., 2016). 
Research by Xiao et al. indicates that teachers with high 
levels of teaching enjoyment typically demonstrate stron­
ger teaching efficacy, greater willingness to innovate, and 
higher openness (Xiao et al., 2022). Teachers with high 
enjoyment of teaching are more willing to experiment with 
AI as an empowering tool to realize this educational phi­
losophy. When facing AI as a powerful teaching tool, teach­
ers are more inclined to view the advent of AI technology 
as an opportunity to provide adaptive support for individual 
students and to engage in pedagogical innovation (Chen et 
al., 2025c; Liu et al., 2025).
Furthermore, teaching decision-making within a sup­
portive environment helps enhance teachers’ enjoyment of 
teaching and facilitates the translation of their pedagogical 
beliefs for AI into teaching intentions or actual instructional 
practices (Liu et al., 2025). On one hand, the teacher lead­
ership and professional autonomy advocated by distrib­
uted leadership can significantly elevate teachers’ sense of 
professional accomplishment and control over their work, 
thereby directly nurturing and strengthening their enjoyment 
of teaching (Wang & Bai, 2025). On the other hand, teach­
ers who experience enjoyment in teaching possess more 
abundant psychological resources and are more willing to 
invest additional effort into exploring and experimenting 
with AI-driven pedagogical innovations promoted by lead­
ership. The organizational support provided by distributed 
leadership—such as resources, training, and collaborative 
opportunities—can be perceived by teachers as respect and 
support for their professional autonomy, thereby enhancing 
their joyful experiences in the teaching process (Nadeem, 
2024). When positive emotions motivate teachers to proac­
tively understand, accept, and attempt to integrate AI into 
their pedagogy, this promotes a positive shift in teachers’ 
pedagogical beliefs for AI (Zhang & Zhang, 2024). There­
fore, this study hypothesizes:
H1  Distributed leadership is positively correlated with 
teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI.
The Mediating Role of Teacher Stress
Teacher work stress refers to the physical and psychologi­
cal tension experienced by teachers in their professional 
environment due to factors such as workload, role conflicts, 
and resource constraints. According to Conservation of 
Resources (COR) theory, an individual’s cognitive, emo­
tional, and behavioral resources are finite (Hobfoll, 1989). 
Specifically, persistent work stress can lead to teacher 
burnout and reduce their motivation to explore new teach­
ing methods (Song et al., 2024). This contradiction is par­
ticularly pronounced in the context of educational reform in 
Vietnam. Although government-driven technology integra­
tion and pedagogical innovation provide new opportunities 
for teacher professional development, the reforms them­
selves also impose additional workload and adaptation pres­
sure (Nguyen et al., 2025). Beyond the explicit burden of 
increasing work hours required to master these new skills, 
teachers also face implicit burdens as they must invest more 
effort in exploring new pedagogical expectations associated 
with such technologies (Liao et al., 2025). This negative 
perception is highly likely to weaken teachers’ pedagogi­
cal beliefs for AI, leading them to adopt a conservative or 
avoidant attitude toward technology application (Țîru et al., 
2025).
In contexts with low school support, the level of teachers’ 
pedagogical beliefs for AI is more strongly influenced by 
teachers’ self-directed efforts. This implies that in resource-
scarce environments, teachers must expend substantial 
existing psychological and cognitive resources to acquire 
new teaching resources, such as AI skills. Although such 
resource investment may yield new teaching competencies 
in the short term, in the long run, if effective organizational 
support remains absent, it may trigger a decline in resource 
conservation motivation, leading teachers to adopt a conser­
vative attitude toward AI teaching that demands additional 
investment (Abedi, 2024). Research by Mpuangnan found 
that in some resource-limited regions, despite distributed 
leadership encouraging teachers to participate in decision-
making and innovation, the lack of accompanying techni­
cal support and professional development resources often 
forces teachers to invest extra time in self-learning AI peda­
gogical knowledge (Mpuangnan, 2024). Studies by Filiz 
et al. also indicate that teachers face adaptive challenges 
such as technical issues, curriculum misalignment, ethical 
concerns, and cultural barriers when integrating AI (Filiz 
et al., 2025). These challenges not only increase teachers’ 
workload but may also lead to cognitive biases toward AI 
teaching due to a lack of professional guidance, thereby

Methods
Data Source
The latest Teaching and Learning International Survey, 
released by the OECD, covers 55 education systems world­
wide, including Vietnam. This study focuses on data from 
Vietnamese teachers who participated in this survey, ana­
lyzing their responses regarding the use of AI as a teach­
ing tool, perceptions of distributed leadership, work-related 
stress, and other relevant aspects. These data provide an 
empirical foundation for systematically examining the rela­
tionship between organizational support factors and teach­
ers’ technology integration behaviors within the Vietnamese 
education system. By analyzing feedback from Vietnamese 
teachers, this research helps elucidate the practical mani­
festations of distributed leadership and its mechanisms of 
influence on teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI within a 
centrally managed educational governance context.
Research Methods
To analyze the relationship between distributed leadership 
and Vietnamese teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI, as well 
as the mediating effects of teacher stress and enjoyment of 
teaching, this study utilized SPSS 26 and AMOS 22 for data

reasonableness. AMOS 22 was used to calculate compos­

other metrics for each variable, ensuring that the reliability 
and validity of all dimensions met statistical requirements. 
Subsequently, AMOS 22 was applied to construct the model

The Chain Mediating Effect of Teacher Stress and 
Enjoyment of Teaching
Distributed leadership empowers teachers with greater pro­
fessional autonomy and psychological security by foster­
ing a collaborative, shared decision-making system and a 
supportive organizational environment (Yao & Ma, 2024). 
According to research by Zhao et al., distributed leadership 
is more likely to encourage teachers to view new technolo­
gies such as artificial intelligence from a developmental per­
spective—as tools for pedagogical innovation rather than 
additional work burdens (Zhao et al., 2025). This indicates 
that a collaborative work environment can effectively allevi­
ate teacher stress. Moreover, reduced teacher stress not only 
protects their cognitive and emotional resources but also 
creates essential conditions for experiencing flow, a sense 
of control, and meaningful engagement in teaching, thereby 
enhancing their enjoyment of teaching (Guo & Xu, 2024). In 
other words, teachers can derive positive emotional rewards 
from teaching interactions, student growth, and professional 
creativity (Zheng et al., 2025). This reinforced enjoyment 
of teaching further transforms into intrinsic motivation for 
teachers to explore educational innovations. In a positive 
emotional state, teachers exhibit higher cognitive openness 
to pedagogical change (Zhang et al., 2025). This process 
promotes the construction of a learner-centered pedagogi­
cal belief system integrated with AI, thereby indirectly and 
systematically reshaping teachers’ deep-seated cognition 
and instructional practices regarding cutting-edge teaching 
technologies. Therefore, this study hypothesizes:
H4  Teacher stress and enjoyment of teaching have a chain 
mediating effect on the relationship between distributed 
leadership and teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI.

ing serve as mediating variables reflecting teachers’ work-
related perceptions, and teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI 
is adopted as the outcome variable. Within this framework, 
distributed leadership is closely linked to teachers’ instruc­

Measurement Items
The measurement items were comprehensively selected

PROCESS Procedure for SPSS Version 3.4.1 to calculate 
the three mediation effect values (see Table 5). The model 
employed the Bootstrap method for significance testing, 
with the sampling set to 5000 iterations. If the Bootstrap

Discussion
The advent of the AI era has placed higher demands on 
teachers’ instructional practices, necessitating that schools 
leverage additional resources to enhance teachers’ peda­
gogical beliefs for AI. Distributed leadership in the educa­
tional field represents such a positive force, which is crucial 
for promoting sustainable educational practices. This study, 
based on the COR Theory as its research framework, regards 
distributed leadership as a key source of teachers’ pedagogi­
cal beliefs for AI and constructs a chain mediation model 
incorporating teacher stress and enjoyment of teaching.
Regarding Research Question 1, distributed leadership 
significantly enhanced Vietnamese secondary school teach­
ers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI. This finding reinforces the 
adaptability of the COR Theory within the context of col­
laborative school leadership (Nadeem, 2024). As an orga­
nizational support resource, distributed leadership directly 
strengthens teachers’ confidence and willingness to inte­
grate AI technologies by fostering a collaborative and 
empowering professional environment. Similar to the find­
ings of Hsieh et al., in the Vietnamese educational context, 
the power-sharing and collective decision-making advo­
cated by distributed leadership effectively buffered teachers’ 
uncertainty when facing technological changes (Hsieh et al., 
2025). This leadership style, which decentralizes power and 
responsibility, provides essential psychological and institu­
tional support for teachers to experiment with innovative AI 
teaching practices by promoting the recognition and support 
of teacher expertise and abilities (Xu, 2015), thereby posi­
tively shaping their pedagogical beliefs.

Conclusion
To promote educational equity, when evaluating educational 
effectiveness, it is essential to draw on the successful expe­
riences of developed countries while also paying attention 
to the specific challenges and potential advantages faced by 
developing nations. This will foster evidence-based cross-
national exchange of experiences and collaborative devel­
opment (Hsieh et al., 2025). In this context, this study, based 
on the Conservation of Resources Theory, explores the 
impact of distributed leadership on AI pedagogical beliefs 
among junior high school teachers in Vietnam. The find­
ings indicate that distributed leadership not only directly 
strengthens teachers’ beliefs in AI-integrated teaching prac­
tices but also indirectly reinforces their confidence in utiliz­
ing AI for instructional innovation by fostering a supportive 
environment that nurtures positive emotions. This provides 
crucial empirical evidence for understanding the integration 
of educational technology under diverse resource conditions 
and cultural backgrounds.
Particularly in developing countries with relatively cen­
tralized educational management systems like Vietnam, 
implementing distributed leadership requires creative inte­
gration with local governance structures and socio-cultural 
contexts. It should not be a simple transplant of international 
management models but rather a localized adaptation aimed 
at fostering teachers’ intrinsic motivation and collective 
wisdom. When promoting AI in education, policymakers 
and school administrators should not only provide neces­
sary technological infrastructure and skills training but also 
focus on building a supportive organizational environment 
to collaboratively address ongoing challenges and maxi­
mize the potential of AI in education (Funa & Gabay, 2025). 
By granting teachers appropriate professional autonomy, 
encouraging cross-hierarchical collaboration, and insti­
tutionally recognizing the value of their emotional labor, 
external technological impetus can be transformed into 
teachers’ intrinsic drive for pedagogical exploration. There­
fore, teachers’ professional development should shift from 
isolated technical skill training to an approach that equally 
emphasizes pedagogical knowledge and emotional support, 
thereby genuinely advancing student-centered digital tech­
nology integration in teaching (Abedi, 2024).

empowering environment fostered by distributed leadership 
can stimulate teachers’ intrinsic motivation and professional 
well-being (Ma & Zhang, 2025), thereby encouraging them 
to approach instructional innovation with a more open and 
exploratory mindset, ultimately strengthening their peda­
gogical beliefs regarding AI technology.
Furthermore, the chain mediation model of this study 
reveals a hierarchical mechanism of influence. As a key 
form of organizational support, distributed leadership not 
only directly alleviates teachers’ occupational stress but 
also promotes increased enjoyment of teaching by reduc­
ing work-related pressure, thereby strengthening teachers’ 
pedagogical beliefs for AI. This forms a transmission path­
way of “organizational support-stress reduction-affective 
enhancement-teaching efficacy.” This finding indicates that 
distributed leadership helps mitigate the depletion of teach­
ers’ psychological resources caused by work stress, thereby 
creating conditions for the accumulation of positive affec­
tive resources. The formation of this emotional capital pro­
vides the necessary psychological foundation for teachers 
to explore instructional innovation. External organizational 
resources can foster individuals’ decision-making to adopt 
new technologies through the dual processes of alleviating 
individual resource depletion and promoting resource gain 
(Malek & Almarri, 2024). This result underscores the impor­
tant role of organizational support systems in maintaining 
momentum, which requires continuous evaluation and feed­
back mechanisms. It highlights that distributed leadership is 
not merely a management style but also a catalytic mecha­
nism for affective and cognitive resources (Nadeem, 2024).
It is noteworthy that Vietnam is a highly centralized 
country. Its education policies and resource allocation are 
typically steered by the central education ministry, leaving 
relatively limited room for autonomous decision-making 
at the school level (Minh Hue, 2024). Under such struc­
tural constraints, the practice of distributed leadership may 
face an empowerment paradox. While school leaders are 
expected to delegate authority downward, their own capac­
ity to distribute power and resources is often constrained 
by the higher-level system (Vinh & Hanh, 2024). Conse­
quently, the distributed leadership examined in this study 
may, in reality, more closely resemble delegated manage­
ment or participatory consultation within a predetermined 
framework, rather than the theoretically ideal, fully flat­
tened power-sharing. The fact that this constrained form 
of distributed leadership still demonstrates a positive influ­
ence on teachers’ pedagogical beliefs for AI suggests that 
even within a centralized system, moderate empowerment 
and collaboration can still stimulate teachers’ affective 
resources and willingness to innovate (Gong, 2025). This

Limitations and Future Research
This study has several limitations. First, while the cross-
sectional data from TALIS is representative, it restricts 
causal inference, and reliance on teacher self-reported vari­
ables may introduce common method bias. Second, vari­
ables such as pedagogical beliefs for AI were not measured 
directly but constructed from related items; future research 
may require more scientifically validated tools for verifica­
tion. Finally, the study focuses on the context of junior high 
school education in Vietnam, and the generalizability of its 
findings across different cultural backgrounds, educational 
levels, or subject areas remains uncertain. It also does not 
adequately account for the moderating effects of organiza­
tional contextual factors such as school resources and geo­
graphical location.
Future research could advance in several directions. 
First, employing longitudinal tracking designs or experi­
mental intervention methods would allow for a more rigor­
ous examination of causal relationships and developmental 
trajectories among variables. Second, constructing multi-
level models (HLM) could simultaneously investigate the 
interactive effects of school-level leadership practices and 
teacher individual characteristics (e.g., digital literacy, 
career stage) to identify differentiated impact mechanisms. 
Third, conducting cross-cultural comparative studies could 
explore how cultural values moderate the effects of distrib­
uted leadership. Additionally, integrating mixed methods, 
such as qualitative interviews, could provide deeper insights 
into the specific processes and contextual dynamics through 
which distributed leadership influences teachers’ beliefs, 
thereby offering more nuanced guidance for practice.
