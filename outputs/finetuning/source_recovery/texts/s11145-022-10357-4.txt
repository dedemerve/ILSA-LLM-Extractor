PISA reading achievement: identifying predictors and
examining model generalizability for multilingual students
Shenghai Dai1 · Tao Hao2 · Yuliya Ardasheva3 · Onur Ramazan4 ·
Robert William Danielson5 · Bruce Austin6
Accepted: 8 September 2022 / Published online: 1 February 2023
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature B.V. 2022

Abstract
Reading research in the United States has mainly focused on early or, less frequently, middle grades and on monolingual (MN or English-only) rather than on multilingual (ML) students. To address these gaps, we focused on factors contributing to
high school ML students’ reading achievement. In particular, we first used machine
learning to identify predictors of high school students’ reading achievement on
PISA 2018. We then conducted multilevel modeling on the entire sample (baseline
model) and tested the model’s generalizability to ML and MN populations. Results
suggest that ML students would benefit from instruction focused on enhancing their
reading self-efficacy and increased learning opportunities for extracurricular reading activities. The results also suggest that students, especially ML students, would
benefit from schools avoiding grade retention policies and focusing on minimizing
truancy and supporting positive peer and teacher relationships. Limitations of the
study and future directions are discussed.
Keywords Multilingual students · PISA · Reading · Model generalizability

According to the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD,
2019), reading literacy can be defined as “the active, purposeful and functional application of reading in a range of situations and for various purposes” (p. 28). Reading
literacy is not only fundamental for student access to disciplinary knowledge and
academic achievement across content areas (e.g., Ardasheva et al., 2021; Shanahan &
Shanahan, 2012; Taboada Barber et al., 2015), but also critical for economic growth
and public health (Toste et al., 2020). These findings are especially important, both
nationally and globally, for multilingual students (MLs), the fastest-growing, yet
still understudied student population (e.g., August & Shanahan 2006; Hussar et al.,
2020). Reading research in the United States (U.S.), in particular, has focused mainly

high school ML students may be the most at-risk student population (OECD, 2019;

school to remedy reading problems, and direct literacy instruction may no longer be

represent a heterogeneous group of students who are often referred to in the literature
as English learners or as limited English proficiency, language minority, or culturally
and linguistically diverse students. Reflecting the terminology of the most recent
English language development standards adopted by over 40 U.S. states and territories (WIDA, 2020), we chose to use the term ML to refer to students who are learning
English as an additional language.
Considering recent conceptualizations of reading literacy as being impacted by a
host of student-, classroom-, and school-level characteristics (OECD, 2019), empirical evidence supporting such conceptualization (Ardasheva & Tretter, 2013; Feng et
al., 2019; Giambona & Porcu, 2015), and the high cost of interventions along with
the documented differential impacts of interventions on MN vs. ML students (e.g.,
Llosa et al., 2016; Vaughn et al., 2015; Vaughn et al., 2019), there is a pressing need
to uncover the underlying impacts of multiple, nested systems on improving reading
literacy for high school students. This is particularly important because while reading
research on high school MLs is limited, evidence from studies focused on younger
readers suggests that the prominence of reading comprehension predictors and reading behaviors may differ for ML as compared to MN readers (Frid & Friesen, 2020;
Jiménez et al., 1996; Melby-Lervåg & Lervåg 2014). Across age groups, the social
aspects of schooling that impact achievement may also differ for MLs (Zaff et al.,
2021) as they may experience discrimination, segregation, cultural stereotyping, and
differential implementation of programs (Ardasheva et al., 2012; Datnow et al., 2005;
Lukes, 2014; Suárez-Orozco & Suárez-Orozco, 2009; Tenenbaum & Ruck 2007).
Identifying and better understanding instructionally malleable factors supporting reading literacy and learning from reading across nested systems is especially
important in upper grades “where the language demands of texts are higher and,
at the same time, texts are often the primary instructional tool” for many teachers
across disciplines (Ardasheva et al., 2021, p. 293), yet not many high school teachers are prepared to support literacy skills development. A focus on MLs is particularly important provided continually documented achievement—including reading
and reading subskills achievement (e.g., August & Shanahan 2006; Townsend et al.,
2020; Uccelli et al., 2015)—gaps for this important student population.
Therefore, in this study, we use machine learning feature selection and multilevel
modeling to explore and identify predictors of student achievement in reading. We
leveraged thousands of variables at all levels (student, teacher, school) from the
U.S. 2018 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) data—the latest
PISA assessment with a primary focus on reading—and examined factors that support reading literacy of high school students, with a focus on ML students. Machine

learning feature, in particular, is well suited to work with large-scale data by allowing to explore robust patterns in the data, which could lead to theory confirmation
or inductive theory building (e.g., Choudhury et al., 2018; Nelson, 2020). That is,
machine learning methods make fewer a priori assumptions about the underlying
model representing the data best, which allows for replicable identification of patterns (Choudhury et al., 2018). In content analysis, for example, machine learning is

“an interpretive engagement with the data” (p. 3) by the researchers, and (c) pattern
confirmation through further computational or statistical techniques. In other words,
machine learning methods act “as a complementary tool” (p. 1) not only for traditional causal inference methods but also for inductive theory-creating methods such
as grounded theory (Choudhury et al., 2018).
Recognizing that ML students negotiate multiple academic and social contexts
influencing their learning, our study seeks to examine the impacts of multiple layers
of social contexts on their reading achievement within an ecological model (Bronfenbrenner, 1977) framework. Below, we describe student-, teacher-, and school-level
variables linked to reading achievement by previous studies, after a brief discussion of the PISA assessment and Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model, the main data
source, and the theoretical grounding for the present study. We use machine learning
to detect and refine patterns at each—student, teacher, and school—level, then use
multilevel modeling to confirm these patterns across levels. We finally test the generalizability of these patterns for ML vs. MN students.

Study background and conceptual framing
“PISA 2018 marks the third time that reading is a major domain and the second time
that the reading literacy framework receives a major revision” (OECD, 2019, p. 22).
Reading is no longer considered an ability acquired early by young children. Rather,
it is viewed as an expanding set of knowledge, drawing on resources, dispositions,
attitudes, motivation, and strategies that individuals build across contexts, including
interactions with parents, peers, teachers, and the community. In other words, reading
does not occur in a vacuum (McCrudden & Schraw, 2007; Snow, 2002). Thus, PISA
2018 considers a host of constructs associated with reading literacy at the student,
teacher, and school levels.
Aligned with PISA 2018 framework, our study was guided by Bronfenbrenner’s
(1977) ecological systems theory. The theory considers factors affecting learning
across multiple nested systems, ranging from the individual (personal characteristics)
and the micro- and meso-systems (the immediate learning contexts and the interface
among them) to the exosystems (larger contexts affecting learning indirectly; Crawford et al., 2020). In particular, our study drew on three of the four key elements
central to Bronfenbrenner’s Process–Person–Context–Time (PPCT) model (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 1998). Aligned with the multiple nested systems and the PISA
2018 reading literacy Framework (OECD, 2019), the three components of the PPCT

Combined, these findings highlight the importance of examining both individual
and environmental factors associated with learning. The constructs central to our
study aligned with Bronfenbrenner’s person, process, and context ecological systems constructs (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 1998) are operationalized in the present
investigation as: student characteristics (e.g., demographics, non-cognitive and metacognitive factors), teacher characteristics (e.g., preparation, job satisfaction; proxies
of classroom experiences and teacher responsiveness to student needs), and school
characteristics (e.g., policies, climate). We further outline these predictors below.
Person: student-level predictors
Research suggests that student background characteristics such as SES, parental education, prior learning, migration status, and reading experiences beyond school may
be important student-level predictors of reading outcomes (e.g., Dewan 2016; Ponitz
et al., 2009; Carhill et al., 2008). Giambona & Porcu (2015), for example, found that
educational resources available to students at home, such as desks, computers, quiet
places to study, and reference books, were important predictors of achievement for
lower-readers. At the same time, the number of books at home was more important
for higher-readers. Higher occupational status and educational levels of parents were
predictive of reading achievement regardless of students’ SES, “supporting intergenerational dependence of educational achievement” (Giambona & Porcu, 2015, p.
102; see also Carhill et al., 2008; Valenzuela et al., 2015). Yet, the positive effects of
parental occupational status and educational levels were lower for lower-achieving
students (Giambona & Porcu, 2015). Such variations suggest a need to extend this
research to compare these relationships by student language status, as even highly
educated parents of ML students may not be able to provide adequate support for
their children reading in an additional language.
Similar to student background characteristics, many empirical studies link higher
reading outcomes with such non-cognitive and metacognitive individual factors
including self-efficacy, metacognition, motivation, and engagement, among both ML
and MN students (e.g., Ardasheva 2016; Cockroft & Atkinson, 2017; Meng et al.,
2016; Okumura, 2014; van Gelderen et al., 2004). For example, Kirsch et al. (2002)
found that the relationship between reading and engagement was larger than that
between SES and reading. Guthrie et al. (1999) found that, controlling for self-efficacy and prior reading achievement/topic knowledge, motivation significantly pre-

Studies also show that instruction focused on non-cognitive and metacognitive

Overall, student background characteristics, non-cognitive and metacognitive factors, as well as their immediate learning environments are important to explore as
they can affect the extent to which students respond to reading interventions (Vaughn
et al., 2019). This is particularly important to consider in light of student language
status, as such factors as self-efficacy or engagement and their impacts across learning outcomes may vary for ML vs. MN students (e.g., Taboada Barber et al., 2015;
Baroody et al., 2014; Griggs et al., 2013). This variance in self-efficacy or engagement could potentially influence the ways ML students interact with their teachers, a
group to which we now turn.
Process: teacher-level predictors
Similar to student characteristics, teacher-level factors such as preparation, self-efficacy, and years of experience have also been found to impact student learning. In a
meta-analysis of meta-analyses, Hattie (2009, as cited in Kim et al., 2019), lists over
250 factors impacting student achievement (the full upgraded list can be found at
https://visible-learning.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/VLPLUS-252-InfluencesHattie-ranking-DEC-2017.pdf). These are categorized under seven sources of impact,
with teacher (e.g., teacher education and attributes) and teaching (e.g., instructional
strategies) occupying a prominent role. Effective teacher preparation, in particular,
plays a significant role in achieving the desired learning outcomes in the classroom.
The disconnect between undergraduate education and teacher preparation programs
generates many obstacles that undermine teacher practices and self-efficacy in implementing classroom instruction (Faez & Valeo, 2012; Enchandia et al., 2007). Feng et
al. (2019), for example, found that teachers who completed literacy-specific coursework during preparation and who received discipline-specific mentoring had higher
self-efficacy. Teacher personality and emotions have also started attracting researchers’ attention in recent years (Chen, 2019; Kim et al., 2019; Uitto et al., 2015).

Teacher emotions intertwine with motivation and cognition and can be influenced by
such factors as professional relationships, school environments, and job satisfaction
(Kim & Schallert, 2011; Uitto et al., 2015; Yin et al., 2017), all impacting instructional behaviors (e.g., Saunders 2013; Trigwell, 2012). Empirical studies have documented that teachers with positive emotions are more likely to produce an effective
learning environment and thus facilitate student learning and development (Yan et
al., 2011). However, these findings are often generalized to all students, whereas the
impacts of such relationships on ML students in particular have not been studied in
depth. Finally, while students and teachers interact with one another within the classroom, they also interact outside the classroom within the bounds of the school itself,
walking through hallways, engaged in clubs, and participating in larger, schoolwide
programming. We now turn to this final level of predictors.
Context: school-level predictors
School-level factors reflecting “norms, goals, values, interpersonal relationships,
teaching and learning practices, and organizational structures” (National School Climate Council, 2007, p. 5) affect the life and learning of students. Indeed, school
effects on the achievement of both ML and MN students are well documented (e.g.,
Fry 2008; Jehangir et al., 2015; Kao & Thompson, 2003; Ruus et al., 2007; Thapa
et al., 2013). For example, Suárez-Orozco et al. (2008) found that the percentage of
low-SES students in a school along with the schools’ racial representation, the average attendance rate, and the average success rate (the percent of students scoring
proficient on the state-mandated language arts test) explained about 32% of the variance in reading test scores of ML students. Similarly, the average school SES level
significantly predicted reading achievement in Okumura (2014). Notably, the schoollevel mean of SES (and that of teacher engagement stimulation) explained more variance in student reading than did student-level predictors – highlighting the potential
of school-level variables exerting influence on learning. That said, it is worthwhile
noting that Okumura’s study used a limited set of variables at the student level, no
variables at individual teacher level, and the analyses were not disaggregated by students’ language status. This is unfortunate, and indicated that attending schools with
lower SES and achievement profiles may have a negative impact on ML students (see
also Ardasheva et al., 2012; Carhill et al., 2008; Yoko, 2007). There is further evidence to suggest that relationships between reading achievement and student background characteristics can be impacted by school funding and curriculum (Jehangir
et al., 2015). Yet, a trend common to ML populations is to find themselves in schools
with “high levels of students living in or near poverty” (Fry, 2008, p. i) as well as in
schools that are less safe and supportive (or perceived as such; Sanders et al., 2018;
Watkins & Melde, 2009). In turn, schools with poor supportive systems tend to have
lower levels of achievement, among other negative outcomes (Astor et al., 2009). By
including student-, teacher-, and school-level variables, we hope to uncover a more
nuanced understanding of the factors influencing student reading performance.

The present study
Multilingual students, a group historically underrepresented in higher-paid jobs (Fraleigh-Lohrfink et al., 2013), often need to negotiate multiple academic and social
contexts that influence their learning across disciplinary domains. Research is needed
on curricular equity and access to understand how academic success of this important
student population is affected by background and instructional factors, educational
policy, school structures, and “the relational aspects of schooling” across content
areas (Callahan, 2018, p. 26).
This is particularly important for reading literacy, foundational for success across
subject areas. Therefore, we examined the impacts of multiple layers of individual
and social contexts on student reading outcomes, starting with selecting predictors
and generating a baseline model using the entire PISA data set, then examining the
model’s generalizability for ML vs. MN students. The following research questions
guided our work:
1. What predictors of reading achievement are most salient at the student, teacher,
and school levels?
2. What are the relative contributions of the most salient student-, teacher-, and
school-level constructs to students’ reading achievement?
3. What are the relative contributions of the most salient student-, teacher-, and
school-level constructs to students’ reading achievement disaggregated by language status?

Method
Data and participants
Data sources
Participants. The U.S. 2018 PISA reading assessment sample data and the accompanying student (including home and parent), teacher, and school questionnaires were
used for the analysis. The sample included 4,838 U.S. students aged 15–16 years old
(49.1% female) and 3,526 teachers (50.6% female; 29.8% reading or language-specific teachers) from 164 schools (6.8% private). Students were 10th (74.4%) or 11th
graders (17.1%). ML sample included 736 (52.6% female) students from 126 schools
and that of MN students was 4054 (48.7% female) students from 163 schools. Most
of the ML students in the sample reported speaking Spanish at home (N = 517). The
average cluster size (i.e., number of students per school) was 30 for the entire sample
(ranging from 1 to 39), 25 for the MN sample (ranging from 2 to 39), and 6 for the
ML sample (ranging from 1 to 22). There were 48 students (1%) who did not report
their language status and were excluded from the study.
Of the 4,838 students in the entire sample, 43.3% reported their race/ethnicity

Variables and measures
Reading outcome. The outcome variables were the 10 sets of plausible values (PVs)
of student reading performance. PVs, proxies of performance drawn from students’
proficiency distributions, are typically used in large-scale assessment programs to
account for the estimation bias due to the Booklet design (Beaton et al., 2011; Braun
& von Davier, 2017). For these reasons, it is recommended to use all sets of PVs for
the analysis (Braun & von Davier, 2017; Rutkowski et al., 2010). Table 2 provides
(unweighted) descriptive statistics. An independent samples t-test was conducted
to examine the mean reading performance differences between ML (MML = 482.16,
SDML = 106.45) and MN students (MMN = 510.89, SDMN = 107.28). The analysis
was conducted by taking into account all the PVs and the student sampling weights.
Results suggested a significant difference in average reading scores across the two
groups, ΔM = 28.73, t = 27.88, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.18.
Contextual factors. The 2018 PISA questionnaire data included a total of 1482
variables across all levels, of which 943, 347, and 192 were at the student, teacher,
and school levels, respectively. Due to space limitations, only the variables identified
as predictors in the present analysis are discussed in the paper. A complete variable list
can be found on the PISA website (https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/2018database/).

Step 1: Preliminary and elastic net analyses
Coming from the penalized regression family, the Elastic Net analysis is a machine
learning feature (or variable selection technique) used to identify meaningful factors
out of a large number of predictors for the outcome (Murphy, 2012). The Elastic Net
is a penalized (also known as regularized or shrinkage) regression approach (Bruce
et al., 2020; James et al., 2013). The method applies penalty (or regularization) to the
regression model in a way that variables with relatively small contributions to the
model will have coefficients approaching zero. That is, if an explanatory variable’s
contribution is only minor in accounting for the variance of the outcome, its coefficient will be shrunk toward zero. The penalty can be applied in different ways (James
et al., 2013), two are pertinent to our study. The first is the L1 penalty which applies
regularization by penalizing the sum of absolute values of the regression coefficients.
The penalized regression with L1 penalty only is known as Lasso. The second is the
L2 penalty which applies constraints by penalizing the sum of squared coefficients.
The penalized regression with L2 penalty only is known as Ridge. The Elastic Net
method applies both L1 and L2 penalties in the same model making it superior to
other approaches (Zou & Hastie, 2005). Another advantage of this approach lies in
its robustness in the presence of small sample sizes—even under situations when the
sample size is much smaller than the number of the variables (Luan et al., 2020; Zou
& Hastie, 2005). The Elastic Net analysis has been widely applied in STEM fields
and is now gaining attention in educational research (Goretzko & Bühner, 2020;
Murphy, 2012; Yoo, 2018). In the current study, the Elastic Net analysis was accom-

Prior to the analysis, nominal variables were dummy coded. Further, significance

to the outcome could be examined. The proportion of missing data ranged from none
to 100% and variables with a missing proportion above 20% were excluded from the
analysis (Peugh & Enders, 2004). For the remaining variables, the predictive mean
matching (PPM) from the mice package (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2021)
was used to impute the missing data. All sets of PVs were used, resulting in 10 sets
of results that served the purpose of variable selection. The overall student sampling
weight was also included.
Step 2: Variable selection
In this step, a final list of the variables was decided through synthesizing the results of
the Elastic Net analysis in consultation with theoretical and empirical considerations
from the existing literature. In practice, it is usually not recommended to interpret
coefficients of the variables from the Elastic Net analysis because the coefficients are
biased due to the penalization or regularization nature of the approach (i.e., a solution for better performance of the predictive model at a cost of introducing bias to the
parameters). Relatedly, and as mentioned earlier, significance tests for the estimated
coefficients are not reliable and thus not reported. Because of this, the results of the
Elastic Net analysis are usually interpreted by ranking the statistical importance of
the variable based on the magnitude of their coefficients. In the current study, the 10
sets of PVs in the PISA data yielded 10 sets of results. The variable rankings varied
across the data sets (e.g., a top-ranked variable for one PV set might get a lower
ranking for another PV set), to some extent. To arrive at the final variable selection,
our study relied on a combination of criteria. First, as noted above, we ranked the
variables in terms of their coefficient values for all 10 sets of the results. Next, a panel
of project team members with expertise in language learning, literacy, and language
assessment examined all sets of ranked variables.The panel focused not only on statistically important variables but also on those with relatively smaller coefficients but
strongly supported in the literature as salient contributors to reading. The results were
discussed by the panel until an agreement was achieved on the final variable selection. The selected variables were then examined using multilevel modeling (the next
step below) in terms of their relative, statistical contributions to reading achievement
at each level.

Results
Elastic net analysis and variable selection
Results of the Elastic Net analysis returned a list of variables ranked based on their
relative statistical contribution to the variance of the outcome across levels for each
PV. The results were not always consistent across PVs. For example, there were variables receiving high (top 20) rankings across all 10 sets of PVs (e.g., books at home);
some variables, however, only made it into the rank on just one PV. Thus, as noted
earlier, the final variable selection relied on a combination of criteria. These variables
were identified by the Elastic Net analysis as statistically important in terms of their
large coefficient magnitudes across PVs and/or theoretically supported. The selection process was accomplished by the panel of authors with corresponding expertise,
by considering both the statistical and theoretical importance of the variables. For
example, two of the student-level background variables—1) the number of books and
2) musical instruments at home—showed high rankings across nearly all the PVs.
The grade repetition variable, however, was only flagged by the results of three PVs.
After the examination of the variables’ statistical and theoretical significance, the
panel decided to include all three for further analysis. As illustrated by this example,
the list of the variables was finalized after the agreement was achieved among the
panelists. A total of 24, 22, and 22 variables at the student, teacher, and school levels
were obtained (Appendix A1 - A3). We discuss these below.
Student-level variables
Aligned with previous studies, a number of background characteristics were strongly
associated with reading achievement. These included: ISCED (educational) level,
grade retention, books and musical instruments at home, and digital affordances
(e.g., “Available for you to use at home: cell phone”). The most salient non-cognitive
and metacognition variables included: assessing credibility, reading strategy, joy of
reading, and academic aspiration. Peer support (e.g., “During the past 12 months,
how often: Other students spread nasty rumors about me”) was identified as another
potentially salient correlate of reading achievement. Finally, a number of variables
capturing student perceptions of their teachers (e.g., “The teacher showed enjoyment
in teaching”) emerged as potentially salient positive correlates of student reading
achievement.
Teacher-level variables
A number of subjects in initial teacher preparation (e.g., religion/ethics, technology,
social studies, arts) and the overlap between initial preparation and current teaching
area (in such subjects as religion/ethics, science, technology, arts) emerged as potentially salient correlates at the teacher level. Furthermore, teacher employment, experience, strategies, beliefs about reading instruction, and reading habits were strongly
associated with their students’ reading performance. The reading-habit variable “I
rarely or never read books,” for instance, was associated with lower levels of student

clearly outweigh the disadvantages”) emerged as an important correlate.
School-level variables
First, school rural location and private ownership emerged as potentially the most
important correlates at the school level. Student enrollment mattered in terms of
admission recommendation by feeder schools, the number of girls, and the number
of students with special needs. Three school resources and climate variables (e.g.,
truancy), one parental engagement variable (i.e., proportion of parents discussing
their children’s progress with teachers), five school-use-of-assessment variables
(e.g., assessment used to monitor yearly progress), and three extracurricular literacy
variables (e.g., contributing to school newsletter or such out-of-school experiences as
collaborating with a local newspaper) were selected. The next five selected variables
focused on the school-level use of digital devices (e.g., adequate number of digital
devices connected to the Internet) and career guidance.
Variable preparation for the MLM analysis
To prevent issues of model non-convergence, multicollinearity, and over-parameterization due to the large number of variables entered, conceptually similar variables were grouped and converted into composite variables (Appendix A1 – A3).
The grouping process was grounded in literature and PISA recommendations for
generating composite scale scores (OECD, 2019). When applicable, confirmatory
factor analysis (CFA) was conducted for each scale as support of construct validation
(i.e., evidence related to internal structure; AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014). The CFA
model fit was deemed satisfactory in the presence of a non-significant χ2 test (p > .05),
the comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker Lewis index (TLI) values over 0.90, and
the root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardized root mean
square residual (SRMR) values below 0.08. The estimated factor scores were used as
the composite variable.
The grouping process resulted in 17 grouped composite variables. Among these
variables, 12 consisted of more than two items supporting CFA use, which was run
using the R package lavaan (Rosseel et al., 2021). Results of the analyses supported
acceptable model-data fit for these variables except for perceptions of instructional
support (model did not converge), teaching strategy (χ2 = 1129.94, df = 5, p < .001,
TLI = 0.60, CFI = 0.80, RMSEA = 0.28, SRMR = 0.10), and use of digital devices
(χ2 = 6.36, df = 2, p < .04, TLI = 0.81, CFI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.12, SRMR = 0.04).
Details of the CFA results are included in the Appendix A5.

two indicators/items, poor CFA model fit) and the estimation of the first PC was used
as the composite variable.3 The same procedure was applied by PISA to compute the

Multilevel models: multilingual vs. monolingual
Table 6 shows the results of the final model for the ML and MN student samples. Not
surprisingly, the results of the MN sample are similar to those of the entire sample
as the MN students account for the majority of the data (4054 out of the 4838 U.S.
participants in PISA 2018). A comparison of the results for the two language group
sub-samples, however, revealed different patterns. First, there are variables showing
a significant relationship with the reading outcome for the MN, but not for the ML,
students including ISCED level (b = 38.53, SE = 6.14, β = 0.10, p < .001), perceptions
of instructional support (b = 4.13, SE = 1.32, β = 0.04, p < .001), and teacher belief (b
= -28.70, SE = 11.87, β = -0.08, p = .02). None of these variables showed a significant
effect on the reading performance of ML students.
Second, extracurricular literacy at the school level revealed a significantly positive relationship with the outcome only for ML students (b = 3.24, SE = 1.56, β =
0.10, p = .04). This variable was unique to the ML student sample. No significant
association between extracurricular literacy and reading performance was detected
neither for the ML nor for the entire student sample.
Third, examining the magnitudes of significant predictors showed that several
associations between the predictors and the outcome were non-trivially stronger for
ML students. For example, grade repetition had a substantially stronger negative
impact on ML students reading achievement (b = -42.54 vs. -25.03, β = -0.14 vs.
-0.07) while higher levels of self-efficacy (b = 20.63 vs. 15.74, β = 0.20 vs. 0.15) and
stronger peer interaction (b = 21.55 vs. 15.26, β = 0.19 vs. 0.14) tended to yield better performance for ML students in PISA reading. Overall, with the only exception of
digital affordance, the magnitude of which was larger for MN students (b = 26.10 vs.
24.78, β = 0.20 vs. 0.21), the associations between the statistically significant predictors and the reading outcome tended to be stronger for ML students.

Discussion
Reading literacy is foundational for full participation in contemporary society, including social, cultural, and political engagement (OECD, 2019). For instance, research
links higher levels of reading literacy with reduced crime, better health, empowerment, and higher employability (Dewan, 2016; Morrisroe, 2014). PISA data represent a critical time period for fulfilling academic requirements for timely graduation
and for setting foundations for future careers. This is particularly important for ML
students, a group that is historically underrepresented in higher-paid jobs (FraleighLohrfink et al., 2013) and is still understudied, especially in higher grades (e.g., de
Araujo et al., 2018; Hall et al., 2017). Thus, it is important to understand what factors
impact reading literacy of ML students who navigate the schooling through an additional language while being held to the same accountability and academic standards
as their MN peers. To address these gaps, the current study applied a three-step analytical strategy with support of previous empirical and theoretical research to identify
the correlates of high school students’ reading performance on the PISA 2018 assessment, with a particular emphasis on ML students.
From a methodological perspective, our study took advantage of a host of
advanced statistical techniques to address our research questions. First, the Elastic
Net approach, a popular machine learning variable selection technique, served to
select the most salient factors associated with student reading (Research Question
1). The Elastic Net approach provides the advantages of efficient handling of a large
number of variables (Chen et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2019), while working with largescale data sets to identify variables with the highest contributions to student performance at all levels. Second, MLM served to explore and interpret the relationship
between the selected factors and student reading performance at the student, teacher,
and school levels (Research Question 2). In particular, the power of MLM analyses
allowed us to evaluate—across nested levels of data—the relative contributions of the
statistically significant variables. Finally, MLM analyses disaggregated by language
status allowed us to test the generalizability of the obtained multilevel model for ML
students, a student population at the heart of the present study (Research Question 3).
Expanding on this methodological discussion, we suggest that methods like Elastic
Net serve a very important purpose in the field – that of theory generation and affirmation from an atheoretical perspective. We draw parallels to traditional approaches
of grounded theory, wherein theories themselves are generated from the data. While
grounded theory and large-scale assessments and surveys may at first glance seem to
be a strange pairing, we echo Walsh et al., (2015) that grounded theory itself is not
bound to any methodological approach, but rather a methodology that embraces any
and all types of data. Glaser & Strauss (1967) make this point explicitly when arguing
that grounded theory, at its core, is “derived from data and then illustrated by characteristic examples of data” (p. 5). If grounded theory is the “discovery of emerging
patterns in data” (p. 593) as Glaser suggests (in Walsh et al., 2015), then applications
of Elastic Net and other bottom-up approaches to data analysis may prove fruitful for
theoretical validation and expansion as the field moves forward.
From a theoretical perspective, this study contributes to a limited body of work
focusing on identifying correlates of high school students’ reading achievement

across multiple nested systems. Consistent with Bronfenbrenner’s (1977) ecological
framework, the results identified a number of variables at each level impacting students’ reading achievement. This finding is also consistent with the well-recognized
and commonly implemented approach in educational research examining the impacts
of multiple levels within school systems. In turn, using machine learning approaches
and testing model generalizability for ML vs. MN student populations, this study also
contributes to generating insights that may lead to inductive theory building (Choudhury et al., 2018). Future studies will need to address the limitations of PISA data
to capture the time and interaction components of Bronfenbrenner’s (1977) model.
In particular, the results of the Elastic Net analysis, supported by the literature,
identified a total of 68 original variables in the PISA data as the most salient correlates
of reading performance across levels (24 at the student and 22 at each the teacher and
the school levels). After grouping and constructing composite variables, their relative associations with reading were explored using MLM. Results for the combined
sample indicated that most of the student-level correlates were significant, including
both negative and positive correlates. The former included grade repetition; the latter
included effort, ISCED level, musical instrument, books at home, digital affordance,
metacognition, self-efficacy in reading, peer interaction, and perceptions of instructional support.
At the teacher and school levels, there were only two significant correlates. Consistent with both theory (Maslow, 1943) and previous literature (Astor et al., 2009), the
one at the school level, student truancy, was a negative correlate of student achievement. Surprisingly, teacher belief, a teacher-level correlate, was also negative for the
combined and MN samples, perhaps due to reflecting teachers’ aspirations rather than
their actual training. This, in combination with current curriculum for adolescents
often not including direct reading instruction (OECD, 2019) and the fact that only
about 30% of teachers in the PISA sample were reading or language-specific teachers, who, by definition, are trained to teach reading comprehension, may explain this
unexpected finding. More research is needed to explore the impacts of content-area
teachers’ actual training to teach reading comprehension, especially for ML students
in high school where the language demands of schooling are typically higher. Finally,
as we discuss next, the examination of the model generalizability revealed additional
differences in model performances for ML students.
From a practical perspective, some background variables at the student level identified as salient and statistically significant correlates of high school readers’ performance are outside of direct impacts of educational systems (e.g., books and musical
instruments at home), unless through parent education, outreach, and financial support programs. That said, a host of student-level variables related to reading performance that remained significant when considering the combined influences at all
levels are manipulable by instruction and/or larger educational systems (Table 4).
Most pertinent to ML students are grade retention, peer interaction, perceptions of
instructional support, and reading self-efficacy. In particular, unlike for the combined
or the MN samples, grade retention was the strongest and negative correlate of high
school ML student reading performance. This is of concern as research suggests that
disparities associated with school punishment practices and academic progress for
ML students persist, especially in urban schools (Peguero et al., 2021). Substantively

larger associations with peer interaction for ML as compared to MN students – captured by two items in our study, namely, language usually spoken with best friend
and frequency of bullying (“nasty rumors”) behaviors – call for more institutional
attention focused on properly fostering this social support factor, especially for ML
students. In particular, schools need to strive for greater diversity in their enrollment
as languages usually spoken with best friends was one of the indicators of positive
peer relationships and there is a substantial body of research supporting the positive role of native language support in academic development, including developing
supportive networks and resiliency to adverse factors (e.g., Stevenson et al., 2019).
Having peers sharing the same language in the school may also minimize bullying
behaviors, provided that schools implement anti-bullying initiatives at the individual,
peer-group, and school-wide levels (Carney et al., 2018; Haeseler, 2010; Salmivalli,
2010). Substantively larger associations with reading self-efficacy, however, do not
necessarily suggest that an instructional focus on this variable is needed given that
our findings regarding self-efficacy impacts may be an artifact of the cross-sectional
nature of the data and some recent findings suggesting that reading achievement
influences reading self-efficacy over time, but the opposite influence may be relatively limited (e.g., Burns et al., 2020; Sewasew & Koester, 2019).
A unique lack of a statistically significant association between ML student
achievement and perceptions of instructional support, operationalized in our study as
student perceptions of teacher instructional support (e.g., amount of assigned readings, teacher summarizing previous lessons or inviting students’ sharing favorite passages) is surprising. This lack of significant association may be attributed to teachers
generally being (under)prepared to meet the needs of their diverse learners (Butcher,
2020; Okhremtchouk & Sellu, 2019; Samson & Collins, 2012), thus likely providing support not targeted to meet the learning needs of ML learners. For example,
inviting publicly sharing a passage without rehearsal (e.g., turn and talk, think-pairshare), may increase levels of anxiety, especially if facing unsupportive peers, and
undermine active participation of ML with still-developing language skills. Somewhat relatedly, extracurricular literacy (additional lectures/seminars, contributing to
school newsletter, collaborating with a local newspaper) was a unique contributor to
ML student reading comprehension, plausibly reflecting ML students’ need for more
meaningful, real-life applications of English literacy skills and additional exposure to
academic English outside of school.
Study limitations and future directions
Every study has limitations, and this one is no different. First, the amount of unexplained variance in reading achievement suggests that variables other than those
included in the present study may need to be included in future investigations. One
such possible variable is a more nuanced conceptualization of the language proficiency for ML learners, as the needs of English language beginners (the most vulnerable ML student population in upper grades) may differ from students who are
early intermediate, intermediate, or early advanced speakers and readers of English.
It may be beneficial to collect such information for future PISA assessment administrations, especially for such subjects as literacy. Further, although ML status—also

often referred to in the literature as limited English proficiency, English learner, or
language minority status—is often associated with lower SES, cultural/ethnic diversity, and overrepresentation in students with disability status (e.g., Becker & Deris
2019; Lobstein et al., 2021), potentially leading to collinearity issues, it may be helpful to separately examine the contributions of these demographic variables to reading
achievement of MLs, both at the student and classroom or school levels. Moderator
analysis will likely also provide a fruitful path for future research especially to help
explain the remaining variance in reading test scores. Economic indicators, such as
ESCS, PISA’s indicator of economic, social, and cultural status, have been used in
prior research as within-level and cross-level moderators and should be explored in
the context of ML student research as well in the future.
The second limitation lies in the large number of variables put forward by the
Elastic Net approach and the potential concerns of multicollinearity, model non-convergence, and over-parameterization in statistical modeling. An advantage of Elastic
Net (and other approaches with similar purposes such as Lasso, Ridge, and Treebased methods) is to assist in the selection of variables with high statistical contributions to the outcome in the presence of a large p (number of variables) and a small
n (sample size). It is possible that the final selected list is still too large and includes
similar variables, especially in the context of large-scale assessments and surveys
where plausible values are used. To address this issue, two options can be used. One
is to combine the variables into composites or indices, while the other is to exclude
variables that have high correlations with other variables (e.g., OECD, 2008). In our
study, we followed the first option by applying CFA and PCA techniques. While most
of the grouped variables were supported either as a factor with a satisfactory CFA
model data fit or a component that accounted for a large proportion of variance in
PCA, we noticed specific variables that need further investigation. For instance, the
perceptions of instructional support component (discussed earlier) only accounted
for about 32% of the variance.
Third, concerns may lie in the sample size differences across the compared student
subsamples (4,054 MNs from 163 schools vs. 736 MLs from 126 schools). While
the sample sizes at both levels are acceptable for the multilevel modeling analysis
(Hox et al., 2018; Snijders & Bosker, 2011), issues may arise when the coefficients
and effects across the two student samples are compared to each other. For instance,
there were three variables (i.e., ISCED level, perceptions of instructional support,
and teacher belief) that showed significant effects on reading literacy for the MN
students only. This difference could potentially be attributed to the smaller sample
size of MLs. Therefore, as recommended in the literature, we examined the effect
sizes in addition to the significance levels of the coefficients. For instance, the variable perceptions of instructional support was a significant predictor of reading performance for MNs (b = 4.13, SE = 1.32, β = 0.04, p < .001), but not for MLs (b = 5.11,
SE = 3.62, β = 0.05, p = .16). However, the very similar size in corresponding standardized coefficients, β = 0.04 and β = 0.05, respectively, suggest that the perceptions of instructional support variable may also have a statistically significant effect
on MLs’ reading literacy, if the ML sample were larger.
To further investigate this issue and inform future studies, we conducted a followup analysis using a random sample of MN students with the same number of schools

13

2788

S. Dai et al.

and students as that of the ML sample. First, we drew a random sample of 126 schools
from the MN sample (the same number of schools in the ML sample). Then, we drew
a random sample of 736 MN (matching the ML size). The same multilevel modeling
analysis was then conducted using the reduced-size sample of MN students. Results
of the reduced subsample analysis supported our full sample analysis conclusions,
on all but one of the variables (see Appendix Table A6). The teacher belief variable
became not significant, p = .32, in the reduced sample (vs. p = .02 in the full sample)
and the magnitude of the effect size also slightly decreased from β = 0.08 in the full
to 0.06 in the reduced sample.
Finally, due to the cross-sectional nature of PISA data, we were not able to explore
the impacts of variables over time, which is important from both theoretical (e.g.,
single episodes vs. systematic reoccurrence; Bronfenbrenner & Morris 1998) and
practical (e.g., informing intervention duration) perspectives.

Conclusion
The results of our study echo previous literature in many respects. The findings also
identify a host of correlates at each level not investigated in the previous research
and highlight their relative, statistically significant associations with reading. The
multistep approach used in this study both identifying the most strongly associated
correlates of reading and then examining their relative associations may shed light
on the existing literature from a new angle and thus contribute to refining existing
theories. The use of machine learning, in particular, which is a largely data-driven
approach with few a priori assumptions, offers a practical application of a methodological innovation—still novel in many areas of educational research—different
from many studies in the field that focus on hypothesis testing. As such, the study, as
pointed out by one of the anonymous reviewers, can serve as a way to validate—and
expand upon—existing findings from theory-based studies.
From a practical perspective, the results suggest that ML students would especially benefit from increased learning opportunities for extracurricular literacy activities. From a policy perspective, the results suggest that all students, but especially
ML students, would benefit from schools avoiding grade retention policies and focusing on promoting a school climate supporting positive peer relationships and tailored teacher practices, which should also minimize truancy. From a future research
perspective, it is paramount to focus more attention on high school ML students, a
burgeoning and often at-risk student population in our schools. Research is needed
that considers the impacts of multiple, nested systems on improving reading literacy
for ML students, especially considering the high cost of interventions and their differential impacts on ML versus MN students.
