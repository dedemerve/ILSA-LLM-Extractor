This study examines a moderated mediation model that links empowering leadership to collective teacher
innovativeness, with collective teacher efficacy serving as the mediator and school power distance as the
moderator. Drawing on a cross-sectional survey design and utilizing data from 926 teachers across 73 schools in
Türkiye, we employed multilevel structural equation modelling with Bayesian estimation to examine the
structural interrelations among the study constructs. The results reaffirmed the mediated effects model, showing
that empowering leadership exerts a significant indirect effect on collective teacher innovativeness through
collective teacher efficacy. Further analyses demonstrated that school power distance functions as a key
contextual variable, amplifying the conditional indirect effect of empowering leadership on collective teacher
innovativeness. We conclude with implications for policy and practice.

1. Introduction
In contemporary educational systems, collective teacher innovativeness is increasingly regarded as indispensable for advancing sustainable school improvement and student learning (Buyukgoze et al.,
2024; Nguyen et al., 2021). The ability of a group of teachers to
collaborate, generate, and implement novel practices has become a
defining factor in schools’ responsiveness to shifting societal expectations, rapid technological change, and complex policy mandates
(Dedering & Pietsch, 2025; Polatcan et al., 2024). Acknowledging this
importance, school leadership scholars have increasingly examined the
potential factors that foster or inhibit collective teacher innovativeness
(Cao et al., 2025; Demir & Kalman, 2025). Within this literature, school
leadership has consistently been identified as a central determinant of
organisational change, shaping professional environments, facilitating
collaborative problem-solving, and cultivating cultures of experimentation that enable innovation to thrive (Beycioglu & Kondakci, 2014;
Meyer et al., 2023).
Yet, despite the growing emphasis on teacher innovativeness, several
critical gaps remain in our understanding of how leadership contributes
to collective innovation in schools. First, existing research has primarily

conceptualised innovativeness as an individual-level outcome, focusing
on teachers' personal creativity, innovative behaviour, or openness to
change (e.g., Gkorezis, 2016; Zhang & Bartol, 2010; Zhou et al., 2024).
While insightful, this body of work has devoted far less attention to
innovativeness as a collective, school-level capacity grounded in shared
beliefs. This imbalance is particularly evident in non-Western and
developing contexts such as Türkiye (e.g., Buyukgoze et al., 2024),
where system-level regulations largely shape school leadership practices. Second, although prior research links leadership approaches such
as instructional (Demir & Kalman, 2025), transformational (Polatcan
et al., 2024), and shared or distributed leadership (O'Shea, 2021) to
teacher innovative practices, these approaches may not fully capture the
participatory and autonomy-supportive practices most conducive to
innovation (Cheong et al., 2019; Sharma & Kirkman, 2015). In contrast,
empowering leadership has been theorised as particularly conducive to
collective forms of innovation by emphasising power sharing and
expanded professional autonomy (Cheong et al., 2019). Accordingly, by
redistributing decision-making authority and strengthening autonomy,
intrinsic motivation, and collective efficacy, empowering leadership
may encourage teachers' innovative practices (Konczak et al., 2000;
Zhang & Bartol, 2010; Zhu et al., 2019). However, empirical evidence

Third, the mechanisms linking empowering leadership and innovativeness require further theorisation (Sharma & Kirkman, 2015). Collective teacher efficacy, conceptualised as teachers’ shared belief in their
collective capacity to affect student learning (Tschannen-Moran & Barr,
2004), offers a compelling mediator. Unlike individual factors such as
job satisfaction or teacher autonomy, collective efficacy captures the
shared socio-cognitive basis that transforms leadership practices into
collective professional action (e.g., Kaya & Demir, 2025). In other
words, when leaders empower teachers, the critical outcome is not only
greater individual agency but also a strengthened sense of collective
capacity, which in turn enables collaborative risk-taking, problem-solving, and innovation (Gkorezis, 2016). Therefore, by positioning
collective teacher efficacy as the mediator, this study highlights the
socio-cognitive pathway through which leadership translates into
innovation at the school level.
Finally, scholars have emphasised that leadership is enacted and
interpreted within cultural contexts shaping how authority and influence are understood (Hallinger et al., 2025; Shengnan & Hallinger,
2021). At this point, power distance, defined as the extent to which
unequal power relations are accepted and legitimised, is particularly
relevant in Türkiye's centralised education system, where hierarchical
norms largely define principal-teacher interactions (Hofstede, 2011).
The arguments on the nature of research regarding cultural values
indicate that cultural orientations exhibit substantial heterogeneity
within national boundaries, with individuals differing not only across
countries but also within the same national context (Kirkman et al.,
2006). Therefore, some scholars analyse cultural variables at the individual level to better understand within-society variation in social
interaction patterns (Kirkman et al., 2009; Shengnan & Hallinger,
2021), while others aggregate individual-level data to examine organisational or school-level contextual effects (Liu & Yin, 2023; Özdemir
et al., 2023). Collectively, this body of research suggests that cultural
value orientations such as power distance can be meaningfully examined as contextual compositions of individual beliefs, which allows
scholars to capture how dominant belief patterns within organisations
shape social interactions and leadership processes.
Building on this perspective, we conceptualise power distance as a
compositional contextual school-level variable (school power distance),
representing the average level of teachers’ power distance beliefs within
a school. This operationalisation does not presume strong within-school
consensus or homogeneity of beliefs; instead, it captures the prevalence
of individual power distance orientations collectively shaping how
leadership practices are interpreted within a school (Enders & Tofighi,
2007). When a greater proportion of teachers in a given school hold
higher power distance beliefs, empowering leadership practices are
more likely to be perceived as legitimate, authority-endorsed signals
rather than discretionary actions consistent with evidence showing that
leadership effects on teacher efficacy are stronger in higher power distance contexts (Hallinger et al., 2025). This shared interpretive framing,
even without strong consensus, may facilitate translating empowering
leadership into collective efficacy beliefs and, ultimately, collective
teacher innovativeness. By contrast, in schools with lower average levels
of individual power distance beliefs, empowering leadership behaviours
may align more closely with prevailing egalitarian expectations, thereby
reducing their added value for strengthening collective efficacy and
innovation.
The contribution of this study to the global school leadership literature is fourfold. First, it integrates mediated and moderated effects into
a single multilevel model within Türkiye's centralised, high powerdistance context, extending beyond Western-centric studies by highlighting empowering leadership as a distinct, autonomy- and
participation-oriented lever (Konczak et al., 2000; Zhu et al., 2019).
Second, it identifies collective teacher efficacy as the socio-cognitive

pathway linking empowering leadership to collective teacher innovativeness. Third, by modelling school power distance as a compositional
contextual moderator, it shows how the effects of empowering leadership on collective teacher efficacy and collective teacher innovativeness
vary across school contexts with different power distance orientations,
thereby clarifying the contextual conditions under which empowering
leadership is more or less effective in high power-distance educational
settings (e.g., Cheong et al., 2019; Walker & Dimmock, 2012). Finally,
the use of nationally stratified sampling and Bayesian multilevel SEM
provides robust tests of direct, indirect, and conditional effects, which
enhances generalisability within centralised systems. Therefore, the
study was guided by the following research questions.
1. What are the direct and indirect effects of empowering leadership on
collective teacher innovativeness, with collective teacher efficacy
serving as a mediator?
2. Does school power distance moderate the effect of empowering
leadership on collective teacher efficacy?
3. Does school power distance condition the indirect relationship of
empowering leadership with collective teacher innovativeness via
collective teacher efficacy?
1.1. Context
The Turkish education system, with nearly 20 million students and
over one million teachers (MoNE, 2024a), has historically been characterised by strong centralisation and hierarchical governance. The
Ministry of National Education (MoNE) exercises control over teacher
recruitment, leadership appointments, curriculum, and school
accountability, leaving little room for local autonomy or participatory
decision-making (Kılınç et al., 2021). Within this structure, principals
have traditionally functioned as administrators of national directives,
while teachers have often been treated as implementers rather than
active agents of school improvement.
Over the past decade, however, national policy priorities have
increasingly shifted toward teacher empowerment, collaboration, and
innovation as key levers for enhancing school quality. Strategic documents such as the Teacher Strategy Paper (MoNE, 2017), the 2023 Education Vision (MoNE, 2018), and the Teaching Profession Law (MoNE,
2024) signal a gradual move toward recognising schools as learning
organisations and positioning principals as instructional leaders. Central
to these reforms is the expectation that school leaders build teacher
capacity, cultivate professional trust, and mobilise collective innovation
to meet evolving educational demands. In this evolving policy landscape, our study directly responds to the priorities articulated in Türkiye's national reform agenda.
Yet, cultural and systemic factors continue to complicate the
implementation of these reforms. Türkiye remains a high-power distance society (Hofstede, 2011), where hierarchical authority is widely
accepted and legitimised. As a result, teachers’ participation in shared
decision-making and innovation is often constrained by entrenched
norms of compliance and deference (Kılınç et al., 2021). By incorporating school power distance into our model as a contextual variable, we
thus capture how it interacts with leadership practices, potentially
amplifying or constraining the effects of policy reforms at the school
level. Beyond Türkiye, these dynamics resonate with other centralised
education systems, such as China, Singapore, and several Middle Eastern
countries, where reform agendas promoting empowerment and innovation must likewise contend with deeply rooted hierarchical traditions
(Liu et al., 2016; Ng & Wong, 2020).

A second line of research underscores the context-dependence of

Informed by contingency theory (Fiedler, 1964), this body of work argues that leadership effects are not uniform across settings but unfold in

Shengnan & Hallinger, 2021; Truong & Hallinger, 2017; Walker &

Effect Models (Pitner, 1988), which explicitly examine whether and how
leadership influence is conditioned by contextual variables such as
cultural value orientations.
Recent scholarship, for instance, demonstrates that hierarchical
cultural orientations, particularly power distance, can attenuate or

amplify leadership effects depending on the alignment between followers' cultural expectations and leaders' enactment of authority (e.g., Li
et al., 2025; Shengnan & Hallinger, 2021). Importantly, contemporary
multilevel research also emphasises that cultural variables such as
power distance can function not only as a national cultural trait but also
as a compositional contextual value orientation varying across schools
based on the aggregated beliefs of individual teachers (Özdemir et al.,
2023; Liu & Yin, 2023). When treated as such, school power distance
reflects the prevailing configuration of individual power distance beliefs
within a school, rather than a shared emergent climate, thereby shaping
how teachers collectively interpret principals’ leadership behaviours (e.
g., Liu & Yin, 2023).
Despite these advances, this contingency-based perspective has yet
to be fully extended to empowering leadership, which is still often
portrayed as universally beneficial rather than context contingent (Kaya
& Demir, 2025; Sharma & Kirkman, 2015). Therefore, the aggregated
configuration of individual power distance beliefs may shape the legitimacy attributed to principals’ leadership behaviours, thereby conditioning whether empowering leadership translates into shared efficacy
beliefs and collective innovativeness. Although Hofstede (1980) originally conceptualised cultural values at the societal level, subsequent
research has established power distance as a theoretically meaningful
individual-level construct, with evidence suggesting that within-society
variation may exceed between-society differences, underscoring the

1.3. Literature review and research hypotheses
1.3.1. Teacher collective innovativeness
Innovation, defined as the deliberate application of novel ideas,
methods, and practices to achieve meaningful improvements within an
organisation (Gopalakrishnan & Damanpour, 1997), rarely emerges
spontaneously. Its development depends on enabling conditions such as
openness to change and receptiveness to new ideas, referring to the
construct of innovativeness (Buyukgoze et al., 2024). Innovativeness has
been studied at multiple levels, from individual attitudes to organisational climates (Blömeke et al., 2021), providing a basis for examining
its collective manifestations in education. As Buske (2018) notes, collective teacher innovativeness transcends the sum of individual dispositions, creating multiplier effects on educational outcomes. In line with
this perspective, the TALIS framework defines teacher innovativeness as
a shared team characteristic and measures it as school team innovativeness (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). Building on this framework and
recent scholarship (Buske, 2018; Nguyen et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2024),
the present study conceptualises it at the school level as the collective
effort of teachers to generate ideas, embrace change, engage in collaborative problem-solving, and support one another in implementing
innovations.
Research consistently identifies leadership as a central driver of
teacher innovativeness. Evidence shows that distributed (Buyukgoze
et al., 2024), transformational (Hidayat & Patras, 2024), and empowering leadership (Zhu et al., 2019) function as key catalysts. Beyond
leadership, a supportive organisational culture characterised by
collaboration, professional autonomy (Nguyen et al., 2021), and
collectivist values (Erdoğan et al., 2024) also nurtures innovativeness.
Individual-level factors such as teacher agency (Polatcan et al., 2024),
self-efficacy (Hidayat & Patras, 2024), and psychological empowerment
(Zhu et al., 2019) contribute to the broader innovative climate, even
when the focus remains collective.

1.3.3. Empowering leadership
Scholarly interest in empowerment within organisations has grown
steadily in recent decades, driven by increasing reliance on collaborative
work, the rising complexity of jobs, and the flattening of organisational
structures (Cheong et al., 2019; Sharma & Kirkman, 2015). Research on
empowerment is typically conceptualised along two complementary
perspectives: structural and psychological. The structural perspective
views empowerment as arising from external conditions, particularly
leader behaviours, whereas the psychological perspective frames it as a
four-dimensional state reflecting employees’ perceptions of meaningfulness, competence, self-determination, and impact (A. Lee et al.,
2018). This study adopts the structural perspective because it captures
the leader-driven practices, such as delegating authority, promoting
self-directed decision-making, and supporting knowledge sharing, that
establish the organisational conditions necessary for teacher initiative
and collective innovation (e.g., Lin et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024).
Accordingly, and drawing on Konczak et al.’s (2000) framework, we
conceptualise empowering leadership as encompassing six interrelated
dimensions: delegation of authority, encouragement of self-directed
decision-making, promotion of self-directed problem-solving, information sharing, skill development, and coaching for innovative performance. Delegation of authority strengthens teachers' autonomy and
intrinsic motivation (Vecchio et al., 2010), while self-directed decision-making and problem-solving foster independent goal setting, proactive planning, and initiative-taking (Lee & Nie, 2017). Information
sharing and skill development equip teachers with the knowledge and
competencies necessary for effective practice, while coaching for innovative performance fosters risk-taking, idea generation, and constructive
feedback, reframing errors as opportunities for learning (Özdemir et al.,
2023). Over the past three decades, such empowerment practices have
become central to organisational designs that prioritise autonomy,
self-leadership, and delegated responsibility (Lee & Nie, 2017). Within
schools, they position principals as key agents in cultivating climates of
resilience, engagement, professional growth, and innovation (Uslukaya,
2025; Zhu et al., 2019; Özdemir et al., 2023).
Empirical evidence consistently shows that empowering leadership
is a robust predictor of teachers' psychological empowerment (Atik &
Çelik, 2020; Lee & Nie, 2017; Zhu et al., 2019), particularly perceptions
of competence and impact, which are central to the development of
efficacy beliefs (Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001). Building on this evidence, an expanding body of research demonstrates that school leadership is strongly associated with collective teacher efficacy (e.g., Çalık
et al., 2012; Goddard et al., 2021; Ross & Gray, 2006). When principals

1.3.2. Collective teacher efficacy
Building on Bandura's concept of self-efficacy in Social Cognitive
Theory (Bandura, 1977), collective efficacy extends efficacy beliefs to
the group level. It reflects a shared conviction in a group's collective
capability (Bandura, 1997). Within schools, collective teacher efficacy
refers to teachers' shared belief that, as a faculty, they can successfully
undertake the actions needed to improve student learning outcomes
(Goddard, 2001). As recent reviews emphasise, this construct is inherently group-level and warrants multilevel investigation, which is largely
neglected in previous studies (Sahli Lozano et al., 2025). In this study,
we adopt this definition and treat collective teacher efficacy as a
school-level construct.
The significance of collective teacher efficacy is well-documented
through its association with critical educational outcomes. Evidence,
for instance, suggests that collective teacher efficacy promotes teacher
job satisfaction across diverse cultures (Klassen et al., 2010). In addition,
it influences classroom processes by driving student achievement, as it

unfolding through shared beliefs and dispositions among teachers.

1.3.4. The moderation of power distance
Power distance refers to the degree to which individuals accept unequal distributions of authority within organisations and societies. Individuals with higher power distance orientations are more likely to
defer to principals’ decisions, comply with authority, and accept limited
involvement in decision-making, whereas those lower in power distance
tend to prefer autonomy, participation, and shared responsibility
(Hofstede, 2011; Kirkman et al., 2009). This cultural orientation has
been shown to shape principal-teacher interactions most strongly in
centralised school systems, where hierarchical authority exerts a
powerful influence on decision-making and teacher agency (Hendawy
Al-Mahdy et al., 2024; Shengnan & Hallinger, 2021). Although power
distance is typically treated as a national-level value (Hofstede, 2011),
researchers also examine the construct at lower levels of analysis, as it
varies significantly among individuals, teams, and organisations within
societies (Alazmi, 2024; Özdemir et al., 2023; Guo & Lu, 2018; Kirkman
et al., 2009; Shengnan & Hallinger, 2021).
Accordingly, cultural value orientations such as power distance can
be conceptualised as individually held beliefs that, when aggregated,
form compositional contextual indicators of the belief environment
within a group or organisation, without presuming strong within-group

2.1. Procedure and participants
This study targeted K-12 schools in Türkiye, with data collected at
the school level in 2025. The sampling strategy was based on the
Nomenclature of Territorial Units for Statistics (NUTS), a classification
system developed by the European Union to provide a standardised
framework for regional statistics (Eurostat, 2016). This system has been
widely adopted in international surveys such as the Teaching and
Learning International Survey (TALIS) and the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA). To ensure national coverage, one
province at the NUTS 2 level was selected to represent each of the 12
NUTS 1 regions in Türkiye. The selected provinces and their NUTS 2
codes were as follows: İstanbul (TR10), Tekirdağ (TR21), İzmir (TR31),
Eskişehir (TR41), Ankara (TR51), Adana (TR62), Kayseri (TR72), Zonguldak (TR81), Erzurum (TRA1), Malatya (TRB1), and Gaziantep

(TRC1). Across these regions, a total of 10,238 schools were identified
(MoNE, 2024). For each province, the number of schools to be included
in the sample was calculated proportionally to the total number of
schools across the 12 provinces. A stratified sampling approach was
applied to maximise representativeness. While no sampling weights
were employed, safeguards such as balanced stratification and
post-stratification validation were implemented to minimise potential

Once institutional consent was obtained, the questionnaire link was
distributed via email and WhatsApp for completion by teachers within
those schools. To ensure anonymity, no personally identifiable information (e.g., names or email addresses) was collected. Google Forms

prior multilevel research in education (e.g., Liu & Yin, 2023). After
applying these criteria, our final sample consisted of 926 teachers nested

School Power Distance (SPD). We measured SPD using the five-item
instrument originally developed by Yoo et al. (2011) to assess Hofstede's cultural dimensions and adapted into Turkish by Saylık (2019).
We modelled SPD as a latent factor. Respondents rated each statement
on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5
(strongly agree). The scale demonstrated satisfactory psychometric
properties, as indicated by composite reliability (ωWithin = 0.866;
ωBetween = 0.999) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results (x2/df

lower positions.”
Empowering Leadership (EL). We assessed this construct using the 17item Empowering Leadership Scale, originally developed by Konczak
et al. (2000) to capture EL behaviours and subsequently adapted into

including delegation of authority, accountability, and support. We

three first-order factors loaded onto a higher-order EL factor that was
used in the structural model. Respondents rated each statement on a

SRMRBetween = 0.082, CFI = 0.949, TLI = 0.943) (Hu & Bentler, 1999).

item is: “My school principal gives me authority equal to my responsibility in the tasks assigned to me.”

discipline efficacy. We estimated a second-order multilevel measurement model, in which these two first-order factors loaded onto a
higher-order CTE factor that was used in the structural analyses. Respondents rated each statement on a five-point Likert scale ranging from
1 (not at all) to 5 (completely). CFA results demonstrated acceptable

SRMRWithin = 0.039, and SRMRBetween = 0.085). Composite reliability

validity. A sample item is: “To what extent can the teachers in your
school enable students to learn in a meaningful (life-oriented, functional, etc.) way?”

item is: “Most teachers in this school make efforts to develop new ideas
for teaching and learning.”

3. Results

2.3. Common method bias

We first conducted descriptive analyses by examining the means,
standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis values of all study variables,
along with their intercorrelations. As presented in Table 1, the average
scores were EL (M = 4.12, SD = 0.25), CTE (M = 3.96, SD = 0.25), CTI
(M = 3.16, SD = 0.24), and SPD (M = 1.97, SD = 0.30). The skewness
and kurtosis values for all variables fell within the acceptable range of
− 2 to +2 (George & Mallery, 2010), suggesting no substantial deviations
from normality. At the school level, EL showed positive correlations
with both CTE (r = 0.534) and CTI (r = 0.421), while CTE was also
positively related to CTI (r = 0.733).
To further assess the interrelationships among the variables, we
employed Bayesian MSEM estimation. We also checked for potential
multicollinearity by examining the tolerance index (TI > 0.20) and the
variance inflation factor (VIF<5). Results showed that TI values ranged
between 0.687 and 0.987, and VIF values were between 1.014 and
1.455, providing evidence that multicollinearity was not a concern. In
Table 1, SPD showed limited between-school variance (ICC(1) = 0.04;
ICC(2) = 0.35). The within-school agreement index was moderate (rWG
(J) = 0.691). Although these values would be insufficient to support SPD
as a strongly emergent shared school climate, prior work suggests that
relatively low ICC(1) values can still be observed in school- and teambased research, particularly when agreement indicators provide supporting evidence (e.g., Chen & Bliese, 2002). Building on the distinction
that aggregated individual perceptions do not necessarily constitute a
shared climate (Ahn et al., 2024) and that individual and collective
constructs are conceptually different (Boyce & Bowers, 2018), we treat
SPD as a contextual school-level indicator rather than an emergent
climate construct. Accordingly, we interpret the Level 2 moderation
associated with SPD as a contextual composition effect and discuss it
with appropriate caution. Accordingly, SPD's school-level moderating
role is interpreted as reflecting a contextual mean orientation rather
than a shared school climate.

3.1. Descriptive analysis

Cross-sectional studies are particularly vulnerable to common
method bias because data on independent and dependent variables are
often obtained from the same respondents (Podsakoff et al., 2003). To
address this concern, we implemented several procedural safeguards.
Participant anonymity was ensured to minimise socially desirable
responding, the survey title was withheld to prevent response priming,
and the order of dependent, mediating, and independent variables was
counterbalanced. Additionally, all items were reviewed by experts to
enhance construct validity and minimise potential methodological effects. To complement these safeguards, we also applied Harman's
one-factor test to assess common method variance statistically. This test
involves an exploratory factor analysis of all measurement items to
determine whether a single factor accounts for more than 50% of the
variance. The analysis showed that the total variance explained by a
single factor was 37%, which is below the threshold and indicates the
absence of a dominant common factor.
2.4. Analytical approach
Given the nested nature of our data, we conducted a two-level
structural equation modelling (SEM) approach. The analysis proceeded in two stages. First, we generated descriptive statistics, including
means, standard deviations, and distributional properties (skewness and
kurtosis). We checked for multicollinearity using the tolerance index
(TI) and variance inflation factor (VIF) values. In addition, we calculated
interrater reliability indices (ICC1, ICC2, and rWG(J)) to justify the
aggregation of teacher responses to the school level. Second, we tested
the study model across two levels (within and between schools) using
Mplus 8.7. Following recommendations in the multilevel SEM literature,
a random-effects specification was applied, and all latent constructs
were estimated with the BAYES estimator and the Gibbs sampler (RW
algorithm). We used Bayesian estimation due to its advantages in
handling complex models with relatively modest Level 2 sample sizes.
Prior research has noted that this method often yields more stable
parameter estimates and improved convergence in multilevel models,
particularly when the number of clusters is limited or when models
include random slopes and latent interactions (Asparouhov & Muthén,
2010; Muthén & Asparouhov, 2017). In our case, frequentist estimators
produced unstable solutions in preliminary tests, whereas the Bayesian
Gibbs sampler achieved satisfactory convergence diagnostics
(PSR<1.05). We employed Mplus default priors for Bayesian estimation.
Regression coefficients and factor loadings were assigned diffuse normal
priors centred at zero. Variance parameters were assigned Mplus default
inverse-gamma priors. Because indicators and covariates were standardised and grand-mean centred prior to estimation, these zero-centred
diffuse priors can be regarded as weakly informative, allowing the
posterior to be primarily data-driven (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). We
relied on item-level data rather than aggregated school-level mean
scores. For each construct, we specified latent factors at the
between-school level using teachers’ responses as indicators. In addition, CTI was modelled as a latent factor at within- and between-levels.
This allowed us to simultaneously evaluate mediation (H4) and
moderation (H5-H6) effects. The substantive interpretation of CTI primarily pertains to the between-school (collective) level, consistent with
its theoretical framing as a school-level construct. To evaluate convergence, we confirmed that the Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) value was
lower than 1.05 (maximum number of iterations = 50,000 and PSR =
1.043), suggesting that our model had successfully converged
(Asparouhov & Muthén, 2010).

3.2. Model testing
3.2.1. Mediating effect analysis
We conducted multilevel structural equation modelling (MSEM)
with Bayesian estimation to test our research questions. The analysis
proceeded in three stages: (i) testing the mediated effects model, specifically examining whether EL exerted direct and indirect effects on CTI
through CTE, (ii) evaluating the moderating effects by testing whether
SPD moderated the relationship between EL and CTE, and (iii) examining the moderated mediation effect, namely whether PD influenced
the indirect pathway from EL to CTI via CTE. To confirm convergence,
we inspected the Mplus TECH8 output. The PSR value reached 1.048,
slightly below the recommended threshold of 1.05, indicating that the
estimation converged successfully (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2010). We
evaluated Bayesian model fit by using posterior predictive checking. The
posterior predictive p-value was close to 0.50 (PPP = 0.43), and the 95%
posterior predictive interval for the χ2 discrepancy included zero, indicating an adequate model fit. We also re-estimated our model with two
different prior specifications to assess the impact of prior selection on
the findings. We compared posterior means and 95% credible intervals
(CrI) for direct, indirect, and conditional indirect effects. Parameter
estimates and uncertainty intervals largely overlapped in the two runs;
direction, magnitude, and inferential results did not change significantly. Therefore, we decided that the findings were not sensitive to
prior selection. Fig. 2 presents the final MSEM results.

only by the leadership style enacted but also by the level of outcomes

5. Implications for policy and practice
Our study has implications for policymakers and practitioners in
Türkiye and other comparable countries with centralised governance
structures, where power distance orientations tend to be more prevalent.
For policymakers, the results underscore that empowering leadership
should be recognised as a strategic and evidence-based lever for
advancing reform goals such as those articulated in the Teacher Strategy
Paper (MoNE, 2017), the 2023 Education Vision (MoNE, 2018), and the
Teaching Profession Law (MoNE, 2024). Leadership standards and
professional development frameworks should explicitly and systematically embed competencies related to participative decision-making,
trust-building, and autonomy support, moving decisively beyond a
narrow focus on administrative compliance. Therefore, principal leadership preparation programs, recently launched by MoNE (2024),
should incorporate structured experiential training modules that simulate empowering leadership practices, provide intensive and structured
mentoring to facilitate teacher-led initiatives, and rigorously develop
principals’ capacity to mobilise collective teacher efficacy as a strategic
lever for collaborative innovation. Policymakers should also consider
how teacher education systems cultivate the shared sense of capability
and collective effort to innovate. Institutional arrangements that integrate pre- and in-service training for teachers and principals offer a key
opportunity to design cohort-based programmes that target group-level
capacities, such as collective efficacy and innovativeness, rather than
focusing solely on individual competencies. At the same time, evaluation and incentive systems must fundamentally shift toward rigorous,
outcome-based criteria that reward principals who empower teachers as
co-owners of school improvement and who can demonstrate measurable
gains in collective efficacy and innovation. Without such binding policy
measures, reform efforts risk remaining symbolic rather than
transformative.
For practitioners, our findings provide evidence-based guidance on
how school leaders can foster collective teacher efficacy and, in turn,
collective innovativeness within centralised education systems. Principals can support this process by establishing teacher-led professional
learning communities focused on concrete school challenges, codesigning instructional improvement initiatives with teachers, and
delegating responsibility for pilot innovations to staff teams with
adequate authority and resources. Beyond delegation, empowering
leadership involves open information sharing, opportunities for professional development, and coaching practices that encourage experimentation and frame mistakes as learning opportunities. Central to these
practices is strengthening teachers’ shared confidence in their collective
capacity, which our study identifies as the key pathway linking leadership to innovation. In Türkiye, this requires working within existing
hierarchical structures by institutionalising teacher voice through joint
decision-making bodies, innovation teams, and protected time for
collaboration. More broadly, in high power-distance contexts, empowering leadership may be particularly beneficial when principals use their
formal authority to legitimise participation while supporting

teacher efficacy and collective innovativeness are more or less pronounced. This contribution responds to longstanding calls to situate
leadership research more explicitly within culturally and institutionally
bounded contexts (e.g., Sharma & Kirkman, 2015) and underscores the
value of examining leadership processes in centralised and hierarchically structured education systems to extend the applicability of
existing leadership theory.

6. Limitations and future research avenues
This study has several limitations that also point to directions for
future research. First, the cross-sectional survey design limits causal
inference regarding the relationships among the constructs. Longitudinal or experimental studies are thus needed to examine how empowering leadership and collective efficacy develop over time in shaping
innovation. Second, reliance on self-reported measures raises the possibility of common method bias and social desirability effects. Although
multilevel modelling helps address these concerns by separating
teacher- and school-level variance, future research would benefit from
using multiple data sources, such as classroom observations, peer or
leader ratings, and external indicators of innovativeness. Third, our sole
reliance on self-reported measures raises the possibility of common
method bias and social desirability effects. Although we implemented
procedural remedies (e.g., assuring anonymity/confidentiality and
counterbalancing item order) to reduce evaluation apprehension and
method effects, such steps can mitigate but do not eliminate common
method bias. In addition, while multilevel modelling partitions variance
at both teacher and school levels, it does not fully rule out shared
method variance; therefore, some associations may be partially inflated.
Future studies could strengthen validity by incorporating multiple data
sources such as classroom observations, peer or leader ratings, and
external assessments of innovativeness, and by using time-lagged or
longitudinal designs. Fourth, by focusing exclusively on power distance
as a moderator, this study provides only a partial view of the broader
cultural dynamics that may shape leadership effects. Fifth, the low ICC
values indicate limited between-school consensus regarding power distance beliefs. Therefore, the aggregated PD measure should not be
interpreted as a shared climate but rather as an approximation of
contextual belief patterns. This also supports the arguments suggesting
that variations in cultural values at the individual level within the same
society can be larger than those between societies, which results in
measuring power distance at the individual level (e.g., Dorfman &
Howell, 1988; Kirkman et al., 2009). This limits strong climate-based
interpretations of the moderation effect. Future research could extend
our model by incorporating additional contextual moderators, such as
uncertainty avoidance or individualism-collectivism, to capture a more
comprehensive picture of cultural contingencies. Finally, while collective teacher efficacy emerged as a robust mediator, other socio-cognitive
and relational mechanisms, including relational trust climate or shared
teacher identity, may also channel the effects of leadership on collective
innovativeness. Investigating these alternative group-level mediators
would provide a more comprehensive account of how leadership drives
school-level innovation.

7. Conclusion
This study examined the interplay of empowering leadership, collective teacher efficacy, and collective teacher innovativeness in the
Turkish school system, with particular attention to the moderating role
of school power distance. The results indicate that empowering leadership exerts a positive indirect effect on collective teacher innovativeness
through collective teacher efficacy, and that school power distance
strengthens the path between empowering leadership and collective
efficacy, yielding a larger conditional indirect effect at higher levels of
power distance. These findings highlight the importance of recognising
not only the socio-cognitive processes through which leadership is
translated into innovation in schools but also the cultural and contextual
conditions that shape the strength of these relationships. By integrating
mediation and moderation within a single multilevel framework and
applying it to a high power-distance educational context, the study
clarifies how empowering leadership operates under specific cultural
