ABSTRACT
The definition and measurement of creative thinking remain subjects of debate. In 2022, the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) introduced creative thinking as a new
domain, conceptualized across three facets: generating diverse ideas, generating creative ideas,
and evaluating and improving ideas. While this framework provides a valuable foundation,
questions regarding the construct validity of PISA’s creative thinking assessment persist. This
study adopts a data-driven approach to examine the construct validity of the three creative
thinking facets by exploring their relationships with psychological constructs, demographic and
process data, and item characteristics. Using item-level response data from PISA 2022, we
modeled how individual item scores relate to these variables. To account for the hierarchical
data structure and potential confounding between item content and score, we employed a multilevel predictive modeling approach based on machine learning algorithms. Explainable AI (XAI)
techniques were applied post-hoc to identify the most influential predictors and their effects.
Results showed that item characteristics such as content domain, process-related indicators,
and academic competences in mathematics, reading, and science were the strongest predictors
for item-level scores across all facets. In contrast, self-reported creativity and engagement
in creative activities showed limited relevance. Accumulated local effects analyses revealed
systematic but modest differences in predicted scores across item domains and facets, indicating
subtle interactions between content, competences, and performance. However, these differences
were small, suggesting limited empirical differentiation between the three facets as implemented
in PISA 2022. From a validity perspective, these findings imply that while PISA’s creative
thinking assessment captures meaningful variance related to engagement and aptitude, it may
not fully distinguish between theoretically distinct aspects of creative thinking. The study further
highlights the utility of machine learning and XAI in construct validation research.

innovation in both personal and professional contexts (OECD, 2019a; World Economic Forum, 2023). Specifically, over the last
decade, the relevance of creativity for education has increased markedly as societies face rapid technological, social, and environmental change (Hernández-Torrano & Ibrayeva, 2020). Acknowledging this importance, the international large-scale Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA) introduced measures of creative thinking as the innovative domain in its 2022 cycle (OECD,

2023b), marking a significant step towards a better understanding of how creative thinking is developed across diverse educational

with its dedicated assessment of creative thinking, provides an unprecedented dataset, offering immense potential for research and

the assessments’ items measure what they are supposed to measure.
Acknowledging the multifaceted nature of creativity (cf. Walia, 2019), the PISA 2022 framework conceptualized creative
thinking in terms of three distinct facets (or processes) (also ‘‘ideation processes’’; cf. OECD, 2024, p. 220): generating diverse
ideas, generating creative ideas, and evaluating and improving ideas. These processes were then assessed systematically across
four content domains: written expression, visual expression, social problem-solving, and scientific problem-solving. Although this
procedure arguably meaningfully extended construct coverage of creative thinking, the official reporting of OECD only ever reported
performance in creative thinking collapsed across items, and thus across facets and domains. This approach, while convenient
and useful for international comparisons, raised fundamental questions regarding the construct validity of the creative thinking
domain (Barbot & Kaufman, 2025) and it remains questionable whether combining these items into a single dimension is defensible
from a validity perspective.
Building on these concerns and acknowledging open questions about the construct validity of creative thinking in PISA 2022,
the current study takes a data-driven, exploratory approach to identify variables that predict item-level responses of the creative
thinking domain of PISA 2022. To do so, we assume that the distinction between the three facets of creative thinking is theoretically
meaningful and we therefore examine item-level predictors for each of the three facets. Using a multi-level predictive modeling
approach combined with explainable AI (XAI) (Dwivedi et al., 2023), we seek to map the distinct ‘conceptual profiles’ of each facet,
taking also into account the four content domains as well as person-related (cognitive and/or dispositional) and background-related
variables as predictors for item-level scores. The herein reported results aim to provide first exploratory insights into the extent to
which facet- and domain-level reporting might capture meaningful variation beyond the currently used composite score of creative
thinking. In the next sections, we will briefly provide a conceptual background of the creative thinking assessment in PISA 2022,
illustrate how numerous predictors of performance are conceivable, and explain how a data-driven machine learning approach could
inform more theory-based future research.

1.1. What the PISA items are supposed to measure
Creativity is often defined in terms of its outcomes. For example, a standard definition of creativity, synthesized by Runco
and Jaeger (2012), posits that in order for an idea or a product to be considered creative, it must be both novel (original) and
useful (effective or appropriate). While this certainly provides a robust foundation, it leaves room for how the underlying cognitive
processes of creativity are conceptualized and hence allows wide variation in how creativity is eventually operationalized in research
and educational assessment. As a result, research and assessment practices have varied widely in what they treat as evidence of
creativity, from the originality of a finished product (e.g., Acar et al., 2017) to the ways in which ideas are generated (e.g., Lubart,
2001).
In PISA 2022, creative thinking was deliberately designed to move beyond such product-based understandings of creativity.
In fact, the PISA assessment defined creative thinking as ‘‘the competence to engage productively in the generation, evaluation,
and improvement of ideas that can result in original and effective solutions, advances in knowledge, and impactful expressions of
imagination’’ (OECD, 2019b, p.11). Delineating this definition in the context of the current PISA assessment, where 15-year old
students were assessed, two frameworks can be identified that informed the development of tasks and items (cf. OECD, 2024). First,
the assessment focused on ‘‘little-c’’ creativity (e.g., Simonton, 2013), which can be understood as everyday, practice-based form of
creativity that any student could demonstrate in more or less familiar contexts (Kaufman & Beghetto, 2009). This stands in contrast
to ‘‘Big-C’’ creativity, which usually refers to some eminent achievements that would be out of scope for the life stages ordinary
students find themselves in.
Second, the assessment targeted the process component of creativity, arguably drawing on frameworks such as the ’4 P’s’
model (Rhodes, 1961), which breaks down the study of creativity into four distinct but interrelated aspects: the creative Person
(examining personality traits and cognitive styles), the creative Process (the cognitive steps and strategies involved), the creative
Product (the tangible or intangible outcomes), and the creative Press (the environmental or contextual factors that influence
creativity). Of these four perspectives, specifically the Process component is most relevant for educational assessments like PISA
because it reflects the cognitive operations that can be taught and developed to produce novel and useful outcomes, aligning with
the definition of creative thinking as a process (Green et al., 2024).
Beyond these frameworks, another central debate in creativity research concerns whether creative thinking reflects a general
ability (Plucker & Beghetto, 2004) or some sort of domain-specific knowledge (Baer, 2016). The generalist view posits a core set of
transferable skills, while the specificist view argues that meaningful creativity is fundamentally tied to domain-specific knowledge
and conventions (cf. Baer, 2016). Contemporary theories often propose a hybrid view, where general processes are contingent upon
domain-specific knowledge (cf. Plucker & Beghetto, 2004). The PISA 2022 framework, with its focus on creative thinking across
different thinking processes and content domains, might provide new insights regarding these debates.

1.2. PISA’s creative thinking assessment: Sources of heterogeneity
Within the PISA process-oriented framework of creative thinking, the OECD specified three ideation processes in their competency
model of creative thinking, that the items were intended to reflect: (1) generate diverse ideas, conceptually emphasizing ideational
fluency (e.g., Weiss et al., 2024a); (2) generate creative ideas, focusing on originality (e.g., Runco & Dumas, 2018); and (3) evaluate
and improving ideas, which focuses on the ability to refine existing ideas to enhance its quality or usefulness (e.g., A. Cropley,
2006; OECD, 2019b). These processes were assessed across four content domains: (1) written expression, (2) visual expression, (3)
social problem-solving, and (4) scientific problem-solving (please see OECD, 2024, p. 222 and 223 for a more detailed description
of these domains). These domains were chosen so that they would reflect a range of contexts relevant to the adolescents’ learning
environments. Given the design of this assessment, variability in students’ performance can arise from multiple sources — most
notably from differences between the three ideation processes themselves and from the four content domains in which they are
embedded.
A useful theoretical lens for interpreting the three facets is the long-standing distinction between divergent and convergent
thinking in creativity research (A. Cropley, 2006; Guilford, 1956; Lubart et al., 2013). Divergent thinking involves generating a
wide variety of ideas, exploring multiple possibilities, and making flexible connections between concepts (Runco & Acar, 2012). It
can be understood as the engine of ideational fluency and originality and the most frequently used indicator of creative thinking in
the literature (Runco & Acar, 2012; Weiss et al., 2024b). Convergent thinking, in contrast, is the process of analyzing, evaluating, and
refining ideas to arrive at the single best or most effective solution (Guilford, 1956), but has received comparatively little attention as
indicators of creative thinking in creativity research. However, both are considered indispensable for successful creative output (D.H.
Cropley, 2006), and both are arguably tapped through items of the PISA 2022 creative thinking assessment (Acar & Shen, 2025).
The facet generate diverse ideas aligns most closely with divergent thinking, as it requires students to produce multiple, flexible
responses that span different conceptual categories. Conversely, generating creative ideas and especially evaluating and improving
ideas involves stronger elements of convergent thinking, emphasizing the deliberate selection, refinement, or elaboration of ideas
to possibly maximize their originality and usefulness.
From a construct validity perspective, these facets may therefore represent partially distinct constructs. In our case, structuring the
assessment around the three ideation processes and focusing on the item-level, reflects this view particularly: creative performance
depends not only on producing many ideas but also on refining them into appropriate, higher-quality responses. This distinction
between divergent and convergent processes is hence central to our study’s construct validity perspective. If the three facets draw
on partly different cognitive skills, then their predictors – and the extent to which they contribute to students’ overall scores – may
differ as well, which eventually has implications for whether a single composite score adequately captures the construct of creative
thinking.
At the same time, it is important to note that validity evidence is often discussed in terms of factorial structure, but construct
validation does not need to rely exclusively on factor-analytic approaches: it also encompasses evidence from a construct’s
nomological network, that is, whether performance shows coherent patterns of convergent and discriminant relations with other
theoretically relevant variables (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). Accordingly, if the three ideation processes proposed by the PISA
framework reflect partly different cognitive underpinnings, it follows that they should show differential relationships with other
variables and, thus, differential predictor profiles across facets. This logic is in line with prediction-oriented construct validation
approaches used in the divergent thinking literature (e.g. Forthmann et al., 2020; Preckel et al., 2011), where performance
under different task conditions (speeded vs. unspeeded) was related to theoretically meaningful predictors (e.g., mental speed,
reasoning) rather than being evaluated primarily via factor-analytic structure. Although these works did not focus on the item level,
they illustrate how construct-relevant inferences can be supported by testing whether expected predictor patterns emerge across
meaningful task conditions.
In addition to cognitive sources of heterogeneity, task- and context-related factors may also influence performance. Ironically,
although the PISA framework conceptualized creative thinking from a process perspective across multiple domains, the evidence of
students’ performance was inevitably product-based. That is, each task required an observable response, for example some written
idea, that could later on be scored. There were facet-specific scoring rubrics, as outlined in the OECD report (OECD, 2024, pp. 223) to
approximately 1̃40.000 students from more than 60 countries and economies. The assessment consisted of 18 distinct contextualized
scenarios, or ‘units’. Within these units, a total of 32 scored items were administered. Each item was designed to measure one of
the three core cognitive facets, while each unit is situated within one of the four content domains. A single unit may have therefore
contained multiple items that tapped into different facets of creative thinking within a specific domain context.
Moreover, the PISA framework states that ‘‘in each domain, students complete everyday tasks that do not require technical
knowledge or skills in order to minimize the role of prior experience as a significant driver of performance’’ (OECD, 2019b, p.
21). However, research has shown that specific characteristics of the task domain itself can elicit fundamentally different response
patterns, even when the tasks are perceived as similar in other respects (Reiter-Palmon et al., 2009). These findings suggest that
the specific cognitive and experiential demands of a task may be a primary driver of creative performance, even when an attempt
is made to keep it simple.
1.3. Current evidence on the construct validity of PISA’s creative thinking assessment
Empirical evidence on the construct validity of PISA’s creative thinking assessment remains scarce. Most available information
comes from the test development phase and from OECD reports that analyzed plausible values of a single composite creative

thinking score rather than the item or facet level (Barbot & Kaufman, 2025). The OECD documented several validation steps
(e.g., international expert reviews, cognitive laboratories, pilot studies, translation reviews, and large-scale field trials) to ensure
procedural quality and cross-country comparability (OECD, 2019b, 2023b). While these steps might establish face, procedural, and
content validity (Goecke et al., 2025), they do not yet address whether the items function as intended to measure the distinct creative
processes of the competency model as offered by the OECD.
Analyses of the composite creative thinking score have yielded a mix of expected and puzzling findings. On the one hand,
the score shows moderate positive associations with academic achievement and with dispositions such as openness and creative
self-efficacy, which is broadly consistent with theory (OECD, 2024). The findings present initial evidence for convergent validity,
noting theoretically anticipated positive correlations with academic achievement and student-level dispositions like openness and
creative self-efficacy (OECD, 2024), but also reveal several perplexing findings that call the overall validity into question (cf. Barbot
& Kaufman, 2025).
On the other hand, two findings raise concerns about what is being measured. First, the correlations between creative
thinking and the core academic domains of mathematics and reading are unusually high compared to the meta-analytical average
correlation of 𝑟 = 0.22 reported in a comprehensive meta-analysis of over 120 studies on the link between creativity and academic
achievement (Gajda et al., 2017), suggesting potential construct confounding. This large discrepancy raises the critical question of
whether the PISA items are overly reliant on general academic competences thereby measuring competences that are not distinct to
creative thinking (cf. Barbot & Kaufman, 2025). Second, the PISA report notes unexpected negative associations between creative
thinking scores and students’ engagement in creative activities (also cf. Kagan & Dumas, 2025), contradicting prior research that
typically finds modest positive links between creative performance and creative activities (e.g. Jauk et al., 2014; Weiss et al., 2025).
Examining obvious background factors such as socio-economic status (SES) and gender revealed that creative thinking performance in PISA 2022 was systematically influenced by broader educational inequalities. Across participating countries, students
from higher-SES backgrounds obtained higher scores, and girls consistently outperformed boys in creative thinking (Goecke et al.,
2026). These patterns paralleled the well-established disparities observed in other PISA domains like reading and mathematics and
indicated that demographic and contextual influences played a significant role in students’ performance on the creative thinking
assessment.
One explanation for these inconsistencies is that aggregating all items into a single score obscures meaningful variation (Barbot
& Kaufman, 2025; Goecke et al., 2026). A student’s performance on a task within a specific content domain (e.g., scientific problemsolving) likely depends on an interaction between their general creative thinking processes and their individual, domain-relevant
skills (cf. Baer, 2016). For example, success in mathematical or scientific creativity often depends on the underlying domain
knowledge in addition to ideational fluency (Schoevers et al., 2020). For instance, a student with strong scientific knowledge
but moderate ideational fluency might excel at a scientific creativity item, while another student with high fluency but weaker
knowledge might struggle. By averaging performance across all facets and domains into a single score, PISA’s reporting method may
neutralize these critical interactions, effectively obscuring any meaningful individual differences that would constitute a student’s
unique creative profile.
In sum, the existing evidence base for construct validity of creative thinking from the PISA 2022 assessment is incomplete. It
remains unclear, first, which cognitive, dispositional, and background characteristics drive performance for each of the three creative
thinking facets: generate diverse ideas, generate creative ideas, and evaluate and improve ideas; and second, to what extent this
performance is sensitive to the content domain in which it is assessed. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond the composite
score to investigate performance patterns at the level of individual items and facets, which is the focus of the present study. Our study
is designed to address precisely this challenge by unpacking the composite score and examining its underlying components from
the bottom up. By adopting an exploratory, data-driven random forest approach, we aim at capturing these relationship patterns,
even if they are complex and possibly non-linear. Moreover, by including the full range of available PISA predictors, the analysis
allows for the identification of unanticipated associations that may remain undetected in strictly hypothesis-driven research.
1.4. The present study
In their framework, the OECD distinguishes between various sources that could potentially influence creative thinking performance in the classroom (OECD, 2024, p. 217). Crucially, creative thinking performance in PISA 2022 is likely shaped by a wide
constellation of these sources of variance. Both internal resources such as cognitive abilities, domain-specific knowledge, openness
to experience, task motivation, and creative self-beliefs, and external influences such as classroom climate, cultural norms, and
educational practices can affect how students approach and solve the assessment’s tasks (OECD, 2024). Prior research suggests that
these influences can interact with one another. For example, supportive classroom environments can encourage students to take
creative risks (e.g., Beghetto & Kaufman, 2014), whereas the impact of self-efficacy on creativity is moderated by the perceived
difficulty of the problem (Reiter-Palmon et al., 2009). These multiple layers of influence imply that no single theoretical framework
can capture all relevant item-level predictors in advance.
Although the OECD reports aggregated all items into a single composite score, the theoretical framework explicitly treats the
facets generate diverse ideas, generate creative ideas, and evaluate and improve ideas as distinct cognitive processes across four
content domains. In this study, we therefore adopted this theoretical stance and treat these facets as potentially separable constructs
that may rely on partly different cognitive skills and thus require separate analysis. In light of this complexity, our study applied
a bottom-up, data-driven approach. Rather than pre-specifying a narrow set of theoretically selected predictors, we considered the
full range of available cognitive, dispositional, background-related and contextual variables of the PISA 2022 dataset to first identify

which variables prove to be the most influential in predicting students’ item-level performance of the creative thinking facets. This
exploratory strategy enables the discovery of unexpected relationships, such as predictors that may be specific to particular facets,
while avoiding over-reliance on any potentially incomplete theoretical model (Breiman, 2001b; Norelli et al., 2025). Although largely
theory-free, the approach is informative for future research because it reveals empirically grounded patterns that can be tested in
subsequent confirmatory studies and can guide the refinement of process-oriented models of creative thinking in education (Hilbert
et al., 2021). Subsequently, we explicitly investigated the role of the content domains by including them as predictors into our model.
Finally, we investigated a specific interaction effect between the item’s domain and students’ corresponding domain-relevant skills.
Using item-level response data from the PISA 2022 sample, we employed a multi-level predictive modeling approach to account
for the nested data structure consisting of multiple item responses per student and multiple students responding to the same item.
To preserve the full data information, item responses were linked via item and person identifiers, indicating which scores belonged
to the same item and which to the same student. Although random forests do not explicitly parameterize this nested data structure,
their high capacity for modeling interactions and nonlinear relationships allows them to capture patterns associated with the nested
data structures as fixed effects, similar to a linear approach using the grouping identifier variables as predictors and specifying
interactive effects with the other covariates. In doing so, we aimed at isolating the mutual information attributable to the predictors.
By using machine learning algorithms, we can capture complex and potentially non-linear relationships between a wide range of
theoretically relevant constructs and the performance on creative thinking items. We applied XAI techniques, which allowed us
to identify the most influential predictors for each creative thinking facet, independent of specific item content. This approach
produced an empirically derived conceptual map of the psychological and contextual processes that drive students’ performance in
PISA’s creative-thinking tasks.
Specifically, we aimed at answering the following research questions:
RQ1a: Which person-related (cognitive or dispositional) and background-related factors best predict performance in each of the
three PISA 2022 creative thinking processes?
RQ1b: Do these predictors vary across the three facets?
RQ2: To what extent do responses in each facet depend on the content domain of the item (written expression, visual expression,
social problem-solving, or scientific problem-solving)?
RQ3: Does the effect of an item’s content domain on facet-level performance change as a function of students’ domain-relevant
skills?
2. Method
2.1. Database (participants and procedure)
For the current study, we reanalyzed data from PISA 2022, which is available online from the OECD data base (https://www.
oecd.org/en/data/datasets/pisa-2022-database.html) (OECD, 2023a). PISA targets 15-year-old students enrolled in formal education.
Within each of the 81 participating countries and economies, nationally representative samples were obtained using a two-stage
stratified sampling design: schools were sampled with probability proportional to size, and students were then randomly selected
within schools (OECD, 2023b). The targeted sample size per country or economy was approximately 6300 students, though final
sizes varied due to national circumstances and exclusion rates.
For the present study, the analytic sample was restricted to students who completed the creative thinking assessment. This
restriction led to an overall sample size of 142,564 students from 63 different countries or economies. The sample was genderbalanced (50.0% identified as female and 50.0% identified as male; 16 out of 142,564 students did not indicate their gender). The
mean age was 15.80 years (𝑆𝐷 = 0.29).
Data collection took place in 2022 and followed standardized international administration protocols of the OECD (OECD,
2023b). Students completed approximately two hours of cognitive testing on computers, followed by a 30–40 min background
questionnaire. The assessment of items belonging to the creative thinking domain was embedded into the cognitive test sessions,
which also included assessments of mathematics, reading, and science. Tasks were distributed across test booklets following a
balanced incomplete block design (Rutkowski et al., 2013). All instruments were translated into the countries’ official languages,
adapted, and quality assured according to international standards to ensure cross-country comparability.
2.2. Variables
To ensure a comprehensive and data-driven perspective, we incorporated all available information from the PISA 2022 dataset
that could plausibly inform students’ performance on creative thinking items. This included cognitive, dispositional, background,
and contextual variables, as well as process data from the computer-based assessment. Rather than restricting analyses to
theoretically preselected constructs, we adopted an inclusive approach to maximize the explanatory potential of the models that we
applied (Hilbert et al., 2025). This allowed us to explore both established and unexpected relationships between creative thinking
performance on item-level and its possible determinants. In the following, we will shortly summarize the main variables from the
creative thinking assessment and provide a short overview of the purposes of the single questionnaires of that we incorporated
variables into our analyses.

2.2.1. Outcomes: Item scores of creative thinking
The PISA 2022 creative thinking assessment measured students’ ability to generate, evaluate, and improve diverse and creative
ideas (OECD, 2023a). In contrast to the OECD’s official reporting, we did not use the overall creative thinking proficiency scores
(expressed as ten plausible values; PVs) as provided in the PISA 2022 dataset. To preserve the conceptual distinctions between the
three facets of creative thinking and to enable item-level analyses, we used the raw item scores as outcome variables. To do so, we
grouped single items according to three core facets of creative thinking from the PISA framework: (a) generate diverse ideas (GDI),
(b) generate creative ideas (GCI), and (c) evaluate and improve ideas (EII). In addition to that, we coded an additional variable
for the four content domains of creative thinking: (a) written expression, (b) visual expression, (c) social problem-solving, and (d)
scientific problem-solving. An overview of how many items were administered from which facet and domain can be found in Table
1. The scale comprised a total of 32 items.
Each student who worked on the creative thinking assessment in PISA responded to open-ended items. Subsequently, these
responses were scored using a three-step partial credit system by trained human raters hired by the OECD (OECD, 2023b). However,
not all items used a full 1, 2, 3 scoring scale. In the GDI facet, some items required only two responses (e.g., the item ‘‘Robot Story’’,
which can be exemplary found in the OECD report on page 274, OECD (2024)). The rationale was the following: when a GDI item
required three responses, students could earn full credit (3 points) if all three responses were appropriate and sufficiently different
from each other; they would be assigned partial credit (2 point) if two responses were different, and no credit (1 points) otherwise.
In contrast, GDI items requiring only two responses were scored dichotomously: full credit was assigned only when both responses
were appropriate and distinct, and no partial credit could be awarded.
2.2.2. Predictor variables
To predict students’ performance on the creative thinking items, we included a comprehensive set of cognitive, dispositional,
background, and contextual variables available from the PISA 2022 datasets. Specifically, we used data from the creative
thinking file (CY08MSP_CRT_COG.sav), the student questionnaire file (CY08MSP_STU_QQQ.sav), and the cognitive item data
file (CY08MSP_STU_COG.sav). Due to the vastness of the datasets, we will next provide only a brief overview of what these
datasets contain. In order to get a full overview of the individual variables that each dataset contains, we refer readers to the
codebooks provided by the OECD, available here: https://www.oecd.org/en/data/datasets/pisa-2022-database.html. For the purpose
of longitudinal replicability, we are also providing these codebooks in our OSF repository at https://osf.io/9r6f4/overview?view_
only=30395440f85349a4ac85ffd20b79d1fc.
2.2.3. Additional creative thinking variables
In addition to the item-level performance scores in creative thinking, the PISA 2022 computer-based assessment also generated
a range of process indicators that provided further information on students’ engagement during the creative thinking tasks. These
process data (sometimes also referred to as paradata) were included as additional predictors into our models to capture how students
interacted with the items. Specifically, they described the time students spent on each task, the latency between task onset and the
first recorded action, the total number of discrete interactions within a task (for example, typing responses or manipulating on-screen
objects), as well as the number and frequency of revisits to a task after it was initially completed. By combining information on
both what students produced and how they engaged with the tasks, we aimed to obtain a more comprehensive picture of the factors
that drive individual differences in creative thinking item-level performance. To preserve meaningful intra-item and intra-person
variability, process data were retained on their original scales and were not standardized.
2.2.4. Competences in cognitive domains
In addition to creative thinking, PISA 2022 assessed three core cognitive domains: mathematics, reading, and science. According
to the OECD framework, ‘‘in mathematics, PISA measures an individual’s capacity to reason mathematically and to formulate, employ,
and interpret mathematics to solve problems in a variety of real-world contexts’’ (OECD, 2023b, p. 22). As the major domain in the
2022 cycle, mathematics featured an updated assessment framework and received more testing time than in previous cycles (cf.
Lewalter et al., 2023). ‘‘The reading assessment in PISA measures an individual’s capacity to understand, use, evaluate, reflect on, and
engage with texts in order to achieve one’s goals, develop one’s knowledge and potential, and participate in society’’ (OECD, 2023, p. 22).
The assessment included sub-skills such as text comprehension and reflection (Heine, 2023). Lastly, ‘‘the science assessment in PISA
covers the ability to engage with science-related issues and with the ideas of science as a reflective citizen. A scientifically literate person is
6

willing to engage in reasoned discourse about science and technology, which requires the competences to explain phenomena scientifically,
evaluate and design scientific enquiry, and interpret data and evidence scientifically’’ (OECD, 2023b, p. 22). Each of these competences
was incorporated into our predictive model.
Please note that all cognitive competences in PISA are estimated using item response theory models and expressed as PVs rather
than individual test scores. PVs are multiple imputed draws from a latent proficiency distribution, designed to represent the posterior
uncertainty of each student’s proficiency given their observed item responses and background information (OECD, 2023b). Each
competence provides ten PVs that are scaled to an international metric (M = 500, SD = 100) to allow cross-country comparability.
In the current study, we used one randomly selected PV per cognitive competence domain as an individual-level indicator of students’
general competence in mathematics, reading, and science, respectively (see section Data Pre-processing for justification).
2.2.5. (Background-)variables from the student questionnaire
The PISA 2022 Student Questionnaire file (CY08MSP_STU_QQQ.sav) contains detailed background information collected from
each participating student. Among others, it includes demographic variables (e.g., age, gender, immigration status, language
use), indicators of socio-economic and cultural background such as parental education, occupation, and home possessions (these
indices are, for example, used to derive comprehensive socio-economic indices like ESCS or HISEI), and information about
students’ educational trajectories and school attendance. In addition, the questionnaire covered a broad range of dispositional
and motivational constructs, including self-efficacy, perseverance, enjoyment of learning, and interest in specific subjects, but also
curiosity. Items on well-being, classroom climate, and perceived teacher support were sought to capture aspects of the psychosocial
learning environment of the respective students. Several questions also assessed engagement in leisure and creative activities,
reading habits, and the use of digital media. The questionnaire furthermore included school- and system-level identifiers, such as
OECD membership, language of assessment, and regional information, which allowed linking individual responses to institutional
and country-level characteristics. These variables together provide a comprehensive view of students’ cognitive, motivational, and
contextual resources that may influence their performance in the creative thinking assessment.

2.3.1. Software
We used the statistical software R, version 4.5.0 (R. Core Team, 2025) for all analyses. Specifically, we used the packages
readxl (Wickham & Bryan, 2025), haven (Wickham et al., 2023), multilevel (Bliese, 2022), and tidyverse (Wickham et al., 2019)
for data pre-processing. The package tidymodels (Kuhn & Wickham, 2020) was used for fitting the random forests; the packages
DALEX (Biecek, 2018) and vip (Greenwell & Boehmke, 2020) were used in the XAI analysis. Finally, ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) was
used for figure creation.
2.3.2. Data pre-processing
For data pre-processing, we first merged the PISA 2022 cognitive, creative thinking, and student questionnaire files into a single
dataset. Character variables with lengthy text entries (e.g., open-ended parental occupation fields) and sampling weights were
excluded to reduce noise and file size. Additionally, we removed the PVs for creative thinking, retaining instead the single-item
scores along with the process data (e.g., response time, latency to first action, number of actions, visits, and short visits).
In total, the dataset comprised ten PVs for each measured cognitive competence. After verifying the strong interrelatedness
between all of them (all 𝑟 ≥ 0.88), we randomly chose one of the ten values per cognitive competence (that is, mathematics,
reading, and science) to circumvent high predictor multicollinearity in the subsequent analysis (Lavelle-Hill et al., 2025). Although
it is generally recommended to conduct analyses separately for each PV and then combine the results (Jewsbury et al., 2024), this
approach was computationally infeasible given the high dimensionality of the data and the number of models estimated. To assess
the robustness of our approach, we repeated all analyses using a second randomly selected set of PVs.
To prepare the dataset for analysis, we restructured it into a univariate format, which stacks student scores across items into
a long-format structure. This transformation resulted in one row per item–person combination, with item and person identifiers
preserving the linkage of responses to their respective items and students. This structure enabled item-level analyses across all
creative thinking tasks and allowed scoring information and process indicators to be integrated within a single analytic framework.
It further enabled the subsequent random forest analysis to take the nested data structure into account. We then made sure that all
full credit scores and all partial credit scores were coded with the same numerical values. This meant that dichotomous items’ scores
were adapted to no credit and full credit scores if necessary. In two final pre-processing steps we divided our dataset by the facets
of generate diverse ideas (GDI), generate creative ideas (GCI) and evaluate and improve ideas (EII). This dataset splitting ensured
that predictor importance scores were allowed to vary between facets, which was necessary in order to investigate RQ1b. In order
to keep information about the respective other facets in the respective datasets, we built student level mean values for their scores
and process items (e.g., total time on task, number of actions, etc.) and included them as predictors in our models as well. Thereby,
we obtained three datasets with 610,868 (GDI), 550,623 (GCI), and 439,826 (EII) observations each and in total 1389 features in
the predictor set. Finally, we added a randomly generated baseline variable to the datasets, which we later used to determine which
predictors’ variable importance should be interpreted.

2.3.3. Random forest analyses
To examine the predictive relations between all predictor variables and students’ performance in creative thinking (RQ1a +
RQ1b), we applied random forest (RF) models separately for each of the three creativity facets (GDI, GCI, and EII). The target variable
consisted of scorings of the creative thinking items with single observations constituting individual item–person combinations,
similar to Donnellan et al. (2023).
Random forests (RF; Breiman, 2001a) are ensemble machine learning methods that aggregate predictions from multiple decision
trees. Decision trees are nonparametric models that partition data into increasingly homogeneous subgroups through binary splits
on predictor variables, aiming to maximize the reduction in node impurity, typically defined as the misclassification rate. Through
the hierarchical structure of successive splits, random forests implicitly capture interaction effects among predictors without
requiring them to be specified a priori. Tree growth stops once nodes reach a minimum size or further splits provide negligible
improvement (Probst et al., 2019). Randomness is introduced by (1) drawing bootstrap samples of observations for each tree and
(2) selecting a random subset of predictors at each split. This dual randomization allows estimation of the out-of-bag error (Breiman,
1996), a measure of generalization performance. Final predictions are obtained by aggregating results across all trees. RF models
were estimated using the ranger engine within the tidymodels framework, specifying 500 trees, a mtry parameter of 37 and
permutation-based variable importance. Classification mode was used with the three-level scoring scheme of creative thinking items
as the outcome and Gini impurity as the loss function. To ensure computational efficiency, analyses were parallelized across 63
CPU cores. For each facet, we stored the fitted models and derived measures of permutation variable importance to assess which
predictors contributed most strongly to classification performance.
2.3.4. Explainable artificial intelligence analysis
Beyond variable importance, we employed XAI techniques to interpret the fitted random forest models. In a first step, we
evaluated permutation variable importance scores to identify the most important predictors. Permutation variable importance
permutes, thus shuffles, all values of the predictor of interest and afterwards receives predictions for the permuted values by
passing them through the random forest. It then computes the loss in prediction accuracy which constitutes the permutation variable
importance score. The logic behind the mechanism is that the permutation destroys predictor information and that the information
destruction of important variables will lead to stronger loss of accuracy than the destruction of information of less important
variables (Hilbert et al., 2025; Molnar, 2020). Following Kraus et al. (2024) we chose to interpret just the predictor variables that
received higher importance scores than the randomly generated baseline variable. Further, we used accumulated local effect plots
(ALEs) (Apley & Zhu, 2020) to investigate how domains, competences, and their interaction influenced predicted probabilities for
different score categories (RQ2 + RQ3) and derived expected item scores. An ALE plot is a model-agnostic method that estimates the
local effect of a predictor on the prediction function by computing differences in the predicted outcome after small, local changes in
the predictor value, and then accumulating these local effects over the observed distribution of the data to obtain a centered estimate
of the feature’s marginal contribution (Hilbert et al., 2021). Unlike partial dependence plots, ALE plots restrict the evaluation of
the prediction function to regions of the predictor space that are actually supported by the data, thereby substantially reducing
bias that can arise from extrapolation and from correlations among predictors. This property makes ALE plots particularly suitable
for high-dimensional settings with correlated covariates, such as educational large-scale assessment data, where marginal effects
should be interpreted as conditional on realistic combinations of predictor values rather than as global average effects (Apley &
Zhu, 2020; Dwivedi et al., 2023). ALEs were generated for both substantive competence indicators (e.g. reading or science) and for
item domains, as well as for the interaction of competence scores and item domains. Due to the intensive computational costs in the
estimation of the ALE profiles, we used 10,000 randomly chosen observations for the relationships between single indicators and
the predicted scoring probabilities and 5000 randomly chosen observations for the estimation of the interactive profiles. To improve
interpretability, ALEs were estimated for all three outcome classes and plotted jointly, enabling comparison of predictor effects across
performance levels. These visualizations provided insights into how behavioral and contextual indicators contributed to students’
expected creativity scores, thereby complementing global variable importance measures with local, feature-specific explanations.

3.2. Accumulated local effects (RQ 2 + 3)
In a second step, we investigated the estimated shape of the relationship between the target variable and our variables of interest
of the item domain and the domain-relevant skills (RQ2 and RQ3).
Using ALE plots, we visualize the expected change in predicted scores when the variable on the 𝑥-axis changes by a small amount,
while the other variables remain constant in a local sense (i.e., a marginal effect). Unlike partial dependence plots (PDPs), ALE plots
do not average over the entire distribution of the remaining predictors. Instead, they compute averages only within regions of
the data where observations have similar values of the focal variable. This means that the estimated effects are based on realistic
combinations of predictor values and avoid implausible situations, such as very high mathematics performance combined with very
low levels in all other competencies.

3.2.1. Relationship between domains, competences and predicted scores (RQ2)
At first, we investigated whether there were differences in the predicted scores depending on the item domain. The results are
shown in Fig. 2.
We found that for all four domains (science problem-solving, social problem-solving, written expression, and verbal expression),
predicted scores were on average lowest in the GDI facet. This can be inferred from the fact that probabilities of category 3 scores

probabilities in the EII facet (expected score = 1.95).
If one compares the patterns of predicted probabilities between different item domains within single facets, a different picture

and EII facets. On the other hand, in GCI and EII the verbal expression items yielded a different pattern from the other facets, with
notably higher probability for a partial score (category 2).
In a second step, we investigated whether there were differences in the predicted scores depending on the students’ abilities in
reading, mathematics, and science. The results are shown in Fig. 3.

4. Discussion
This study employed a data-driven machine learning approach to investigate the construct validity of the PISA 2022 creative
thinking assessment at the facet level. Our analyses yield three central findings. First, performance on creative thinking items
was most strongly predicted not by creativity-related dispositions, but by behavioral engagement indicators, general academic
competences, and students’ socio-economic background. Second, the profiles of these influential predictors were remarkably similar
across the three theoretically distinct facets of creative thinking. Third, while we observed small but complex interactions between
students’ academic skills and the items’ content domains, these effects were often subtle and highlighted the domain-specific nature
of performance.
4.1. The nature of PISA creative thinking
First of all, we used permutation variable importance to investigate which person-related (cognitive and/or dispositional) and
background-related factors best predict performance in each of the three PISA 2022 creative thinking processes (RQ1a) and whether
there were differences in these patterns (RQ1b).
We found that item characteristics as well as person characteristics were among the strongest predictors. Given that the target
variable represented scores of specific item–person combinations, this finding was not surprising. Item characteristics such as
the domain and the item indicators had the strongest influence on the scoring. This speaks to meaningful variability in item
characteristics because splitting the sample on whether the observed scores belonged to one specific item yielded more valuable
information on the likelihood of this scoring being a partial or a full score than knowing which person had responded to that item.
Further, technical variables, such as the specific test version yielded predictive information on the item scores. Since, however, the
test version can be seen as a second-level type of information about the items, indicating whether a certain set of items was scored
or not, we refrain from interpretation about these variables.

But also student-related demographic and competence variables yielded notable impact, speaking to substantial discriminatory

reflect broader educational inequalities rather than purely domain-specific creativity skills (cf. Goecke et al., 2026) Even though
consistent with previous analyses on the same dataset (Barbot & Kaufman, 2025), it was somewhat surprising that the performanceand engagement related variables, such as the time on tasks (timing), number of actions, and the competence scores in reading,
mathematics, and science had a relatively strong influence. In theory, creative thinking tasks should capture things like originality
and novelty of thought rather than persistence or test-taking behavior (Runco & Acar, 2012). Yet, the strong contribution of
behavioral engagement indicators suggests that sustained effort and task investment played a non-trivial role in determining
scores. Students who interacted more extensively with tasks tended to achieve better scores, which could mean that creative
thinking performance partly reflects motivational and self-regulatory components rather than creativity per se. This pattern raises
an important validity concern: if performance indicators like time on task and number of actions predict creative thinking scores as
strongly as, or even more strongly than, conceptual creativity measures, the assessment may conflate creativity with engagement
or general academic diligence.
Similarly, the relatively high variable importance of academic competence scores (in reading, mathematics, and science)
reinforces the possibility of construct confounding. Students with stronger domain-general abilities may be advantaged simply
because many creative thinking items still rely on comprehension, logical structuring, or content knowledge embedded in verbal or
problem-solving contexts. Consequently, part of what is scored as creative performance might actually reflect general reasoning or
literacy skills rather than an ideational construct. Taken together, these findings suggest that PISA’s creative thinking items may be
partly driven by motivational and cognitive resources beyond the creativity construct itself. This does not necessarily undermine
their educational relevance but complicates their interpretation as pure indicators of creative thinking.
Furthermore, social background indicators, such as socio-economic status (HOMEPOS) and parental education (ISCEDP), also
ranked high among predictors. While consistent with prior research on socio-economic disparities in creativity (Goecke et al.,
2026), these results could point to potential biases in how creative thinking is operationalized—favoring students from educationally

A core premise of our study was to test whether the three theoretically distinct facets of creative thinking would exhibit different

profiles were remarkably consistent across the ‘generate diverse ideas’, ‘generate creative ideas’, and ‘evaluate and improve ideas’
facets (see Fig. 1). In all three models, a similar set of predictors – engagement, academic skills, and background variables – emerged
as dominant, and their relative importance remained largely stable.
This finding challenges the empirical separability of the facets as measured by the PISA items. While the framework’s distinction
between divergent and convergent thinking processes is theoretically sound, the items may not be sufficiently distinct to capture
these processes separately, or the predictor set might not be able to differentiate between them in their convergent validity. It is
possible that all tasks, regardless of their intended facet, draw upon a common pool of underlying resources, such as task engagement,
comprehension, and the ability to articulate responses in a structured manner. Paradoxically, this lack of empirical differentiation
could be interpreted as a post-hoc justification for aggregating the items into a single score. However, this justification would not
rest on evidence for a coherent underlying construct of creative thinking, but rather on the assessment’s limited validity in capturing
the theoretically intended distinctions between its sub-dimensions.

4.3. Implications for the use of the PISA creative thinking scale
When considered within a nomological network perspective, our findings do not fundamentally challenge the aggregation of items
into a single creative thinking scale, but they call for a more cautious and nuanced interpretation of its scores. More specifically,
three key implications emerged.
First, the strong influence of engagement and general academic skills suggests that the composite score should not be interpreted
as a ‘‘pure’’ measure of creative potential. It more likely reflects a student’s ability to effectively deploy cognitive and motivational
resources to solve novel, open-ended problems within a formal assessment context. Second, the lack of empirical differentiation
between the facets questions the practical utility of the three-facet model for reporting purposes. While theoretically valuable for
test design, our results do not support the creation of separate facet-level sub-scores from a construct validity perspective. Third, the
dependence of scores on item composition in the booklets and the observed interactions among facets, domains, and competences
raise questions about the comparability of PISA booklets. We therefore recommend that, in research and applied contexts, the full
item set of 32 tasks should be used whenever possible to ensure construct representative and fair measurement. Moreover, our
results can only be interpreted from a standpoint of convergent validity rather than from a standpoint of factorial validity. The
present analyses do not address issues of scalability, reliability, or dimensionality, but tackle the conceptual question of what the
PISA creative thinking tasks capture.
4.4. Outlook and conclusion
Future research should aim to disentangle creative thinking performance from confounding influences of general ability and
engagement. Future investigations should make specific use of the process data, provided by the computerized testing procedure, and
investigate the different facets from a psychometric (e.g., factorial validity) or even a cognitive process perspective. Further, crosscultural comparisons and possibly comparative qualitative research could clarify whether the current operationalization of creativity
reflect domain-general creative thinking or context-dependent cognitive–motivational behavior. Addressing these questions is critical
not only for the refinement of the PISA creative thinking framework but also for ensuring equitable and theoretically grounded
assessment of creativity across diverse populations.
In conclusion, we find that creativity performance in PISA 2022 reflects a complex interplay of cognitive, motivational, and
contextual factors. While the assessment represents a valuable step towards measuring 21st-century skills across the globe, its
construct validity is still not straightforward. The theoretical distinction into facets finds little empirical support in our predictive
models, and the scores are heavily influenced by general academic diligence. Strengthening the theoretical and measurement
foundations of the scale will be essential to ensure fair, valid, and cross-culturally comparable assessments of students’ creative
thinking.
