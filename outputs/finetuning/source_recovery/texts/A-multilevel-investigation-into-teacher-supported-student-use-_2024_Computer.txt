A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Student technology use
Teacher-supported student use of technology
Multilevel structural equation modeling
Teaching and learning international survey

The integration of technology into learning is a crucial capability for students to address future
challenges. However, East Asian teachers exhibit a relatively lower level of support for students’
technology use compared with the OECD average. The present study tries to uncover the foundational factors influencing teacher-supported student use of technology in five East Asian
countries (economies) by analyzing Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018
data.
The multilevel structural equation modeling was adopted to analyze the sample data, which
combined the teacher-level and school-level factors. Additionally, teacher-level variables are
aggregated to the school level to examine contextual effect. Results indicate that teachers’ professional collaboration in lessons, team innovativeness, and self-efficacy in student engagement
positively and significantly correlated with teacher-supported student use of technology. Notably,
an opposing association is observed between teachers’ perceptions of disciplinary climate and
technology use in Shanghai and Singapore. However, in comparison to teacher-level variables,
school-level and contextual variables, particularly fundamental school features such as material
resources, budgets, and number of enrolled students, marginally explain associations. Importantly, these associations demonstrate variations across the five east Asian countries (economies),
attributable to the fact that these economies have developed their educational technology policies
grounded in technology determinism, emphasizing the necessity of equipping schools with the
latest devices. Consequently, this study posits that there is a requisite shift in policy evolution
from technology determinism to technology instrumentalism. This entails a pragmatic focus
transition from merely distributing devices to the integration of technology within instructional
practices.

1. Introduction
Technology plays a pivotal role in teaching and learning within classroom environments. Consequently, a plethora of studies have
delved into the multifaceted factors associated with both teachers’ and students’ technology use (Drossel, Erickelmann, & Gerick,
2017; Gil-Flores, Rodríguez-Santero, & Torres-Gordillo, 2017; Lomos, Luyten, & Tieck, 2023; Schmitz, Antonietti, Cattaneo, & Gonon,
2022; van den Beemt & Dispstraten, 2016). However, while considerable attention has been devoted to teachers’ technology use, the
landscape of students’ technology use is notably more intricate. This complexity stems from the inherent power differentials between
teachers and students, wherein teachers occupy a position of authority within the educational hierarchy (Beckman, Bennett, &
Lockyer, 2014). That means, students’ technology use in educational settings is profoundly influenced by the support provided by
teachers (Lai, Wang, & Lei, 2012; Ritzhaupt, Dawson, & Cauanaugh, 2012) and the regulatory frameworks imposed by institutional
policies and infrastructural provisions (Bulfin, Johnson, Nemorin, & Selwyn, 2016). Therefore, understanding why and how teachers
support students to use technology at school is an essential prerequisite to improve students’ technology use within classroom.
Similarly, the concept of teacher-supported student use of technology (TSSUT) diverges from students’ independent technology use
due to its closer alignment with teachers’ pedagogical beliefs and instructional practices, as well as the curriculum mandates (Siddiq,
Scherer, & Tondeur, 2016). Importantly, attempting to directly extrapolate factors associated with teachers’ technology use to explain
students’ technology use at school poses considerable challenges (Claro et al., 2018). This is primarily attributable to the fact that
students’ technology use is predominantly governed or encouraged by teachers, including parameters such as frequency and duration
of technology use (Siddiq et al., 2016). Thus, scholarly attention ought to be directed towards elucidating the factors underpinning
teacher-supported student use of technology, recognizing its critical role in shaping students’ technological engagement within
educational settings.
Moreover, the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 unveiled a paradoxical finding regarding teacherssupported student use of technology within the Chinese-Shanghai sample. On one hand, Chinese-Shanghai teachers emphasize the
importance of undergoing professional training in technology use and equipping themselves with technological devices for their
teaching. On the other hand, the inclination of Shanghai teachers to support students use of technology for learning purposes falls
markedly below the average level observed across OECD member countries (Xu & Zhu, 2023). In essence, while Shanghai teachers
recognize the importance of using technology in teaching, while their practical behaviors diverge from their professed beliefs
regarding students’ use of technology. This conundrum is not exclusive to Shanghai but has also been documented among teachers in
other Asian regions, including Singapore and Korea (OECD, 2018a).
Acknowledging the identified gaps in the both scholarly literature and practical applications, this study endeavors to explore
potential associated factors influencing teacher-supported student use of technology from two levels - teachers and schools - using
samples from East Asia.
2. Theoretical framework
2.1. Diffusion of innovation theory
Numerous theoretical frameworks have been proposed to interpret the technology use by teachers and student, including the “WillSkill-Tool” and “Will-Skill-Tool-Pedagogy” Model (Knezek & Christensen, 2016), the Technology Integration Planning Model (Roblyer
& Doering, 2013), and Bourdieu’s theory of Field, Habitus and Capital (Apps, Beckman, & Bennett, 2019; Beckman, Apps, Bennett, &
Lockyer, 2018). However, a significant limitation inherent to these theories is their collective failure to concurrently assess the impact
of individual characteristics alongside contextual factors. For instance, the “Will-Skill-Tool-Pedagogy” Model and the Technology
Integration Planning Model predominantly focus on interpreting the influence of teachers’ beliefs, attitudes towards technology, and
instructional strategies, as well as related technological factors. Conversely, Bourdieu’s theory is often applied to explain technological
disparities through social and systemic factors. Given that the adoption and utilization of technology by teachers and students occur
within the tangible contexts of educational institutions, it necessitates an integrative approach that consider both individual and school
factors. This necessity underpins the rationale for this study to adopt the diffusion of innovations theory as its guiding theoretical
framework, aiming to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing technology use within educational
settings.
The diffusion of innovations theory has been extensively employed to interpret the adoption or non-adoption of innovations across
a diverse range of fields, including politic science, marketing, sociology, public health, economics, and technology (MacVaugh &
Schiavone, 2010; Sahin, 2006). Surry and Farquhar (1997) indicated that the diffusion of innovations theory does not represent a
singular, well-defined framework but rather encompasses various branches that describe different facets of the innovation process.
Rogers (2003) equated innovations and technology, posting the diffusion of innovation theory as particularly suited for analyzing
technology usage in educational settings (Sahin, 2006).
Furthermore, Rogers’ overarching diffusion of innovation theory has been further elaborated within the realm of instructional
technology adoption, bifurcating into the Systemic Change Theory on macro level and the Product Utilization Theory on micro level
(Surry & Farquhar, 1997). These theories espouse distinct philosophical perspective on technology and its adoption objectives (Surry &
Farquhar, 1997). The viewpoints on technology span a continuum from technological determinism to technological instrumentalism
(Surry & Farquhar, 1997). Technological determinism views technology as an autonomous force capable of driving revolutionary
social change beyond control. In contrast, technological instrumentalism perceives technology as a tool subject to human control,
capable of being used for beneficial or detrimental purposes, and emphasizes that human agency and social conditions are the most

potent forces for evolutionary social change (Surry & Farquhar, 1997). Furthermore, those with expertise in technology often align
with technological determinism, positioning themselves as developers of new technologies, whereas technological instrumentalists
typically assume the role of adopters of new technologies.
In addition, it becomes evident that technology use within educational context should predominantly aligns with the perspective of
technological instrumentalism, wherein the principal focus is on enhancing the effectiveness of technology utilization. To address this
critical concern, Surry and Farquhar (1997) suggested that both the needs and perspectives of adopters at the micro level—encompassing individual characteristics—and the broader socio-political and professional contexts at the macro level within
specific organizations be given on considerable attention. These recommendations can be contextualized within the discourse on
technology use in education through two principal arguments: firstly, at the micro level, the importance of acknowledging teachers’
and students’ needs, characteristics, their interpersonal dynamics, instructional beliefs, and practices, and secondly, at the macro level,
the necessity of a thorough examination of school technological conditions and policies, alongside the provision of support for
technology use. This comprehensive evaluation is crucial in identifying potential factors associated with technology use in educational
contexts (Miranda & Russell, 2011, 2012).
2.2. Teacher characteristics and technology use in classroom
This study identified a total of 21 studies that have examined the correlation between teacher characteristics and their technology
use (including technology integration) spanning from 2010 to 2024 with detailed accounts of these studies provided in Appendix 1.
Dolan (2016) categorized the predominant factors associated with teachers’ technology use into two distinct groups: internal and
external. This classification underscores the multifaceted nature of the influences on technology use by teachers, suggesting that both
internal beliefs and attributes, and external environmental and systemic conditions, play a crucial role in shaping their engagement
with technological tools and resources.
Among the internal factors influencing teachers’ technology use, teachers’ belief emerge as a significantly frequently cited element,
with various studies highlighting different types of beliefs correlate with technology use (Lawrence & Lentle-Keenan, 2013; Schmitz
et al., 2023). These encompass beliefs about teaching (Kim, Kim, Lee, Spector, & DeMeester, 2013), teachers’ beliefs about learning
(Jacobson et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2013), teachers’ beliefs about the benefits of technology (Miranda & Russell, 2011, 2012), and
teachers’ ICT beliefs (Lomos et al., 2023).
Moreover, Cifuentes, Maxwell, and Bulu (2011) argued that technology becomes more embedded in teaching, there may be a shift
from traditional, teacher-directed instruction towards more student-centered approaches. This transition could lead to teachers
placing less emphasis on classroom management and discipline due to the changing dynamics of student engagement facilitated by
technology. Conversely, Gil-Flores et al. (2017) observed that teachers’ pedagogical beliefs significantly influence their ICT use,
indicating that teachers’ pedagogical beliefs can shape technology usage practice.
Furthermore, a significant body of studies indicated that teachers’ positive attitude towards technology, their prior experiences
with technology, and their technological skills are essential internal factors that positively influence their use of technology in
educational settings (Akram, Abdelrady, Ai-Adwan, & Ramzan, 2022; Cabellos, Siddiq, & Scherer, 2024; Cifuentes et al., 2011; Dogan,
Dogan, & Celik, 2021; Lomos et al., 2023; Xu & Zhu, 2019). Additionally, teachers’ self-efficacy, including self-efficacy in ICT usage
and computer self-efficacy, has been identified as directly correlating with an increased propensity to incorporate technology into their
teaching practices (Gil-Flores et al., 2017; Kaya-Kasikci, Zayim-Kurtay, & Kondakci, 2023; Xu & Zhu, 2023).
The external factors that influencing teachers’ technology use encompass several key dimensions, notably including the opportunity for professional development specifically focused on Information and Communication Technology (ICT) (Marwan & Sweeney,
2010; Xu & Zhu, 2023). The support received from technological specialists (Dogan et al., 2021), access to technology resources (Liu,
Ritzhaupt, Dawson, & Barron, 2017), and financial support from schools (Kormos, 2021) also play crucial roles or inhibiting teachers’
technology use.
Additionally, some studies have identified significant barriers that prevent teachers from fully embracing technology in their
instructional practices. A common challenge cited is the lack of time (Lawrence & Lentle-Keenan, 2013; Thorsteinsson & Nuiculescu,
2012), with educators often viewing the incorporation of technology as an additional task that compounds their existing workload
(Lawrence & Lentle-Keenan, 2013). This perception of technology use as an extra burden is further corroborated by findings from a
study conducted within a college context, which highlights a negative correlation between technology use and teachers’ perceived
workload (Marwan & Sweeney, 2010).
Beyond the internal and external factors previously discussed, a notable body of research has delved into the interplay between
teacher collaboration and their technology use within educational settings. Findings from these studies consistently reveal a positive
correlation between teacher collaboration and the incorporation of technology into classroom practices (Aldunate & Nussbaum, 2012;
Kim et al., 2013; Lomos et al., 2023). This relationship also extends to other collaborative types, such as the collaboration with external
partners (Erickelmann, 2011), and the ICT-related collaboration (Krumsvik, 2005). Gil-Flores et al. (2017) posited that the influence of
teacher collaboration on ICT use might even surpass that of the availability of ICT infrastructure. Moreover, at the school level, the
presence of a collaborative environment has been identified as a significant predictor of teachers’ ICT use in classrooms (Dexter,
Seashore, & Anderson, 2002).
Furthermore, Tang (2021) revealed that the use of technology in teaching can foster greater innovation in teaching methods.
Similarly, teachers working in an innovative climate are more inclined to incorporate technology into their teaching practices (Chou,
Shen, Hsiao, & Shen, 2019; Ninkovic, Floric, & Momcilovic, 2023). These findings collectively imply that teachers who teach in
environments characterized by collaboration or innovation are more likely to utilize technology in their instructional methods.

Recent studies have extensively investigated the role and impact of teachers on students’ use of technology, particularly within the
realm of media education research (Livingstone, Mascheroni, & Staksrud, 2017). For instance, Kalmus, von Feilitzen, and Siibak
(2012) indicated that teachers often serve as both safeguards and coaches, offering guidance and fostering opportunities for students to
use the internet safely. This notion was further supported by Garmendia, Karrera, Larranaga, and Garitaonandia (2021), who
demonstrated that when schools create a more inclusive environment for digital technology use, students are more likely to develop
enhanced digital skills and competencies.
Moreover, Karaseva, Siibak, and Pruulmann-Vengerfeldt (2015) identified that teachers’ approaches to guiding students’ use of
technology are influenced by their pedagogical beliefs and the subjects they teach. Scherer, Rohatgi, and Hatlevik (2017) further
established a positive correlation between teachers’ support and students’ use of Information and Communication Technology (ICT).
Moreover, Zhao, Lu, Wang, and Huang (2011) proposed that this positive relationship could be partially explained by the mediating
effect of students’ curiosity, suggesting that curiosity plays a key role in linking teacher support to increased technology use among
students.
A more recent study by Han and Geng (2023) examined university students’ engagement with online learning technology and
found that teachers’ instructional support is positively associated with a deep approach to using online learning technology, while
showing a negative relationship with a surface-level approach. These findings collectively underscore the significance of teachers’
roles in fostering students’ effective and safe use of technology.
Conversely, two studies conducted by van Braak and colleagues revealed that there were no significant associations between
students’ ICT skills or competencies and various teacher characteristics, such as teachers’ attitudes toward ICT, their professional
development in ICT, their ICT competencies, or their experiences with using ICT (Aesaert et al., 2015; Aesaert & van Braak, 2014). In a
study set in the Chinese context, a complex relationship was observed between teachers’ characteristics and students’ information
literacy. Specifically, Zhu, Yang, MacLeod, Yu, and Wu (2019) found that while teachers’ ICT self-efficacy, negative attitudes towards
ICT, and collaborative use of ICT positively correlated with students’ information literacy, other factors, such as positive attitudes
toward ICT, the use of ICT for teaching, and the use of ICT for learning, had a negative correlation with students’ information literacy.
These findings suggest that the relationship between teachers’ characteristics and students’ technology-related skills and literacy
can vary across different contexts and might depend on a variety of factors. It is crucial to consider these nuances when assessing the
impact of teachers’ ICT-related characteristics on students’ digital competencies.
This section primarily focuses on identifying factors influencing technology use by teachers and students from the teachers’
perspective. The existing research on determinants of teacher-supported student use of technology is relatively limited. To bridge this
gap, this study explores related concepts from existing literature.
Miranda and Russell (2011, 2012) introduced the concept of ‘teacher-directed student use of technology’ (TDSUT), emphasizing
that teachers’ technology experiences, beliefs, and attitudes are positively correlated with their efforts to facilitate students’ use of
technology. Bowman, Vongkulluksn, Jiang, and Xie (2020) discovered that teachers’ exposure to professional development directly
influences their use of instructional technology, and indirectly influences it by altering their value perceptions of technology.
Secondly, in the field of media education research, teachers are seen as social agents who mediate students’ use of technology in the
classroom (Kalmus et al., 2012; Karaseva et al., 2015). Despite having a smaller influence compared to students themselves (Garmendia et al., 2021), teachers play a critical role as safeguards and coaches, providing guidance to protect teenage students from online
risks and harm (Kalmus et al., 2012).
Thirdly, within the interactive, constructive, active, and passive (ICAP) framework (Chi & Wylie, 2014), Antonietti et al. (2023)
shifted the focus of classroom technology assessment from frequency of use during lessons to integration across diverse learning and
teaching activities. They further demonstrated that this approach is closely linked to students’ learning behaviors and signified a
deeper understanding of how technology use in classrooms impacts learning outcomes and student engagement.
The current studies underscore the significance of teachers’ personal engagement with and perceptions of technology as critical
components that can enhance or inhibit their support for students’ technology use. This suggests a ripple effect, where teachers’
positive experiences and attitudes towards technology not only augment their own integration of digital tools into teaching practices
but also positively influence their propensity to encourage and support students’ use of technology in learning. The paucity of research
specifically targeting the factors affecting teacher-supported student technology use points to a gap in the literature, indicating a need
for further investigation to fully understand and optimize the dynamics of technology-enhanced learning environments.
2.3. School characteristics and technology use in classroom
Based on school perspective, many studies explored the relationships between various school characteristics and teachers’ technology use (see in Appendix 2). One of the most critical influential factors is school leadership, which has positive associations with
teachers’ technology use (Lomos et al., 2023; Marwan & Sweeney, 2010). Some studies further investigated the influences of different
types of school leadership on teachers’ technology use, yielding mixed effects. Leng (2008) highlighted the beneficial impact of
transformational leadership on ICT integration, a finding echoed and confirmed in subsequent research (Vermeulen, Kreijins, van
Buuren, & van Acker, 2017; Vermeulen, Van Acker, Kreijins, & van Buuren, 2014). These studies also suggested that principals’
instructional leadership is positively associated with ICT use in schools (Berkovich & Hassan, 2022; Chen, 2013), proposing that
principals who exhibit transformational leadership behaviors likely possess more progressive attitudes towards technology integration
in educational settings (Laschou, Kollias, & Karasavvidis, 2018). However, contrasting these findings, a more recent study employing
multilevel modeling analysis challenged the significant impact of principals’ transformational leadership on teachers’ technology use
(Schmitz et al., 2023). This divergence in findings points to the complexity of the relationship between school leadership and

technology use, suggesting that while leadership style is crucial, its effect might be contingent on other variables or contexts.
The existing research presents mixed findings regarding the impact of school technological infrastructure on teachers’ use of
technology at the school level. On one hand, several studies have identified a positive relationship between the availability of technological resources within schools and the extent to which teachers incorporate technology into their teaching practices (Akram et al.,
2022; Gil-Flores et al., 2017; Marwan & Sweeney, 2010). Conversely, two more recent studies challenge this assertion, reporting a
non-significant relationship between the presence of technological resources in schools and teachers’ utilization of these technologies
(Xu & Zhu, 2019, 2023). These contradictory findings emphasize the complexity of factors influencing technology integration in
schools and suggest the need for a more nuanced approach to understanding and supporting technology use in education.
Beyond the influence of school technological infrastructure, research has identified several other school-level factors that positively
affect teachers’ use of technology. These factors include a collaborative school culture (Jacobson et al., 2010; Lomos et al., 2023), a
positive overall school climate (Ninkovic et al., 2023), teachers’ perceived norms within school regarding technology use (Kaya-Kasikci et al., 2023), and school facilitating conditions that support technology use (Cabellos et al., 2024). These findings highlight the
importance of a supportive and conducive school environment in encouraging teachers to incorporate technology into their teaching
practices.
Meanwhile, the experiences of school principals with technology have been shown to positively predict teachers’ technology use
(Miranda & Russell, 2012), suggesting that leadership experiences and attitudes towards technology can influence the school’s
technological culture. However, the impact of principals’ support on technology use has been debated. Ninkovic et al. (2023) found no
significant effect of principal support on teachers’ technology use, contrasting with earlier assertions about the importance of leadership in facilitating technology integration. Dexter and Richardson (2019) provided insights into how principals could effectively
support teachers, including offering professional learning opportunities, fostering communities of practice, and addressing teachers’
individualized needs.
In conclusion, this review of extant literature, encompassing both teacher- and school-level analyses, identifies three primary
research gaps warranting further exploration. Firstly, an observed discrepancy in the impact of technological infrastructure on
technology utilization reveals different patterns depending on the level of analysis. At the teacher level, there is a positive correlation
between infrastructure and technology use (Kormos, 2021), whereas this relationship becomes non-significant at the school level (Xu
& Zhu, 2023). This inconsistency suggests a nuanced contextual effect, indicating that different factors might influence technology use
at various educational echelons. The discrepancy also highlights the importance of using multilevel modeling as an analytical approach
that can more accurately capture the complexity and layered nature of these influences. By employing multilevel modeling, researchers can account for variability within and across different levels, offering a more detailed understanding of how individual and
institutional factors impact technology utilization in educational settings.
Secondly, the predominance of studies focusing on either internal factor, such as teachers’ beliefs, attitudes, and self-efficacy, or
external factors, including the availability of devices, financial resources, or time constraints, tends to overlook the critical role of
instructional-related factors. This oversight suggests a need for comprehensive research that integrates pedagogical considerations into
the analysis of technology adoption and use in educational settings. Thirdly, the majority of existing research is situated within
Western contexts, indicating a significant gap in understanding how technology integration is influenced by and interacts with diverse
socio-cultural norms and values. Future research should, therefore, seek to broaden its geographical and cultural scope to better
comprehend the global dynamics of technology use in education, acknowledging the profound influence of social culture and norms
(Beckman et al., 2018) on educational technology practices.
2.4. Technology education in the five East Asian countries (economies)
The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) formulated the ICT Development Index (IDI) as a composite metric to evaluate
the level of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) advancement across countries by amalgamating 11 indicators
encompassing ICT access, usage, and skills (ITU, 2016). In the 2016 iteration of the IDI, South Korea achieved the highest ranking
globally with a score of 7.76, surpassing the global average of 4.94, and indicating a leading position in ICT development. In contrast,
mainland China secured the 10th position within the Asia-Pacific region with an IDI score of 5.19 (ITU, 2016). Additionally, Japan and
Singapore were notable for their high rankings among Asia-Pacific economies, with IDI scores of 8.37 (ranking 3rd) and 7.95 (ranking
6th), respectively (ITU, 2016). The 2016 IDI scores of these four Asian countries reveal substantial disparities, underscoring the
significant variations in ICT infrastructure and development levels among them.
Since the late 1990s, several international large-scale surveys have been conducted to assess ICT in education, providing valuable
insights into how different countries integrate and perceive the role of ICT in the learning environment (Fraillon, 2018). One notable
study, the Second Information Technology in Education Study (SITES) 2006, carried out by the International Association for the
Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA), revealed several key findings (Yuen & Hew, 2018):
(1) The recognition of ICT as a catalyst for school change varied significantly among countries, with 47% of school principals in
Taiwan, 44% in Singapore, and only 16% in Japan acknowledging this potential.
(2) In terms of professional development, Taiwan and Singapore were reported to offer substantial training courses aimed at
supporting teachers in integrating ICT into their instruction, with more than three-quarters of teachers in these countries having
access to such training. Conversely, in Japan, similar training opportunities for teachers were notably scarce.
(3) The provision of laptops to teachers for educational purposes also differed, with Taiwan and Singapore actively providing
laptops to teachers and encouraging ICT use in teaching, whereas Japan was much less likely to adopt such practices.

Another pivotal survey, the International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS), with its rounds in 2013 among others,
provided further insight into ICT education on a global scale. Notably, in Korea, only 42% of students reported engaging in
communication with others using messaging or social networks, a figure substantially lower than the international average (Fraillon,
Ainley, Schulz, Friedman, & Gebhardt, 2014). This data highlights the diverse approaches and challenges in integrating ICT within
educational systems across different countries, underscoring the need for tailored strategies to leverage technology effectively in
enhancing teaching and learning outcomes.
The educational technology policies across five selected economies have predominantly focused on enhancing the information and
digital infrastructure within the education system.
● Singapore has been proactive in the development of ICT in education through the implementation of five distinct ICT Master Plans
since 1997, with the most recent transition to the Educational Technology (EdTech) Plan (2020–2030). This plan is designed to
foster a technology-enriched school environment that supports self-directed, personalized, connected, and human-centered
learning.
● Korea has emphasized digital infrastructure as a priority, particularly in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, by equipping schools
and teachers with digital devices and ensuring widespread Wi-Fi coverage. In 2023, Korea government pays more attention to
change teachers’ role to learning designers, coaches, and mentors, and establish a new ecosystem to improve the co-development of
education and technological industry (OECD, 2023a).
● Japan introduced the Global and Innovation Gateway for All (GIGA) initiative in 2018, with a key objective of providing digital
devices to all students and enhancing the infrastructure in schools to support ICT in teaching and learning. The pandemic further
accelerated the enhancement of digital infrastructure, significantly increasing the Wi-Fi adoption in schools from 48.9% in 2020 to
94.8% in 2022 (OECD, 2023a).
● The mainland China has laid out strategic plans to boost digital infrastructure in schools, including the 13th Five-Year Plan for
Education Informatisation announced in 2016, aiming to establish a comprehensive education informatization system by 2020.
This was followed by the Education Informatisation 2.0 Action Plan in 2018, which sought to ensure that all teachers and students
have access to teaching and learning applications respectively by 2022 (Yan & Yang, 2021).
● Taiwan has evolved two stages to integrate ICT in education, with the initial phase (2001–2008) focusing on integrating technology with students’ daily lives and various subjects. The subsequent phase (2008–2012) saw ICT becoming a standalone subject,
with an increased emphasis on ICT literacy. The ICT Education Blueprint (2016–2020) further articulated a vision centered on
aiding students in developing deep learning capabilities and becoming digital citizens (Huang, 2015).
These policies reflect a shared Aisa commitment to leveraging digital advancements for educational purposes. The emphasis on
providing digital devices, enhancing internet infrastructure, and reshaping the role of teachers aligns with the broader goals of preparing students for a digitalized world, fostering digital citizenship, and supporting innovative and personalized learning experiences.
2.5. The present study
Based on the above review, this study aims to fill the identified gaps by conducting a multilevel analysis with East Asia samples
from TALIS 2018 to reveal related factors on teacher-supported student use of technology. The rationale for utilizing two-level
analytical models in this study is threefold. Firstly, teachers are naturally clustered within schools, resulting in a multilevel
complexity in their behaviors and competencies (Alt, 2018). Recognizing this, a two-level model helps account for the interdependencies among teachers within the same school context.
Secondly, the study aims to investigate the contextual effects of school characteristics on teacher support for student technology
use. This approach allows for an examination of whether various school attributes moderate the impact of teacher characteristics on
their support for student technology use.
Thirdly, as noted in previous literature review, the study indicates that while teacher collaboration consistently influences technology use at both the teacher and school levels, there is a discrepancy regarding the influence of technological infrastructure on
teacher technology use. This suggests that the same factors may exert different effects at various levels, emphasizing the need for
multilevel modeling to capture such variations. Given these considerations, a two-level model provides a more comprehensive
framework to explore the complex relationships between teachers, their environment, and technology utilization, leading to more
accurate and context-sensitive insights.
Specifically, considering the linking characteristics of TSSUT, this study primarily incorporates teaching-related variables from
teacher- and school-level, and the school basic information. These variables include teachers’ professional collaboration, team
innovativeness, disciplinary climate, self-efficacy in student engagement, workload stress, school leadership, lack of material resources, number of enrolled students, and budgeting to explain the TSSUT, which includes teacher-supported student use of information technology (TSSUT1), and teacher-supported student use of digital technology (TSSUT2). Overall, this study aims to address
two research questions:
(1) To what extent are teacher characteristics associated with the teacher-supported student use of information technology and of
digital technology within classrooms? (Teacher-level effects)
(2) To what extent are collective teacher and school characteristics associated with the teacher-supported student use of information technology and of digital technology within schools and classrooms? (School-level effects and contextual effects)

3. Methodology
3.1. Data source
The OECD Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) is an international large-scale survey of teachers and principals
designed to assess their teaching and learning environments in five areas, including school policies supporting effectiveness, developing teachers within the profession, effective teachers and teaching, attracting teachers to the profession, and retaining teachers in
the profession (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). TALIS is launched by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD)
and involved 49 countries (economies) in the 2018 round. TALIS 2018 utilized a stratified two-stage probability sampling design to
collect data (OECD, 2018b). This design involved the random selection of schools in the first stage, and approximately 20 teachers were
randomly selected from each chosen school in the second stage.
The current study utilizes the teacher and principal (ISCED level 21) datasets from five East Asian countries (economies) that
participated in TALIS 2018 (Chinese Shanghai [CSH], Chinese Taipei [TWN], Korea [KOR], Japan [JPN], Singapore [SGP]). The aim of
this study is to investigate the associated factors from teachers and schools with teacher-supported student use of technology. Specifically, the full sample consisted of 14,025 teachers in 852 schools. Within the full sample, the proportion of female teachers ranged
from 26% (CSH) to 57.5% (JPN).
3.2. Measures
This study utilized two items from TALIS 2018 teachers’ questionnaire to measure the two outcome variables: (1) teachersupported student use of information technology (TSSUT1), examining by the question “Thinking about your teaching in the
<target class>, how often do you do the following? The item of P ‘I let students use ICT (information and communication technology)
for projects or class work’“, and (2) teacher-supported student use of digital technology (TSSUT2) by the question “In your teaching, to
what extent can you do the following? The item of M ‘support student learning through the use of digital technology (e.g., computers,
tablets, smart boards’)”. The two outcome items were measured with four level Likert-scale (1 = Never or almost never, and 4 =
Always). All independent variables were measured by the Weighted Likelihood Estimates (WLEs). The details of computing WLEs were
reported in the TALIS 2018 technical report (OECD, 2018b).
3.2.1. Teacher-level variables
Five independent variables were adopted to explain teacher-supported student use of technology. The variable professional
collaboration in lessons among teachers (T3COLES) concerns teachers’ collaboration, and was measured by the question “on average,
how often do you do the following in this school.” It covers four aspects: joint as a team, provide feedback to other teachers, joint
activities, and collaborative professional learning. The T3COLES scale was developed by conducting confirmatory factor analysis
(CFA) using teachers’ responses to the aforementioned question. The T3COLES scale received acceptable scale reliability and reached
metric invariance among the five East Asia countries (economies) (OECD, 2018b).
Team innovativeness (T3TEAM) was created by conducting CFA using teachers’ responses to the question “thinking about the
teachers in this school, how strongly do you agree or disagree with the following statements?“. The T3TEAM scale was confirmed with
good scale reliability, good model fit in all populations and reached scalar invariance (OECD, 2018b).
Teachers perceived disciplinary climate (T3DISC) was developed using CFA with teachers’ perceptions regarding student behaviors
in classroom. In TALIS 2018, the T3DISC was measured with the approach of maintaining classroom orders, controlling students’
misbehaviors, and classroom management (OECD, 2018b). The T3DISC scale obtained high reliability, acceptable model fits and
reached metric invariance for all populations (OECD, 2018b).
Teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement (T3SEENG) was developed based on teachers’ responses to the question “in your
teaching, to what extent can you do the following?” using CFA. It covers four aspects: support students to believe they can do well, help
students value learning, motivate students, and help students think critically. The T3SEENG scale reached high reliabilities and metric
invariance in five East Asia countries (economies) (OECD, 2018b).
Teachers’ workload stress (T3WLOAD) was created by using CFA with teachers’ responses, which included the topics such as lesson
preparation, lessons to teach, marking, administrative work, and extra duties. The T3WLOAD scale was reported have acceptable scale

3.2.2. School-level variables
The independent variables at the school level were obtained in two ways: (1) variables aggregated from the teacher-level to
represent collective teachers’ perceptions and behaviors; (2) variables from TALIS 2018 principal’s questionnaire, namely school
autonomy for budgeting (T3PAUTB), school autonomy for instructional policies (T3PAUTI), school leadership (T3PLEADS), lack of
material resources (T3PLACMA), and number of enrolled students (NENRSTUD).
Specifically, both scales of school autonomy for budgeting and school autonomy for instructional policies were created using recordings of the items’ frequencies (OECD, 2018b). The school leadership scale was created by conducting a confirmatory factor
analysis (CFA) using principals’ response to the question “please indicate how frequently you engaged in the following activities in this
school during the last 12 months”. The specific activities entail fostering collaboration among educators, ensuring that teachers assume
responsibility for enhancing their instructional abilities, and guaranteeing that instructors feel accountable for their students’
educational outcomes. The T3PLEADS scale has achieved high scale reliability and reached the metric invariance level among the
selected five East Asia countries (economies) (OECD, 2018b).
The lack of material resources scale was recoded based on principals’ responses to the question “to what extent is this school’s
capacity to provide quality instruction currently hindered by any of the following issues?” This includes the shortage of instructional
materials, digital technology for instruction, insufficient internet access, and inadequacy of library materials. The T3PLACMA was
measured with three categories: 1 for “not a problem”, 2 for “a bit of a problem”, and 3 for “a problem”. The number of enrolled
students scale was created by recoding principals’ responses to the question about the current school enrolment, which has five
categories (1 = less than 250 students, 2 = 250–499, 3 = 500–749, 4 = 750–999, 5 = over 1000 students).
3.3. Data analysis
This study employed two-level structural equation modeling (MSEM) to analyze the collected data for two main reasons: (1)
teachers’ teaching is nested within schools, and variances in teaching among schools cannot be overlooked (Klieme, 2020); (2) TALIS
2018 data provides an opportunity to explore influential factors by combining teacher and school data. Importantly, compared to
traditional multilevel regression analysis, MSEM has three advantages. Firstly, MSEM can account for measurement errors, resulting in
8

less biased outcomes (Preacher, 2011). Secondly, by decomposing observed teacher-level variables into latent teacher- and
school-level variables, MSEM helps reduce bias when estimating the contextual effects of teacher-level variables (Lüdtke et al., 2008).
Finally, MSEM provides model fit indices that indicate the goodness-of-fit between the analyzed model and the raw data (Lüdtke,
Marsh, Robitzsch, & Trautwein, 2011).
Among the five East Asia countries (economies), the proportion of missing data ranged from 1.5% to 3.1%, except for Japan, which
reached 22.2%. To address the missing data, this study employed the full-information-maximum-likelihood procedure to estimate the
data (Enders, 2010). Considering the complex sampling design, this study incorporated the teacher level (within level) weights
(TCHWGT) and school level (between level) weights (SCHWGT) in all data analyses (Asparouhov, 2005). Sampling weights is a critical
issue when analyzing international large-scale survey data. This approach allows for the accurate representation of the survey’s
complex design within your model, ensuring that estimates reflect the population structure and sampling scheme accurately. Level one
weights adjust for individual-level sampling probabilities, while level two weights correct for group-level variations, addressing the
hierarchical structure of the data. This dual weighting is essential for minimizing bias and making valid inferences that are representative of the broader population. According to the suggestion of Rutkowski, Gonzales, Joncas, and Davier (2010), this study
computed the participating teacher weight as the product of (WGTFAC1 x WGTADJ1 x WGTFAC2 x WGTADJ2 x WGTADJ3 x
WGTADJ4), and the school weight was created as the product of WGTFAC1 x WGTADJ1 (OECD, 2018b). In addition, robust
maximum-likelihood estimation (MLR), the default estimator in Mplus 8.3, was used to analyze the data.

4.2. Teacher-level effects (RQ1)
4.2.1. Effects for the full sample
The MSEM results of analyzing the full sample, which includes data from the five East Asian countries (economies), are reported in
Table 4. The full sample MSEM model has achieved a good model fit (SRMR value for within level = 0, SRMR value for between level =
0.001, AIC = 349415.975, BIC = 349702.821). Overall, except for the team innovativeness (T3TEAM) and the teachers’ perceived
disciplinary climate (T3DISC), all other predictors show positive regression coefficients for TSSUT1 and TSSUT 2 (βs = 0.063–0.285).
Teachers with higher professional collaborations in lessons are more likely to support students’ use of information and digital technology in the classroom, and similar effects are also indicated for teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement (T3SEENG) and
teachers’ workload stress (T3WLOAD).
4.2.2. Effects for the five countries (economics) sample
This study further explores each country’s (economy’s) dataset as a reference to answer research question one, and the details have
been reported in Tables 5–9. Teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement (T3SEENG) has positive regression coefficients with
TSSUT1 (βs = 0.109–0.226), and TSSUT 2 (βs = 0.219–0.441) in all five individual samples.
For TSSUT1, except for Shanghai, the professional collaboration in lessons among teachers (T3COLES) has positive and significant
relations with TSSUT1 (βs = 0.073–0.18) in the other remaining countries (economies). Interestingly, only in the Shanghai sample,
teachers’ team innovativeness (T3TEAM) was found positively and significantly associated with TSSUT1 (β = 0.045). Teachers
perceived disciplinary climate (T3DISC) has positively and significantly related with TSSUT 1 among Shanghai teachers, while the
relation becomes negative among Singapore teachers. In addition, with only two exceptions from Taipei, and Shanghai, teachers’
workload stress (T3WLOAD) was positively influencing TSSUT1, with the regression coefficients ranging from 0.078 to 0.11.
Regarding TSSUT2, with two exceptions from Korea and Japan, the T3COLES was positively associated with teacher-supported
student use of digital technology, with regression coefficients ranging from 0.071 to 0.078. Similarly, positive regression coefficients were found between the T3TEAM and TSSUT2 in the Shanghai and Singapore samples. Regarding the influence of teacher
workload stress (T3WLOAD), positive effects were only reported in the samples of Singapore and Japan.
In summary, the analysis reveals significant associations between teacher-supported student use of information technology
(TSSUT1) and digital technology (TSSUT2) and various endogenous factors at the teacher level, with these relationships exhibiting
variability across the five examined countries/economies. The key findings are outlined as follows:
For TSSUT1:
● Teachers’ Self-Efficacy in Student Engagement shows a positive influence in all five countries/economies, underscoring the
universal importance of teacher confidence in engaging students for the successful integration of information technology in
teaching.
● Teachers’ Professional Collaboration in Lessons is positively associated in Taipei, Korea, Japan, and Singapore, highlighting the
role of collaborative practices in enhancing the use of information technology.
● Team Innovativeness is found to have a positive effect exclusively in Shanghai, indicating a particular emphasis on innovation in
this economy for the adoption of information technology.
● Teachers’ Workload Stress is positively linked in Korea, Japan, and Singapore, suggesting that in these contexts, the pressure of
workload may motivate higher levels of technology use.
● The influence of the Disciplinary Climate on TSSUT1 varies, being positive in Shanghai but negative in Singapore, indicating
differing impacts of classroom management on technology integration.
For TSSUT2:
● Teachers’ Professional Collaboration in Lessons positively affects TSSUT2 in Taipei, Shanghai, and Singapore, again emphasizing the value of collaboration among teachers for integrating digital technology into teaching.
● Team Innovativeness has a positive impact in Shanghai and Singapore, reflecting the significance of innovative team dynamics in
the use of digital technology in these economies.

4.3. School-level and contextual effects (RQ2)
4.3.1. Effects for the full sample
For the full sample, collective teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement (T3SEENG) was positively associated with TSSUT1 (β =
0.325, p = 0.007) and TSSUT2 (β = 0.311, p = 0.006). Conversely, the lack of material resources (T3PLACMA) at the school level was
negatively and significantly related to TSSUT1 (β = − 0.201, p = 0.004) and TSSUT2 (β = − 0.163, p = 0.01). Moreover, collective team
innovativeness (T3TEAM) and the number of enrolled students (NENRSTUD) at the school level were positively and significantly
related to the TSSUT2 with regression coefficients 0.307 (p = 0.007) and 0.212 (p = 0.003), respectively.
Considering the contextual effects (Table 10), only collective team innovativeness (T3TEAM) and the collective teachers’ selfefficacy in student engagement have positive influences on teacher-supported student use of digital technology (TSSUT2) with
regression coefficients of 0.281 (p = 0.007) and 0.172 (p = 0.042). According to these two coefficients, teachers working among highly
team-innovative colleagues or among highly self-efficacious colleagues in student engagement are more likely to support student use of
digital technology.
4.3.2. Effects for the five countries (economies) sample
Across the five East Asian countries (economies), only a few significant relations were revealed with teacher-supported student use
of information technology (TSSUT1). For example, in the Shanghai sample, the lack of material resources (T3PLACMA) was negatively
and significantly related to TSSUT1 (β = − 0.396,p = 0.045). This result suggests that a lack of material resources at the school level
may have a negative impact on TSSUT1. Similarly, a negative relation was also found in the Japanese sample between the number of
student enrolled and the TSSUT1 (β = − 0.207,p = 0.046). That is to say, increasing the number of students enrolled in school may
reduce teacher-supported student use of information technology. Conversely, collective professional collaboration in lessons and
collective teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement have a positive and significant association with TSSUT1 (β = 0.471, p = 0.01).
Considering the effects on teacher-supported student use of digital technology (TSSUT2), in the Taipei sample, four predictors have
significant relations with TSSUT2. On the one hand, the collective team innovativeness (T3TEAM) and the collective teacher workload
stress (T3WLOAD) were positively and significantly related with TSSUT2 with β = 0.513,p = 0.023, β = 0.753,p = 0.014, respectively;
on the other hand, the number of enrolled students and school autonomy on budgeting were negatively related to TSSUT2, and the
regression coefficients are β = − 0.537,p = 0.032, β = − 0.308,p = 0.004. In the Singapore sample, collective team innovativeness
(T3TEAM) and collective teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement (T3SEENG) have a positive and significant association with
TSSUT2. In the Japanese sample, teachers’ professional collaboration in lessons and teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement
positively and significantly related with TSSUT2.
Concerning the contextual effects, only one negative effect was identified in the Korea sample. That is, professional collaboration in
lessons among teachers negatively and significantly related with TSSUT2 (β = − 0.163,p = 0.044). This result indicates that in the
context of Korea, active engagement in professional collaboration in lessons among teachers might have some adverse effects on
supporting students in using digital technology.
At the school level, the analysis reveals significant disparities in the factors influencing teacher-supported student use of information technology (TSSUT1) and digital technology (TSSUT2), highlighting the complexity of educational technology integration
across different contexts. The findings indicate:

● Collective Teachers’ Professional Collaboration in Lessons is positively associated in Japan, suggesting that a school-wide

5. Discussion
5.1. Teacher-level effects for the full sample
The first research question addressed the associations between teacher-level variables and teacher-supported student use of information technology (TSSUT1) and of digital technology (TSSUT2). By analyzing the full sample and samples from the five East Asian
countries (economics), this study found that teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement is the only one predictor positively associated with both outcome variables across the full and the five countries (economics). This finding is echoed with the previous studies
by indicating the positive relationship between teachers’ self-efficacy and technology use (Gil-Flores et al., 2017; Thorsteinsson &
Nuiculescu, 2012). By distinguishing the specific dimension of self-efficacy related to student engagement, this study adds nuance to
our understanding of how different facets of self-efficacy influence technology integration in teaching practices. The connection between teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement and technology use can be interpreted through the lens of the broader relationship
between teachers’ pedagogical beliefs and their approach to technology. Firstly, teacher-supported student use of technology is not
merely about students’ technology behaviors but is also linked to teachers’ instructional beliefs and practices (Siddiq et al., 2016;
Siddiq & Scherer, 2016). Secondly, considering the function of technology in helping students maintain focus on learning and
increasing engagement (Vahedi, Zannella, & C Want, 2021), teachers who emphasize the importance of student engagement and
believe in their ability to support it are more likely to endorse and facilitate students’ use of technology in their learning.
Another interesting finding is the significant and positive relationships observed between teachers’ professional collaboration in
lessons and workload stress with the two outcome variables. These results align with previous research, highlighting the interconnected nature of teacher collaboration and innovation in the context of supporting use of technology (Cifuentes et al., 2011; Tang,
2021).
5.2. Teacher-level effects for the five countries (economies) sample
This study finds that positive relationship between team innovativeness and TSSUT1 only existed among Shanghai teachers. This
finding is partially aligns with the findings of Xu and Zhu (2023), indicating that team innovativeness does not significantly correlate
with technology use at the school level. The distinction between the impact of team innovativeness on teacher and school levels could
be attributed to several factors. At the teacher level, innovativeness within teams may directly influence individual teachers’ practices
and their openness to incorporating technology into their instruction. Innovative teams are likely to foster a culture of experimentation
and support for using new teaching methods and technologies, directly affecting teachers’ attitudes and behaviors towards technology
use. However, the lack of a significant relationship at the school level may indicate that the influence of team innovativeness is more
nuanced and possibly diluted when considering broader school-wide technology use. This could be due to the varying degrees of
innovative culture across different teams within a school, which might not uniformly contribute to a cohesive school-wide approach to
technology use.
Moreover, this study finds that a positive relationship exists between teachers’ workload stress and the teacher-supported student
use of technology in Korea, Japan, and Singapore aligns with previous research indicating that teachers’ workload tends to increase as
they incorporate technology more frequently into their teaching (Lawrence & Lentle-Keenan, 2013; Marwan & Sweeney, 2010;
Thorsteinsson & Nuiculescu, 2012). This phenomenon can be analyzed through the lens of the diffusion of innovations theory,

particularly focusing on the aspect of technological determinism. Technological determinism, as adopted by the governments of Korea,
Japan, and Singapore, places an overarching emphasis on the importance of technology itself, rather than viewing technology as a
means to enhance educational practices (Surry & Farquhar, 1997). This philosophical approach is evident in the educational technology policies of these countries, which prioritize equipping schools, teachers, and students with the latest technological devices,
sometimes at the expense of integrating technology into teaching practices in a meaningful way (OECD, 2023a).
In addition, the influence of teachers’ perceived disciplinary climate on teacher-supported student use of information technology
(TSSUT1) demonstrated inconsistency between Shanghai and Singapore. This variance could be attributed to the measurement
approach of the teachers’ perceived disciplinary climate in TALIS 2018, which focused on the control of undesirable behaviors.
Accordingly, a higher score signifies a more negative disciplinary climate and a stronger control over students. It appears that teachers
in Shanghai may prioritize preventing students’ misbehaviors, leading to a situation where the more Shanghai teachers exert control
over their students, the more they support students in using technology. Conversely, teachers in Singapore exhibit opposite perceptions
regarding the disciplinary climate, which negatively affects TSSUT1. This negative relationship can be elucidated through the lens of
Cifuentes et al. (2011), who suggest that teachers’ experiences with technology can alter their pedagogical beliefs, shifting from a
teacher-centered to a student-centered approach. As a result, teachers may deemphasize strict classroom order and exert less control
over students, fostering a more open environment for technology use in education.
5.3. School-level effects for the full sample
The second research question aims to explore the influences of collective teacher and school characteristics on teacher-supported
student use of technology. This study found that the lack of material resources is significantly and negatively related to the two
outcome variables, consistent with the findings of Akram et al. (2022) and Gil-Flores et al. (2017). Additionally, collective teachers’
self-efficacy in engaging students was positively and significantly associated with teacher-supported student use of technology. This is
a key contribution of this study, highlighting the considerable impact of teachers’ collective self-efficacy in student engagement at both
the teacher and school levels. Based on this result, the study argues that to encourage teachers to support student technology use,
school leaders should foster a school climate that helps teachers believe in their capabilities to engage students effectively in teaching
and learning.
Furthermore, the study found that collective teacher team innovativeness and the number of enrolled students were positively and
significantly associated with TSSUT2. This finding aligns with the conclusions of Chou et al. (2019) and Ninkovic et al. (2023). The
positive relationship between the number of enrolled students and TSSUT2 could be explained by the fact that digital technology can
be a powerful tool to help teachers manage the challenges that come with an increasing student population.
5.4. School-level effects for the five countries (economies) sample
This study identified a significant negative relationship between the lack of material resources and TSSUT1, which is differential
with the finding by Xu and Zhu (2023). Additionally, the study revealed an intriguing negative correlation between the number of
enrolled students and TSSUT1 in the Japanese sample, which differs from the overall trend observed in the full sample. Similarly, a
negative relationship between the number of enrolled students and TSSUT2 was noted in the Taipei sample.
This negative relationship could be due to the intense competition within educational systems in Japan and Taiwan, particularly
regarding university entrance exams (Chan & Lin, 2015; Knipprath & Arimoto, 2007). In larger classes, maintaining order and
discipline becomes a crucial task for teachers. To ensure teaching quality in such competitive environments, teachers might prefer
traditional, conservative approaches over technology-based methods. This tendency may explain the negative relationship observed
between larger class sizes and reduced technology use in these contexts.
This study uncovers another interesting finding: collective teacher workload stress is positively associated with TSSUT2 in the
Taipei sample. This could be explained by the idea that when teachers experience higher levels of workload stress, one possible solution is to use technology more frequently to streamline their work (Selwood & Pilkington, 2005). Moreover, the study found positive
correlations between collective team innovativeness and TSSUT2 in both the Taipei and Singapore samples. This result is not surprising, given that teachers in both Taiwan and Singapore are widely recognized for their proactive approach to technology in education. The importance of integrating ICT into teaching is a central theme in their ICT curriculum policies (Huang, 2015; Yuen & Hew,
2018), suggesting that both regions are fostering environments that encourage innovative technology use among educators.
In addition, school leadership and autonomy in instruction did not show significant effects on teacher-supported student use of
technology in both the full and the five East Asian sub-samples. This contradicts the findings of Ninkovic et al. (2023), who argued that
principal instructional support leadership could enhance technology use. Therefore, this study suggests that school and contextual
characteristics can only account for a limited variance in teacher-supported student use of technology. This observation aligns with the
conclusions of Drossel et al. (2017) and Konstantinidou and Scherer (2022), asserting that only marginally variances in technology use
can be attributed to school and contextual variables.
5.5. Contextual effects for the full and five countries (economies) sample
The contextual effects observed across the full sample are consistent with the school-level effects, indicating that collective team
innovativeness and collective teachers’ self-efficacy in student engagement positively and significantly influence TSSUT2. However, an
unexpected contextual effect was found in the Korean sample, where professional collaboration among teachers had a negative impact

on TSSUT2. This outcome might be understood in the context of the Korean education system’s strong focus on high-stakes testing. In
this environment, teachers’ professional collaboration is often centered on aligning curriculum and teaching strategies to prepare
students for standardized tests. This focus on test preparation would limit teachers’ ability to explore technology-based teaching
methods, as the pressure to meet testing goals constrains their flexibility and creativity. Consequently, technology integration in
Korean classrooms may be negatively affected by this form of professional collaboration.
5.6. Limitations and future directions
This study comes with several limitations due to its research design and data format. Firstly, the utilization of cross-sectional data
from TALIS 2018 restricts the establishment of causal relationships between teacher and school characteristics and teacher-supported
student use of technology. Future studies could enhance the robustness of these relationships by employing longitudinal or experimental designs. Secondly, despite the attempt to compare results across the five East Asian countries (economies), there is room for
expanding this study by incorporating more countries with diverse cultural backgrounds. Additionally, incorporating country-level
characteristics as independent variables could provide further insights into their influences on technology use. Thirdly, the measurement of teacher-supported student use of technology was operationalized by asking teachers to rate the frequency with which they
allow students to use technology in class. This approach, however, may yield different results compared to collecting data directly from
students regarding their actual frequency of technology use in class. Moreover, this study measured teacher-supported student use of
technology independently on teachers’ perspective and this would lead to a mono-source bias. Therefore, future studies should
consider employing varied methods of measurement and undertake comparative analyses to further investigate these discrepancies.
6. Conclusion and implications
Due to the linking feature, the factors influencing teacher-supported student use of technology are distinct from those affecting
teachers’ or students’ technology use. This study highlights the significance of teacher-specific variables, particularly those related to
teaching practices, in shaping teacher-supported student use of technology. Variables such as professional collaboration in lessons,
self-efficacy in student engagement, and team innovativeness emerged as influential. Consequently, to foster teachers’ intentions to
support students in using technology for learning, principals should focus on providing opportunities for collaboration, fostering
innovation, and enhancing student engagement.
Furthermore, this study suggests that variances in outcomes are marginally attributed to school-level and contextual factors,
particularly school basic characteristics like material resources, budgeting, and the number of enrolled students. To promote teachers’
intentions to support students’ technology use in learning, principals and policymakers could provide support by enhancing material
resources and funding.
Moreover, this study suggests that national policies on educational technology may account for some of the observed variations in
effects across the five countries (economies), particularly regarding teachers’ workload stress and their perceived disciplinary climate.
Thus, it is proposed that national educational technology policies should evolve their underlying philosophical approach from technological determinism to technological instrumentalism. This shift would redirect the focus of policies from merely provisioning
devices towards the meaningful integration of technology into educational practices. Such a change would emphasize the development
of both teachers’ and students’ competencies in utilizing technology effectively in teaching and learning contexts. This recommendation aligns with and is further reinforced by the insights provided in the most recent OECD Digital Education Outlook (OECD,
2023b).
