Numerous studies have explored the complex relationship between culture, cognitive competence, and economic
performance globally. However, findings from these investigations vary significantly and occasionally contradict
each other. This study delves into this connection by analyzing variables or dimensions from three distinct
models of national culture concerning the results of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
and the economic strength and growth of 86 nations between 2000 and 2018. Religious affiliations emerge as a
significant statistical explanatory factor, accounting for a substantial portion of the variance in overall PISA
performance, ranging from 24% to 40%. Remarkably, an underutilized cultural model, the Axiological Cube,
surpasses others, exhibiting explanatory power ranging from 45% to 63%. Path analyses rooted in both Human
Capital Theory and Cognitive Capitalism Theory reveal that cultural variables exert their influence on economic
growth mainly indirectly through their impact on student competence. Cultural variables exhibit robust predictive capacity for overall student competences, as indicated by PISA mean scores. However, they prove
inadequate in explaining certain cognitive competence patterns, such as disparities in inequality and subjectspecific variations, like mathematics and reading. This study also highlights uncertainties surrounding the effects of Confucianism and East Asian religions, prompting further discussion and investigation.

1. Introduction
1.1. National culture, cognitive competences and wealth
The interactions of a society’s traditions, values, and mindset with
outcomes in education and wealth have been discussed since the early
days of social science. The German philosopher Immanuel Kant argues
in his 1784 work Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose
that a well-developed culture and education are essential for the prosperity of a society (Kant, 2009). He wrote that a culture not only shapes
an individual’s mind and abilities but also leads to greater prosperity by
enhancing people’s intellectual and creative capabilities, thereby supporting innovation and economic growth. Kant emphasizes that the
education of an individual should be guided by universal and rational
principles that are independent of cultural differences. However, he also
recognizes that a society’s culture and values can impact education, as
history and cultural traditions can influence the development of its

members.
More than a century later, Max Weber took a different position. In his
1905 work The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism he argued that
educational achievement is heavily influenced by certain cultural values
and religious beliefs (Weber, 1930/1992). He saw a direct connection
between the Protestant work ethic and the emergence of the capitalist
spirit in the Western world. According to Weber’s perspective, education
and intellectual development were highly valued and encouraged in
Protestants, and typical Protestant values, particularly diligence, discipline, thrift, and an ethical commitment to work, support educational
achievement.
Max Weber also addressed the influence of Confucianism and Buddhism on educational achievement (Ertman, 2017). Weber assumed an
effect similar to that of Protestantism originating from Confucianism
and Buddhism in East Asian societies. While Weber assessed Confucianism and its focus on education, discipline, and hierarchy as an East
Asian counterpart to Protestantism, he argued that Buddhism’s path to

More recent work has focused on empirically testing these theories.
In several studies involving country differences in education, cognitive
competences, and economic wealth, Rindermann (e.g., Rindermann,
2012, 2018; see also, Rindermann & Carl, 2020) demonstrated that
values like rationality, diligence, discipline, self-discipline, achievement
striving, meritocracy, pragmatism, functionalism, and collectivism are
positively associated with cognitive competences, technological and
scientific progress, modernization, political stability, and wealth at the
cross-national level. Rindermann (2018) named this set of values the
Burgher-Civic world, influenced by Protestant ethics and enlightenment
philosophy, and considered it both a result of and a prerequisite for high
cognitive competences.
In his 2018 book Cognitive Capitalism, Rindermann showed that the
cultural background of a country is a powerful predictor of its wealth,
stability, and modernization, directly or indirectly affecting several
paths, with a particular emphasis on cognitive competences (pp.
371–387). A recent study by Wang et al. (2023) cited 156 peer-reviewed
articles on factors associated with performance in PISA (Programme for
International Student Assessment) and identified national culture as one
of the macro sociological factors. Specifically, the collected studies
pointed to a positive association with Confucianism and long-term
orientation, while showing a negative association with individualism,
uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity. Wang et al. (2023) argued,
referring to Breton (2021), that national culture is a crucial factor in
resource and talent allocation within educational systems. According to
Breton’s hypothesis, national culture, defined as beliefs and practices,
shapes the educational system and learning culture at home, which in
turn affect student competence. However, the influence of national
culture’s path is not only direct but could also run simultaneously along
decision-making processes in religious and political organizations,
influencing social values and norms.
National culture might have a top-down effect, exemplified or specified by leading institutions, and a bottom-up effect through society’s
way of life, practicing or at least communicating norms and values
(Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Vygotsky, 1978). Overall performance in international student assessment studies may be influenced by culture,
which in turn may affect specific abilities, referred to as tilts. The concept
of tilts was originally introduced by Park et al. (2007), estimated by
math SAT score minus verbal SAT score. Becker et al. (2022) showed
that the math tilt in PISA, defined as a country’s difference in mathematical and reading competencies (math score – reading score; Coyle,
2018, 2019; Hunt & Wittmann, 2008; Lubinski, 2009, 2016), is overall
negative in countries with a Christian majority but strongly positive in
countries with a majority of East Asian cultural tradition. Tilt, in turn,
has a positive effect on economic growth. The positive effect can be
attributed to the influence of math tilt (math > verbal) on achievements
in STEM (science, technology, engineering, math), which drive economic growth (e.g., Rindermann, 2018).
A large amount of theoretical and empirical literature based on the
Human Capital Theory (Schultz, 1971) examines the connection between
student competence and economic strength. The common economic
term human capital is often defined as the sum of abilities and competencies of a society (e.g., Azam, 2017; Burhan et al., 2014, 2017,b; Coyle
et al., 2016, 2018; Hanushek & Kimko, 2000; Hanushek & Woessman,
2007; Rindermann, 2018). These abilities and competencies are related
to the efficient allocation of resources that improve institutions, which
in turn create the prerequisites for a good economy, generating technological and scientific innovations and ensuring competitiveness in
regional or global markets.
In this context, country results in international student assessment

studies like PISA, TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and
Science Study), and PIRLS (Progress in International Reading Literacy
Study) have been found to predict national economic performance and
growth (e.g., Becker et al., 2022; Rindermann, 2008, 2018; Rindermann
& Thompson, 2011). This might not be surprising, as the development of
international student assessment studies has strongly focused on economic needs (Hastedt, 2020), especially in the case of PISA, which was
developed by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). Quoting verbatim, “The quality of their schools today
will feed into the strength of their economies tomorrow” (OECD, 2020,
p. 3).
Numerous studies have demonstrated a significant and robust association between the outcomes of international student assessments and
national IQ values based on psychometric tests. Positive and strong
correlations, ranging from r = .70 to .97, have been reported (for a review, see Lynn & Becker, 2019, p. 208; see also, Lynn & Meisenberg,
2010; Rindermann, 2018). Additionally, cross-national analyses have
revealed factor loadings of approximately λ = .99 (e.g., Rindermann,
2007). The results suggest that the student assessments and national IQ
results are strong indicators of g (general intelligence), which in turn
influences and is influenced by biological, cultural and economic factors
(for a review, see Rindermann, 2018). In this study, the terms “education” and “cognitive competences” are therefore used synonymously.
1.2. Culture and national culture
Wang et al. (2023) showed that factors of National culture predict
country results in international student assessment studies. However,
variations in operationalizing National culture were not compared. For
example, the approach from Rindermann (2018, p. 380) and Becker
et al. (2022) was based solely on religious affiliations (shares of populations affiliated to a certain religion), and data for cultural dimensions
from Hofstede and Inglehart (see next paragraphs) were applied in
Breton (2021) and Hu et al. (2018). Tan (2017) used a dummy-variable
named “Countries’ Confucian heritage culture (CHC)”, comparing
countries with or without such a culture. Fang et al. (2013) was the only
work whose results allowed such a comparison. They used cultural dimensions from Hofstede and Inglehart and two cultural dimensions from
the World Value Survey, named (1) traditional values versus secularrational values, and (2) survival values versus self-expression values, but
explained variance in PISA-means was largely identical (R2 = .41 to .53).
The various methods for operationalizing national culture impede
comparisons. This difficulty is exacerbated by weaknesses within the
respective operationalizations and definitions. In a broader sense, culture
has been defined as “the way of life, especially the general customs and
beliefs of a particular group of people at a particular time” and “the
attitudes, behavior, opinions, etc. of a particular group of people within
society,” or “as a concept that calls attention to the nonbiological,
nonracial, noninstinctual dimension of human life” (White, 2022).
Similarly, the Dutch cultural scientist and social psychologist Geert
Hofstede defined culture as “the programming of the human mind by
which one group of people distinguishes itself from another group,”
divided into a more tangible layer (e.g., symbols, food, monuments, role
models) and a more abstract layer of rituals and social behavior
(Hofstede Insights, 2023a). He also defined National culture as a more or
less conspicuous social agreement between people brought up in the
same country about expectations of life and the resulting values
(Hofstede Insights, 2023b) and made the assumption that there are
cross-country differences between these agreements.
When speaking about social values and group-defining mindsets, one
is not far from the concept of religion, which, according to Haluza-DeLay
(2014, p. 261), “includes beliefs, worldviews, practices, and institutions
that cross borders, time, and scale from the level of individuals all the
way to transnational and transhistorical movements.” The assumption
that religion is one of the most important cultural factors of a society,
impacting communication, social structures, economics, legal systems,

According to the theories above, National culture describes, among
other things, different ethical norms about what kind of knowledge and
skills are essential for a good life, as well as what makes a good life at all,
and thus leads to different collective/political decisions about education
and demands on the education system. Also, differences in mean individual attitudes and behavior can influence the interests and motivations of students, parents, and teachers and focus their learning or
teaching on different educational contents. For example, a higher
acceptance of meritocratic principles could lead to higher learning
discipline and a greater willingness to prioritize harder subjects over
easier ones. In contrast, a higher degree of individualism could lead to a
stronger tendency to deviate from collective moral agreements and to an
educational orientation that serves one’s individual preferences more
than society as a whole.
There are different ways to operationalize culture at the crosscountry level. A simple approach is to use religious affiliation as an indicator of culture. The advantage of this approach is the easy accessibility of data for almost all countries in the world, for example, from The
World Christian Encyclopedia by Barrett et al. (2001). However, this
simplicity could also turn out to be a weakness. Religious affiliation at
the country level is often determined through self-attribution or nonstandardized demographic survey methods. The freedom to openly
identify with a certain religion may vary across countries, and governments may not count individuals belonging to certain religions for political reasons (United States Commission on International Religious
Freedom – USCIRF, 2023). Demographic data might not be collected due
to the low capacity of countries to collect and analyze such data (The
World Bank, 2023b). Being affiliated with a certain religion in one
country might not reflect the same association with cultural norms and
values as being affiliated with the same religion in another country, for
example, Catholicism in Europe versus other parts of the world (Starr,
2018, December 19th). In addition, the affiliation to a certain religion is
not necessarily associated with sharing its fundamental norms and
values, and it might not be clear with which norms and values a certain
religion is associated.
These problems are evident in the example of the Three Teachings of
East Asia: Confucianism, Taoism (or Daoism) and Buddhism. A recent
online survey study by Lin et al. (2021) developed the item-based TTEA
(Three Teachings of East Asia Inventory) to measure tenets of the Three
Teachings. In an international survey of 2091 individuals, they discovered a factor structure congruent with the Three Teachings (one each for
Taoism and Buddhism plus two for Empowering and Restrictive
Confucianism), with positive intercorrelations between the factors,
indicating that the Three Teachings are both distinct and overlapping.
Surprisingly, the Three Teachings are not predominant in today’s China
in terms of open identification. According to various sources, <1%
identify as Confucianists or Taoists, around 8% to 18% as Buddhists,
while 42% to 52% identify as non-religious or unaffiliated, and 22% to
31% as Chinese folk-religionists (Albert & Maizland, 2020, September
25th; Barrett et al., 2001; CIA, 2023a). A reason for these differences is
that, during the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution and the Great Leap

Forward from 1966 to 1976, Mao Zedong proclaimed a “national community of solidarity,” viewing all divergent tendencies, such as classes,
strata, various forms of property, heterogeneous ethnic and religious
movements, regional and local features, or family and clan interests, as
detrimental (Herberer, 2009). In contrast, Taiwan and Singapore, two
countries showing strong ethnic and cultural ties to China, have a
different religious landscape. According to diverse sources (Albert &
Maizland, 2020, September 25th; Barrett et al., 2001; CIA, 2023a), 21%
to 35% of Taiwanese identify as Buddhists, 10% to 33% as Taoists, and
up to 51% as Chinese folk-religionists, including Confucianists. In
Singapore, the same sources reported 15% to 31% as Buddhists, up to
9% as Taoists, and up to 43% as Chinese folk-religionists.
Compared to Abrahamic religions, it is difficult to estimate the
number of individuals affiliated with the Three Teachings. As Weiming
(2023) notes: “East Asians may profess themselves to be Shintōists,
Daoists, Buddhists, Muslims, or Christians, but, by announcing their
religious affiliations, seldom do they cease to be Confucians”. Additionally, Chinese folk-religionists comprise 29% of China’s population
(Barrett et al., 2001), with such religions forming the roots of Confucianism and Taoism (Duignan, 2018; Weiming, 2023).
Similar problems of definition also apply to other religions or world
regions. More sophisticated approaches to operationalize national culture
are described by Miguel E. Basáñez (2016) in his book A World of Three
Cultures – Honor, Achievement, and Joy. First, according to Basáñez
(2016, pp. 44–50), the Four Axes Model (4-D) is based on an a-posteriori
concept by Geert Hofstede, describing the cultures of countries as software of the mind, and “not universally constant, but rather are mediated
by a nation’s cultural unity; they are the sum of history, language, law,
religion, and other factors expressed in the structure of institutions” (p.
45). Hofstede started his empirical work in 1967 by giving questionnaires to 88,000 employees of IBM in 72 countries (p. 45). From the
responses, Hofstede identified four dimensions of global cultural differences: (1) Power Distance, which is the degree of acceptance of
inequality in a society and of obedience and respect to socially higherranking individuals by those with lower social status, thus also the degree of impermeability of social hierarchies in ways other than hard
work; (2) Uncertainty Avoidance, which is the degree a society meets
uncertainty and ambiguity caused by future or hypothetical dangers
with pragmatism or rigid principles; (3) Individualism vs. Collectivism,
which distinguishes between societies preferring individual freedom
over attachment to fixed structures, such as family, and thus the
importance of achieving individual life goals regardless of their overall
societal consequences; (4) Masculinity vs. Femininity, which can be seen
as synonymous with competition vs. cooperation, with societies with
higher masculinity preferring striving for success and achievements in
material and social pursuits regardless of, or if necessary, also at the
expense of the weak. Second, the Two Axes Model from Ronald Inglehart
combines two binomial concepts: Survival vs. Self-Expression Values and
Traditional vs. Secular-Rational Values. These dimensions appear under
different names (cf. Hofstede Insight, 2022; Hofstede Insights, 2023a,
2023b), including (1) Long-Term vs. Short-Term Orientation, or the
openness and flexibility regarding changes necessary to deal with new or
future problems vs. the bondage to well-established solutions and
traditional orientations, and (2) Indulgence vs. Restraint, which faces the
gratification of pleasures through the observance of social norms.
Interestingly, Long-Term vs. Short-Term Orientation was first referred to
as Confucian Dynamism (Reimer, 2005, p. 16).
Whereas Hofstede and Inglehart followed an a-posteriori approach,
Shalom Schwartz’s Three Axes Model was built a-priori on the theoretical
assumption of three universal human requirements: biological, social
interactional, and social institutional (Basáñez, 2016, pp. 55–63).
Through empirical proof and evidence, these three requirements were
transformed into three axes: (1) Autonomy vs. Embeddedness, which
pertains to cultures attaching great importance to individual rights,
freedoms, and hedonism versus collectivistic cultures in which individuals have significant responsibilities when selecting their ways of

Based on Schwartz’s Model, Basáñez (2016) suggested a new model
called the Axiological Cube (Fig. 1) with three universal human requirements (economic, political, social) as the core, forming six “walls”,
one at each end of the requirements (hierarchy, meritocracy, harmony,
mastery, embeddedness, and autonomy). A special feature of this model
is that it distinguishes between different historical developments that
led to changes in cultures and thus to the formation of today’s cultural
characteristics. It distinguishes civilizations that grew richer from
abundant agriculture (A to B), increased power as population and pyramidal hierarchies grew (A to C), or increased in social differentiation
and specialization (A to E). By linear combination of the three axes three
cultures are formed: (1) The Culture of Honor (corner H) is defined by
high values of harmony, embeddedness and hierarchy, emphasizing
tradition, respect, hierarchy, discipline, loyalty, obedience, God, religion, etc. Islamic or oil-rich Arab societies with a strong focus on humility before God were named as the primary examples (Basáñez, 2016,
pp. 73–74). (2) The Culture of Achievement is defined by high values of
egalitarianism, autonomy, and mastery, thus a goal-oriented rationality,
with “Confucian, Nordic, and some of the English Protestant” civilizations as typical representatives (Basáñez, 2016, p. 74). (3) The Culture of
Joy takes a centered position, searching for a balance on each of
Schwartz’s three axes, represented today by “colonial Catholic countries” (Basáñez, 2016, p. 77). Overall, the Axiological Cube shows that
certain cultural values are mutually exclusive or had to be sacrificed in
the course of historical development.

1.3. Aims of this study
The primary aim of this work is to clarify the connections of culture
and student assessment results at the cross-national level, by applying
the various National culture models and comparing their effects. The
study examines different models and operationalizations of national
culture, and weighs them against each other in terms of their suitability
for predicting differences in country results in international student
assessment studies. Specifically, two models or operationalizations of
National culture have been extensively used in the empirical literature:
religious affiliations (shares of people affiliated to a specific religious
group) and the 4-D or 6-D model from Hofstede and Inglehart. The
Axiological Cube model from Basáñez (2016) has not been considered so
far. The simultaneous use of several culture models may also be helpful
in interpreting conflicting results. Furthermore, the set of dependent
variables is expanded by competence patterns in international student
assessment results, in particular spans (differences between the scores of
the 5th and 95th percentile) as a measurement of educational inequality
and tilts (math – reading) as a measurement of educational STEMorientation.
Several premises need to be established (see Fig. 2). According to the
Human Capital Theory, at the cross-national level, national economic
performance and growth are influenced by national student competence. The latter is a result of the interaction of various factors, such as
values and mindsets, which, in turn, also exerts a direct influence on the
economy. The central model, therefore, tests the suitability of national
student competence as a mediator between national culture and the national economy, while also considering the direct effects. Direct and
mediated effects of national culture are controlled geospatial autocorrelation (Pattnaik, 2020, April 7). Feedback loops from the national
economy to student competence are considered, as described below (see
dotted line in Fig. 2; cf. Rindermann, 2018).
Despite the primarily exploratory approach, hypotheses are based on
the theories of culture described above and examine associations among
national culture, student competence, and economic outcomes. Such
theories assume a positive relationship between student competence and
meritocratic culture variables such as masculinity (competitiveness),
long-term orientation, and educational achievement. Such assumptions
accord well with the views of Max Weber and others (Weber, 1930/
1992; see also, Ertman, 2017) on the role of religion and culture on
economic growth. In addition, consistent with Becker et al. (2022) tilt
research, higher math than reading scores on international student

2. Methods
2.1. Overview of variables and approach

2.2. Data

The research was conducted at the cross-national level using data
from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
spanning from 2000 to 2018. The second (and third) major series of
international large-scale assessment studies, TIMSS and PIRLS, were not
utilized. Unlike PISA, TIMSS and PIRLS place a greater emphasis on
countries’ curricula and education processes rather than on human
capital (Hastedt, 2020). Additionally, results from the latest PISA cycle
in 2022 were excluded from the analysis because it remains uncertain to
what extent the COVID-19 pandemic and the resulting changes in
educational processes may have influenced countries’ cognitive
competence, potentially introducing distorting PISA results (Bonacini &
Murat, 2023; Bryant et al., 2022, April 4th; OECD, 2021, December 1st).
From these data, the triad of PISA variables (means, spans, tilts) is
calculated.
Three sets of variables served as control variables: PISA controls,
geographic controls and economic controls. Multiple statistical interactions
between the PISA variables can be assumed, e.g. a higher mean PISA
score which, by introduce a ceiling effect, limiting both the range of
scores within a population and the difference between math and
reading. Becker et al. (2022, Fig. 4) could not find such an effect of mean
PISA score on tilts (β = − .02|.03) but did not consider PISA span.
Therefore, we consider it necessary to re-examine these possible effects
here PISA controls are the two of three PISA variables (mean, tilt, span)
that were not the dependent variable in the respective model. Geographic
controls are considered essential because cultural transitions often occur
between neighboring countries. As a result, countries with similar cultures frequently share geographic proximity as well as similar environmental and economic conditions (geo-spatial autocorrelations; Pattnaik,
2020, April 7). Economic outcomes, particularly wealth and economic
growth, are commonly used as dependent variables in the literature and
are therefore used here as economic controls. However, it is important to
consider reverse effects.
Cross-national data is often available in the form of annual or periodic variables, offering the advantage of eliminating short-term fluctuations and other potential sources of error through aggregation.
However, these data are frequently incomplete, necessitating appropriate handling of missing values to avoid case exclusions. Furthermore,
the number of cases is constrained by the number of countries participating in studies, resulting in relatively low case-to-variable ratios in
multiple regression models.
The Factor Score Regression (FSR) approach (Croon, 2014), was
employed: Factor scores for latent variables were initially extracted from
structural equation models and subsequently used as manifest scores in
second-stage analyses. This approach simplifies the models by representing latent variables with a single pseudo-observed indicator, which
is crucial given the limited number of cases (countries) and the need to
maintain high case-to-variable ratios. However, it means that the
observed variables can no longer be considered direct reflections but
instead serve as composite measures or estimators of the latent variables. FSR is employed whenever a variable is available for different
time periods (years) or sub-scales, and the data structure is permissible.

packages bda, birtr, car, correlation, DescTools, lavaan, lsr, ltm, nFactors, psy, psych, and stringr. Multiple regression analyses (MRA), path

2.2.1. PISA
Country scores in PISA were taken from the official reports for the
Programme for International Student Assessment (e.g., OECD, 2021).
Data are available across seven waves (2000, 2003, 2006, 2009, 2012,
2015, 2018) and three tested competencies (mathematical literacy,
reading literacy, science literacy) for up to 86 different countries. A
score for a general factor was extracted across all seven waves and
competencies, as well as a general factor for reading literacy and for
mathematical literacy (suitability confirmed by: MSAoverall ≥ .88, pBarlett
≤ .001). These factor scores were extracted for all 86 different countries.
PISA spans were estimated by first calculating the difference between
PISA means on the original scale for the 5th and 95th percentile. This
was done for each wave and competency separately. In the second step,
a general factor was extracted across the calculated spans (suitability
confirmed by: MSAoverall = .70, pBarlett ≤ .001). The factor score was
extracted for 86 different countries. PISA tilts were calculated as the
difference between the factor scores for reading literacy and the factor
score for mathematical literacy (tilt = math – reading), thus also
available for 86 countries. Positive tilt scores (math > reading) indicated
math tilt, implying math strength (relative to reading). Negative tilt
scores (reading > math) indicated reading tilt, implying verbal strength
(relative to math).
2.2.2. Religious affiliations
Shares of people affiliated with a certain religion or religious group
were taken from the World Christian Encyclopedia (Barrett et al., 2001).
The means from the estimated 2000 and extrapolated 2025 data were
calculated, as they were considered to be more representative for the
observed period. Religions were limited to Catholic Christians, Protestant Christians, Muslims and the Three Teachings of East Asia, since all
others had relevant shares in only a few countries. The shares of Catholic
Christians and Muslims were taken directly, while the shares of Protestant Christians were calculated as the sum of Protestants and Anglicans. Becker et al. (2022) used the term “Eastern Traditions” to
summarize Chinese folk-religions, Confucianism, Shintōism, and
Taoism. In this study, the approach was slightly altered to meet the
points made in the theoretical discussion about world religions. According to the CIA (2023a, 2023b), around 70.5% of today’s Japanese
population is counted as Shintōists. Shintōism is the native religion of
Japan and the most important religion next to Buddhism, and is integrated into Japan’s learning, teaching, working, and leadership
(Setyanto et al., 2020). Similar to China, both religions went through a
long period of mutual influence, fertilization, and fusion (Jimura, 2023).
However, Barrett et al. (2001) counted only 2% Shintōists in Japan, but
a large proportion of 26% as New-Religionists. Thus, shares of people
affiliated with Buddhism were added in general and New Religions in the
case of Japan. The result creates the variable Three Teachings of East Asia.
These data were calculated for all 86 countries that participated in PISA.
2.2.3. 6-D cultural model
The 6-D Model differentiates between six dimensions of national
culture: (1) Power Distance, (2) Individualism vs. Collectivism, (3)
Masculinity vs. Femininity, (4) Uncertainty Avoidance, (5) Long-term
Orientation vs. Short-term (normative) Orientation, and (6)

Indulgence vs. Restraint. Country data from 2022 for these dimensions
were directly taken from the Country Comparison Tool of Hofstede Insight
(2022). First, it should be noted that the data for two dimensions have
been recoded in this study to facilitate the interpretation of the results.
Power Distance means the acceptance of inequality and reflects the
given hierarchical order by the less powerful members of society,
whereas Individualism vs. Collectivism refers to the flexibility and vertical permeability of the social framework. The correlation between the
two dimensions is − .65, which supports the assumption that both dimensions measure opposite things in some ways. Thus, scores for Individualism vs. Collectivism were inverted, and the variable was renamed
to Collectivism. Long-term Orientation means thrift and efforts in the
present to gain future benefits, and Indulgence represents a preference
for the fulfillment of pleasure instead of following strict social norms.
The correlation is − .50. Thus, scores for Indulgence vs. Restraint were
inverted, and the variable was renamed Restraint.
Second, it is suggested that these data show some factor structure. A
KMO-test and EFA partly supported this assumption. The overall MSA
with six dimensions was .54, and only Masculinity vs. Femininity
showed an MSA < .50 (MSAMvsF = .14). Excluding Masculinity vs.
Femininity increased the overall MSA to .57, with no MSA of single dimensions <.50. An EFA on the remaining five dimensions suggested a
bifactorial solution, with two dimensions each loading on a common
factor (factor 1: λPower Distance = .85; λCollectivism = .77; factor 2: λLong-term
Orientation = .66; λRestraint = .74).
A MSA of .57 can be considered problematic, despite Kaiser (1974)
suggesting .50 as the minimum threshold, and Field (2009, p. 547) labeling values between .50 and .70 as mediocre. However, our acceptance was also based on the fulfillment of other criteria: (1) Theoretical
Relevance: The variables should be theoretically related and contribute
to measuring a specific construct. Even if the correlations between
variables are low, they may still reflect a common underlying construct.
For instance, both Power Distance and Collectivism describe an individual’s willingness to subordinate themselves to a socially superior
power and to restrain themselves in the pursuit of individual social
advancement. Similarly, Long-term Orientation and Restraint share the
theme of exercising restraint when faced with short-term desires. (2)
Testing other factor analysis assumptions: Sphericity is confirmed with
p < .001 even when considering all four cultural dimensions, and a
medium to strong statistical relationship can be inferred for both pairs of
variables, even when accounting for confidence intervals (rPDIxCOL = .65,
95% CIs [.54, .75]; rPDIxCOL = .50, 95% CIs [.33, .64]).
The two factors were negatively and moderately correlated (r =
− .39). Thus, it was decided to use the scores of the first factor named as
Power Distance & Collectivism and the second factor named as Longterm Orientation & Restraint. The two remaining dimensions, Masculinity vs. Femininity and Uncertainty Avoidance, did not load on the
above two factors and were used as variables without any further
treatment. Masculinity vs. femininity will now be referred to simply as
Masculinity for the sake of simplicity.

2.2.5. Geographic controls
Grades for latitude and longitude of countries’ centroid or center
point were taken from CIA (2021). Higher scores represent a more
northward (relative to southward) or eastward (relative to westward)
position.
2.2.6. Economic controls
From the database of The World Bank (2023a) annual GDP/c PPP
from 2000 to 2019 were taken, from which a longitudinal mean GDP/c
PPP was calculated. GDP/c PPP is expressed in current international
dollars but converted by a purchasing power parity (PPP) conversion
factor that controls for country differences in price levels. The results
were logarithmized to correct for advantages of economic backwardness
(Gerschenkron, 1962). Additionally, economic growth was not just
calculated as the %change from 2000 to the 2019 (last available year),
but by averaging the annual percentage change (±%) between 2000 and
2019 to take compound interest into consideration.

As mentioned in the introduction, national IQ (NIQ) was neither used

However, to verify the reasons behind this decision, NIQ is still retained

appendix. Data came from Becker’s NIQ dataset V1.3.5 (Becker, 2023).
Only the NIQ variable based solely on psychometric data (referred to as

2.3.1. Multiple regression
Multiple regression analyses (MRA) were conducted using a triple

(means, spans, and tilts) as the dependent variable. In each regression

analysis was extended to incorporate control variables, specifically the

further expanded to include economic controls.
The three models of national culture, Religious Affiliations (REL),
Hofstede’s Cultural Dimensions (HCD), and Axiological Cube (AXC),
were analyzed separately. Within each cultural model, the dimensions
should be mutually exclusive in terms of the theoretical construct underlying them. However, across the three models, individual dimensions
may measure similar theoretical constructs. R2 values are presented for
all variables within a model, as well as for cultural variables separately.
This presentation aims to assess the overall variance explained by national culture independently of the control variables.

4. Discussion
4.1. Summary

3.3. Path analyses

The analyses revealed that national culture, measured by the three
selected operationalizations (religious affiliations, Hofstede’s 6-D Cultural Model, Axiological cube), can account for significant variance in
PISA results at the cross-national level, even after controlling for other
factors. However, these effects are limited to specific dimensions. The
use of data from the World Value Surveys, particularly in the context of
the Axiological Cube, emerged as the best predictor, primarily due to
dimensions like Religiosity & Authoritarianism and Sexual Traditionalism, followed closely by religious affiliations, with the latter being
predominantly associated with the share of Muslims. On the other hand,
the dimensions of Hofstede and Inglehart were found to be less predictive, yet they still explained almost half of the variance in means and at
least around one-third of the variance in spans and tilts. This suggests
that utilizing data from the World Value Surveys is worthwhile, despite
the additional workload involved when compared to using religious
affiliations alone.
However, it is important to emphasize that cultural factors seem to
explain a nation’s performance level effectively and performance spans
satisfactorily, but they appear less suitable for explaining tilts. Path
analyses provide some support for the Cognitive Capital Theory
(Rindermann, 2018), as cultural factors seem to have a more indirect
influence on economic growth through their effects on PISA variables
rather than a direct one.
Some support for the first hypothesis was found. This includes, in
particular, the positive effects of Protestantism and the Three Teachings,
as well as the negative effects of Islam and Religiosity & Authoritarianism on PISA means. With regard to the latter, it should be emphasized
that this is the only variable negatively associated with the Culture of
Achievement that has a negative effect on PISA means, albeit the
strongest of all the effects found. However, the expected effects of other
cultural variables assessed as meritocratic, such as masculinity and longterm orientation, did not materialize. This is particularly unexpected
with regard to the variable Power Distance & Collectivism, which according to theory should initially be strongly associated with Confucianism, but showed no correlation with Three Teachings and
subsequently showed a clearly negative effect on PISA means. The second hypothesis is only supported by the positive connection between
Sexual Traditionalism and PISA spans, while the cultural dimension of
Inequality Acceptance shows no effect.

The results from the path analyses on direct and indirect effects on
economic growth (±% GDP/c PPP) are presented in Table 7. These
analyses were limited to the dimensions of national culture that
demonstrated significant effects on PISA variables in the MRA models B.
One striking finding is that the explained variance in models without
control variables is, on average, a quarter or less of the explained variance in models with control variables (0% to 14% vs. 49% to 55%).
However, this aligns with earlier findings by Becker et al. (2022).
Additionally, the direct effect of PISA tilt on economic growth in the
controlled model B (βDir. = .272) is consistent with previous research (e.
g., Becker et al., 2022).
When considering control variables, significant indirect effects were
found for PISA means as a mediator for Three Teachings, Power Distance
& Collectivism, Religiosity & Authoritarianism and Sexual Traditionalism. Share of Muslims also exhibited significant indirect effects in the
absence of control variables. Furthermore, most of the significant direct
effects disappeared in the controlled model. In summary, countries with
higher proportions of Three Teachings tend to exhibit better student
competence, leading to a positive or stronger economic growth (see also
Fig. 4). However, the remaining direct effect of Protestants on economic
growth is negative but nonsignificant. Conversely, countries with higher
Power Distance & Collectivism Religiosity & Authoritarianism and
Sexual Traditionalism tend to have worse student competence and,
consequently, lower or negative economic growth. Nevertheless, the
remaining effect of Power Distance & Collectivism and Sexual Traditionalism on economic growth is positive.
There is only one significant indirect effect in models using PISA
spans as a mediator. Initially, countries with higher values in Long-term
Orientation & Restraint demonstrate better student competence, leading
to positive or higher economic growth. However, this effect disappeared
after adding control variables due to a vanishing direct effect from the
mediator to economic growth (βAxB = .100 → .027) (Table 7).
Finally, in the uncontrolled model, there is no significant direct or
mediated effect of Religiosity & Authoritarianism along PISA tilt on
economic growth. However, after adding control variables, the direct
effect of Religiosity & Authoritarianism on PISA tilt increased in
strength (βB = − .011 → –.421), and this effect is mediated to economic
growth (βAxB = − .115). Consequently, countries with higher Religiosity
& Authoritarianism exhibit worse student competence and negative or
lower economic growth.

suggests that the factorization performed in this study would also be
minimally affected by any changes.

models, a finding that may be attributed to its advanced theoretical
foundation and the measurement validity established through years of
empirical work and the congruence of periods during which the independent and dependent variables were collected. However, as noted

surprising. The content and measurement validity of religious affiliations may be questioned, and the survey periods may be subject to
criticism, with religious affiliations collected in the year 2000 and
extrapolated to 2025 based on trends observed before 2000. Notwithstanding these caveats, the three Abrahamic religions were significantly
better predictors of cognitive competence and economic outcomes than
the Three Teachings of East Asia. Such findings should be cautiously
interpreted due to criticisms of religious affiliation as cultural indicators,
especially for East Asian religions or philosophies, which are heterogenous and therefore challenging to measure. Conversely, employing
concrete and tightly defined cultural dimensions as predictors may be
better suited for uncovering theoretical explanations for effects, as
compared to the broader and less specific definitions associated with
religions.

4.3. Cultural dimensions promoting cognitive competence and wealth
Countries with higher levels of religiosity, authoritarianism, power
distance, and collectivism tended to exhibit lower student competence.
This contradicts the assumption that Confucianism has a positive impact
on student competence, as three out of the four dimensions mentioned
are typically associated with Confucianism. However, the share of Three
Teachings of East Asia correlated positively with student competence
and, consequently, with economic growth. Such a finding contradicts
Max Weber’s assumption of an Confucianist orientation that is friendly
to education but lags behind Protestant societies in economic development (Harris, 2013, p. 79), as education works as a positive mediator
between Three Teachings and economic growth. The assumption that
Confucianism would have a positive effect on educational content that is
unworldly or of little relevance to economic performance is therefore
not confirmed by our results. The simplest explanation could be that the
proportion of individuals affiliated with the Three Teachings does not
align with the dissemination of Confucian principles. This misalignment
might be due to these principles being misattributed or overshadowed
by Taoism’s and Buddhism’s principles. Research beyond the Three
Teachings of East Asia (TTEA) Inventory (Lin et al., 2021) describes
Confucianism as a blend of principles that limit individual needs and

The analysis of cultural dimensions such as Power Distance &

The acceptance of power distances in society may lead to lower
educational aspirations, as social advancement through education is
perceived as significantly less likely. This effect could be even more
pronounced when combined with Sexual Traditionalism, which, among
other things, hinders gender equality in the labor market and reduces
the perceived value of education for approximately half of the population. This may also explain the negative effect of religiosity, which often

2009; Goldscheider et al., 2014). Additionally, stronger religiosity might
lead to the persistence of pre-modern school systems and a focus on
religious, rather than science-related, teaching content. A substantial
body of literature suggests a negative connection between religiosity
and rational thinking (Oesterdiekhoff, 2012, 2021; Rindermann &
Ackermann, 2020; Rindermann et al., 2014). Interestingly, this is supported by the negative effect of Religiosity & Authoritarianism on PISA
tilts, indicating that countries with higher values in this dimension tend
to prioritize non-STEM fields.

developed countries need to prioritize economic growth over creating
equality, as reflected in their cultural values. In line with our models,
equality might be perceived as a short-term and hedonistic goal that

4.5. Implications
Based on our results, we recommend the use of data from the World
Value Survey (WVS) for future research on the relationship between
cognitive competence and culture at a cross-national level. Models like
the Axiological Cube can assist in identifying relevant variables from the
extensive array of available WVS items. While religious affiliation appears to be suitable for Abrahamic religions, when conducting research
on Confucianism or East Asian religions or philosophies as a whole,
using variables that capture specific pertinent aspects of these cultural
systems has to be suggested. This approach also allows for the examination of potentially relevant cultural elements beyond the geographical
regions where the Three Teachings of East Asia are prevalent, thus
introducing greater variability into the analyzed variables.
We consider some further implications worth mentioning. Since
religious education is not part of the material tested in PISA, a negative
association would initially be appropriate as a hypothesis. However, this
association could be influenced by the “distance” between the religious
material taught and the skills and knowledge required to complete PISA
tasks. For example, if school discipline or logical reasoning are promoted
through religious education, PISA results could be improved through
religious education. Additionally, our findings underscore the importance of including geographical coordinates to account for geospatial
autocorrelation, as this adjustment can significantly impact model outcomes. Finally, PISA results correlate strongly with IQ tests and general
intelligence (g) and with economic growth (Rindermann, 2018).
Therefore, in the current study, the mediation effects of PISA on culturaleconomic relations may be related to g rather than specific (non-g)
abilities related to schooling. Contrary to this possibility, PISA means
have been found to measure non-g academic abilities and tilts (math,
reading, science), as well as g (e.g., Becker et al., 2022), suggesting that
the mediation effects of PISA are related to both g and non-g factors.
Future research should examine the degree to which the g and non-g
components of PISA contribute to its mediating impact on culture-

4.4. Limitations
The results of this study must consider some limitations that lie in
both theory and method. One theoretical problem is the chicken-andegg problem regarding effects involving education and economic performance and includes effects involving culture and education. For
example, higher education at the administrative level could lead to
increased trust in institutions among the population due to higher efficiency and better functionality, as described within the Hive-Minde
theory by Jones (2016). Higher education could thus indirectly influence social values through trust and role modeling.
A second limitation is that national populations may include subgroups that differentially influence economic prosperity. For example,
the smart fraction in a population (i.e., top 5% in ability level) is typically a better or at least additional predictor in economic models (e.g.
Rindermann, 2018, p. 378). Furthermore, the smart fraction of a population may adopt culture values that differ from others in a population,
a possibility that could be addressed by comparing cultural values in the
smart fraction to the rest of the population.
A third limitation is related to a potential time-delayed effect of PISA
performance to economic growth. As shown by Rindermann and Becker
(2018), a time lag of 10 to 15 years should be expected before a change
in educational abilities is reflected in a change in economic

Appendix A. Axiological cube factor analyses
There are no representative country data available for the dimensions of the Axiological Cube for the time span observed in this study. Therefore,
these dimensions were recalculated based on current survey results from the World Value Survey. A total of 36 items were selected from waves 4
(1999–2004), 5 (2005–2009), 6 (2010–2014), and 7 (2017–2022) of the World Value Survey (Haerpfer et al., 2022; Inglehart et al., 2014a, 2014b,
2014c). These items were chosen based on their contrasts between cultures of honor and achievement, as listed by Basáñez (2016), his Table 5.1). An
additional 12 items were selected from other tables in the source (Table A1).
Methodological inconsistencies between the four survey waves were considered and corrected when necessary. Items were checked to measure at
the same scale in each wave. This was the case for 33 of the 36 items. The item “Political action recently done: signing a petition” [WVV_21] was not
measured in wave 4, dichotomous in wave 5, trichotomous in wave 7, and measured at a 1 to 5 scale with reversed polarity in wave 6. It was decided to
exclude this item from all further steps. The item “Pray to God outside religious services” [WVV_25] was excluded in waves 5 and 6 but measured on a
1 to 7 scale in wave 4 and a 1 to 8 scale in wave 7. An adjustment would have been necessary here but was not carried out (see next paragraph). The
item asking for “National Pride” [WVV_31] was measured on a 1 to 5 scale in waves 4, 6, and 7, but on a 1 to 4 in wave 5. However, the wording of the
fifth wave was always “I am not [nationality]” and thus just an extension of the 1 to 4 scale in wave 5, and therefore no recoding was necessary. Also
the item “Belong to religious denomination” [WVV_32] was excluded, as it did not use a metric or ordinal scale (Table A2). Individual responses to
these items were first aggregated to the country level, then aggregated across the 4 waves. Extracting longitudinal factor scores for each item was
planned but rejected due to missing sphericity and sampling adequacy. It was therefore decided to calculate longitudinal mean values. However, for
three items [WVV_6, WVV_13, WVV_16], no data could be found in any of the waves, thus they were excluded from analysis.
According to the model concepts of Basáñez (2016), the selected items should show some factor structure, even if the exact structure is not clear
from the source. Using all 31 remaining items showed insufficient suitability for factor analyses (MSAoverall = .50; pBarlett = 1.000). A solution was to
remove all remaining items with data for Nctry < 65 (66% from the maximum of 98 [WVV_4, WVV_8, WVV_12, WVV_14, WVV_25, WVV_27]).
However, five items [WVV_2, WVV_7, WVV_26, WVV_32, WVV_35] showed negative λ on the general factor and were inverted. The final selection of
25 items was tested again and rated as suitable for factor analyses (MSAoverall = .78; pBarlett < .001).
A combined approach of EFA and CFA was used to reveal the factor structure best suited for extracting factor scores (Table A3 and A4). First, the
number of hypothetical factors was increased stepwise to increase cumulative variance explained to at least 2/3. Second, CFA was performed based on
a model structure derived from the factor loadings found by EFA. Each item was assigned to the one factor on which it showed the highest loading.
Cross-loadings were integrated only if the second highest λ was <.10 below the highest.
Within the single factor solution, the general factor explained 48% of the total item variance, but with λ between .97 and .15 and bad model fit (CFI
= .637; TLI = .599; SRMR = .226). A stepwise increase in the hypothetical number of factors made the 3-factor solution most appropriate, explaining
67% of the total variance with only one item not loading on any factor with |λ| > .30. The parallel CFA still revealed poor model fit (CFI = .806; TLI =
.770; SRMR = .095). However, increasing the number of factors to 4 worsened the fit (CFI = .774; TLI = .743; SRMR = .102). Including a fifth factor
did not increase the explained total variance significantly and extracted a factor with an SS-loading of only .615.
In order to ensure a meaningful interpretation of the factors in terms of content, items were first summarized by category: 8 items dealing with
religious issues, both beliefs and participation in religious communities [WVV_1, WVV_2, WVV_5, WVV_11, WVV_15, WVV_17, WVV_20, WVV_24], 4
items dealing with the benevolence toward traditional authorities [WVV_9, WVV_22, WVV_23, WVV_29], 3 items dealing with acceptance of traditional sex roles [WVV_18, WVV_19, WVV_28], 3 items dealing with feelings about life satisfaction [WVV_26, WVV_33, WVV_34], 2 items dealing with
acceptance of homosexuality [WVV_3, WVV_10], 2 items dealing with acceptance of inequality [WVV_35, WVV_36], 1 item dealing with active
political participation [WVV_7], 1 item dealing with national pride [WVV_31], and 1 item dealing with trust in people [WVV_31]. According to the
results of the two-factor EFA, all items about religion and authorities loaded on the first factor along with items about trust in people, national pride
and one item about inequality, whereas the second factor combined all items related to homosexuality, political participation, satisfaction and sex
roles. A second item about inequality did not load on any factors. The three-factor EFA showed strongest loadings on the first factor by almost all items
about religiosity, authority and trust in people, on the second by all items about homosexuality, sex roles, satisfaction and one item about political
participation. However, there were many cross-loadings of items about authority and religion on the first and third factor, together with items about
inequality. Finally, the four-factor EFA made the distinction more clearly in the categories assumed. The first factor referred to all items about
religiosity, trust in people, national pride, and two out of four items about authority. The second factor included all items about homosexuality, sex
roles and political participation, but also one item about authority. Both inequality-items and one about authority loaded on factor three, and the three
items about satisfaction on factor four. However, the authority item that loaded on the second factor asked about the acceptance of having the army
rule. This acceptance can be seen as an extreme form of trusting in authority, but it is much less traditional than trust in religious institutions. As the
army is a refuge for very traditional gender roles, this might explain the strong loading of this item on the same factor as opinions about homosexuality
13

and gender equality. The item about confidence in churches surprisingly loaded on the same factor as both inequality items; however, it also showed a
strong loading on the first factor.
Despite the better fit of the three factors solution, it was decided to use the four factor model to create factor scores from the items, as it represented
the best combination of empirical and theoretical suitability. The first factor was named Religiosity & Authority (kitems = 13) and higher scores represented stronger religiosity and submission to authority; the second was named Sexual Traditionalism (kitems = 7) and higher scores represented
stronger clinging to traditional role models and more regulated sexual morals; the third was named Inequality Acceptance (kitems = 2) and higher scores
represented stronger agreement to competition and inequality; and the fourth was named Satisfaction (kitems = 3) and higher scores represented more
satisfaction and happiness. Scores for these factors were extracted for all 86 countries ever participating in PISA until 2018.
A validation of these scores was done by correlating them with country rankings given by Basáñez (2016), Table 5.6 to 5.8). Results are presented
in Table A5. Countries with stronger Religiosity & Authority, Sexual Traditionalism and Inequality acceptance have, on average, cultures of honor and
respect for tradition and authority, but no cultures of achievement, punctuality and efficiency. For countries with higher satisfaction among their
citizens, the opposite is true. Thus, both types of culture are well represented by the extracted factor scores. The picture is less clear for cultures of joy
and friendship. It seems that these cultures show less Sexual Traditionalism and Inequality acceptance but higher Satisfaction. Correlations are
significant at a mostly moderate level.
