Many calls have been made for more research on social studies teachers' practices and
preservice training. Instructional practices employed by teachers are important for
encouraging student learning. However, there is a history of social studies teachers
focusing much of their time on teacher-centered instructional techniques that have not
demonstrated strong learning for students. Therefore it is important to examine not just
how teachers chose to teach, but also where they may have learned to teach. This study
examined data from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 of 240
secondary social studies teachers to understand what instructional practices they report
employing and their feelings about their preparation. Data analysis provided direct
empirical evidence of the power of teacher preparation programs to shape social studies
teachers’ instructional practices well into their teaching careers.

1. Examining the relationship between social studies teacher training and instructional practice
There has been a long history of, and attention paid to, the instructional methods employed by social studies teachers in
the United States (Bollinger & Warren, 2007). The intersection of a wide variety of beliefs about what social studies instruction
should be and what the curriculum should encompass complicate the examination of social studies instructional methods
(Larson, 2017; Shaughnessy & Haladyna, 1985). However, it is clear that what is taught (e.g., Banks, 2003; Hirsch, 1987; Ross,
1996) and how it is taught (e.g. Fonger et al., 2018; Klette et al., 2017; Tullis & Goldstone, 2020) matter to student learning in
general and in social studies classrooms in particular (eg. Gaston et al., 2016; Heafner, 2004; Mergendoller et al., 2006) and
that teaching approaches can be difﬁcult for teachers to change (National Council of the Social Studies [NCSS], 2013).
Given the importance of teachers' instructional decisions are to student learning in social studies classrooms, it makes
sense to examine what instructional decisions teachers are making. This study draws on a national survey of instructional
practices of social studies teachers take from the Teaching and Learning International Survey 2018 (TALIS; Ainley & Carstens,
2018). While understanding the instructional practices of social studies teachers is important, research should also examine
where teachers learn these practices. As the vast majority of teachers are trained through initial teacher education (ITE)
programs (National Research Council, 2010), the links of these programs to teachers' instructional practices is an important
phenomenon for continued study. TALIS 2018 survey data provides an opportunity to examine both social studies teachers’

2. Instructional decisions matter for student learning
Classrooms are extremely complex systems where there are myriad social, emotional, and cognitive things happening at
once (Ahmadian & Tavakoli, 2011; Harjunen, 2012; Burns & Knox, 2011). The most effective teachers understand the social,
emotional, and cognitive needs of each student and supports growth in every area (Pianta & Hamre, 2009). In this paper we
focus on the instructional decisions of teachersdan area that research has shown to impact student learning (Council of Chief
State School Ofﬁcers, 2011).
The empirical research literature is replete with research articles demonstrating how instructional strategies can inﬂuence
student learning. While there is no one “right” way to teach social studies, research has provided numerous approaches with
empirical support. Social studies teachers should select instructional activities that align with the goals of social studies
education which according to Russell III et al. (2014) includes preparing responsible citizens, developing awareness of
contemporary issues, developing healthy self-concepts, developing problem solving skills, and creating global citizens. The
social studies literature has shown that importance of moving away from strategies narrowly focused on knowledge
acquisition to strategies focused on developing skills and deep understandings. Or, as van Hover and Hicks (2017) put it, Best
practice research … elucidates a vision of inquiry-based discipline-speciﬁc literacy work in which students learn the
knowledge, understandings, and tools that allow them make sense of the world, past, present and future (p.274)
Social studies researchers have called for a move away from traditional teaching techniques of drilling and memorization
(Cuban, 1991) to a broader range of instructional practices (Russell III, 2010). The C3 Framework (NCSS, 2013)da set of
standards for social studies educationdillustrates that effective social studies should consist of four dimensions: developing
questions, applying disciplinary tools and concepts, evaluating sources and using evidence, and communicating conclusions
and taking informed action. Researchers have demonstrated the beneﬁts of different instructional approaches including
constructivism (Mohammad & Kinyo, 2020; van Hover & Hicks, 2017), cooperative learning (Larson, 2017; Nagel, 2008), and
inquiry-based practices (Grant et al., 2017; Saye, 2017). When conducting empirical research on instructional practices it can
be helpful to create or adopt a framework for organizing the multitude of different instructional options each teacher can
pursue.
3. A multidimensional framework for understanding teaching
TALIS is an international large-scale survey designed to collect information about teachers' experiences teaching and in
their schools environments (Ainley & Carstens, 2018) whereby one area of focus is instructional practices. The framework for
understanding teaching in the TALIS is built on the power of instructional practices to predict student learning. It describes
effective teaching as encompassing a number of aspects that encourage students’ motivation and achievement (Ainley &
Carstens, 2018). Based on years of research, TALIS describes effective instructional practices as a multidimensional framework. The four dimensions explored by TALIS are clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, classroom management, and
extended activities (Ainsley & Carstens, 2018; OECD, 2018). In this study, we focus on the ﬁrst three dimensions as the most
relevant to our study and do not include extended activities due to the limited items in this dimension on the TALIS. The three
components of effective teaching described here are not considered to be the entirety of effective teaching and does not
address things such as feedback, assessment, and professional dispositions. However, these practices are associated with
higher learning outcomes for students and teacher development through professional support serves a vital role in ensuring
teachers change and develop their instructional practices (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). The TALIS was created for upper middle
school or lower high school teachers in all subjects; therefore the framework for examining teaching practices is purposefully
not subject-speciﬁc. Each of the three TALIS dimensions used in this study will be described below.
4. Clarity of Instruction
Clarity of instruction represents a collection of different instructional strategies that focus on ensuring the learning of
students through goal setting, re-teaching, relating content meaningfully, and summarizing content (Ainsley & Carstens,
2018; OECD, 2018). Teachers who effectively utilize clarity of instruction provide students with highly speciﬁc directions
and explanations in regards to course organization and content, and do not assume that students will learn the material
automatically (Bulger et al., 2002; Chesebro & McCroskey, 2001). Clarity has been called a “deﬁning characteristic” of effective
teachers (Armstrong et al., 2015, p. 176). In an examination of videos of introductory physics classes, Seidel et al. (2005) found
that high levels of clarity and coherence of lesson goals was associated with increased competence development. Because of
its focus on meeting objectives, making clear what is expected, and making connections between learning targets, clarity of
instruction is seen as an important component of successful student learning in the classroom (Ainsley & Carstens, 2018;
OECD, 2018).
Teachers that provide effective structure also have been shown to improve student engagement (Hospel & Galand, 2016).
Within the structure of a lesson, repetition of skills can help students learn (Cabrera & Martinez, 2001; Saville, 2011).
Additionally, designing lessons that make real-world connections that help students master concepts can be beneﬁcial to

to achieve van Hover and Hicks’s (2017) description of ideal social studies teaching and learning.
5. Cognitive Activation
On the other hand, cognitive activation represents a set of different instructional strategies that give students opportunities to engage with content on a high level, whether through evaluation, complex problem-solving, and/or critical thinking,
and is commonly associated with group collaboration (Ainsley & Carstens, 2018; OECD, 2018). Cognitive activation encourages students to evaluate themselves when it comes to their individual work, to engage in metacognitive tasks, and to
participate in class discussions in which the teacher is not focusing upon right or wrong answers (Baumert et al., 2010).
Cognitive activation is generally less frequent in classrooms compared to some of the other instructional practices, likely due
to the high demand and difﬁculty for teachers (OECD, 2018). When present, however, they are an important indicator of
successful student learning that is more holistic and involves developing a set of complex and sophisticated reasoning skills
(Ainsley & Carstens, 2018; OECD, 2018).
6. Classroom Management
Classroom management represents the practices a teacher utilizes to achieve a safe and orderly environment where
student learning is possible and classroom instructional time is used effectively (Ainsley & Carstens, 2018; OECD, 2018).
Effective classroom management supports and facilitates both academic and socio-emotional learning, and is a necessary
component of successful student learning (Nkhata et al., 2016; Evertson & Weinstein, 2006). Creating a more accessible and
approachable environment, and wasting less redirecting student behavior, are some of the potential factors that contribute
with improved student learning in classrooms with more efﬁcient classroom management (OECD, 2018). However, achieving

Raudenbush & Jean, 2014). However, in the most effective learning environments teachers instructional decisions by teachers
as well as well-established routines and procedures ensure that teachers do not need to spend a large portion of their

7. How do social studies teachers teach?
One major goal of this study is to understand what instructional techniques a national sample of secondary social studies
teachers employ in their classrooms. It is ﬁrst important to situate our study in what we already know about social studies
teachers’ instructional practices. There is a perception among many that social studies teachers rely on traditional teaching
methods that include lecture, drilling, and memorization (Barton & Levstik, 2003; Cuban, 1991; Levstik, 2008). Meanwhile,
some studies indicate that students do not value social studies as compared to other subjects (Chapin, 2006; Chiodo & Byford,
2004; Dundar & Rapoport, 2014). There are few studies in the last two decades that attempt to understand the instructional
practices of social studies teachers on a large scale.
Contrary to the common narrative, social studies research has provided numerous examples of van Hover and Hicks’s
(2017) idea of inquiry-based, discipline-speciﬁc literacy. For example, researchers have illustrated social studies teaching
instruction using historical inquiry (Levstik & Barton, 2010; VanSledright, 2010), authentic social studies (King et al., 2009)
and citizenship education (Merryﬁeld & Wilson, 2005; Parker, 2003). Others have shown social studies educators that have
uses instructional methods to help students re-conceptualize their ideas of citizenship (Sabzalian et al., 2019; Vickery, 2016)
to provide culturally relevant teaching (Martell, 2013; Martell & Stevens, 2019) and to use social studies education as a tool to
support student suffering political trauma (Yoder, 2020). The question is whether these examples are the norm or exceptions
to the norm of social studies classrooms generally. Consistently, large-scale research conducted in the past two decades has
shown a tendency by social studies teachers to rely on teacher-centered, traditional instructional strategies particularly in the
high school grades (Thieman et al., 2013). For example, over 500 eighth and eleventh grade students spread across diverse
districts indicated that they experienced lecture as the most common instructional technique (Wanzek et al., 2015). Bollinger
and Warren (2007) surveyed 102 elementary and 38 secondary teachers in a mid-sized Indiana school district. They found
that lecture was the most commonly used technique followed by individual class projects. Together these two techniques
along with worksheets accounted for 52.4% of class time (Bollinger & Warren, 2007). Meanwhile, Doughty (2017) surveyed
158 high school history teachers in Oregon and found that among the seven instructional practices provided, teachers
employed lecture and teacher-led whole group discussions the most frequently. There is also some evidence that social
studies instructional practices may vary by school in New York City (Crocco & Thornton, 2002).
In a national survey, Leming et al. (2006) reported that social studies teachers professed to prefer student-centered
learning strategies, but actually enacted teacher-centered traditional instruction. In another national study, Russell III
(2010) conducted a survey of 281 social studies teachers from across the United States and asked them to indicate the

Levstik’s (2008) point still appears to be accurate, “Case students make it clear that interesting and excitging things can do

strategies are less effective and more disliked by students (Chiodo & Byford, 2004; Leming et al., 2006; Russell & Waters,
2010). Previous research has clearly indicated that these traditional instructional strategies are popular with social studies
teachers (Beck & Eno, 2012) in studies that focus generally on teachers concentrated in a small geographic area or research
that is more than a decade old. There is continued need for research that explores the current instructional preferences of
social studies teachers on a national scale to understand if the ﬁeld is making progress on implementing the inquiry arc
described in the C3 Framework (NCSS, 2013) and other reform-minded initiatives.
8. Where social studies teachers learn to teach
In addition to understanding how social studies teachers teach, it is also important for social studies teacher educators and
researchers to understand how initial teacher education (ITE) experiences may predict the instructional practices of social
studies teachers. Formal, professional ITE has developed predominantly within institutions of higher education in the United
States (Labaree, 2008; Fraser, 2007). The National Research Council (2010) found that 70e80% of new teachers were trained in
traditional teacher education programs. However, prior to entering these programs teachers spend many years in classrooms
as students in what Lortie (1975) has termed the apprenticeship of observation. When faced with the complexities of a realworld classroom, teachers revert to employing the same instructional strategies they experienced as students because they
are familiar and most comfortable to them (Feldon, 2007).
Research in social studies ITE has shown that participation in a teacher preparation program can change preservice
teachers' ways of thinking. This includes developing new purpose for teaching (Hawley & Crowe, 2016), shifting ideological
understandings (Kenyon, 2017), adopting more critical stances towards instructional planning (Hostetler et al., 2018), and
changing pedagogical beliefs (Doppen, 2007). Other studies have shown an increase in preservice teachers’ self-efﬁcacy
through ITE (Gloria & Oluwadara, 2016), as well as pedagogical content knowledge (Ayers, 2016; Nowell & Poindexter,
2019). Yet there remains a lack of research connecting ITE to teaching practices of in-service teachers which leaves the
question of the relationship of ITE top instructional decision-making. This paper begins to ﬁll this void in the literature by
connecting ITE experiences to the instructional practices of current teachers.
Finally, social studies researchers have published studies on strategies to prepare preservice social studies teachers for
effective instructional practice (e.g., Brush & Saye, 2009; Doppen, 2007; Sibbett & Au, 2018). However, given that much
research on the preparation of social studies teachers has focused on speciﬁc contexts with limited generalizability (Barton &
Avery, 2016), there have been repeated calls for research on the preparation of social studies teachers that links to a broader
context (Adler, 2008; Crocco & Livingston, 2017; Shaver, 2001; Sleeter, 2014). This study attempts to address these calls by
drawing on recent survey responses of social studies teachers across the United States.
9. The current study
In an effort to better understand the current instructional practices of U.S.-based social studies teachers and how their
preservice training might predict their instructional behaviors, this study seeks to answer the following research questions:
1. What instructional practices are being employed by U.S. social studies teachers as recorded in the Teaching and Learning
International Survey (TALIS) 2018 dataset?
2. How do social studies teachers reﬂect on the quality of their preservice teacher training?
3. What is the association between preservice teacher training and instructional practices for social studies teachers?
Using the TALIS 2018 dataset provides an opportunity to explore these questions “more systematically and comprehensively” (Crocco & Livingston, 2017, p. 378) than previous studies in the social studies education literature and to address
previous calls for this type of study (Adler, 2008; Crocco & Livingston, 2017; Shaver, 2001; Sleeter, 2014).

10.1. Participants
TALIS 2018 includes responses from teachers and school leaders from 48 countries and economies and is comprised of 11
different themes regarding the features and practices related to the professional and pedagogical aspects of teaching. The
targeted populations of TALIS 2018 were lower secondary teachers and school leaders (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). For this study

we focused only on teachers who indicated that they teach social studies in the United States, which included a total of 240
teachers, or roughly 10% of the entire number of participants surveyed for TALIS (N ¼ 2560). TALIS is designed to construct a
representative sample of teachers in each country it surveys. While this study cannot claim representation because we only
draw approximately 10% of the total survey population, it does provide a range of social studies teachers from across the
country.
The TALIS dataset provides limited demographic information on the teachers sampled. What data is provided indicates
that teachers in this study had an average of just over 14 total years of total teaching experience (M ¼ 14.12, SD ¼ 8.64) and 9.5
years of teaching experience at their current school (M ¼ 9.55, SD ¼ 7.73). Additionally, of the socials studies teachers, 48.1%
self-identiﬁed as male and 51.9% identiﬁed as female.1
10.2. Instrument
The TALIS survey was developed by the TALIS 2018 International Consortium led by the International Association for the
Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) and operated in partnership with educational agencies in each participating
country (OECD, 2018). The survey was created by the Questionnaire Expert Group (QEG) and built upon previous iterations of
the survey from 2008 to 2013 (Ainley & Carstens, 2018). The OECD collected feedback from 20 OECD-member countries and 5
partner countries to organize and prioritize themes for the 2018 survey (Ainley & Carstens, 2018).
The TALIS survey relies on teacher report of instructional practices and ITE experiences. Self-report has been shown to be a
valid approach to educational research (Wagner et al., 2016) with a history of demonstrated empirical results (eg. Wiens,
Andrei, Chou, Smith, & Anassour, 2018; Holzberger et al., 2013; Korpershoek et al., 2016). As Ainley and Carstens (2018)
put it, “Although [participants'] perspective might be subject to response bias, teachers’ self-reports can provide valid information” (p. 55). Therefore, while imperfect, using teacher self-report is a reasonable research strategy for collecting data
and understanding phenomena.
Instructional Practices. To understand social studies teachers’ instructional practices, we drew on a block of questions in the
TALIS 2018 in which participants indicate how frequently they implement speciﬁc teaching strategies. Participants selected
from 1 ¼ “Never or almost never”, 2 ¼ “Occasionally”, 3 ¼ “Frequently”, 4 ¼ “Always”. The TALIS framework then groups these
instructional strategies (see Table 1) into three composites: clarity of instruction (a ¼ 0.759), cognitive activation (a ¼ 0.672),
and classroom management (a ¼ 0.882). Clarity of instruction is deﬁned as, “clear and comprehensive instruction and
learning goals”; cognitive activation is deﬁned as “instructional activities that require students to evaluate, integrate, and
apply knowledge”; and classroom management is “the actions teachers take to ensure an orderly environment” (Ainley &
Carstens, 2018, p. 54).

pedagogy elements as well as teaching diverse learners, classroom management, and information and computer technology

10.3. Analysis
To answer our research questions we conducted a set of three analyses. First, we examined the instructional practices of
social studies teachers by computing descriptive statistics for the instructional practices individually and also for the composite variables (i.e., clarity of instruction, cognitive activation, and classroom management). Next, we examined social
studies teachers’ reﬂections on their preparation by calculating descriptive statistics on the ITE elements. Finally, to understand if feelings about the quality of ITE were predictive of instructional practices, we calculated linear regression equations
(Pedhazur, 1997) in which the ITE elements were the predictors and the instructional practice composites were the
dependent variables. This resulted in three separate regression equations. Because teaching experience has been shown to be
related to teaching practices (Cappella et al., 2016), we also included a variable for total years of teaching experience in the
regressions. Also, because gender is commonly examined in educational research, we included a binary (male-female) gender
variable.
11. Results
To answer our ﬁrst research question, we examined the teaching practices of social studies teachers in the United States.
On a four-point scale, instructional practices in the clarity of instruction domain were the most frequently employed
(M ¼ 3.10). Classroom management was the second most frequent instructional activity (M ¼ 2.75). Cognitive activation was
the least frequent category of instructional practices (M ¼ 2.51). Paired-samples T-tests indicated that the mean differences
between the three instructional practice categories were statistically signiﬁcant at p < .001. In terms of individual practices,
the most frequently employed practice was “I explain what I expect the students to learn” (M ¼ 3.42), followed by “I explain
how new and old topics are related” (M ¼ 3.37), and “I set goals at the beginning of instruction” (M ¼ 3.29). The least frequent
was “I present tasks for which there is no obvious solution” (M ¼ 2.00). See Table 1 for full results.
Our second question was answered by examining social studies teachers’ thoughts on their ITE. As shown in Table 2, social
studies teachers felt best prepared in the content of their subject (M ¼ 3.19) and general pedagogy (M ¼ 3.11). They felt least
well-prepared in use of information and communication technology teaching (M ¼ 2.42), teaching in a multicultural or
multilingual setting (M ¼ 2.52), and teaching in a mixed ability setting (M ¼ 2.56).
The ﬁnal research question sought to understand the connection between quality of ITE and teaching practices. The results
for the three regression equations can be seen in Table 3. Results show a signiﬁcant relationship between teaching experience,
gender, and ITE with instructional practices. All three equations were statistically signiﬁcant; however, the relationship with
cognitive activation was only signiﬁcant at the less strenuous p < .10 level. The predictor variables explained between 15.7%
(Clarity of Instruction), 5% (Classroom Management) and 3.7% (Cognitive Activation) of the variance in teaching practices of
teachers. In an activity that is as complex as teaching, this is a substantial amount of variance explained by the models. Gender
was a signiﬁcant predictor of clarity of instruction and classroom management. This indicates that self-identiﬁed females
spend signiﬁcantly more instructional time on clarity of instruction and classroom management then do their male peers.
Teaching experience had signiﬁcantly negative relationship to classroom management, indicating that teachers with more
years of teaching experience spend less time focused on student behavior.
In terms of elements of teacher preparation, content of the subject, pedagogy of subjects, ICT training, and monitoring
students’ development and learning were positively associated with teaching practices. Strong preparation in subject area
content resulted in more time spent in clarity of instruction; however, there was no relationship between content preparation
and cognitive activation. This relationship was also present for classroom management and cognitive activation, but was not
statistically signiﬁcant. Meanwhile, strong preparation in pedagogy of the subject taught resulted in less instructional time

12. Discussion
Scholars have demonstrated the beneﬁts of instructional activities that promote deep learning and focus on student
construction of knowledge (van Hover & Hicks, 2017; Nagel, 2008; Grant et al., 2017), what TALIS framers call cognitive
activation. However, data from TALIS 2018 does not indicate that social studies teachers are employing these strategies at high
frequencies. Social studies teachers respond that they use cognitive activation instructional practices between occasionally
and frequently (M ¼ 2.51). In other words, as curricular-instructional gatekeepers (Thornton, 2001) teachers more frequently
engage in teacher-centered activities and classroom management than they do in more student-centered and deep-learning
strategies. This ﬁnding provides an update and continued conﬁrmation of previous research that found social studies teachers
to prefer traditional instructional techniques that focus on practice and memorization (Bollinger & Warren, 2007; Knowles
et al., 2020; Leming et al., 2006; Russell III, 2010; Wanzek et al., 2015). Yet the recent data presented in the present study
may provide some hope for social studies teacher educators. While social studies teachers reported spending more time on
teacher-centered instruction (i.e., clarity of instruction), the difference, while signiﬁcant, was not terribly large. However,
these ﬁndings continue to indicate the necessity of professional development and ITE efforts to equip current and future
teachers to facilitate the open-ended learning tasks reﬂective of the “inquiry-based discipline-speciﬁc literacy work” (van
Hover & Hicks, 2017, p. 274) described in the C3 Framework (Grant et al., 2017; NCSS, 2013; Saye, 2017).
Data analysis revealed that secondary social studies teachers felt best prepared in content knowledge, general pedagogy,
and content-speciﬁc pedagogy. This may not be surprising given the common program construction in ITEs for preservice
social studies teachers consisting of large numbers of credits in the social sciences as well as courses in both general and social
studies-speciﬁc teaching methods (Bitmann et al., 2017).
Of particular concern, however, are the ﬁndings that social studies teachers felt less prepared to work with diverse learners
and implement ICT. Given the changing demographics of the United States, social studies teachers need to be well-prepared to
support the learning of students from different cultural and linguistic backgrounds (Barton & Avery, 2016). The interesting
caveat to this ﬁnding is that ITE in multicultural or multilingual settings was not associated with differences in instruction.
The need for improved multicultural teacher education is certainly not new (Gay & Howard, 2000), but clearly continues to be
an issue for teacher education and professional development.
Instructional technology similarly provides a resource for engaging students in the early secondary social studies classrooms where the participants teach (Chiodo & Byford, 2004; Heafner, 2004). However, social studies teachers’ perceived
preparation in this area was positively related to more teacher-centered strategies and to a lesser degree to cognitive activation. There is not a clear explanation for why this may be the case. Research indicates that ITE programs that provide the
best growth in ICT as measure by technology pedagogical content knowledge are those that integrate ICT instruction
throughout the program and do not isolate these skills in technology-focused courses only (Borthwick & Hansen, 2017;
Mouza et al., 2017). It may be the case that ITE programs that integrate technology pedagogy throughout their programs

provide a stronger learning experiences for preservice teachers to use as a platform for their instructional decisions once they
become full time teacher themselves. This is certainly an area that requires further study.
Social studies education research has not previously connected the ITE experiences of teachers to their instructional
practices. While there are many factors that play into the instructional decisions of social studies teachers (Crocco & Thornton,
2002; Lortie, 1975; Thornton, 2001), it is incumbent on social studies ITE programs and social studies teacher educators to
demonstrate that teacher preparation makes a difference in how teachers teach. Data presented in this study begin to make
an empirical case for the relationship between ITE and instructional practice. Regression analysis demonstrated a predictive
relationship between the quality of ITE and teaching practices in content preparation, general pedagogy, ICT, and monitoring
students’ development and learning. The fact that the ITE areas most predictive of teaching practices were the same areas for
which social studies teachers felt the best trained prior to beginning their careers provides support for existing literature on
effective preservice social studies teacher education (e.g., Brush & Saye, 2009; Doppen, 2007; Sibbett & Au, 2018). Paradoxically, the analysis revealed a negative relationship between ITE preparation in subject-speciﬁc pedagogy and cognitive
activation. While social studies teachers who feel well-prepared may develop an understanding that “always” facilitating
inquiry-based instruction is not appropriate or sustainable (Grant et al., 2017), another possible explanation is that social
studies methods courses have rarely prepared teachers to plan instruction that “asks students to decide on their own procedures for solving complex tasks” or “present tasks for which there is no obvious solution.” In any event, the nuances in the
present study suggest that the relationship between social studies pedagogy and the instructional practices constructs
included in the TALIS survey necessitates further analysis.
Overall, the quality of ITE was predictive of TALIS-identiﬁed instructional practices, particularly in the area of clarity of
instruction as teachers who generally felt well-prepared used these instructional practices more frequently. Previous scholars
have called for a broader examination of ITE that social studies teachers receive and how that impacts their teaching (Adler,
2008; Crocco & Livingston, 2017; Sleeter, 2014). By examining data on social studies teachers from across the United States,
we can see that ITE does predict teaching later in the career. At the same time, the present study suggests that further
investigation of the relationship between ITE and cognitive activation practices is necessary.
13. Limitations
This study does present certain limitations that must be considered when understanding the data and results presented
here. Given that the TALIS 2018 survey was designed for all subject area teachers, the questions asked were not targeted
speciﬁcally to methods common and speciﬁc to social studies classrooms. Previous studies (Knowles et al., 2020; Leming
et al., 2006; Russell III, 2010) have focused speciﬁcally on social studies methods and were able to narrow in on such practices such as lecture and whole-class discussions. Using TALIS requires an understanding of how those instructional practices
map onto the clarity of instruction domain.
This is not a longitudinal study. The TALIS 2018 survey asks teachers to reﬂect on their ITE experiences which may have
occurred many years ago while also providing information on their current practices. The ﬁeld would beneﬁt from longitudinal studies tracking teachers out of their ITE and throughout their careers. In the absences of expensive, broad-ranging
longitudinal data, using self-report surveys is an effective way to further our understanding of social studies instruction, ITE,
and the connection between the two.
Finally, it is important to understand that the TALIS provides only the broadest picture of social studies educators’
practices. It is not possible to disaggregate the data due to the fact that racial or geographic information is not provided for the
participants. Additionally, the gender information provided is limited to binary male/female categories which does not
capture all expressions of gender present in the teaching population. There is a need to develop social studies education
datasets that capture a more nuanced picture of both social studies teachers, their practices, and their contexts.
14. Conclusion
This study responds to previous calls for research on teaching and ITE related to a broader context (Adler, 2008; Crocco &
Livingston, 2017; Sleeter, 2014) by describing instructional practices of social studies teachers and providing evidence that
training impacts those practices. Data presented in this study provides further evidence of social studies teachers' instructional practices. The analysis also provides direct empirical evidence of the power of ITE to shape social studies teachers’
instructional practices well into their teaching careers.
