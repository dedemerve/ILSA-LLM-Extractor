The innovative PISA domain “Learning in the digital world (LDW)” integrates the assessment of knowledge and
skills with opportunities for learning. To investigate whether learning took place during the assessment, we
analyzed data from 737 German PISA 2022 students and modeled individual differences in test performance that
could not be explained by students'prior knowledge and skills. We then related these differences to learning
activity (the use of worked examples during a task) and learning prerequisites (general intelligence and mastery
orientation) to validate their interpretation as performance based on knowledge and skills acquired during the
task. We found substantial remaining variance in performance after controlling for prior knowledge (21 % of
variance). Significant relationships of these differences with both learning prerequisites and learning activity
provide further evidence for interpreting found differences as a result of learning during the task.
Educational relevance statement: This study provides initial evidence that a learning process takes place during
PISA-LDW assessments. Students that made use of the provided learning opportunities and/or have high intelligence consistently performed better than would be expected based on their prior knowledge. The use of
learning opportunities was related to the learning goal orientation of students. Digital environments should be
designed in a way that is conducive to learning goal orientations and provide explicit learning opportunitites.

1.1. Separating prior knowledge from acquired knowledge

environment (Helmke & Weinert, 1997; Reinhold et al., 2024). Learning
activities are often categorized as being Interactive, Constructive, Active
or Passive (ICAP, Chi & Wylie, 2014).
Learning activity can occur during any point of the solution process.
Therefore, it is not necessarily represented in learners' final answer
(response data). By utilizing trace data generated during task processing,
we can derive process indicators (Goldhammer & Zehner, 2017) that
represent information about the task solving process. Process data are
inferred from log events and represent what happened in the process of
task solving – such as the press of a button (Kroehne & Goldhammer,
2018). By contextualizing log events – which specific button is pressed on
which screen - we can define specific actions and then interpret those as
indicators of cognitive processes (see Hahnel et al., 2019).
In the present study, we are mainly focusing on one specific indicator
of intentional learning activity: the use of worked examples that are
offered as a learning resource in the environment. Interacting with
worked examples has repeatedly been shown to foster learning in a wide
variety of subjects and contexts (e.g. Barbieri et al., 2023; Chen et al.,
2015; Schwonke et al., 2009) and those needing to be actively accessed
on-demand by the test-takers (rather than being prompted or automatically presented) indicates that their usage can indeed be conceptualized
as intentional learning activity. According to the ICAP framework (Chi &
Wylie, 2014), accessing worked examples triggers self-explanation and
can thus be categorized as a constructive activity. However, it is also
possible that students simply read through the example without
engaging in self-explanation, which would rather be considered a passive learning activity. In either case, we would assume that students with
a higher tendency to access these worked examples would be more likely
to acquire knowledge during the assessment. The indicator is derived
from the contextualized event (Kroehne & Goldhammer, 2018) that is
registered when test-takers click on the “example“button. The interpretation of the indicator as “intentional learning activity“needs to be
subject to validation (Goldhammer et al., 2021).

There are several options to separate prior from acquired knowledge
and skills in data analysis. The most rigorous way would be by
comparing outcomes between groups in randomized controlled trials
(RCTs), of which one group receives learning opportunities while the
other does not. Even in randomized studies, only the availability of the
learning opportunity can be experimentally assigned, while the actual
use remains an observed moderator variable (see Reinhold et al., 2024).
In cases where RCTs are not possible or not desired, the most common ways to separate prior from acquired knowledge are the
covariance-analytical approach and the latent change score approach –
both of which have their own advantages, disadvantages and design
requirements (see Köhler et al., 2021). Both of these approaches require
the variable of interest to be measured at at least two timepoints (prepost design) and conceptualize learning as a change in that variable.
If neither RCT nor pre-post designs using parallel (or linked) instruments are possible or desired, a different indicator of relevant prior
knowledge can be used as a control variable in the model, predicting the
performance after the learning session or intervention (e.g. Kunter et al.,
2013). By using an indicator of prior knowledge and skills to predict
performance at the end of a learning session or period, we obtain a residual which informs us—on a between person level—whether someone
performed better or worse than it would be expected from their prior
knowledge. The variance that is not explained by the prior performance
(residual variance) can then be attributed to either differences in the
measured constructs and/or differences in the testing situation and/or
changes over time (such as learning). This residual approach can be
conceptualized as a variant of the covariance-analytical approach, in
which the pre-test is not identical (or even parallel) to the post-test but is
still assumed to measure the same – or related – constructs. It also
conceptualizes acquired knowledge as the amount of variance in an
outcome that is not explained by prior knowledge.
The closer the outcome measure is aligned with the prior knowledge
measure, the more likely it is that effects of superficial task familiarity
get erroneously conceptualized as learning (see Scharfen et al., 2018; te
Nijenhuis et al., 2007). The more the outcome measure diverges from
the prior knowledge measure, the more likely it is that differences between the measures get erroneously attributed to learning. In both cases
– as long as a control group without learning opportunities is not an
option - it is prudent to look at further sources of evidence in order to
validate the interpretation of these residuals as learning. By using validity arguments (Kane, 2013), those interpretations of residual variance
can be related to other sources of evidence and thus be differentiated
from each other. If the residual variance is systematically related to
learning activities and/or learning prerequisites not included in the
pretest, this provides convergent evidence (AERA et al., 2014) for its
interpretation as representing acquired knowledge and skills.
Combining these information sources in a triangulation approach (e.g.
Bans-Akutey & Tiimub, 2021) helps to validate the used indicators and
scores and potentially also contributes to understanding interindividual
differences in learning processes. It is important to note here, that this
approach – by controlling for prior knowledge – also controls for
knowledge acquisition that is contingent on prior knowledge. According
to the “knowledge is power” hypothesis (Ausubel, 1968; Greve et al.,
2019), prior knowledge is one of the most important determinants of
learning, but recent meta-analytic findings (Simonsmeier et al., 2022)
have challenged this hypothesis.

1.3. Cognitive and motivational learning prerequisites
Apart from specific learning activity during the task, certain relatively stable person-characteristics may also facilitate learning processes. They can either increase the likelihood of learning activity (e.g.
through motivational aspects) or the efficiency of learning activity (e.g.
through cognitive aspects). According to Skill Acquisition Theory
(Ackerman, 1988; VanLehn, 1996), the acquisition of cognitive skills
can be understood as a process consisting of successive phases, with
general cognitive abilities being the most important determinant of
learning performance in the early phases. Later extensions on the theory
have also emphasized the importance of emotional and motivational
factors as well (e.g. Langan-Fox et al., 2002). Thus, in this study, we
wanted to focus on one cognitive and one motivational learning
prerequisite.
As a motivational learning prerequisite, mastery orientation (Hsieh,
2011; Meyer, 2023) has been shown to influence learning in a specific
task by increasing the likelihood of (successful) learning activity
(Pintrich, 2000; Yokoyama & Miwa, 2020). Mastery orientation is
defined as having the goal of learning and mastering the task according
to self-set standards. “[A mastery-oriented] learner is focused on
developing new skills, improving, and acquiring additional knowledge“(Hsieh, 2011, p. 915), and its positive association with learning
has been shown in multiple meta-analyses (e.g. Cellar et al., 2011;
Utman, 1997). The assumed mechanism of this association is that a
desire to learn increases the chance of behavior that is specifically targeted at learning to occur (Hsieh, 2011).
As a cognitive learning prerequisite, general intelligence (or
reasoning ability) has often been conceptualized as the ability to learn
(Ackerman, 1987, 1992; Jensen, 1989; Montuori & Montefiori, 2022).
Sternberg (2018, p. 145) even summarizes the common thread in the

2. Methods
The current study was carried out as an add-on study to PISA 2022.
Test-takers went through a battery of computer-based tests. These consisted of two different LDW units that guide students through a series of
progressively more difficult tasks while providing learning opportunities, as well as a selection of other ability tests and self-report questionnaires. The study was approved by the ethics committee of
BLINDED. The PISA 2022 data collection was subject to a content and
data protection review. Approval was granted by the Ministries of Education and Cultural Affairs of all federal states. Further details can be
found in the national report on PISA 2022 (Lewalter et al., 2023).

1.4. Alternative explanations: Complex problem solving

2.1. Sample

As the prior knowledge and the outcome measures used in this study
are not completely identical in the factors that contribute to respective
performance, it is important to conceptualize these differences in order
not to erroneously attribute differences in the measured constructs to
learning. For the prior knowledge measure, the performance is assumed
to be a function of domain-specific prior knowledge, component skills
and the ability to apply those. For the outcome measure, the performance is a function of domain-specific prior and acquired knowledge,
component skills and the ability to apply those. Additionally, the tasks
that represent the outcome measure are complex, meaning that not all
required information is presented from the outset and the test-takers
need to interact with the problem in order to gather the information
needed to solve it (Frensch & Funke, 1995). Performance in the outcome
measure should thus also be influenced by the complex problem solving
(CPS) ability of test-takers.
By regressing the outcome measure on the prior knowledge measure
we obtain a residual that should represent the differences between these
functions. We can thus conceptualize the variance in the outcome
measure that is not shared with the prior knowledge measure as containing mainly the CPS ability as well as acquired knowledge and skills.
By controlling for CPS in addition to the pretest, we can further isolate
the variance of the outcome measure that is expected to be associated
with acquired knowledge and skills. It is important to note that CPSmeasurement is often conceptualized as incorporating knowledge
acquisition as well (e.g. Herrmann et al., 2023). In this case there would
be shared variance with the acquired knowledge component of the
outcome measure, leading to a systematic underestimation of acquired
knowledge in models controlling for CPS.

Data from students who participated in the PISA 2022 main study in
spring 2022 were collected in November and December 2022. Schools
and students participated in the study on a voluntary basis and received
no compensation. Our sample consisted of 737 students that also took
part in the PISA 2022 main study. Of those students, 49 % identified as
female, 46 % as male, and 4 % as diverse, with less than 1 % declining to
answer. They mostly attended the 10th grade and were between 14 and
18 years old (M = 15.59, SD = 0.64) at the time of data collection.
Students were nested in 46 different schools, with 51 % of students
attending academic secondary schools. As the participation was voluntary and no preselection based on sampling criteria was made, it is not a
representative sample of the German PISA 2022 student population.
While the PISA target population consisted of students that were 15–16
years old at the time of testing (April – May 2022), we used a class-based
sampling approach. Accordingly, some students did not participate in
PISA 2022 and thus might be older or younger. Due to differing policies
between the different federal states, participation was mandatory only
in one of the federal states, leading to a significant overrepresentation of
students from that state.
2.2. Instruments and measures
2.2.1. LDW-units
The current study is situated in the context of the PISA 2025 innovative domain – “Learning in the Digital World“and used early prototype
versions of the tasks that are administered in the main PISA study in
2025. Learning in the digital world is defined as “the capacity to engage
in an iterative process of knowledge building and problem solving using
computational tools and practices“(OECD, 2023) and comprises the
constructs self-regulated learning on the one hand and computational
and scientific inquiry practices on the other hand.
Six units of the types “System Modelling” and “Block-based Programming” (three each) were used in the study. Each student completed
two units (one per type), with one unit lasting up to 30 min. Within a
unit, students first received an introduction to the environment, followed by a closed response format test to assess relevant prior knowledge. After that, the students went through a series of three interactive
learning tasks (Learning Centre; LC), in which they could work out solutions step by step and use learning materials and resources in form of
worked examples offered in the learning environment.
Finally, students were tested in a “Big Challenge” (BC) task that integrated all the requirements of the previous learning tasks. Performance in the BC task served as an indicator of the combination of prior
knowledge and acquired knowledge and used as a dependent variable
for further analyses.

1.5. Research questions
In the present study we use data from the German national add-on
study to PISA 2025 – Learning in the digital world (LDW) to explore
whether we can capture acquired knowledge by triangulating information from learning outcomes (residual of final performance controlled
for prior knowledge and skills), intentional learning activity (use of
worked examples) and learning prerequisites (intelligence and mastery
orientation). This leads us to the following research questions:
1) Are there systematic inter-individual differences in the outcome
measure that cannot be explained by prior knowledge and skills of
the participants?
2) Can these differences be interpreted as knowledge and skills acquired
during the learning process? If learning takes place during the
assessment, we would expect these differences to be related to test

2.2.1.1. Scoring and scaling of cognitive performance. The pretest items
from each unit were scored binary (correct/incorrect) as they were
closed response formats with clearly defined correct answers. They were
then integrated across units into a single scale using a 1PL model, as the
pretest items from the different LDW units reflect different relevant aspects of the construct of interest and should thus be weighted equally
(Robitzsch & Lüdtke, 2021). This scale was used as our measure of
domain specific prior knowledge.
For the LC and BC task performance we used a partial credit scoring
(0–3 points). Tasks from the block-based programming units were
awarded full score when the correct result was obtained with the minimum possible amount of code blocks. Decreasing amounts of partial
credit were awarded for correct solutions with more than the minimum
possible amount of code blocks or for solutions that were close to
obtaining the correct result. For tasks from the system modelling units,
full credit was awarded when a control of variables (CoV) strategy was
demonstrated (only one variable of interest was manipulated at a time,
while the rest was kept constant) and the correct conclusions were
drawn from the experiments. Partial credit was awarded when either
CoV was demonstrated but the wrong conclusions were drawn, or the
right conclusions were drawn from an incomplete set of CoV experiments. We integrated the resulting scores into a single scale across both
units using a partial credit model. The EAP ability estimates from the BC
tasks were used as our outcome measure. We also compared a onedimensional model where the scores from the pretest, learning tasks
and big challenge are all indicators of a single latent ability with a threedimensional model where they represent three (correlated) subdimensions of this ability. Detailed information on dimensionality, itemfit and reliability of the resulting scales is reported in the results section
and the supplementary materials.

2.4. Data analyses
In order to estimate whether knowledge acquired during the LC tasks
is influencing the performance in the BC task we need to isolate those
capabilities, skills and knowledge components that were already present
at the beginning of the unit (RQ1). To this end we set up a model in
which the BC score variable is explained by both the pretest score (with a
freely estimated regression weight) and the derived residual variable –
RES (with a regression weight set to one). RES is introduced to represent
the unexplained (residual) variance of BC performance which in turn is
set to zero. Moreover, the correlation between the pretest and the RES
variable is set to zero. This allows us to predict the residuals in the same
model, using indicators of learning prerequisites and learning activity to
validate their interpretation as acquired knowledge (Muthén, 2011). As
a next step we added indicators of learning as predictors for the RES
variable to the model, starting with general intelligence and the use of
worked examples, as those were assumed to directly influence learning
(Fig. 4). This approach of initializing the residual as a separate variable
that is uncorrelated with the prior knowledge measures also allows us to
include predictors that are assumed to correlate with prior knowledge
without running into collinearity issues (Freckleton, 2002; García et al.,
2020).

to that residual (β = 0.23 p ≤0.01) (RQ3). This can be partially
explained by the high correlations between CPS, Intelligence and Prior
Knowledge (see Table 3).
3.3. Validating the interpretation of “use of worked examples” as
learning activity

3. Results

The use of worked examples could be significantly explained by
students' mastery orientation, whereby the positive effect of mastery
orientation on the residual was completely mediated by the use of
worked examples (Fig. 8), leading to a significant indirect effect of
mastery orientation on the residual (β = 0.22, p = .01). This finding
supports the interpretation of the indicator “use of worked examples” as
intentional learning activity and is in line with theory on mastery
orientation (e.g. Hsieh, 2011).
Together, these findings provide initial evidence that there is indeed
a learning process involved in solving LDW units and that the residual
variable can be interpreted at the interindividual level as an indicator of
“acquired knowledge”.

We obtained item Infit parameters ranging from 0.92 to 1.02 for the
pretest items and ranging from 0.91 to 1.11 for the BC items (Fig. 5). As a

(Linacre, 2002). A table with detailed fit-statistics and solution rates at
the item-level can be found in the supplementary materials (Table S2

while the BC items did not represent the lower ability range well (Fig. 6).
The 3-dimensional model (Pretest, LC, BC) achieved a significantly
better fit than the 1-dimensional model (chi2 = 16.75; df = 5, p = .005),
indicating that the different parts of the unit measure slightly different,
albeit highly correlated abilities. The high correlation between the PRE
and BC dimensions (r = 0.91) legitimizes our use of the pretest as an
indicator of prior ability, as this conceptualization only makes sense if
the pretest measures roughly the same (or a highly related precursor)

If a residual (or even an implied residual that is not extracted as a
specific parameter) is interpreted in any way that goes beyond “variance
component that is not explained by the predictors“that interpretation
can and should be supported by validation arguments (Kane, 2001,
2013). In the present study, we employed a triangulation approach
(Bans-Akutey & Tiimub, 2021; Cook et al., 2015; Kane, 1992) where we
used a combination of indicators of learning processes (resource usage)
and learning prerequisites (intelligence and mastery orientation) to
approach learning from different perspectives and use the convergence
of these indicators as evidence for the validity of the interpretation
learning outcomes (residual of final performance).
While the use of process data in general is becoming more and more
common (Becker et al., 2022), the true potential of those data lies in
their combination with other data sources (Goldhammer et al., 2017).
This not only allows for the validation of measures in a triangulation
approach but also offers avenues of investigation into the mechanisms
that lead to specific outcomes (e.g. Zhang et al., 2023). In the case of this
study, the significant positive relation between mastery orientation and
use of worked examples strengthens the interpretation of the later as
learning activity as this relation would not be expected when people just
click on the button by accident or look at the example without engaging
with it. Further contextualizing the indicator, for example by incorporating the time those examples were viewed or the actions that immediately preceded the use of the example could further reduce the amount
of construct-irrelevant variance.

learning resources enabled or facilitated learning processes that lead to
increased performance not just in the specific Learning Center (LC) tasks
that they were designed to facilitate, but also in the Big Challenge (BC)

intentional learning activity is supported by its relationship with

learning process is responsible for the observed differences, as intelligence is known as one of the main cognitive prerequisites for learning.

As shown by previous research, an important factor for effective use

variables such as self-monitoring or self-evaluation.
In the student questionnaire, mastery orientation was operationalized as a trait. While this conceptualization is common and intuitively
plausible, some studies found that there is substantial within-person
variability in mastery orientation (e.g. Huang et al., 2017). A metaanalysis by Utman (1997) could also show that variance in state
mastery orientation can be induced by the environment and that these
induced states also correspond to better learning outcomes, relative to
induced performance orientation. This leads to interesting implications
for instructional design: Learning environments which rely on the selfregulation of learning processes by the learner and/or offer resources
that can be accessed on demand could be designed in a way that is
conducive to the activation of mastery orientation. Options to achieve
this include varying the instruction to put a focus on achieving mastery
(e.g. Graham & Golan, 1991), varying feedback to emphasize selfreferencing standards (Johnson et al., 1993) or reducing pressure to
perform well (e.g. Dweck & Leggett, 1988).

There are three major limitations of the current study, all grounded
in the fact that the present study is part of a large-scale assessment. The
first major limitation is the lack of a conventional pre-post design as well
as the lack of any experimental manipulation. The most rigorous way to
separate prior from acquired knowledge would be to have a linked preand posttest, with students randomly assigned to either the learning
tasks of a unit or an irrelevant time-equivalent task between the pre- and
post-testing. The difference in performance delta between the groups
would be a clear indicator of knowledge and skills acquired during the
learning tasks. Even without experimental manipulation, having a pretest that is conceptually equivalent and/or psychometrically linked to
the outcome measure would have allowed for a more precise estimation

4.3. Implications for instructional design
As the present study has shown, making use of the resources offered
in digital learning environments is an important predictor for learning
success in those environments.
Previous research showed that the choice of different kinds of
learning resources or strategies are systematically related to both characteristics of the task and the learner (e.g. Zhang et al., 2023) and similar
influences were also found for the effectiveness of these resources or
strategies (e.g. Kalyuga, 2007; Kalyuga et al., 2001; Renkl, 1997). In

The second major limitation of the present study is the operationalization of acquired knowledge as a residual at the inter-individual
level. Strictly speaking, the residual cannot be interpreted as being
caused by learning, only by differences in learning. If all test-takers showed
some learning during the task, this would not be represented in the residual. In a similar vein, even the amount of learning that differs between people, but is completely contingent on the prior knowledge of
the test-taker will not be represented in that residual. Given the extensive research that has shown prior knowledge to be an important predictor for learning (e.g. Brod, 2021; Chase & Simon, 1973), this is an
important limitation. Another implication of this operationalization is
that a residual of zero (or even a negative residual) does not imply an
absence of learning (or negative learning), but just that this person
learned less than would be expected given their prior knowledge.
The third major limitation is that the LDW-units were limited to 30
min, leading to a substantial part of the sample (between 8 % and 38 %
depending on the specific unit) not being able to complete the challenge
task in time. This does not only cause a loss of data for that task but could
also introduce biases if the missing mechanism is not ignorable (Mislevy,
2017). In addition to missing data, the limited timeframe for solving the
units also potentially introduces time pressure, which could in turn
affect students' learning activity. Generally speaking, time pressure has
been shown to differentially affect people and items based on not just
ability level and item difficulty but also other person-level characteristics such as need for cognition or working memory capacity (Gonthier,
2023).

5. Conclusion
The study demonstrates that we can validly approximate relative
learning on an interindividual level by estimating residuals of final
performance measures (controlled for prior knowledge and skills). This
approach circumvents the strict design requirements of change measurement via pre-post-RCT studies, but carries some important limitations concerning the interpretation of the results. The specific indicators
used to validate those residuals are in line with established theories of
learning but imply that different pathways to learning success exist
within the same environment. By using a triangulation approach to
validation that encompasses indicators of learning outcomes, learning
processes, and learning prerequisites, we also demonstrate that the
interpretation of residuals as acquired knowledge can be validated. The
presented work thus provides a starting point for further analyses of
learning processes, not just specifically in the PISA-LDW context, but
also in learning (or assessment) environments in general. More specifically, this work shows that the performance in the PISA-LDW assessment does indeed appear to contain a mixture of prior and acquired
knowledge.

4.5. Future directions
The current study only used one indicator for learning activity and
two indicators (one cognitive and one motivational) for learning prerequisites. Future research could extend this selection, for example, by
incorporating more fine-grained or contextualized indicators of learning
activity and/or engagement within the environment to further validate
the interpretation of the residual as acquired knowledge and skills. This
potentially also allows to differentiate between different learning strategies and their requirements (e.g. worked examples vs. problem solving
given specific expertise; Kalyuga et al., 2001) that lead to systematic
differences in final performance beyond those that can be explained by
prior knowledge and skills. Other research has shown that the interpretation of process indicators might vary based on both the task context
and characteristics of the test-taker (e.g. Hahnel et al., 2023). Thus,
future research could investigate whether further contextualization of
this indicator allows to differentiate the interpretation between effective
and ineffective use of resources. The selection of learning prerequisites
could also be extended to incorporate measures of metacognition (e.g.
Veenman et al., 2006), executive functions (e.g. Garner, 2009) or taskspecific motivation.
Learning processes operate on different timescales and the parameters that influence learning success differ between those timescales
(Tetzlaff et al., 2021). The current study only looked at learning on the
micro-scale - that is within one learning session. Especially in the context
of self-regulated learning in the digital world, the meso-scale (learning
across multiple units/sessions) and even the macro-scale (selecting and
pursuing overarching learning goals) are arguably as least as important
to look at.
As another direction of future research, cognitive diagnosis models
(de la Torre, 2009; Templin & Henson, 2006) and related approaches
can estimate student skills, proficiencies and misconceptions as well as
their development over the course of the learning session directly (e.g.
Levy, 2019). This allows a more fine-grained conceptualization of
learning, moving away from domain-level effects to specific isolated
components. These components could then again be related to learning
prerequisites, learning activity and more general domain-level learning
outcomes.
