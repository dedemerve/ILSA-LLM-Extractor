Despite the attention to the impact of school resources on student achievement, little is known about how the
school effect differs by students’ socioeconomic backgrounds. Using PISA 2015, we find that privileged students
receive much more academic benefits from high quality teachers than their disadvantaged peers do. Additionally,
the level of economic development across countries explains the heterogenous effect of school resources. Our
results suggest that the socioeconomic achievement gap could be worsened as a function of the difference in
school resources across schools, particularly in non-OECD countries. We call for further studies on the heterogeneous effect of school resources with the viewpoint of educational equity.

1. Introduction
Many countries spend substantial money on educating students. The
total public expenditure on education accounts for 11.3% of the total
government expenditure in OECD countries (Organization for Economic
Co-operation and Development, 2017a). The proportion varies across
countries from less than 8% in Italy to 16% in New Zealand. The Republic of Korea, a nation known for its high academic achievement and
quality teachers, spends 14.5% of the total government expenditure on
public education, which approximately amounts to $63 billion.2 Policymakers assume that substantial investment in public education helps
students learn advanced knowledge and skills, pursue more years of
education, receive higher wages, and thereby contribute to social
development in the countries (Chetty et al., 2011; Jackson et al., 2016).
However, there has been a long-lasting dispute over whether investment in school resources raises student performance (Coleman et al.,
1966; Greenwald et al., 1996; Hanushek, 1986; 1994). Some empirical
studies have shown that additional investment in school resources increase student performance (Angrist and Lavy, 1999; Card and Payne,
2002), while counter findings have maintained that more school resources do not guarantee to improve student achievement (Gundlach

et al., 2001; Fuchs and Wößmann, 2004). The importance of school resources is not an issue limited to a particular country, resulting in a
growing body of research in the field of comparative and international
education. Prior work suggests that human resources in a school (e.g.,
teacher quality) are strongly related to student performance, while the
impact of material resources (e.g., school expenditure) are disputable
(Denny and Oppedisano, 2013; Hanushek et al., 2019; Hanushek and
Wößmann, 2014). This inconsistent evidence has led researchers to
further explore the relationship between school resources and student
performance with a wide range of data, methods, periods, and contexts.
Although scholars have extensively examined the average impact of
school resources on student performance, they have relatively overlooked the question of whom receives more academic benefits from
additional school resources, which is a critical inquiry for educational
equity and social justice. Persistent performance gaps among more or
less advantaged students have called for education policies aimed at the
achievement of educational equity across the countries. For instance,
Every Student Succeeds Act in the United States extends the focus on
equity by providing more school resources for underserved students
(Young et al., 2017). Korean government also tries to enact a law that
supports marginalized students with additional funding and guarantees

Previous studies have yielded mixed results that school resources are
more effective for underrepresented students, while students with
higher socioeconomic status receive more benefits from additional
school resources in both international (Chiu and Khoo, 2005; Fuchs and
Wößmann 2004; Hanushek et al., 2019) and national contexts (Greenwald et al., 1996; Guryan, 2001; Loeb et al., 2010). For example, Chi and
Khoo (2005) found using international data of student assessment
(OECD-PISA) that schools with more privileged students had more
quality teachers, leading to unequal learning opportunities and outcomes. In a national context, Guryan (2001) showed that an increase in
local education spending was associated with improved test scores of
low-achieving students in the United States.
Therefore, it is necessary to investigate whether, how, and why the
effect of school resources on student performance differs by students’
socioeconomic backgrounds. Earlier studies suggest that the effects of
school resources on students are identical regardless of the influence of
students’ backgrounds (Hanushek, 1986). However, subsequent
scholars argue that additional school resources benefit poorer students
more than richer students because school resources supplement differences in family resources across students, thereby decreasing the marginal returns to school resources as the socioeconomic status of students
increases (Harris, 2007; Heyneman and Loxley, 1983). Thus, many education leaders around the world provide disadvantaged students with
additional school resources for equal learning opportunities (Deke et al.,
2012; Garces et al., 2002). On the other hand, richer students would
experience more academic benefits from additional school resources
than poorer students because affluent students typically attend schools
with more educational materials. Their parents also tend to use their
social and financial capital to obtain more school resources for their
children (Chiu and Khoo, 2005; Fuller and Clarke, 1994). This conflicting possibility and mixed evidence around the globe have drawn
growing attention from education stakeholders and call for further
research on the heterogenous impact of school resources.
In addition, little is known about how the heterogeneous effect of
school resources on student achievement differs across the countries.
Previous studies have found that the effect of school resources on students vary internationally due to different social, political, and economic conditions surrounding the K-12 public education system
(Bouhlila, 2015; Hanushek and Luque, 2003). Heyneman and Loxley
(1983) argued that the impact of school resources on students are
greater in developing counties as compared to developed countries.
Although their findings have been widely used to justify additional investment in school resources in developing countries, it is imperative to
revisit this ‘Heyneman-Loxley effect’ with the latest international data
and advanced methods. Since the Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA) includes rich information on school resources and
student achievement as well as a battery of individual- and school-level
characteristics, we are able to investigate the impact of school resources
as well as heterogeneities of the effects by students’ family backgrounds
and country characteristics.
Echoing the gaps in the previous literature, we seek to answer three
research questions. We first examine to what extent school resources
affect student achievement. We then turn to the heterogenous effect of
school resources on student achievement depending on students’ socioeconomic status. We assume that return to school resources differ by
students’ socioeconomic status, even though they access to the same
amounts or quality of school resources. While it may contribute to
educational equity if the impact of school resources is greater for underrepresented students, it may worsen socioeconomic achievement gap
if the impact of school resources is greater for privileged students. Lastly,
we explore whether the heterogeneous effect of school resources on
student achievement differ between OECD and non-OECD countries.
The rest of this study is organized as follows: Section II reviews the
theoretical backgrounds on the relationship between school resources
and student achievement. Section III shows data, variables, and

empirical strategies we employ. Section IV demonstrates our main
findings. In Section V, we discuss our results and suggest policy
implications.
2. Theoretical background
2.1. School resources and student achievement
While a substantial body of research has examined the impact of
school resources on student achievement, empirical evidence to date has
not successfully identified the relationship between those factors.
Echoing the findings from the Coleman report (Coleman et al., 1966),
Hanushek (1986) found that class size reduction and experienced
teachers did not affect student outcomes, suggesting the inefficiency of
school operation. Greenwald et al. (1996) employed a meta-analysis on
the link between school resources and student achievement, and refuted
Hanushek (1986)’s findings, arguing that additional school inputs have
a positive effect on student achievement. Scholars and policymakers
have paid a growing attention to these inconsistent results, leading to
succeeding studies.
In this study, we categorize school inputs as material (e.g., school
finance, facilities, technical services) and human (e.g., teachers, principals, support staff) resources. Prior studies have investigated the
impact of various material resources such as school expenditure and
class size on student outcomes (Angrist and Lavy, 1999; Card and Payne,
2002). However, their mixed results have raised concerns whether
additional investment in the traditional school inputs is effective at
raising student achievement. Meanwhile, scholars have maintained that
teacher quality is a key determinant of student performance, suggesting
the indispensability of human resources in schools. Previous studies
have shown that high quality teachers contribute to improving student
outcomes (Aaronson et al., 2007; Chetty et al., 2014) and that teacher
quality such as years of experience and credentials are unequally
distributed across schools (Loeb et al., 2010; Engel et al., 2014).
The question of whether school resources matter for student
achievement is a worldwide issue (Bouhlila,2015; Lee and Zuze, 2011;
Vegas and Coffin, 2015). Public education is one of the largest components of government spending across the countries (Organization for
Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017a), foreseeing that substantial investment in school resources would raise student achievement
and reduce inequality in student outcomes. Since public K-12 school
systems differ across the countries by various factors such as level of
authority over curriculum and student assessment, resource allocation
structure, and education policies, the impact of school resources on
student performance would also differ across the countries. Given that
these significant differences in school system rarely exist within a
country, these variations allow scholars to conduct a variety of international comparative studies. Additionally, international surveys (e.g.,
PISA and TIMSS) provide a rich set of student- and school-level characteristics as well as standardized test scores, making it possible to
explore the impact of material and human resources in schools on student achievement in a worldwide manner.
Researchers have used international surveys to explore the impact of
school resources on students, which has yielded mixed results. In terms
of material resources, Gundlach et al. (2001) found that the productivity
of schooling measured by the change in student achievement scores was
stagnant, although the price of schooling increased in most OECD
countries. Fuchs and Wößmann (2004) also revealed an insignificant
relationship between the availability of computers at school and student
performance. Regarding human resources, Hanushek (2010) claimed
that high quality teachers were the essential determinant of student
achievement, although a question remains as to what characteristics
determine the teacher quality. Hanushek et al. (2019) found that higher
teacher numeracy skills led to students’ greater achievement in reading
and mathematics. Despite the growing attention to school effectiveness,
the international and comparative education research has not reached a

high quality teachers. These findings confirm that academic return to
school resources is greater for low SES students and that the benefit
diminishes as students’ SES level increases (Grissmer et al., 2000; Molnar et al., 1999).
The third scenario is opposite to the second cases; students with
higher SES receive more academic benefits from additional school resources as compared to their peers with lower SES. Parents with high
SES tend to influence school-level resource allocation through their
money, power, and organization. Chiu and Khoo (2005) described these
parental behaviors as ‘privileged student bias,’ showing that school resources are more likely to be allocated to privileged students. A school
can also allocate its resources unequally among its students. For
example, ‘teacher sorting’ explains how human resources are unequally
distributed between schools and even within a school. Previous studies
found that affluent students are more likely to be matched to teachers
with higher educational credentials and more years of experience
(Borman and Kimball, 2005; Rutledge et al., 2008; Loeb et al., 2010).
Although teachers’ preference for working conditions is one of the
reasons of teacher sorting, parents are also actively involved in the
process of teacher hiring and placement, allocating high quality teachers
to their children (Figlio and Hart, 2014; Krueger, 1999). Privileged
student bias and teacher sorting demonstrate that parents with high SES
continually use their power and social capital to influence resource
decisions in schools, often at the expense of marginalized students
(Posey-Maddox, 2014).
Additionally, even though the same amount of school resources is
distributed to students, students with high SES would better use the
additional resources than students with low SES. Richer students learn
more cognitive skills and social and cultural norms effectively from their
parents (Swick and Broadway, 1997). Because parents with more human
and financial capital have larger social networks, their children can
benefit through frequent interactions with these networks (Horvat et al.,
2003). Therefore, students with high SES would establish quality relationships with superior students and teachers in their school and
classroom, understand how additional school resources can help their
success, and thus show better outcomes such as higher education expectations and test scores (Cochran and Dean, 1991; Wells and Crain,

In addition, the previous studies on the effect of school resources
have relatively neglected the possibility that the estimated effects could
differ by other student-, teacher-, school-level characteristics. For
example, students’ family backgrounds may lead to a strong reduction or
increment in the dependence of student performance on school resources. Wößmann and West (2006) also pointed to the possibility of
heterogeneous class-size effects on students. Some countries experienced higher student achievement when reducing class size, while other
countries showed a weak relationship between the two factors. With few
exceptional studies, prior work has mainly centered on the average (or
overall) school effects, thereby overlooking the heterogeneous effects of
school resources on student performance. Given that reducing the socioeconomic achievement gap is one of the primary goals in most
countries, it is essential to explore whether, how, and why the effect of
school resources differs by students’ socioeconomic backgrounds. This
inquiry also contributes to the education policy discussion for educational equity and social justice.
2.2. Heterogeneous effect of school resources by students’ socioeconomic
background
It is well known that students’ family backgrounds are one of major
factors predicting their academic achievement (Benito et al., 2013;
Wößmann, 2004). The body of research has employed various family
characteristics (e.g., parental education, occupation status, income) as
proxies of students’ socioeconomic status (hereafter SES). The impact of
family backgrounds is quite straightforward; students with higher SES
tend to show higher academic achievement level as compared to their
peers with lower SES, when holding other confounders constant.
Although there are many reasons why the SES-based achievement gap
appears, scholars have payed attention to the role of parents. Parents
with higher SES are more likely to provide financial and cultural support
to their children, resulting in the students’ academic success in the
school (Bouhlila, 2015; Buchmann and DiPrete, 2006; Konstantopoulos
and Borman, 2011; Sirin, 2005).
We build on the previous research on the direct role of family
backgrounds on student outcomes, introducing three possible scenarios
that students’ SES would have indirect impacts on the dependence of
student achievement on school resources (Figlio, 1999). The first scenario is that students’ SES does not influence on the relationship between school resources and student achievement. Earlier research based
on education production function assumes that the marginal effect of
school resources on student performance would be identical regardless
of the influence of other inputs. Hanushek (1986) supported this scenario that the effect of school resources on students was constant in both
high and low SES groups. However, subsequent researchers refute this
assumption by suggesting the interactive role of students’ SES on the
relationship between school resources and student performance (Figlio,
1999; Gyimah-Brempong and Gyapong, 1992; Harris, 2007). Numerous
scholars, as a result, showed that the return to school resources varies
greatly by student backgrounds, school quality, and country contexts
(Chudgar and Luschei, 2009; Heyneman and Loxley, 1983; Luschei and
Jeong, 2018).
The second scenario is that additional school resources are more
likely to be beneficial to students with lower SES, which contributes to
educational equity. Because extra school resources supplement differences in family resources across students, less advantaged students
would take much higher returns than richer students even though they
all receive the same amount of school investment (Baker et al., 2002;
Chiu and Khoo, 2005). Following such concept of diminishing marginal
returns, governments distribute more resources to students with lower
SES than students with higher SES (Young et al., 2017), expecting more
equal distribution of school resources and better student outcomes.

2.3. Heterogeneous effect of school resources in developed and developing
countries
Every country has own social, political, and economic conditions,
which affect the level of investment in school resources and the extent of
effectiveness of school resources. Heyneman and Loxley (1983) verified
that the effect of school resources on student performance was greater in
developing countries than those in developed countries. The authors
attributed the results to different power of educational attainment across
countries. Students in developing countries would get more social benefits through educational investment because lots of occupations related
to higher social class are quite competitive and certainly determined by
educational achievement. The authors also found that the influence of
students’ SES on achievement are weaker in developing countries than
in developed countries. This ‘Heyneman-Loxley effect’ has been broadly
used to encourage additional investment in school resources for
reducing educational inequity in developing countries. Chudgar and

Luschei (2009) also found using TIMSS 2003 data that school resources
are important in explaining variations in student achievement, particularly in poor and unequal countries. They also revealed that school
resources reduce socioeconomic achievement gap in some cases.
However, Hanushek and Luque (2003) argued that there was no
evidence that the underprivileged students had more benefits from
additional school resources in developing countries. Baker et al. (2002)
also pointed out that Heyneman and Loxley’s approach which used
simple variance decomposition was not enough to reveal the convincing
evidence that school resources matter much more in developing countries. The authors employed TIMSS data and linear regression models in
an alternative way and found that interaction terms between school
resources and measures of national income were statistically insignificant. Bouhlila (2015) reaffirmed that Heyneman–Loxley effect has
vanished in the 2000s in developing countries, attributing the results to
inefficient allocation of school resources and centralization of the
educational government. Alexander (2001) found that a lack of variance
in school resources across in a country explains the lack of school effects
on students. Recently, Luschei and Jeong (2018) maintained that school
inequality measured by teacher sorting increased with national income
level, suggesting that developed countries have a concern for unequal
distribution of human resources.
While previous studies have focused on to what extent the direct
effects of school resources and family backgrounds on student outcomes
vary across the countries, little is still known about how the indirect
effect of students’ SES on the dependence of student achievement on
school resources differ across the countries. From the viewpoint of
educational equity, it is necessary to examine whether those indirect
effects based on students’ family backgrounds are limited to specific
countries or broad phenomenon. As Gamoran and Long (2007) summarized using a wide range of international studies on school effects,
developing countries have experienced a diminishing marginal return to
additional school resources because economic growth has attenuated
the threshold at which schools are so underfunded that school resources
matter. However, it is still uncertain whether the heterogenous effects of
school resources depending on students’ SES vary by the level of economic development. Given that policymakers throughout the world
prioritize reducing socioeconomic achievement gaps, thereby providing
disadvantaged students with additional school resources, this study
contributes to a deeper understanding of how school resources and
students’ family backgrounds interact on each other across the
countries.
Previous studies have examined how and why the degree of economic development across countries is associated with the effects of
school resources on students, particularly by using the classification of
OECD membership. Scholars agreed that more school resources
contribute to students’ academic growth in less-developed countries,
while the school effects fade in industrialized countries (Baker et al.,
2002; Heyneman and Loxley, 1983; Montt, 2011). This difference may
be partially explained by the facts that poorer countries fail to fully fund
schools and that there is huge disparity in the distribution of school
resources across schools in these countries. Falck et al. (2018) found that
positive effects of using computers to look up information in school were
mostly limited to OECD countries and stronger for students with high
SES. The little effects of computer use in developing countries were
attributed to lower quality of teaching and school resources. However,
most countries have established systematic school funding systems in
recent years, and rather unequal distribution of school resources is more
pronounced in richer countries (Luschei and Jeong, 2018). OECD requires two fundamental requirements, democratic societies and open,
transparent and free-market economies, to prospective countries (Trade
Union Advisory Committee, 2018). Given that a series of economic,
social, political, and cultural conditions are representative components
of the national power, this study takes advantage of OECD classification.
Many researchers have used OECD membership to explore heterogenous
impacts of school resources depending on country backgrounds because

economic development is highly associated with school policies and
education systems (Cavalletti and Corsi, 2018; Czernich et al., 2011;
Falck et al., 2018; Kobus et al., 2019).
3. Methods
3.1. Data
We use the data on fifteen-year-old students in 39 countries and
economies that participated in the PISA 2015. As one of the most
influential international surveys administered by OECD, PISA has
assessed and standardized levels of students’ cognitive skills in reading,
mathematics, and science across countries every three years since 2000.
PISA has also collected a rich set of information on students’ sociodemographic characteristics as well as their schools’ organizational
characteristics. Therefore, the PISA 2015 is well suited to address the
three research questions: the overall impact of school resources on student achievement; the heterogenous effect of school resources on student achievement by their SES; and the difference in the heterogeneous
effect between OECD and non-OECD countries. In addition, the broad
range of countries in the data allow us to draw more general conclusion
based on our findings. In this study, we identify missing values and
impute them with country- or school-mean values for continuous variables and with country- or school-median values for categorical variables. Therefore, we exclude observations if its values are missed across
all students within a school or all schools within a country. Our final
dataset includes 300,218 students in 23 OECD and 16 non-OECD
countries and economics. We provide more information of these countries including GPD per capita, the share of government expenditure on
education, OECD classification, and World Bank classification in
Table A1 of the Appendix.
We use student test scores in mathematics, reading, science as the
core dependent variables. Although some scholars and educators have
criticized that contributions of school resources to students cannot be
adequately explained by the change in test scores only, we argue that
academic improvement accounts for a large portion of students’ holistic
growth and future success. Student performance in mathematics has
been widely used to represent students’ cognitive skills as a function of
school and family factors because mathematics performance is regarded
as one of the basic skills for productivity on the labor market (Borghans
and Ter Weel, 2004) and mathematics test scores are relatively comparable across countries given the hierarchical structure of mathematics
knowledge (Hanushek et al., 2013). However, recent research documented that students’ academic benefit from investment in school resources vary by subjects and that such differences are important for
policy implications (Agasisti et al., 2020). Previous studies have also
paid attention to reading literacy and science as well as mathematics
(Fariña et al., 2015; Spiezia, 2011; Chiu and Khoo, 2005). Test scores in
the PISA 2015 were estimated as a form of ten plausible values because
students completed a different subset of test items. These plausible
values represent the distribution of potential scores for all students
based on item response theory. The test scores are mapped on a scale
from 0 to 1000 with a mean of 500 points and a standard variation of
100 points across the countries.
The main variables of interest are school resources, which are classified into material and human resources. The material resources are
measured as the number of computers per student in a school, proxying
for how many material resources are feasible in schools. Among many
school resource variables (e.g., school expenditure, class size), the use
and access to computer have been paid increasing attention in the past
decades (Bulman and Fairlie, 2016; Falck et al., 2018; Mora et al., 2018).
Computer use has become a regular part of classroom instruction in most
countries (Bulman and Fairlie, 2016), so the average numbers of computer per student in school have sharply increased across the countries
in recent years (Spiezia, 2011). Scholars have utilized the availability of
computer at school to examine the relation between school resources

and student performance (Baker et al., 2002; Coley et al., 1997; Fuchs
and Wößmann, 2004; Wells and Lewis, 2006). Given that many countries have promoted the use of computer for teaching and learning
through policy implementation and substantial amounts of budget decisions, our measure is representative of educational expenditures for
material resources in school systems (Biagi and Loi, 2013; Comi et al.,
2017).
We measure the human resources as the ratio of teachers who have
master’s degrees or higher in a school, proxying for teacher quality in
schools. Among many teacher quality measures, such as cognitive skills,
knowledge assessments, certification, and experience (Akiba, 2017;
Hanushek et al., 2019), the advanced degree indicates teacher qualification, which is related to student performance (Lee, 2018). Although
previous studies have provided mixed evidence of the effect of human
resources on students (Hanushek et al., 2019; Lee and Lee, 2020; Montt,
2011; Rivkin et al., 2005), the number of teachers with a master’s degree
have doubled over the last 50 years (Lee, 2018). Many governments
have also tried to maintain teacher quality by providing incentive for
teachers who obtain advanced degrees. For example, schools in the
United States have spent more than $14 billion on compensating
teachers who hold a master’s degree or above (Miller and Roza, 2012).
Therefore, our measure is representative of teacher quality for human
resources in schools.
The other key variable is students’ level of SES. PISA provides the
index of economic, social, and cultural status, based on three family
background measures: parental education, parental occupation, and
home possessions (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2017b). This index is scaled to have a mean of zero and a
standard deviation of one among students. Our measures of the interest
variables are paralleled in the previous international comparative education literature that used the PISA data (Baker et al., 2002; Bouhlila,
2015; Fuchs and Wößmann, 2004; Hanushek et al., 2019; Park and
Byun, 2015; Wößmann and West, 2006).
We employ student- and school-level control variables that capture
demographic and organizational characteristics (see Table A2 in the
Appendix for variable names and detailed coding schemes of all variables) based on the prior literature (Bouhlila, 2015; Fuchs and
Wößmann, 2004; Hanushek et al., 2013; Hanushek and Wößmann, 2014;
Wößmann, 2004). We consider the following student- and family-level
characteristics: an indicator for whether a student is female; age; an
indicator for whether a student repeated a grade; the age when a student
started primary education; an indicator for whether a student received
preprimary education; indicators for whether a student is first or second
generation other than native; an indicator for whether a student use
other languages at home; and learning time.
We control for the following school-level variables: an indicator for
whether a school is private; indicators for the school location; average
test scores at the school; student enrollment; the degree of professional
development; the degree of after-school activities; an index for educational leadership; the portion of benefactor among total school funding.
We also created a number of index variables, followed by previous
studies (Luschei and Jeong, 2018), which captures the degrees of
school-level autonomy, democracy, tracking, student selection,
accountability, privatization, and welfare. We impute missing values of
continuous variables identified at school- or student- level with countryor school-mean. For missing values of categorical variables at student- or
school-level, we impute these values with median estimated from whole
sample data within school or country, respectively.
Although multiple imputation can be a better strategy to account for
data missing, we could not apply multiple imputation in this study
because our approach that employs plausible values of test scores and
replicated weights is based on multiple imputation. Therefore, it is a
challenge to take into account both multiple imputation approaches at
the same time. We instead use mean or median substitution that has
been widely used in previous studies using international data (Park and
Byun, 2015). In addition, we include an indicator for missing variables

In this model, γ hetero represents how the estimated magnitude varies
depending on the changes in students’ SES, while all other terms are
same as defined in Eq. (1). Thus, γ hetero tests whether additional school
resources are beneficial or detrimental for the underprivileged students.
If γ hetero is positive and statistically significant, the additional school resources relatively work for students with high SES, which exacerbates
socioeconomic achievement gap. On the other hand, the negative coefficient represents that students with low SES receive additional benefits from the investment in school resources, which contributes to
educational equity and social justice.

δOECD is the primary interest in this model, which measures how the

To obtain unbiased parameter estimates and standard errors when
utilizing PISA data, it is highly recommended to use final student weight
and replicate weights (Organization for Economic Co-operation and
Development, 2017b) and to employ plausible values for student proficiency estimates (see the PISA Data Analysis Manual for details). PISA
2015 provides a final student weight variable, five plausible values of
student performance in each subject, and 80 replicated weights. We
compute estimates of parameter and standard error using replicate
weights. This approach accounts for complex survey designs in the
estimation of sampling variances, allowing for analyses with plausible
values which are created through multiple imputation approach. As a
result, the average estimator across plausible values is reported and the
imputation error is added to the variance estimator.

and methods (e.g., Hanushek et al., 2019), these points deserve more
in-depth analysis in the future studies.
In addition, we demonstrate that privileged students are more likely
to take advantages from additional human resources than their underprivileged peers, reaffirming the importance of families across diverse
national contexts (Baker et al., 2002; Hanushek and Luque, 2003). Our
finding stands in contrast to the evidence that the effect of school resources is greater in the disadvantaged students (Guryan, 2001;
Hanushek et al., 2019). Our finding further indicates that higher SES
leads to a significant increment in the dependence of student achievement on school resources, which worsen the SES-based academic
achievement gap. A few studies pointed out possible mechanisms with
reference to the inequitable heterogeneous effect of school human
resource on student achievement. To illustrate, high-income parents are
more likely to participate in school operation to allocate more school
resources for their children (Chiu and Khoo, 2005; Posey-Maddox,
2014), while underprivileged students tend to be taught by
less-qualified teachers due to teacher sorting between and within
schools (Figlio and Hart, 2014; Loeb et al., 2010). In addition, students
with high SES tend to have frequent and quality interactions with superior students and teachers in their classroom (Cochran and Dean,
1991; Wells and Crain, 1994), which allows them to receive higher
educational expectations from teachers and to result in better academic
performance. While it is essential to investigate the manifestations and
mechanisms of the interactive role of students’ SES on the relationship
between school resources and student performance more thoroughly,
policy recommendations should place greater weight on equitable
resource distribution and student performance as well as investment in
school resources. Also, identifying the roles of schools—for both lowand high-SES students—could serve as an important guide for policy
makers as they allocate scarce educational resources.
Lastly, it is evident that the heterogeneous effect of school human
resource is associated with country contexts. Although the heterogeneous effects of the material resources are not statistically significant in
both OECD and non-OECD countries, we find that privileged students
receive more academic benefits (reading test scores) from additional
human resources than underprivileged peers in both OECD and nonOECD countries. After Heyneman and Loxley (1983) found that school
resources explain a greater variation in academic performance in
developing countries than in developed countries, subsequent studies
verified that the Heyneman-Loxley effect has declined over time (Baker
et al., 2002; Bouhlila, 2015). These findings support the latter studies
that many developing countries have also established systematic school
funding systems in the last decades, which attenuates the impact of
material school resources on students. We further suggest that developing countries also need to consider how additional school resources
could reduce SES-based achievement gap across students for educational
equity. The heterogenous effect of human resources that benefits more
to students with high SES suggests that both OECD and non-OECD
countries may experience uneven allocation of school human resources depending on students’ SES. Previous studies showed huge
disparity in the distribution of human resources across schools in
developing countries (Chiu and Khoo, 2005), while higher income
countries also experience increasing within-school teacher sorting
(Kalogrides et al., 2013; Luschei and Jeong, 2018). In addition, students
may have different relationships and interactions with quality teachers
in their school and classroom according to their socioeconomic status
and parental influence. These mechanisms imply a major hindrance to
building education system designed to pursue both educational equity

5. Conclusion
Although education researchers have paid growing attention to the
importance of school resources on student performance, previous
studies provide limited and conflicting results (Chudgar and Luschei,
2009; Coleman et al., 1966; Greenwald et al., 1996; Hanushek et al.,
2019; Jackson et al., 2016). In addition, relatively little is known about
whether the school effectiveness differs according to students’ SES and
country contexts. Echoing the gaps in prior work, we draw on student
and school information of 39 countries and economies in PISA 2015 to
explore whether school resources affect student achievement, how the
effect of school resources differ by students’ SES, and whether the heterogeneous effect of school resources on student achievement differ
between OECD and non-OECD countries.
We find that school resources are not related to students’ academic
performance, as previous studies demonstrated using international databases (Gundlach et al., 2001; Fuchs and Wößmann, 2004). In particular, the improvement of students’ test scores in math, reading, and
science is not associated with the additional material resources (the
number of computers per student in a school) and human resources (the
proportion of teachers who hold a master’s degree). This finding is not
consistent with Chudgar and Luschei (2009) that school resources are an
important source of variation in student performance. We attribute this
discrepancy to differences in data and methodological approach. While
they employed the OLS and the two-level Hierarchical Linear Model to
TIMSS 2003, we employ a country fixed effects model to PISA 2015 in
order to take out the influence from unobservable time-invariant
country characteristics. Although we do not investigate specific mechanisms of why the school resources do not affect student achievement,
previous studies pointed out inefficiencies of resource allocation and the
centralization of the education sector (Bouhlila, 2015; Hanushek, 1986).
Bouhlila (2015) showed using TIMSS 2007 data that school resources
are spent inefficiently, thus hindering school operation and student
learning. The centralization of the education sector may also restrain
schools from exerting discretionary effort for student learning such as

math education major for math teachers), rather than teachers’ general

a more longitudinal and comprehensive set of student and school information to address the particular mechanisms of why school resources
have different impacts on student achievement depending on students’
family backgrounds and national contexts.
Second, we are unable to make causal claims due to the limitations of
cross-sectional data, although the inclusion of country fixed effects
removes the influence of time-invariant country characteristics. Meanwhile, the covariates that we include at both the student and school
levels are limited to the information collected in PISA 2015. Thus, there
remains a possibility that our estimates are contaminated by other timevariant or unobservable factors. Future research may benefit from
finding exogenous policy variations within/across countries and
applying quasi-experimental designs (Agasisti et al., 2020; Wößmann
and West, 2006) for causal interpretations of how students’ academic
performance is intertwined with school resources, family backgrounds,
and national contexts.
Third, our study does not include a broad spectrum of countries
around the globe, though PISA 2015 provides a set of information on
students and schools in various OECD and non-OECD countries. For
instance, only one lower middle income country (Tunisia) is in the nonOECD group in our sample, while other non-OECD countries are categorized as upper middle- or high-income country in World Bank classification. Such limitation may come from our mean/median
substitution for missing data problems. With advanced statistical approaches that keep observations from being omitted due to missing
variables, future research needs to utilize a wide range of countries that
cover diverse economic, social, political, and cultural conditions.

Furthermore, our findings indicate that economic development
levels explain the interactive effect of students’ SES on the relationship
between school resources and student performance. When excluding
OECD countries not categorized as high-income countries and nonOECD countries categorized as high-income countries in our sample,
we see a clear contrasting pattern that additional school material resources may widen the SES-based achievement gap in non-OECD
countries, while investment in school resources helps to reduce the
achievement gap between low- and high-SES students in OECD countries. Although several non-OECD countries have tried to fund their
schools and established systematic school funding systems in recent
years, our findings suggest their efforts may not fully change their school
systems including unequal distribution of school resources and lower
quality of teaching and school resources (Bouhlila, 2015; Falck et al.,
2018). Numerous international comparative studies agree that economic development is highly associated with school policies and education systems (Baker et al., 2002; Heyneman and Loxley, 1983; Kobus
et al., 2019; Luschei and Jeong, 2018). Our study further contributes to
the existing literature by discovering how such difference in the level of
economic development across countries affect the SES-based achievement gaps. Although we could not fully investigate how school resources
are equally allocated across schools and how the distributed resources
are effectively used in a school in developing countries, educational
leaders in these countries may benefit from revisiting their school systems and budget decisions, such as how school resources are allocated to
students between and within schools and how teachers and students use
the resources.
Taken together, we suggest educational policies and practice that
school resources are fully invested and equally distributed for equitable
educational opportunity and outcomes. In addition, the watchful
monitoring system on school resource allocation should be established,
particularly in developing countries, beyond just supplying school resources at the minimum level.

international data (Chudgar and Luschei, 2009). Although we utilize the
number of computers per student and the proportion of teachers who
hold a master’s degree in a school as proxies of school resources based
on the previous literature (Fuchs and Wößmann, 2004), these measures
