The current article investigated the role that the school context plays in teachers’ well-being in terms of
emotional exhaustion and job satisfaction. First, we analyzed between-school variation in well-being and indicators of social interactions. Second, we examined the association between these indicators and well-being on
the teacher and school levels. Based on two large teacher samples (Study 1: N = 1022; Study 2: N = 2886), the
results of multilevel regression analyses showed low between-school variance in well-being but substantial
variation in indicators of social interactions. Moreover, well-being was tendentially related to interindividual
differences in the perception of social climate.

Teachers’ well-being, in terms of job satisfaction and low burnout
symptoms, is a central prerequisite for successfully meeting the demands
of the teaching profession. Job satisfaction and low burnout symptoms
have been shown to be related to lower absenteeism and reduced intentions to quit, better teaching quality, and higher student motivation
and performance (Arens & Morin, 2016; Braun et al., 2019; Klusmann
et al., 2021a; Salvagioni et al., 2017; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011, 2020).
At the same time, teachers have reported lower well-being, in terms of
higher burnout, compared to other occupational groups (García-Carmona et al., 2019; Herman et al., 2020; Iriarte Redín & Erro-Garcés,
2020; Johnson et al., 2005). Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, teachers
faced significant challenges, which further affected their well-being in
terms of reduced job satisfaction and higher burnout levels (Alves et al.,
2020; Klusmann et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2022; Voss, Klusmann et al.,
2023).
Before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, the quality of social interactions at schools was empirically identified as a significant predictor
of well-being. Teachers who felt supported and who cooperated with
their colleagues and school principals reported higher well-being

(Hakanen et al., 2006; Halbesleben, 2006; Hansen et al., 2022; Kim
et al., 2022; Klusmann et al., 2023; Košir et al., 2022). Subjective reports
of specific schools that have either a favorable or unfavorable social
climate and proposed connections between this and teachers’
well-being, as well as theoretical approaches to explaining burnout,
emphasize the importance of the respective school context (Leiter &
Maslach, 2016; Maslach et al., 2001). Thereby, previous studies have
rarely described and explained between-school differences beyond individual factors in teachers’ well-being and the indicators of social interactions among colleagues and with principals (van Droogenbroeck
et al., 2021). However, it is important to know whether differences in
teachers’ occupational well-being are located merely on the individual
teacher level or whether, for example, there is strong variation within
every school or on the school level, that is, between schools. Knowing
the primary source of variation in teachers’ well-being could have
important implications for intervention approaches because it would
indicate whether it is more appropriate to target individual or organizational factors.

Recent evidence suggests that teachers’ occupational well-being was
severely affected during the COVID-19 pandemic (Ozamiz-Etxebarria
et al., 2021). In their meta-analysis, Ma et al. (2022) summarized 54
studies from 22 countries and found relatively high levels of teachers’
stress, depression, and anxiety. Samples from Italy and Germany, (Pellerone, 2021; Voss, Klusmann et al., 2023) found, on average, an increase in burnout symptoms compared to teachers’ prepandemic levels.
The results of another study with German teachers, which was conducted in October 2020, suggest individual differences in whether
teachers experienced emotional exhaustion. While some teachers reported high levels of exhaustion (21.0%), others reported low levels
(55.3%). However, more than 56.4% reported feelings of being more
exhausted due to the COVID-19 pandemic (Klusmann et al., 2023).
These results are in line with a representative survey that was conducted
in April 2022. It showed that about half of the surveyed teachers suffered
from physical and mental exhaustion. At the same time, 74% of the
teachers reported that they were rather or very satisfied with their job
(Robert Bosch Stiftung, 2022), indicating differential mechanisms for
positive and negative dimensions of occupational well-being. In summary, previous empirical findings indicate that teachers’ occupational
well-being was negatively affected during the pandemic.

1. Teachers’ occupational well-being: Concept and current
prevalence
Current understanding of occupational well-being is based on the
early research of Diener et al. (1999) on subjective well-being, which
includes both positive and negative cognitive and emotional evaluations
of the individual experience in the work context. Although there are
many different approaches to defining well-being (Hascher & Waber,
2021; van Horn et al., 2004), there is overall agreement that high
occupational well-being is characterized by the experience of predominantly positive emotions and rare negative emotions (Bakker & Oerlemans, 2011; Diener et al., 1999; Watson et al., 1988). Following these
assumptions, we considered both negative and positive aspects of
occupational well-being in our studies on burnout and job satisfaction.
Burnout is a phenomenon associated with the work context that is
seen as a consequence of prolonged work stressors (Maslach et al.,
2001). Emotional exhaustion is the core symptom of burnout; it describes the affective state of feeling emotionally exhausted and depleted
of one’s emotional resources (Leiter & Maslach, 2016; Maslach et al.,
2001). In our article, we refer to Weiss’s definition of job satisfaction as a
“positive (or negative) evaluative judgment one makes about one’s job
or job situation” (Weiss, 2002, p. 175). Accordingly, job satisfaction
comprises a cognitive evaluation of whether individual expectations of
the occupation match the perceived actual state (Spector, 2022).
The occupational well-being of teachers is a central predictor of
multiple outcomes. Impaired well-being in terms of higher burnout
symptoms and reduced job satisfaction is associated with lower professional performance (Arens & Morin, 2016; Braun et al., 2019; Keller
et al., 2014; Klusmann et al., 2008a); higher intention to leave the
profession (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011, 2020) and lower physical health
(Salvagioni et al., 2017). It shows also relations to student outcomes.
Previous studies have shown that occupational well-being is associated
with motivation among students, student achievement, and the quality
of teacher-student relationships (Addimando, 2019; Arens & Morin,
2016; Klusmann et al., 2016; Klusmann et al., 2021a). Overall, low
occupational well-being can have a negative impact on the health of the
teachers themselves, their students, and schools as organizations.
Teachers often report impaired occupational well-being (García-Carmona et al., 2019). Johnson et al. (2005) studied 11,000 British
employees from 26 occupations, and teachers reported the lowest levels
of physical and psychological well-being and had below-average values
in job satisfaction compared to other occupational groups. Iriarte Redín
and Erro-Garcés (2020) found high levels of stress and anxiety among
teachers in their review, but also high levels of job satisfaction. In

2. Explaining teachers’ well-being: individual, transactional,
and organizational factors
According to Chang (2009), there are three categories of factors that
contribute to occupational well-being: individual, transactional, and
organizational. These factors can be assigned to one level in the hierarchy as teachers are nested in schools (individual and transactional =
teacher level; organizational = school level). Individual factors include,
for example, age, gender, work experience, and personality. Basically,
all factors related to the question of “who” is more or less affected by
impaired occupational well-being are addressed by this aspect. Transactional factors comprise the individual perception of the working
environment (e.g., student misbehavior, social support from colleagues
and principals). Individual and transactional factors are closely related.
Thus, a transactional factor is determined both by individual characteristics of a person and by aspects of the environment itself and, according to the transactional stress model, is relevant to occupational
well-being (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Thus, a perceived external demand does not affect all teachers in the same way.
Finally, organizational factors subsume the variables that describe
the environment in which teachers experience occupational well-being
(e.g., leadership style, size of the school, and students’ socioeconomic
background; Chang, 2009). The job demands-resources model (JD-R;
Demerouti et al., 2001; Schaufeli & Taris, 2014) implies that the work
context, with its specific organizational factors, influences the individual
well-being of teachers. This model can also be used to explain differences between schools as it can be assumed that work conditions differ
between schools (Leiter & Maslach, 2016; Maslach et al., 2001; Schaufeli
& Taris, 2014). In the JD-R model, two basic classifications of job
characteristics are important: demands (e.g., work overload and role
conflict) and resources (e.g., feedback and social support). High demands and a lack of resources can lead to burnout and reduced
well-being over time (Demerouti et al., 2001; Schaufeli & Taris, 2014).
Thus, it can be expected that the risk of burnout is lower for teachers in a
more supportive and resilient school environment.
Both models (the JD-R model and the transactional stress model)
describe different perspectives on the development of occupational wellbeing. On the one hand, the focus is on organizational factors of the
work environment and, on the other hand, the focus is on the individual
perceptions of work aspects.

2.1. Empirical evidence: Disentangling the source of variation
Hence, valid empirical evidence for the relevance of the school
context for teachers’ well-being is scarce. In their systematic review, van
Droogenbroeck et al. (2021) found only 14 studies that adequately
examined burnout hierarchically, which is a negligible number considering that teachers’ burnout is a frequently researched construct. In their
review, they found results prior to the COVID-19 pandemic that showed
only a relatively small between-school variance, ranging between 1%
and 6.2%, for teachers’ emotional exhaustion, which indicated that
schools hardly differed in terms of burnout (van Droogenbroeck et al.,
2021). Such an overview in the form of a systematic review is lacking for
job satisfaction; so far, the findings for job satisfaction are mixed. In the
OECD Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS), there was a
between-school variance of 4% for satisfaction with the profession and
13% for satisfaction with the current work environment across all
countries (OECD, 2020b). Lopes and Oliveira (2020) showed a
between-school variance of 7%. Other empirical findings suggest a
slightly higher variance between schools, for example, Kapa and Gimbert (2018) reported 11.6% between-school variance and Pietsch et al.
(2019) 29%.

3. The quality of social interactions among teachers and with
principals
Previous research has shown the relevance of social interactions in
terms of social support and collegial cooperation as an important
resource (Halbesleben, 2006; Hansen et al., 2022; Olsen & Huang, 2019;
van Droogenbroeck et al., 2021). Different diary studies showed that
social interactions are of central importance in the daily lives of teachers
(Aldrup et al, 2017 and Klusmann et al., 2021b). In a survey of more
than 1000 teachers in Germany, 90% of the teachers indicated that they
considered collegial cooperation to be important (Richter & Pant, 2016).
Social support can refer to different content areas, such as emotional,
instrumental, and informational support (Klauer, 2009). The quality of
cooperation can also be distinguished (Hartmann et al., 2021; Little,
1990). Exchange describes, for example, the sharing of information and
the transfer of teaching materials. This type of cooperation takes place at
a rather low level and involves little effort on the part of those involved.
Whereas, more intensive forms of cooperation, professional collaboration, include, for example, team teaching or preparing lessons together
(Hartmann et al., 2021). Even the frequency of cooperation has been
shown to vary considerably. For example, an exchange of teaching
materials was found to take place much more frequently than more
intensive cooperation in the form of shared lessons. Previous research
has shown that less exhausted teachers cooperated more often, especially through less intensive collaboration such as exchange (Richter &
Pant, 2016). It has been widely demonstrated that perceived and actual
social interactions are important resources for occupational well-being
(Hakanen et al., 2006; Halbesleben, 2006; Toropova et al., 2021); this
was also shown for these interactions during the COVID-19 pandemic
(Kim & Asbury, 2020; Kim et al., 2022; Košir et al., 2022; Pressley et al.,
2022; Sokal et al., 2020).
Most studies examined social interactions as transactional factors
and only a few additionally considered indicators of social interactions
as an organizational factor. Therefore, it is important to consider the
questions of whether and, if so, to what degree the perceptions of the
social interactions vary from school to school. Ford et al. (2019) reported relatively high agreement within schools, with 25%
between-school variance for autonomy support and 27% for faculty trust
in principals. Other studies (Klusmann et al., 2008b; van Maele & van

2.2. Empirical evidence: Predicting well-being
Previous empirical studies have already examined various factors in
an attempt to explain differences in the development of occupational
well-being (Cansoy, 2019; Hakanen et al., 2006; Roloff et al., 2022;
Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2020), in particular during the COVID-19
pandemic (Klusmann et al., 2023; Westphal et al., 2022). First, teachers’ personality traits are crucial in explaining differences in the experience of well-being (Roloff et al., 2022), even during the COVID-19
pandemic (Voss, Klusmann et al., 2023). Hence, neuroticism is associated with more burnout symptoms, whereas extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness are related to lower burnout (Roloff et al.,
2022). Second, concerns about one’s health were identified as
demanding for teachers and were particularly related to the COVID-19
pandemic (Carreon et al., 2021; Klusmann et al., 2023; Oducado et al.,
2021; Pressley et al., 2022). Even during the long period of distance
learning in the winter of 2021, there were always considerations of
reopening schools, which would have potentially increased the risk of
infection not only for students but also for teachers (Magnusson et al.,
2021). Third, even before the pandemic, teachers reported a high
workload (Hakanen et al., 2006; Kyriacou, 2011), which increased again
due to the challenges of distance learning. Also, the technical equipment
varied greatly from school to school. Likewise, not all students had access to the technology necessary for successful distance learning at
home. The opportunities for teachers to acquire or expand digital skills
in the school context are very limited in Germany compared to other
countries (OECD, 2020c). About half of the teachers reported that they
did not use digital media regularly in class (Lorenz et al., 2017). Recent
empirical evidence suggests that these additional requirements of

teachers’ occupational well-being due to individual, transactional, and
organizational factors. While there is plenty of evidence that individual
and transactional factors play a role in job satisfaction and burnout, only
few studies have additionally investigated the role of indicators of social
interactions and differences between schools, respectively. This is an
important research gap as, particularly in times of global external
stressors such as the COVID-19 pandemic, contextual factors such as
supportive and collegial work environments might be particularly
relevant for teachers.

5.1. Method

Emotional exhaustion. Emotional exhaustion, the core symptom of
burnout, was surveyed on the basis of an established German adaptation
(Baumert et al., 2008) of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI; Maslach
et al., 1996). The scale included four items (e.g., “I often feel exhausted.”); each was answered on a 4-point response scale (1 = strongly
disagree to 4 = strongly agree). The internal consistency was α = 0.77.
Individual factors. Digital competency was assessed using nine
items in which teachers indicated whether they could perform certain
activities (e.g., “Find useful teaching materials on the internet.”).
Teachers could choose from the following response options: 0 = I don’t
believe I can do it and 1 = I was able to do that before the COVID-19-related
school closings and This is what I learned during the COVID-19-related
school closings. The scale was created by calculating the mean value
for each teacher across all relevant items. The scale had an internal
consistency of α = 0.64. Health concerns were measured using one item
that explicitly asked about the COVID-19 pandemic (“Since the COVID19 pandemic started, I have become more concerned about my health.”)
with a 4-point response scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree).
The additional workload caused by distance learning was asked about in
comparison to the workload before the pandemic. Participants rated a
single item (“The amount of work I do in distance learning compared to
face-to-face teaching is:”) on a 3-point response scale ranging from − 1 =
lower to 1 = higher.
Transactional factors. The perceived support by principals was
captured with a single item (“All in all, how well did you feel supported
by your school principal during the distance learning phase?”) using a 6point response scale (1 = not well at all to 6 = very well). Cooperation
with colleagues was asked about with a single item (“In distance
learning, I found the collaboration with teachers of the same class/year
to be good.”) using a 4-point response scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 =
strongly agree).
Control variables. The teachers’ age was asked about in an open
format (“How old are you?”). To identify the gender that each teacher
identified with, teachers could choose between three options (male, female, and diverse). In addition, the type of school was controlled for at
the school level.

5.1.1. Participants and procedure
We used data from the DiScH project (“Distanzlernen in SchleswigHolstein”: distance learning in Schleswig-Holstein). The aim of the
project was to investigate the handling of distance learning in schools as
well as to evaluate the impact of the pandemic on different people in
everyday school life. The COVID-19 pandemic changed everyone’s life
and also fundamentally affected teachers and students in Germany. In
March 2020, schools were closed from one day to the next and teachers
were required to support students’ distance learning. The two-week
school closure that was initially announced turned into two months.
(Maaz & Diedrich, 2020). After two months, limited face-to-face
teaching at schools took place again, with the students constantly
alternating between distance and face-to-face learning. At the same
time, teaching and learning in schools was far from regular. Strict and
frequently changing hygiene rules had to be implemented and monitored. Shortly before Christmas 2020, the schools were completely
closed again and distance learning became necessary. Until the middle
of March 2021, regular face-to-face schooling was not possible (Maaz &
Diedrich, 2020). The specific organization of this second lockdown in
terms of length and restrictions differed between federal states, school
types, and age groups (Kultusministerkonferenz [Standing Conference of
the Ministers of Education and Cultural Affairs of the Federal States in
Germany], 2021). The focus of the survey was on this long period of
recurrent distance learning.
In Germany, it quickly became apparent that the school system was
not prepared for digital learning in terms of digital skills of teachers and
students and technical equipment. Internet access in schools, digital
devices and platforms on which information can be exchanged and used
for online teaching were not sufficiently available. In an international
comparison, Germany’s standing on the digitalization of schools was
significantly lower than that of other countries (Eickelmann et al.,
2019). In Germany, the focus of the national support programs was on
equipping both teachers and students with digital devices (Bundesministerium für Bildung und Forschung [Federal Ministry of Education and
Research], 2020, 2021). In addition, more training courses on digital
skills were offered (Maaz & Diedrich, 2020).
Before Study 1 was started, the Ministry of Education, Science,
Research and Culture of Schleswig-Holstein verified that the project was
ethically acceptable. Schools were randomly selected within each school
type in Schleswig-Holstein, resulting in a sample of 151 schools. The
selection process was carried out by the Institute for Quality Development in Schools in Schleswig-Holstein (IQSH). Each selected school
received a letter with information about the project and a request to
participate. In the sample for Study 1, teachers from special needs
schools were excluded because, in this school form, it was often the case
that no instruction took place during the pandemic. In addition, we only
considered schools in Study 1 that provided at least one teacher’s
response for a relevant variable. The survey was conducted online from
March to April 2021 and the participation was voluntary for teachers.
The final sample consisted of 1022 teachers from 120 schools (M =
8.52 teachers per school; range: 1–43); 71 elementary schools, 30
schools with a nonacademic track, and 19 schools with an academic
track were included. In Germany, after four years in primary school,
students make the transition to secondary school. They can choose between schools with a nonacademic track and schools with an academic
track, where they can pursue different educational qualifications. The
percentage of female participants was 77.3% and, on average, the

5.1.3. Statistical Analysis
We conducted multilevel regression analyses with random intercepts
to take the hierarchical structure of teachers clustered within different
schools into account. To obtain coefficients for the effects that were
independent of the measurement units, we calculated standardized coefficients, that is, coefficients whose scale units are expressed in standard deviations. The indicators of social interactions were considered on
both the teacher level and the school level. We aggregated individual
teacher reports on principals’ support and cooperation with colleagues
within each school in order to capture the shared perception of the
teachers’ work context at the school level.
To determine the amount of variance between the schools, the
intraclass correlation coefficient ICC(1) was calculated (Lüdtke et al.,
2006); the variance between schools was divided by the total variance.
The ICC(1) was calculated by using a separate null model for each
variable. Additionally, the intraclass correlation coefficient ICC(2) was
calculated to specify the reliability of the aggregated variables. In the
calculation, the average cluster size was taken into account as the
number of respondents per school varied (Lüdtke et al., 2006).
All participants who achieved at least one response on an examined
variable were included. In the final sample, between 0% and 8.2% of the
data were missing. We assumed that the missing data were randomly
distributed; therefore, a full information maximum likelihood (Enders,
2010) approach was used to estimate missing values.
The control variables at the teacher level (gender and age), were
grand-mean centered to control for group differences at the school level
(Lüdtke et al., 2009). All other variables at the teacher level were
group-mean centered to disentangle the variation between the two

In Study 1, on the one hand, we found meaningful differences between schools in the indicators of social interactions (principals’ support
and cooperation with colleagues) but, on the other hand, we found little
variation between schools in teachers’ emotional exhaustion during

6. Study 2
In Study 2, we considered both the negative and the positive aspects
of occupational well-being (emotional exhaustion and job satisfaction),
allowing for a more nuanced view. Furthermore, we focused on a specific subgroup of teachers who worked in schools in socially challenging
settings. Two important social indicators of the school context were
investigated simultaneously at the teacher and school levels: exchange
and professional collaboration with colleagues.

6.1.1. Participants and procedure
For Study 2, we used data from the federal state program “SchuMaS –
Schule macht stark” (school makes you strong), for which 200 schools
across Germany were surveyed. Different types of elementary and secondary schools were included. The program aims to create better
educational opportunities for socially disadvantaged pupils; in this
context, the professionalization of teachers is also considered a central
component. The survey took place before the start of the program, using
an online questionnaire, from August to October 2021. Some questions
were randomly assigned to the teachers in the survey to reduce the
processing time. Participation was voluntary for teachers.
The sample included 2886 teachers from 196 schools (M = 14.72 per
school; range: 1–66). The percentage of female teachers was 81.1% and
the participants had, on average, 12.6 (SD = 10.26; range: 0–48) years of
work experience.

One possible explanation for this could be the different political conditions that exist in the 16 federal states in Germany. The supportive
services (e.g., school social worker or additional teacher) vary considerably across federal states (Beierle et al., 2019). Consequently, it is
conceivable that the particular challenges posed by socioeconomically
disadvantaged and low-performing students are coped with to varying
degrees in different federal states. This—in turn— may result in differences between schools in teachers’ experience of exhaustion. Dealing
with challenging student behavior requires the professionalization of
teachers in particular. Although there is an obligation to provide further
training in Germany, there are no uniform regulations on the scope and
content of such training. The decision about which training program to
attend therefore depends on the teacher and the principal (Schewe et al.,
2022). On the one hand, teachers themselves can select specific training
programs to help them deal with demands such as increased classroom
disruptions and social-emotional problems among students, and, on the
other hand, principals can positively influence the choice of training
programs among the teaching staff. Participation in in-service training
can be seen as an important component of teachers’ ongoing professionalization and, thus, could be relevant not only for student outcomes
but also for teachers’ occupational well-being (Kennedy, 2016; Richter
& Richter, 2020). In-service training programs must account for the fact
that teachers usually participate voluntarily (Gorozidis & Papaioannou,
2014); frequent reasons for nonparticipation are a lack of time and a
workload that is already too high, as well as the low quality of the
in-service training offered (Richter et al., 2018). Therefore,
needs-oriented in-service training should focus on being realistically
feasible as well as meeting individual and organizational conditions
(Weber et al., in press). It could be promising to already prepare preservice teachers during university training for social-emotional challenges of the teaching profession by offering courses on
stress-management, emotion regulation, and classroom management
(Carstensen et al., 2019). However, this approach cannot replace further
professionalization.
Our finding for job satisfaction, with 6.6% between-school variance,
was comparable with that of some previous studies (Lopes & Oliveira,
2020; OECD, 2020b). However, our result was lower than the findings of
Pietsch et al. (2019) or Kapa and Gimbert (2018). One possible explanation for this could be the different methods of assessment (OECD,
2020b; Spector, 2022). While, in our assessment, the focus was on the
tasks of the teaching profession, other studies explicitly asked about
occupational aspects such as satisfaction with the work climate (Pietsch
et al., 2019) or satisfaction with working at the current school (Kapa &
Gimbert, 2018). In that case, more variation between schools could be
expected as the questions relate to satisfaction with specific school
factors rather than to satisfaction with the overall profession.

transactional stress model (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), the individual
perception of the work environment seems to be more crucial in the
experience of emotional exhaustion than organizational factors are; this
is possibly due to individual factors such as personality, personal resources, and coping strategies.
Another possible explanation for why previous results showed little
difference between schools in teachers’ emotional exhaustion may be
due to the way in which the burnout facet was measured. Van Droogenbroeck et al. (2021) formulated the idea that social comparison
processes within the school, but also between schools, could play a role
in the individual assessment of the experience of exhaustion. The
assessment of emotional exhaustion with the MBI, which is widely used
and was also used in our studies, sets an individual reference, which
could implicitly initiate comparison processes. In this way, teachers’
individual ratings might depend on whether they rate their emotional
experience in comparison to other colleagues within the school or from
other schools. A more differentiated assessment, which could possibly
better reflect differences in the exhaustion between schools, could
conceivably be achieved by formulating a clear reference. For example,
González-Morales et al. (2012) found substantial differences in the ICC

in this school” and those with individual references.

7.2. What role do the indicators of social interactions have for well-being?
Previous findings often showed the importance of social resources for
teachers’ well-being (Hakanen et al., 2006; Halbesleben, 2006; Kim
et al., 2022; Pressley et al., 2022; van Droogenbroeck et al., 2021),
although the distinction between individual perceptions (transactional
at the teacher level) and shared perceptions (organizational at the school
level) was often not clearly defined. In contrast to those previous findings, our results enable us to discuss in more detail the level at which the
indicators of social interactions are particularly relevant for teachers’
occupational well-being. In both studies, there was meaningful variation
across schools in the indicators of social interactions, suggesting a
relatively high degree of agreement in the individual assessment within
schools and substantial differences in the shared perception between
schools.
While the importance of social interactions in explaining differences
in teachers’ experience of exhaustion was clearly evident at the teacher
level in Study 1, a more differentiated pattern emerged for schools in
socially challenging settings. The shared perception of indicators of social interactions seems to be more relevant for the experience of
emotional exhaustion than the individual evaluation. This is particularly
evident from the fact that teachers from schools with more exchange
between colleagues reported significantly lower levels of emotional
exhaustion. This could suggest that a cooperative social environment is
more important at schools in socially challenging settings to enable
teachers to cope with the increased demands of problematic student
behavior. This would be in line with findings that have suggested that
higher social support in a school is associated with a lower socioeconomic status of the students (Klusmann et al., 2008b) and that the
incidence of cooperative behavior in a school increases when, for
example, schools have inclusion programs or have more students with
special educational needs (Richter & Pant, 2016). In contrast, for
teachers’ job satisfaction, the tendency was observed that individual
perceptions of indicators of social interactions seemed to be more
meaningful than shared perceptions. In particular, this is indicated by
the fact that teachers who reported deeper cooperation in form of more
professional collaboration were more satisfied with their jobs.
For the social indicators examined in Study 2 as predictors of
teachers’ occupational well-being, a more nuanced view could provide
further insights. The following considerations should be seen in light of
the fact that, on the one hand, the relations were mostly marginally
significant and, on the other hand, there were high correlations between
the two forms of cooperation, which could have influenced the coefficients in the regression analyses. Exchange and professional collaboration showed an inverse relationship with emotional exhaustion at
both the teacher and the school level. At the teacher level, teachers
tended to report more emotional exhaustion when they cooperated more
frequently through exchange in their professional lives. However, at the
school level, more exchange was significantly associated with lower
exhaustion. One possible interpretation of these results could be that
more exhausted teachers cooperate more perfunctorily, whereas a social
environment that provides more low-threshold cooperation in the form
of more exchange could be meaningful in the experience of emotional

7.3. Limitations and future research
There are some limitations to consider in our studies. First of all, we
used only cross-sectional data, so we cannot make any assumptions
about causality. Second, at the school level, we examined teachers’
shared perceptions in both studies, which emerged from aggregate
teacher-level data within each school. Thus, these shared perceptions
were based, in some cases, on only a few individual statements per
school, which should be seen against the background of individual experiences and possibly also personal likes or dislikes. Future research
should therefore consider other measures to capture the context, such as
data from principals or objective external criteria. Third, our focus was
on the indicators of social interactions, although other predictors such as
school demands (e.g., lack of technical equipment) may also be meaningful in explaining differences in teachers’ experience of exhaustion.
Fourth, it should be noted, especially in our first study, that COVID-19specific predictors were mainly used, which explicitly referred to the
distance learning phase. It is conceivable that predictors not specifically
related to the pandemic situation, such as the general workload of
teachers, are also relevant.
8. Conclusion and practical implications
How can we answer the question of how meaningful the context is in
research on teachers’ occupational well-being? First of all, we found
little evidence to support the assumption that context is central to
explaining differences in teachers’ occupational well-being. However,
evidence emerged that differences between schools in certain environments, in our case, schools in socially challenging settings, may play a
significant role. But, overall, the variation was found mostly at the
teacher level, so we found more or less exhausted teachers in every
school. The experience of occupational well-being may be related to an
individual’s perception of the school environment, which is probably
related to their own resources and coping strategies (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). However, it is also important to consider the complexity of
the school context, where many factors have differential effects on the
individual teachers’ occupational well-being. Simultaneously, it is

A further question concerns the recommendations that can be
derived from these studies for the practical implementation of interventions. Even though previous empirical findings have mainly
emphasized individual perceptions and evaluations and have thus suggested a design of interventions predominantly on the individual level,
this issue has to be considered in a more differentiated way. Increasingly, the importance of fertile ground is becoming clear to facilitate a
helpful environment for personal development and, most importantly,
to promote the success of an intervention (Walton & Yeager, 2020). It
can be assumed that a teacher can benefit more from an intervention if
the school environment is open to possible changes. Thus, an intervention should optimally involve both the school environment and the individual teacher (Semmer, 2006). In other settings, organizational and
individual intervention approaches are already combined. An example
of such an intervention program can be seen in Munz et al. (2001). They
combined individual self-management training with a stressor-reduction
process at the organizational level. Transferring this to the school
context, a combination of individual and organizational aspects in an
intervention could therefore be expected to have a positive impact on
both individual well-being and school-wide performance and, thus, to
influence student outcomes. To design and conduct an intervention
based only on school characteristics would overlook teachers who teach
in schools that do not have these characteristics but who may still suffer
from low levels of occupational well-being. Further, interventions that
are implemented purely at the organizational level are more difficult to
implement and may affect individuals who do not need to participate in
an intervention (Nielsen et al., 2010).
