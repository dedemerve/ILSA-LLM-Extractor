Abstract 
The international instruments of the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 
require national adaptations and translation before data collection processes begin. This 
paper provides an assessment of the processes used in TALIS 2018, based on an analysis 
of the documentation available, as well as countries’ descriptions of the processes and 
challenges undergone during that cycle. The author starts by identifying several positive 
aspects, highlighting the work of the international contractor, the national centres and 
national project managers but also suggests areas for improvement. Finally, the paper looks 
at the new translatability assessment in the current cycle (TALIS 2024) and how it could 
be even more effective, as well as recommending a series of proposals that could support 
adaptation and translation processes in future cycles.

1. Translation and adaptation processes in TALIS 2018 
1.1. Introduction 
The international instruments of the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 
require national adaptations and translation before data collection processes begin. Even if 
the survey is administered in English, national centres (NC) are required to adapt the 
questionnaires to their national contexts. After adaptation, the translation of the source 
instruments follows the rules of the target language and cultural context of implementation. 
This ensures that the translated text has the same meaning as the international source text 
to safeguard international comparability. This also applies when adapting the international 
version to the forms of English used in a given country. Review and verification processes 
are used to ensure accurate adaptations and translations of the international source 
materials. 
This paper looks into the adaptation, translation and verification processes and procedures 
that were performed for TALIS 2018, and identifies positive outcomes and areas for 
improvement, hinting at alternative processes that may improve results. In order to achieve 
a better understanding of all processes, procedures and subsequent translation and 
verification outcomes, available documentation on the TALIS 2018 implementation was 
analysed. Additionally, a set of countries that faced translation problems were identified, 
as well as those with good translation practices. Representatives from these countries were 
asked to respond to a survey regarding adaptation, translation and verification processes, 
and the potential difficulties they encountered. While most analyses focused on the English 
source version of the instruments, the French international version was also examined as 
part of this process. 
The first section provides an overview of the TALIS 2018 adaptation, translation and 
verification procedures for both the field trial (FT) and the main survey (MS), and of the 
reported outcomes. The second section focuses on the survey and the analysis of the 
participant’s responses, while the third discusses key learnings and subsequent 
recommendations. 
As one of the key learnings point to the usefulness of using a translatability assessment to 
identify translation and adaptation issues in the instruments’ source version, the last section 
of this document provides an overview of the translatability assessment that was performed 
for TALIS 2024, the outcomes and the resulting pros and cons. 
1.2. Overview of adaptation, translation and verification in TALIS 2018 
1.2.1. Adaptation, translation and verification for the field trial  
The source version of the survey instruments for implementation was released in English. 
A French international version was also prepared to be used for adaptation and translation. 
In order to conduct the TALIS 2018 FT, countries had to adapt and/or translate the 
international versions of the teacher and principal questionnaires (OECD, 2018[1]; OECD, 
2018[2]) into a local language or languages. 
Instruments’ adaptation, translation and verification for the FT occurred using the 
eAssessment system developed by the International Association for the Evaluation of 
Educational Achievement (IEA). The eAssesment system was a new feature of the TALIS 
2018 FT that included different functionalities relating to the different stages of instrument

preparation, adaptation and translation, and administration. The intention was for all 
instruments to be adapted, translated, verified, and made available for online administration 
using this platform. 
As a first step, all adaptations1 had to be submitted to the International Study Centre (ISC) 
before translation. These adaptations needed to be back translated by the NC staff into 
English for review and approval by the ISC team. However, items for which there were no 
required adaptations or for which participants did not have additional adaptations could be 
translated immediately. Note that, for the FT, it was possible for a participant to request 
additional adaptations during translation in using a specific comment type in the 
eAssessment system, but it was not permitted to change an adaptation already approved by 
the ISC. 
The next step was to perform translation of the new instruments. There were 
recommendations for this process but no specific requirements (e.g. no compulsory 
requirements standardised for all participants). The recommended procedure was to 
perform a single translation followed by a review. The desired profile of the translator and 
the reviewer were specified, and broad translation guidelines and a glossary provided.  
After translation, verification of the new instruments was performed by trained verifiers, 
who had to correct, provide feedback, severity codes and suggestions within the 
eAssessment system. Participants were then able to accept, modify or reject the verifier’s 
feedback and version. 
Verifiers checked the national versions sentence by sentence against the English source 
version, making sure all the information included in the source was also present in the target 
version. If the sentence included an approved adaptation, the verifier had to confirm the 
correctness of the adaptation (e.g. the verifier also needed to check the back translation 
provided by participants during the adaptation phase). In case of deviation versus the source 
(either at the linguistic or adaptation level), the translation verifier had to select a severity 
code, then correct the deviation and provide a brief comment. Severity codes used were the 
following:  
• 
Code 1 for serious errors 
• 
Code 2 for minor errors 
• 
Code 3 for suggestions for improvement/alternative 
• 
Code 4 to confirm adaptations 
• 
Code 1? when in doubt about the code to apply. 
For trend instruments, participants were asked to enter the translation manually, ensuring 
they matched their 2013 national version, but no international systematic verification was 
performed by trained verifiers. 
The FT instrument preparation process ended with layout verification to ensure that adapted 
and translated texts were displaying properly and according to source.

The contractor designed and communicated a template for national quality control 
programmes to be carried out during the FT by national quality observers (NQOs) 
appointed by countries. They reported to the NPMs, who were requested to record the 
findings from the programme in order to identify any systematic problems that may have 
occurred during the administration of the survey. 
1.2.2. Adaptation, translation and verification for the main survey 
The use of the eAssessment system for the FT preparation created several issues. First, the 
system required quite a number of updates and improvements to solve technical issues, 
which resulted in delays to the process. Participants also required more time than allocated 
to get used to the system and half of them reported that adaptations and localisation2 were 
difficult to implement. As a result, the adaptation, translation and verification processes for 
preparing the instruments for the MS were different from the FT.3 While the recommended 
procedure was still single translation followed by a review, the eAssessment system was 
no longer used and translation and verification were performed in Microsoft Word. 
Participants received the questionnaires in Word format, in which FT national translations 
were included except for those MS questions or items for which the FT version’s content 
had been modified. They were asked to translate all new and revised text, reflecting the FT-
to-MS changes implemented by the contractor, and to reimplement FT structural 
adaptations that were approved.4 
A colour code was used in order to help participants identifying FT-to-MS changes in item 
content: black for items not changed, pink for items with no FT translation available and 
turquoise for items where some FT-to-MS changes were introduced. 
All adaptations (including adaptations already approved for the FT) had to be documented 
into a national adaptation form (NAF) and it was possible to change or add non-structural 
adaptations prior to translation. Changes in structural adaptations were limited to 
modification or non-administration of the approved FT adaptation. 
MS verification of new instruments was, as in the FT, a complete verification, sentence by 
sentence, and verifiers edited the national version directly in the Word files using the track 
changes mode. Verifiers, again, received comprehensive step-by-step training. All 
“severity Code 1”, “severity Code 1?” and “severity Code 4” comments were listed in the 
NAFs and Word documents, while the “severity Code 2” and “severity Code 3” comments 
were only listed in the Word documents, unless the comment was linked to a national 
adaptation. At post-verification review, participants were then asked to review the 
Microsoft Word document accepting, rejecting or modifying the verifier remarks, and to 
describe and explain any major difference of opinions between the NC staff and the 
verifiers in the NAF.

In contrast to the FT, trend items were verified. Verifiers were asked to check the 2018

For the MS, the contractor designed an international quality control programme carried out 
by international quality observers (IQOs), who received two days of training to implement 
the programme. Among their tasks, IQOs had to collect the national versions of the survey 
instruments and were expected to complete a translation verification report (one per 
language of administration), in which they had to assess and document consistency across 
instruments, and comment on the use of the verifier advice. The OECD TALIS 2018 
Technical report (OECD, 2019[3]) states that: 
“IQOs recorded feedback from the translation verification process and their review 
of the verified national instruments and School Co-ordinator Manual(s) in the 
translation verification report. The IQOs completed one translation verification 
report per surveyed language (maximum of two national languages). The reports 
provided a starting point for determining whether any translation problems made 
the item(s) internationally non-comparable. Translation verification was 
implemented to help ensure the international comparability of the survey 
instruments.” (p. 133)

1.2.3. Reported outcomes on adaptation, translation and verification 
From the field trial 
Following the FT, the contractors explained in the progress reports that there had been a 
higher number of requests for dropping items compared to 2013 (i.e. structural 
adaptations). The main reasons were mostly linked to cultural and country-specific issues 
such as sensitivity, inappropriateness and incomprehensible or non-applicable concepts. 
They also mentioned extensive adaptations from some NCs and attempts from NCs to 
improve and modify the wording of the English source version and the French international 
version.

Trend wording that did not match TALIS 2013 national versions were reported as being 
due to corrections of errors, improvements (2018 version more appropriate than 2013 
version), and changes to the educational system. In addition, the progress reports noted that 
the suggested requirements made by the contractors about the profile of translators and 
reviewers were not met by a few participants. 
Countries’ feedback on FT verification was positive, with satisfaction expressed by most 
of the participants. A majority found the verifier checks and comments useful or very 
useful. However, instrument adaptations and translations were considered by participants 
to be time-consuming and – to some extent – difficult, due to software errors, restricted 
functionality, and system unavailability caused by necessary software updates, 
improvements and new releases. It was concluded that there was a need to change the 
procedure for the MS. 
From the main survey 
The technical report notes that 29 of the 48 participants experienced no difficulties 
translating the source questionnaires into national language(s) and adapting them to the 
local context, and that 41 of the 48 said that they corrected errors identified by the verifiers. 
In the data adjudication chapter, the contractor explains that several issues arose with, for 
example, national adaptations and that some problems were discovered only during data 
processing, weighting and scaling. Reported examples of such issues are (i) incomplete 
questionnaires, (ii) changes to national versions of trend items and (iii) reported issues in 
the quality observers’ reports. Noted among the latter were differences between national 
decisions about the verifier feedback and actual implementation in the final questionnaires, 
and/or unnatural and non-fluent national translations. A number of items also needed to be 
withdrawn for a few countries because of lack of clarity in the national language or due to 
translation issues. Some of these withdrawals were done at the country’s request. 
In the lessons learnt from the FT and MS translation, adaptation and verification processes, 
the contractors listed the need to: 
• 
simplify the terminology and increase the clarity of the question 
• 
maintain and ensure consistent procedures (modes of instrument preparation) 
• 
improve guidelines for checking trends 
• 
provide clearer instructions on editing trend translations to match revised trend 
items 
• 
improve communication regarding country timelines to enhance a smooth 
workflow. 
1.3. Translation, adaptation and verification survey responses 
Based on the reported outcomes and country feedback on TALIS 2018 adaptation, 
translation and verification procedures, the OECD Secretariat identified 14 countries from 
different cultures and which tested in different languages that were then asked to respond 
to a survey on adaptation, translation and verification in TALIS 2018. A 15th country was 
also selected to try and investigate linguistic adaptations to French and the overall quality 
of the French international version. 
Unfortunately, five of these countries were either not able to answer the survey (2) or only 
able to answer partially (3) because the NC or people involved in TALIS 2018 were no 
longer able to be reached. Two additional countries were then identified, one of which

tested in two different languages. Therefore, this survey collected responses from 15 
countries about 16 different language versions, but with 3 incomplete surveys. 
The survey included three to five sections, depending on the country profile. Country 
profiles included countries identified by the ISC as having good language versions, 
countries that seemed to have experienced issues during the adaptation, translation and 
verification processes, countries who had to drop items because of translation issues, or 
countries with a combination of the aforementioned issues. Therefore, the survey was 
tailored to each country while including a common set of questions. 
The first part of the survey asked about countries’ experiences with the adaptation process 
(i.e. non-structural adaptations that were compulsory – wording between angle brackets 
<….> – or, on request, and/or structural adaptations), with all adaptations needing to be 
approved prior to the translation process. This part was identical for all but the participants 
testing in English and French.5 While structural and non-structural adaptations are not part 
of the actual translation process, issues with identification of the adaptations and/or with 
their back translation may render an item nonsensical and affect the data. Therefore, it was 
deemed necessary to ask questions on that topic. 
The second part of the survey explored the actual translation procedures and processes, 
except for two countries that did not translate but made linguistic adaptations to, 
respectively, the English source version and the French international version. 
The third part of the survey then looked at countries’ FT and MS post-verification 
experience. 
The fourth part of the survey was included only for countries that chose or had to drop 
items after data collection due to a translation issue and, finally, the last section invited 
countries to provide any comments about adaptation, translation and verification in TALIS, 
including suggestions for improvement. 
The following subsections provide the survey questions and the collated country responses 
for all countries except the country testing in French, which will be discussed separately. 
1.3.1. Field trial and main survey adaptation process 
To avoid any potential misunderstanding about the focus of this first part of the survey, 
participants were provided with the following definition: “By ‘Adaptation’, we mean 
non-structural adaptations that were compulsory (wording between angle brackets <….>) 
or on request, and/or structural adaptations on request. All of which needed to be approved 
prior to the translation process.” 
The first question asked participants who was in charge of the TALIS 2018 FT and MS 
adaptation processes in their country and what this person’s qualifications and/or 
professional background were. They were also asked to include the profiles of all people 
involved in the process. 
In most cases, people involved in the process for requesting and negotiating structural and 
non-structural adaptations were specialists in the field of education with experience in 
educational surveys and qualifications in the education sector and often included the 
country’s NPM.

1.3.4. Dropped items 
Eight countries were asked to provide specific information about items that had to be 
withdrawn because of a lack of clarity or an inaccurate translation and to specify whether 
the dropped item had been the subject of a verifier comment and/or correction. Therefore, 
responses were language- or country-specific and cannot be aggregated.  
One respondent pointed to an issue with the classification levels when asking teachers 
about their educational level. The notion of “licence”, for example (Licenciatura, which 
should not be confused with the licence in France for example), which should have been 
associated with the “master’s degree”-level (ISCED 7) was wrongly associated with the 
“bachelor’s degree”-level (ISCED 6) which would have resulted in a number of teachers 
responding that they had a bachelor’s degree when, in fact, they had a “licence/master’s 
degree”. 
This issue is clearly linked to the adaptation process and implicitly to the adaptation 
back-translation process. However, as verifiers have to check the accuracy of the 
adaptations’ back translation, a native verifier would have been expected to spot the issue, 
knowing that the licenciatura is a longer course than the bachelor’s. Given that verifiers’ 
feedback from the FT is not available, it is not possible to know if this was subject to a 
verifier’s comment during the FT preparation. 
For another respondent, the translation issue was similar for the four dropped items. This 
was a consistency issue, which means that the same wording was not translated identically 
in all occurrences. “Professional development activities” was translated verbatim in TQ24, 
25, 26 and 28 while, in other occurrences, it was translated as “activities that help 
professional development”. Though both translations are correct and understandable, the 
NPM thought it was best to only include in the international reports data for items where 
the translations were in line with each other. They assumed that answers for the withdrawn 
items would not have been 100% reliable.

The issue with TQ43-a is that the wording deviates too much from the English source. The 
question to which this item refers was “How often do you use the following methods of 
assessing student learning in the <target class>?” The use of “manage” for “administer” 
and the addition of “process” changes the meaning as it is no longer about administering 
an assessment the teachers designed themselves. Rather, it is about the management of a 
process, which is much vaguer and may, therefore, be subject to different interpretations. 
Therefore, the respondent adapted the wording into “I administer an assessment I designed 
myself”. In this case, it would have been better for the French international version to 
follow the English wording more closely. Again, a review by a French education expert 
would likely have identified this issue. 
The third example in Table 7 is more subtle. At first sight, translating “I experience stress 
in my work” as “I suffer from stress at work” may seem fine, but the French wording 
conveys a stronger feeling because of the use of “suffer”. In French, “suffer” has two, 
slightly different meanings: “experiencing” and “experiencing (physical or mental) pain”. 
Given the two possible interpretations, responses to this item might be biased. If a teacher 
experiences stress at work but does not suffer from it and has interpreted “suffer” as 
“experimenting mental pain”, then it is likely they will answer differently. The issue with 
translating correctly “to experience” is a well-known issue for French domain experts and

A second possible issue with this wording is the translation of “in my work” as “at work”. 
For teachers, “at work” generally means “at school”, while a non-negligeable part of a 
teacher’s work is not performed at school (lesson preparation, homework corrections,

considered the stress experienced while teaching or when at school and not the possible 
stress experienced when working on preparing lessons, assessments, correcting students’ 
assignments, etc. outside school. The exact meaning should be checked with questionnaire 
authors and may then be conveyed to all participants through a translation guideline. 
In the last example provided, the first issue partly comes from a well-known syntactic issue 
in the English source, which uses the passive voice (Dept, Ferrari and Halleux, 2017[4]). 
While it may be good to use the active voice to provide an example to countries translating 
into languages in which the passive voice does not exist or is hardly ever used, changing 
“each teacher” into “the teachers” misses the question’s intent. Indeed, principals may then 
answer this question thinking about the teachers as a whole and not about each teacher. The 
suggested national version follows the English syntax and uses passive voice. To help with 
translation into other languages, the French international version could keep an active voice 
syntax in referring to each teacher, such as: “On average, how often do the following people 
formally appraise each teacher?”. 
The fourth survey question asked what types of linguistic adaptations they made and, if 
there were many, to list the three most important types. The answers listed: 
(i) simplification of the register, (ii) issue of meaning compared to English and (ii) 
national-context specificities. 
When countries make adaptations to a source version, it would be expected for them to 
only have to adapt the language to their national context (i.e. localisation). They should 
rarely have to change the language register or correct the meaning to be closer to the other 
source version.

This section outlines the lessons learnt based on both the surveys’ responses and the review

1.4.1. Preparing for adaptation and translation 
Ensuring translation quality within and across national versions requires (i) an 
unambiguous English version, (ii) additional guidance about the aims of the questions and 
the translation of complex or specific terminology and (iii) translator training. The survey’s 
responses clearly showed a demand for more guidance and concerns about source 
complexity. 
Item developers in other surveys and assessments (e.g. PISA), are trained on how to avoid 
translation and cultural issues when writing items. In the case of PISA, this training is based 
on the experience gained over all its cycles and is supported by a manual developed for 
this. Training also stresses the basics of what is known to lead to systematic translation 
issues. 
Ideally, the questionnaire’s authors should at least receive guidelines about known 
translation issues to be followed when developing the new questions and items. 
Translation-related issues can render an item immediately unusable, and often such issues 
do not come to light until the item development process has advanced considerably. 
Preventing translation problems as early as possible often results in better items and a more 
effective and cost-efficient item development process.  
Collecting countries’ comments about questionnaire content and their suggestions for 
adaptations or improvements made during FT preparation may also be very useful to item 
developers when reviewing the FT instruments for the MS. Even if this review comes at a 
later stage – as happened in PISA – it can efficiently reduce some equivalence issues 
between national versions and sources. 
Yet, developing an English version that would be unambiguous and equivalently 
translatable in a high number of languages is challenging and there are currently several 
approaches/strategies used in OECD and non-OECD studies. Among those, four are 
highlighted below: 
• 
development of a second source version

Second source version 
The respondent from the French-testing country mentioned the usefulness of a French 
international version, which, notably, provides a degree of translation/adaptation freedom. 
With the translation procedure recommended for TALIS (single translation + review) and 
considering that, in TALIS 2018, the French international version was only used by the two 
French-testing countries, having a French source version did not provide any insights to 
other countries when they were preparing their national instruments. Using a double 
translation, reconciliation procedure or recommending to countries that they perform the 
review by referring to the French source version, if possible, would allow for more 
countries to benefit from the second source.  
While it seems that this would only be true for countries translating from French or using 
French for cross-checks when reconciling or reviewing, it should be noted that 
item-by-item guidelines drafted when preparing the French international version often 
include back translation of the French wording into English. 
Developing a second source version has two main benefits. First, as it is prepared during 
the development of the English source, well before the questionnaires are provided to 
countries for adaptation and translation, it helps to improve the translatability of the English 
version. Secondly, it provides participants with additional item-by-item guidelines and 
alternative wording and/or shows the degree of translation and adaptation freedom. 
The development of a second source version, such as the French source in PISA, includes 
several steps during which translation and adaptation issues are documented and provided 
to item developers. Comments from the different players include content queries, 
translation queries, suggestions for change in English and item-by-item translation 
guidelines. The process systematically results in changes in the English source.  
Therefore, this is an additional means of “improving” the English source version in terms 
of translatability, and of identifying or fine-tuning item-by-item guidelines. The respondent 
to the survey about French-testing noted that the French international version was presented 
as being a “source” version. The TALIS 2018 documentation indeed sometimes refer to it 
as the “French source version” but also as an “international version”. Therefore, it is unclear 
what exactly the status of this version was. Based on the respondent’s feedback, the French 
international version seemed to include a number of weaknesses.

A second source version is supposed to be equivalent to the first source. This means that 
countries can decide to only use that source for translation and adaptation without 
necessarily having to refer to the other source (though it is always encouraged) and that 
French-testing participants should normally only have to focus on localisation. 
Achieving the ambitious goal of equivalence of both versions would require, at least, the 
involvement of a French domain expert and an expert in equivalence. This would also 
require extensive communication between the French preparation team and the 
questionnaire authors. As mentioned earlier, the English wording may need to be changed 
to ensure better equivalence with the French (in case the French cannot match the English), 
or to be more easily translatable into other languages (translation issues identified during 
French preparation). However, if the French uses alternative wording to the English, which 
would provide countries with an idea of translation freedom, the French wording may need 
to be endorsed by questionnaire authors. 
In comparison, as mentioned earlier, PISA uses a more complex process: double 
translation, reconciliation, French domain expert review, lead reconciler review, 
equivalence expert review and linguist review. After the lead reconciler and equivalence 
expert review, questionnaire authors receive complete feedback about (i) translation issues 
that may lead to additional guidelines or changes to the English source, (ii) requests for 
changes to the English to ensure better equivalence with the French, (iii) content issues or 
queries from the domain experts and (iv) back translation of the adapted French wording 
for endorsement. Therefore, there are two rounds of full discussion with the developers. 
Obviously, given the processes described above, producing a second source version as 
equivalent as possible to the English version requires both additional expertise and financial 
resources. 
Guidelines 
In response to these surveys, but also to the surveys conducted by the ISC, some 
respondents expressed concerns about wording complexity and the lack of specific 
guidance. They also pointed out the need to receive more information about each question’s 
aim/intent.  
While the TALIS 2018 glossary was impressive and quite comprehensive, it required 
translator(s) and reviewer(s) to go from the questionnaire to the separate glossary and back, 
which may not have been the most user-friendly process. Glossary definitions were also 
provided outside of the actual question or item context. 
Translation guidelines were also well designed, though too concise and very general. Such 
guidelines may need to be supplemented by item-by-item guidelines that are item-specific 
and that can be provided either in the monitoring tool or within the questionnaire as specific 
notes. For TALIS questionnaires, the second approach might be the best as it would allow 
the inclusion of not only guidelines about translation but also notes about each question’s 

30  EDU/WKP(2023)5 
TRANSLATION AND ADAPTATION PROCESSES IN TALIS 2018 
For Official Use 
aim/intent, as well as relevant glossary extracts (as requested by some of the respondents 
to the survey). This approach was used in PISA, where participants received an English 
version with notes included under each question to guide them during the adaptation 
process, double translation and reconciliation. 
Going back on the general translation guidelines, some of them may need to be reviewed. 
Contrary to cognitive items, which absolutely need to be linguistically equivalent to 
sources, the main aim in the translation of questionnaires is to ask the same question: 
Ask-the-same-question (ASQ) model (Harkness, 2003[5]). This means that, according to the 
national context or language specificities, the language register or the syntax may need to 
differ from the source while still asking the same question. It is wrong to assume that just 
because principals and teachers are educated adults, clumsy wording or syntax in the 
national language would not affect the way they interpret and respond to the questionnaire. 
Similarly, asking translators to make sure the meaning of idioms is correctly rendered in 
the translation is quite puzzling for an assessment in a multinational and multicultural 
context. Indeed, if a culture or language does not have a similar idiom, translators may 
provide approximate idioms that convey a slightly different idea, or with their own 
interpretation of an idiom’s meaning, which often leads to equivalence issues. In fact, 
idioms should be avoided in the source questionnaires and should be rephrased using the 
exact meaning. 
Translators’/reviewers’ training 
Regarding translators and reviewers, survey literature strongly recommends translators’ 
training and briefing (Dept, Ferrari and Halleux, 2017[4]; Harkness, 2003[5]). Even if they 
are experienced in survey translation, translators and reviewers should be briefed about the 
type of translation required, the aim of the survey and the targeted audience. 
For the actual training and practice, old/released materials could be used and manuals 
tailored to translator and reviewer roles should be provided. In OECD studies, PISA and 
PIAAC include such material in their translation kit/training packages and training is 
provided to NPMs or translation team leaders who, in turn, train their national translators 
and reconcilers/reviewers. From the available documentation, it is unclear if a similar 
package was provided for TALIS 2018.  
In addition, as shown in the survey results, countries with better translation quality all hired 
professional translators. While involving education experts (either external or from the 
NCs) adds value to translation during review and/or adjudication, it is wrong to think that 
because experts have a good knowledge and/or understanding of English, they will be able 
to produce fluent and natural (i.e. without the well-known “translation flavour”) national 
versions. Trained professional translators, provided with specific user guides and 
translation guidelines, would definitely be the best people to perform the initial 
translation(s). It should be noted that this recommendation was made clear by the ISC; 
though, as is also the case in PISA, some participants did not follow it. 
1.4.2. Adaptations 
Globally, based on the survey responses, structural and non-structural adaptations did not 
cause major issues; though, in at least one case, an item had to be withdrawn because of a 
non-structural adaptation issue and, in another case, the respondent mentioned difficulties 
in identifying all adaptations prior to translation as was required. In all cases, people in 
charge of identifying and requesting these adaptations were people with experience in 
education and often with experience in international education assessment.

Adaptation identification and back translation 
The review of the MS NAFs of the surveyed participants shows that there were actually 
quite a number of cases where additional adaptations were made at the time of translation. 
While the NC(s) did not always identify these deviations as adaptations, they were correctly 
identified by verifiers. In these cases, some countries documented the adaptation(s) in the 
dedicated parts of the NAF but, as the adaptation phase was over, they remained 
non-validated by the ISC in the NAF. 
While it is well known that back translation is not the best means of assessing translation 
equivalence and localisation quality (Dept, Ferrari and Halleux, 2017[4]), which is 
illustrated by the dropped item issue mentioned above, it did not seem to be problematic 
for most countries and is likely to be the most efficient way to negotiate structural and 
non-structural adaptations with the ISC. However, it is challenging for countries to identify 
all adaptations prior to translation and for verifiers to review and assess the national 
adaptations in the target language, as this requires a sound and up-to-date knowledge of the 
national educational system. 
Improving the adaptation process would require either for the ISC to have a team of experts 
fluent in all testing languages, or for the verifiers to be either both experienced translators 
and specialised in the current national education system, or to work in pairs (one 
professional translator focusing on the language, and one education expert focusing on 
adaptations and specific educational concepts back translated into English). Given the 
additional resources that such improvements would require, the TALIS 2018 adaptation 
process sounds efficient and cost-effective, though participants should be allowed to 
change or add adaptations in light of translation and review outcomes (as this has happened 
to some extent anyway), which would require an additional review from the questionnaire 
team and/or a translation adaptation referee. 
As shown in the survey responses, there were mixed feelings about the documentation and 
negotiation of these adaptations. It was a repetitive process that some respondents found 
cumbersome and endless and this was partly due to the eAssessment system drop-out. As 
a result, more attention should be paid, from the very beginning of the project design, to 
identifying and using identical tools for the FT and the MS, so that countries would not 
need to document the same adaptations again. Having an adaptation documentation tool or 
system that would follow a national version from one cycle to another would greatly help 
countries to focus on what is new or on what changed compared to the previous TALIS 
cycle. Some respondents expressed their expectations in this regard with the use of the new 
IEA StudyExpert programme in TALIS 2024.

Translation procedures 
Regarding the actual national translation procedures, responses to the survey show that 
countries identified by the ISC as having better translation quality hired skilled and 
experience professional translators (as recommended in the TALIS 2018 NPM Manual, the 
Guidelines for Translation, and the SOP3 document) and had a multiple-step review 
process, either using a team approach or using successive reviews, which goes beyond the 
translation + review + NPM adjudication process recommended by the consortium. Others 
followed the first two steps of the recommended procedure (translation and review) while 
adjudication by the NPM did not seem to be used much. 
Double translation, which is used in quite a number of OECD studies, could help with 
increasing the translation quality, both in terms of fluency and education system 
appropriateness, mostly if the first translation was performed by a professional translator 
and the second translation by a national education specialist or a certified translator 
specialised in the education field.

also simplifies its management from the NC point of view, though it would require 
additional resources for the second translation.

about some national versions in language(s) used in other countries, encouraging those 
countries sharing a same testing language to work together could help improve translation 
quality. This process, sometimes referred to as “harmonisation”, can be achieved in various 
ways: countries can translate separately and share their translations then meet to discuss 
them, or one country may produce the fully verified translated version, then share it as a 
reference for other countries, etc. 
1.4.4. Verification and post-verification 
Verification 
The instructions and training provided to verifiers were impressive. For the FT, they were 
particularly complex due to the use of the eAssessment system, but documentation of FT 
verification outcomes could not be reviewed as part of this research and development 
activity. One respondent to the survey mentioned that this documentation was not available 
at the country level either. 
For the MS, the NAFs systematically included verifier comments for severity Codes 1, 1? 
and 4 but not for severity Codes 2 and 3, which were only included in the Word file (i.e. 
the national version of the questionnaire) if no change was documented by the NC in the 
NAF. This means that a number of corrections considered as minor, or some verifier 
suggestions, were only documented in the Word file and, therefore, the NAFs did not 
provide a clear view on the extent of the verifier corrections. A national version that would 
require a lot of Code 2 and Code 3 interventions may reflect an issue with the overall 
translation quality. 
It may also happen that a Code 2 or Code 3 correction or suggestion would require some 
follow-up, as clearly illustrated in the issue described in the Verification and post-
verification section above. Complete documentation within the monitoring tool (Dept, 
Ferrari and Wäyrynen, 2010[6]) would allow for a better evaluation of the translation quality 
and a monitoring/documentation of all translation issues. 
In some NAFs, there were a few cases where the NC had requested the verifier advice and 
possible recommendation. These requests remained unanswered by the verifier, which is 
quite unusual, and may be due to the fact that these requests were actually documented

after verification was completed. Given that there was no systematic review of the country 
feedback, the NC had to come up with a solution on its own. The inclusion of a translation 
referee review may help in solving such issues. 
For trend items, the verifiers were often put in a situation where they themselves had to 
assess whether the suggested changes were acceptable or not. While verifiers can clearly 
identify whether the change is an improvement or if it corrects an outright error, they should 
not be in charge of approving trend changes as they may not be aware of all the 
psychometric implications the changes may have. 
In addition, from the reviewed NAFs, it is clear that verifiers did not all apply the same 
rules for trend change approvals or rejections. For example, some agreed on improvements 
and better wording changes, while others stuck to the rule that “only outright errors can be 
corrected”. As a result, some countries were allowed to improve their trend versions while 
others were not. 
Post-verification 
Verification review and feedback did not seem to be an issue for the majority of the 
respondents, though some expressed concerns about not being allowed to improve trends 
or about unfamiliarity with the current national educational system, translation that was too 
literal or inappropriate wording. Most of them also considered that having the opportunity 
to discuss some verifier corrections, comments or suggestions with a third party would be 
useful. As TALIS 2024 will include a translation referee, it is expected that such 
discussions will be made possible at least for the “Code1” and “Code 1?” corrections. 
The intervention of a translation referee would also possibly allow for a systematic review 
of all or some of the disagreements between the verifier and the NC (such as in case of 
translation that is too literal or inappropriateness), or even of all verifier interventions (as 
done in PISA). It is also expected that the translation referee review would reduce the 
number of dropped items, as would have been the case for the two dropped items mentioned 
in the survey responses. 
Regarding reported inconsistencies missed by the verifiers, as explained before, they are 
difficult to avoid when performing translation and verification in Word. Using a computer 
assisted tool (CAT), such as OmegaT, which is used in PISA and PIAAC, would help to 
reduce such issues because CAT tools include translation memories. 
All surveyed NCs duly documented their responses to the verifier comments about “Code1” 
and “Code1?” corrections and suggestions in the NAFs, but to different extents. In the case 
of disagreement or rejections, some provided long explanations and rationales and provided 
the final wording, while some just expressed their disagreement. 
When comparing the NCs’ responses to the verifiers’ comments with the IQO notes, there 
were also a number of discrepancies. For example, there were cases where the participants 
noted they agreed with the verifier’s correction but the IQO noted that the correction was 
not accepted/implemented. This again points out the need for the systematic review of, at 
least, the “Code1” and “Code1?” verifiers’ and countries’ comments. 
1.4.5. Trends 
Trends preparation and verification require particular attention. Some respondents 
mentioned in the survey activity questionnaires and in the NAFs and, to a lesser extent, in 
this activity survey, that changes in trends that were agreed for the FT were changed back 
to the TALIS 2013 version by the verifiers (as they were instructed to do) or that it was 
difficult to convince verifiers of the need to review a trend item. As explained earlier, 

36  EDU/WKP(2023)5

For Official Use 
verifiers on their own may not be the best placed to approve or reject changes in trends, 
whether for the FT or the MS. 
In addition, having changes in trends agreed by the ISC without any verification for the FT 
caused a lot of confusion among the different players. It is important to note that the 
documentation about these agreed changes in trends was within the eAssessment system, 
which could not be used for the MS. Therefore, the absence of FT documentation increased 
the difficulty of the verifiers’ task. 
To avoid such issues, national versions of unchanged trend items (referred to as “strict 
trends items” in PIAAC) could be locked, which would mean that NCs would not be able 
to change the trend wording themselves. Instead, they would need to document and request 
changes in describing the issue with the initial wording and providing rationales. 
The contractor (with the help of the verifiers, as needed) would then review the change 
requests and adjudicate which are to be implemented by the verifier. In PISA, for example, 
this adjudication of change requests is performed by the translation referee and the verifiers 
in three steps: (i) the translation referee review of change requests, (ii) implementation of 
agreed changes and/or advice from the verifier, (iii) final ruling on the change requests by 
the referee. 
Alternatively, or in case of disagreement between the contractor and the participants, the 
trend version and the reviewed trend version could be tested in the FT. The computer-based 
format should quite easily allow for such testing. Results from the FT would then inform 
the decision about the trend review. 
Trend items that are revised by the contractor would then need specific translation 
guidelines, specifying the extent of the revisions that countries would be allowed to 
implement. Again, these items could be locked and countries could be asked to document 
the reviewed translation for implementation by the verifiers. 
However, while locking trends would help with the management and control of national 
revisions, it would still not meet the needs of some NCs to improve trend wording. 
1.5. Translatability assessment (TA) in TALIS 2024 
1.5.1. Structure and organisation 
The main aim of a translatability assessment is to assess whether the English wording is 
easily translatable in other languages or if difficulties might be encountered. In case of 
difficulties, recommendations about changes in the English version or about guidelines to 
be provided to TALIS translators and reviewers are made to questionnaire item developers. 
Improving the English wording for translatability into different language groups and 
providing item-by-item guidelines to translators and reviewers is expected to increase the 
overall translation quality of national versions, their equivalence to the source version and, 
therefore, their comparability. 
One of the main innovations in the adaptation, translation and verification processes and 
procedures for TALIS 2024 was the introduction of a translatability assessment (TA). As 
part of this feature, for the teacher questionnaire, cApStAn hired three linguists 
representing the following language groups: Germanic (German), Japonic (Japanese) and 
Sino-Tibetan (Chinese). The TA of the principal questionnaire was performed by another 
set of linguists from three other languages groups: Romance (French), Semitic (Hebrew) 
and Slavic (Polish). Finally, the staff and the U3 (staff teaching children younger than 3 
years) questionnaires in the TALIS Starting Strong survey were reviewed by a Chinese, a 
German and a Japanese linguist, and the Starting Strong lead questionnaire was assessed

for translatability by a German, a Hebrew and a Polish linguist. The Teacher Knowledge 
Survey test booklets and questionnaire were assessed for translatability by a French, a 
Hebrew and a Polish linguist. 
Linguists were first asked to produce a draft translation of the questionnaire and then to 
provide feedback using 14 translatability assessment categories (see Translatability

for an addition to the glossary. 
Once the three linguists were done with their task, a senior staff member from cApStAn

included additional comments and possible solutions.

assessment report, (ii) translatability categories and (iii) general guidelines, while the 
remaining worksheets included the translatability report with the linguists’ feedback 
reviewed and consolidated by cApStAn senior staff, and the actual translatability feedback 
per linguist/language. 
Item developers were asked to address comments and queries within the consolidated 
reports (at least when they were marked as “Requires follow-up” by cApStAn in column H) 
and also to review the suggested general guidelines for translators and reviewers. Individual 
linguists’ reports were provided for transparency reasons only and were locked.

was performed, the linguist and cApStAn’s senior consultant feedback and suggestions, 
and an indication on whether the issue required follow-up from questionnaire authors.

Before looking closer into the TA outcomes, it is worth considering the linguists’ selection. 
Having representativity of three different language groups for each review makes a lot of 
sense as it raises specific language-group issues that might not otherwise be detected if the 
material was only reviewed by different linguists from the same or a similar language

representativity of the non-Indo-European languages used in the assessment as these 
languages have semantic, grammatical and syntactic specificities very different from 
Indo-European languages. Mandarin Chinese, for example, is from the Sino-Tibetan 
language group; Hebrew, a Semitic language from the Afroasiatic language family; and 
Japanese is considered as quite a unique language. 
However, cultural differences and language representativity within countries participating 
in TALIS 2024 could have been better targeted. For example, given that, after English, 
Spanish is the most common language used, it is surprising that no Spanish linguist was 
involved in the review. Even though it is not the main aim for the translatability assessment 
to point out potential issues for a specific language, it could have been an additional insight 
on what challenges the five Spanish-testing countries could face. Similar is the group of 
Semitic languages, with Arabic being used by four countries, while Hebrew by one only. 
At the cultural level, while Japan and China undoubtably present significant cultural 
differences, they also have a set of similarities. For the review of the teacher questionnaire, 
it might have been better to replace the Chinese or Japanese linguist with a linguist from a 
country with a very different cultural and/or educational approach, such as Türkiye or 
Uzbekistan (Altaic language group). On their website (cApStAn, 2022[7]), cApStAn defines 
the translatability assessment as:

1.5.2. Item-by-item guidelines 
Numerous item-by-item guidelines were suggested to help participants with translation. 
Given that the general guidelines provided in the Survey Operational Procedures 3 (SOP3) 
manual were very general, adding item-by-item guidelines is a great improvement and also 
meets the expectations expressed by countries in the TALIS 2018 surveys and reports. 
This review indicated that four main types of item-by-item guidelines were identified: 
• 
translation guidelines providing either an alternative wording or the meaning of the 
wording  
• 
adaptation guidelines when it is expected that wording adaptation would be needed 
to fit the national context 
• 
guidelines drawing attention to terms (qualifiers, concepts, etc.) not to be omitted 
or that can be omitted 
• 
references to either the general guidelines or to glossary entries. 
Translation guidelines 
Translation guidelines were of various natures and often very useful. For example, when a 
single English term included several meanings and could, therefore, be translated 
differently to the intended meaning, then there was a need to clarify what was meant. For 
example, the term “empower” is used several times in the teacher questionnaire with subtle 
differences in meaning. In this specific case though, the best would have been for the 
English source to use different terms with the same meaning in order to increase 
equivalence between national versions, but this was not often done. 
In other cases, they also helped with the translation of forms that do not exist in the target 
languages (e.g. passive voice), or by providing an alternative wording for particularly 
complex syntax used in the source. For example, the wording “On average, how often is 
each individual teacher formally appraised in this school by the following people?” would 
have the following translation note: “If passive voice is not natural or does not exist in your 
language, it can be rendered as ‘On average, how often do the following people perform a 
formal evaluation/appraisal of each individual teacher?’”. 
Most interestingly, quite a number of guidelines were suggested to help the translator with 
the translation of expressions specifically related to education, and that were complex 
and/or difficult to translate for TALIS 2018. In these cases, questionnaire authors were 
asked to either check or complete the guidelines, such as: “‘professional learning 
community’ means…” or “Question authors to confirm the suggested note: Translate 

42  EDU/WKP(2023)5 
TRANSLATION AND ADAPTATION PROCESSES IN TALIS 2018 
For Official Use 
‘professional growth and development activities’ in the sense of activities aimed at the 
professional growth and development.” Again, it was not possible for us to check whether 
these guidelines had been reviewed or completed and included as translation/adaptation 
notes. 
Suggested translation guidelines for trend wording applied to new participants and included 
a note asking participants in the previous TALIS cycle to use their 2018 version. However, 
it is assumed that, if a translator or reviewer sees that the guideline is not met in their trend 
version, they will be allowed to request a change in trends. 
For example, when the guideline states “‘Nurse’ here refers to a school nurse specifically. 
This may be translated as ‘school nurse’ if ‘nurse’ alone refers to nurses in general in your 
language.” and a country that participated in TALIS 2018 realises they should have used 
‘school nurse’, they would be allowed to correct the issue in order to meet the guideline.  
Adaptation guidelines 
Adaptation guidelines are particularly useful in identifying wording that is likely to require 
adaptation to the national context or education system (and such wording would be even 
better identified if, as explained previously, linguists provided a set of clear different 
cultural or educational contexts). In very few cases, the English was changed by placing 
the wording or part of the wording between angle brackets < >, which was then highlighted 
in yellow to show that adaptation was compulsory. But, in most cases, the English stayed 
unchanged and the guidelines then drew the participant’s attention to the possible need for 
adaptations. 
For example, the item reading “External individuals or bodies (e.g. inspectors, municipality 
representatives, districts/jurisdictions office personnel, or other persons from outside the 
school)” should have an adaptation guideline reading: “Adapt examples in brackets to local 
context”.  
What is not clear yet is whether such adaptations would need to be negotiated at the 
structural and non-structural adaptation phase or at the translation and review phase. If such 
adaptations should be requested prior to translation, then, given that linguists drew 
questionnaire authors attention to the issue, the best would have been to mark the text as a 
forced adaptation: “External individuals or bodies (e.g. <inspectors, municipality 
representatives, districts/jurisdictions office personnel>, or other persons from outside the 
school)”. However, this was not often done.  
Changes in the English source version for the FT were only sparingly implemented to better 
mark where adaptations would be needed. 
Guidelines aimed at drawing the attention of the translator or reviewer to the importance 
of wording not to be omitted may seem unnecessary for professional translators; however, 
they are essential for making sure the question will be answered correctly. For example, 
when responding to a question asking how concerned the principal is about climate change, 
translating the item “The world’s climate has been changing about equally due to natural 
processes and human activity” and omitting “about equally” would likely lead to response 
biases. 
In preparing the guidelines allowing some terms to be omitted, the cApStAn senior 
consultant always sought the advice of the questionnaire authors. It is assumed only 
guidelines approved by authors were kept.

transliterated from another language.

and/or from the cApStAn senior consultant, so it is difficult to understand why the English 
wording remained unchanged.

As a result, English wording of these three items remained unchanged, and item j) which 
read “Assessments do not include climate change” was changed into “Assessments do not 
include it”, which complexifies rather than clarifies the issue. Unless authors advised

cApStAn about the meaning of “it” in each item and a clear guideline was added, the exact 
meaning of the items is left to the translator’s or reviewer’s understanding. 
In another case, the three linguists expressed concerns about the acronyms used in “Work 
with experts (e.g. scientists, staff at NGOs or CBOs) to improve teaching about climate 
change”. Their comments read: (i) “The meaning of CBO cannot be identified. Please give 
its full form.”, (ii) “CBO - this term is not well known yet in Japan.”, and (iii) “The acronym 
‘CBO’ is not commonly known in Germany. An explanation would be helpful”. Therefore, 
the cApStAn consultant suggested that the English use the full term but the suggestion was 
not followed and, thus, it is likely that a guideline was used instead. Given the unanimity 
of linguists’ concerns, it seems that it would have been better for the English to be changed, 
especially as acronyms are hardly used in some languages. 
There were also a couple of cases where linguists asked about the exact meaning of phrasal 
verbs. Phrasal verbs are known for creating translation issues even when translated by 
professional translators. It is even more complex if the phrasal verb is included in a passive 
sentence such as “Students can be counted on to do their work”. Instead of providing a 
guideline explaining the meaning of the phrasal verb, it would be highly recommended for 
the English to use a verb that could be directly and equivalently translated into most 
languages. Indeed, if there are several translations possible for a phrasal verb, it is likely 
national versions will differ to various extents. 
This is even more obvious with the following wording: “Students at this school get put 
down because of their ethnicity.” One linguist noted “‘get put down’ can be rendered as 
‘get discriminated’ [sic] ”, while the guideline suggested by the cApStAn consultant read 
“put down is used in the sense of ‘degrade, belittle, criticize’”. General understanding is 
that “to put somebody down” means “to humiliate someone”. This means that, if the 
English remains unchanged, national versions may get worded in terms of degradation or 
criticism or humiliation, which is likely to affect data comparability. 
Finally, there were basic and easy-to-solve translation issues that were not consistently 
taken into consideration. For example, it is well known that using “the following” without 
explaining what “the following” refers to will not work in many languages, e.g. “How 
strongly do you agree or disagree that the following present barriers to your participation 
in professional learning?”. A rephrasing such as “How strongly do you agree or disagree 
that the following aspects present barriers to your participation in professional learning?” 
would be easy to implement and would ensure consistent and equivalent translation across 
languages. Questionnaire developers corrected some but not all occurrences, even in new 
questions. 
1.5.4. Pros and cons of the two approaches 
Guidelines 
The different types of guidelines described above will undoubtably alleviate some 
translation and adaptation issues. They will be useful not only at the adaptation and 
translation stages but also during review and verification, assuming that, as in other OECD 
studies, the verifier will be asked to check if guidelines were met. 
This process will not only allow for better equivalence between the source and the national 
version, but also across versions, notably by ensuring the verifier’s consistency in remarks 
linked to wording that is the subject of guidelines. 
However, this review indicated that a number of the guidelines did not seem necessary for 
professional translators, e.g. “Translate ‘significant’ in the sense of ‘important’ in ‘who has 
a significant responsibility for the following tasks?’” or “If needed ‘This questionnaire 

46  EDU/WKP(2023)5 
TRANSLATION AND ADAPTATION PROCESSES IN TALIS 2018 
For Official Use 
should take approximately 45 to 60 minutes to complete.’ can be rephrased as ‘you might 
need approximately 45 to 60 minutes to complete the questionnaire’”. Or else for the 
wording “The world’s climate has not been changing.” with the following translation note: 
“Note: this is a present perfect continuous tense, make sure not to use a past tense (has 
changed).” 
In her talk at the OECD-GESIS seminar on Translating and Adapting Instruments in Large 
Scale Assessments (2018), Britta Upsing presented the outcomes of her assessment of how 
translators and other people involved in preparing instruments in national languages 
perceived the translation and adaptation guidelines in international studies such as PISA 
and PIAAC (Behr, 2019[8]). She emphasised that “[d]etailed, that is, item-by-item 
guidelines might be misunderstood and prevent critical thinking. Hence, their number, 
length, and content should always be weighed against what is really needed.” (p. 4[8]) 
Therefore, having too many “obvious” guidelines could be counterproductive and the 
emphasis on important aspects could be lost, i.e. professional translators would eventually 
think they do not really need to refer to the guidelines as they are obvious, or they would 
try to stick too closely to each guideline when, in some cases, the English wording did not 
pose any issue regarding translation into their language. 
Apart from this potential drawback of having unnecessary guidelines, item-by-item 
guidelines may be seen as only beneficial for all parties involved in translation, adaptation, 
review and verification. 
English source 
While it might be understandable that questionnaire authors would be reluctant to change 
wording that is fluent and to the point for native English and English-testing participants, 
the English source version needs to consider translation and adaptation issues. The resulting 
English source version may sound less natural but will have more chance of being 
equivalently translated into the different national languages. 
Evidently, this does not mean that all suggestions or any linguist’s comments should lead 
to changes in the English wording. From experience – and as described previously – a 
drawback of the TA would be to reword the English to solve an issue pointed out in the 
report by one linguist only and, in turn, create translation issues in a number of other 
languages.  
Finding the right balance between fluency and translatability while taking into account 
feedback from other sources (an expert group, for example) is a very difficult exercise that 
might become more manageable on the developers’ side over time. 
1.6. Conclusions 
The analysis of the available information documenting adaptation and translation processes 
from TALIS 2018, together with the feedback from a selection of participating countries 
suggests that the recommended procedures may not have been sufficient to alleviate or 
reduce some issues encountered by participants during the cycle. 
This conclusion is to be taken with some nuance, nonetheless. For example, throughout the 
analysis of processes, it was noted that most countries identified by the ISC as having good 
translation quality versions of the instruments, are among the countries who hired 
professional translators for the FT as recommended in the translation guidelines. However, 
it was also found that in about half of countries approached for this exercise (which are 
most of the countries identified as having good quality translation and adaptation 
processes), the translation review actually included additional steps to those described in

the guidelines (e.g. discussion groups and expert consultation meetings, more reviews than 
suggested). 
Improving the adaptation processes and their documentation in order to reduce the demand 
on countries in the FT and the MS, or even from cycle to cycle, is necessary. The rather 
abrupt change in the systems (i.e. abandoning the eAssessment system) throughout the 
TALIS 2018 cycle likely introduced a series of significant challenges and induced 
misunderstandings across countries. It is highly recommended that a comprehensive 
monitoring tool be used that would provide full documentation about adaptations, all types 
of verifier interventions, post-verification, etc., would be easily searchable and would make 
information fully accessible to countries. This monitoring tool would be essential, 
especially at the early stages of the process, as one of the conclusions of this exercise is 
that overall translation quality of new instruments seems to be primarily determined by the 
FT adaptation, translation and verification processes. The use of the new IEA ExpertStudy 
system for TALIS 2024 may allow for this improvement. 
Trends management requires improvements as well. Our analyses of the NAFs showed 
issues with the review of trends, for the most part based in a conflict between the NCs’ 
suggestions and ISC’s requests. Nonetheless, this is particularly challenging, as trends 
management must meet the right balance between keeping trends back to previous cycles 
and providing the best possible question wording to teachers and principals in current 
cycles (as well as keeping questions accurate and relevant in the current state of the system). 
Overall assessments of these processes allow for identification of issues, or potential issues 
that may arise during adaptation and translation. Issues with adaptation and translation 
processes can render an item inaccurate and, therefore, unusable and, often, this sort of 
problem is identified quite late in the process. As mentioned in this paper, guidelines about 
known translation issues to be followed when developing the new questions and items 
should be made available to authors of questionnaires. Preventing translation problems at 
the earliest possible stage will likely lead to the development of better items, and a more 
effective and cost-efficient item development process. 
One clear advantage of the TALIS processes identified in this paper is its pilot study. It was 
concluded that it would be important to increase efforts to encourage more countries to 
participate to the pilot in order to identify cultural, translation and appropriateness issues 
before the source questionnaire is finalised for the FT. This was achieved in TALIS 2024, 
engaging all participating countries in the pilot study. 
Across this document, several approaches and strategies for developing unambiguous 
translatable source instruments have been discussed. While further work would be 
necessary to unpack the many specificities of those strategies, at this stage they can be seen 
as pointers for potential improvements to TALIS adaptation and translation processes. 
Among them, the particular relevance of four of them should be highlighted: the use of a 
team approach, a double translation and reconciliation procedure, making a second source 
version available and using a translatability assessment. 
Given the specificities of the TALIS questionnaires, using a team approach might be the 
most efficient approach, though it may be difficult to implement given the tight timelines, 
the additional staffing and financial resources and management it would require, including 
pre-testing/piloting the instruments. 
A double translation and reconciliation procedure – where the translation team would 
include a professional translator and an education specialist (such as a teacher) or a 
professional translator specialising in the education domain, and reconciliation would be 
performed by an expert in the education system and in educational surveys (or by a team 

48  EDU/WKP(2023)5

For Official Use 
of experts) – may prove to be as efficient in improving translation and adaptation quality 
and would be less demanding in terms of additional time and resources. 
Providing a second source version would also be likely to increase both the translatability 
of the English source version and the quality of national translations on the condition that 
it would be developed through a process including review by multiple experts (domain 
expert, international studies expert, equivalence expert, etc.) ensuring the same level of 
quality for the second source version. 
Finally, including a translatability assessment in TALIS 2024 met some of the participants’ 
concerns and the questions raised in TALIS 2018. Undoubtably, the translatability 
assessment has served to identify important translation and adaptation issues early in the 
process. A complete and finalised translatability report would bring much more 
effectiveness for further analysis and future cycles. Completed translatability reports 
should be made available, completed by the questionnaire developers and, in the case of 
TALIS 2024, returned to cApStAn (even if the authors’ feedback would eventually not 
correspond to the final version of the instruments). 
For the purpose of the analyses undertaken here, it is important to highlight three main 
conclusions regarding the TALIS 2024 TA: 
• 
While it is expected to help translators and reviewers dealing with complex 
sentences or vocabulary, derived item-by-item guidelines should be made with 
great care, since “too many guidelines may kill the guidelines”. 
• 
The English source wording of new questions and items could have been further 
reworded to meet translation and adaptation concerns expressed in the TA report 
and to allow for better equivalence between national versions. 
• 
Some additional consideration should also be given to the selection of the linguists 
to add a cultural representativity component. 
Nevertheless, this is a very welcome improvement for the TALIS adaptation and translation 
processes. The TALIS 2024 translatability assessment has allowed early identification of 
translation and adaptation issues and provided targeted guidance to participants.

DOUBLE-BARRELLED 
A question touches upon more than one issue, yet allows only for one answer. Many

POTENTIAL LAYOUT ISSUE 
The draft source layout may not work in the target language. Typical examples are table headings 
or text boxes that are tailored to a concise English expression, and may not accommodate 
cross-linguistic expansion.  
AGREEMENT ISSUE 
There is either an agreement issue within the segment (e.g. subject-verb agreement, or sequence

(e.g. no grammatical match between a question and response options). 
INCONSISTENCY 
In this segment, a different term, expression or form of address has been used versus other 
occurrences of similar content; and this inconsistency seems to be unintentional. 
POTENTIAL REGISTER ISSUE 
The segment is grammatically correct and straightforward to translate/adapt, but uses a register 
that may not be suitable for the target population (e.g. register too elevated, or too formal, or too 
informal). The issue seems to be unintentional. 
REDUNDANCY ISSUE 
This segment contains a tautology or unnecessary repetition. Removing it would not alter the

LOGICAL PROBLEM 
This segment contains a logical problem or there is a logical problem between this segment and 
another segment, and this issue seems to be unintentional. 
OTHER POTENTIAL ISSUES 
The current wording or content of this segment is likely to give rise to translation or adaptation 
problems in some languages, to the extent that functional equivalence may be difficult to achieve.
