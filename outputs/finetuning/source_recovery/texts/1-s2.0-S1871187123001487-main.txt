This paper presents a research study that evaluated the score ChatGPT would get when summarizing a reading comprehension text from the PISA international tests with a prompt that made
it simulate doing this as if it were a 15-year-old student. For this purpose, the text was camouflaged among 30 other summaries made by real 15-year-old students and was evaluated by 30
Spanish language teachers with different profiles in terms of age, professional experience, and
gender who were unaware that one of the texts was made by artificial intelligence (AI). The
evaluation of the summary, for which a homogeneous rubric is used, is based on two fundamental
criteria: content and style. For the data analysis descriptive and inferential statistical techniques
were used. The results show that the ChatGPT summary obtained the best marks in terms of
content and style, with its respective marks being 3 and 2.5 points higher than those of the
students. Therefore, we can deduce that the style and content of the ChatGPT summary greatly
exceeded those presented by the students. These results are independent of the ages, levels of
professional experience, and genders of the teachers who corrected the summary. The integration
of AI tools such as ChatGPT must be based on solid methodological proposals that integrate their
use from a creative and critical perspective that allows learning with the support of these tools
and not using them as substitutes for the development of basic student competencies.

1. Introduction
The emergence of tools such as ChatGPT based on the use of large language models has led to a rethinking of the activities and work
that students can perform inside and outside the classroom and is generating great debate among the educational community on
ethical issues concerning plagiarism and cheating and the limitation of certain student competencies such as critical thinking and
communicative and writing skills, among others (Choi et al., 2023; Cotton et al., 2023). Likewise, there is also a debate on the didactic
possibilities and the implications in evaluating the use of these generative artificial intelligences (AIs) (Khosravi et al., 2022; News,
2023). To date, studies and experiences have shown that ChatGPT is performing very well in natural language-related tasks such as
translation, question answering, summarizing, writing coherent essays, and computer programming, among others. In this sense,
numerous studies have been published on the functionality of ChatGPT to solve different summary and reflection tasks, as well as for
passing exams or standardized tests mainly in Higher Education, but no rigorous analysis has been carried out contrasting the

2. Background
2.1. ChatGPT in education
The language model for ChatGPT, an OpenAI chatbot that debuted in November 2022, was created in 2018, making it the fastestgrowing consumer application of this decade (Hu, 2023). The model is based on a deep learning architecture known as Generative
Pre-trained Transformer (GPT), which is trained based on a vast amount of text data to produce human-like responses in natural
language conversations. In June 2018, the GPT-1 initial release, also known as GPT-1, was made available for download (Digital,
2023). It had 117 million unique parameters. In June 2019, OpenAI announced the availability of GPT-2, an even more complex
language model with 1.5 billion parameters. The model attracted a lot of attention because it could produce excellent prose that was
almost indistinguishable from human writing. Furthermore, GPT-2 was the first model to raise issues concerning the potential misuse
of AI-generated content. The release of GPT-3, a more sophisticated version of GPT-2 with 175 billion parameters, was announced by
OpenAI in 2020. It is one of the most sophisticated AI language models that is currently available, being capable of doing a variety of
activities utilizing natural language, including authoring essays, poetry, and inquiries. The creation of ChatGPT is essential because it
has the capacity to fundamentally alter how people interact with machines, thus opening up new possibilities in industries such as
education, healthcare, finance, and customer service. The new GPT-4 version is currently more collaborative and innovative than ever.
When working with users on creative and technical writing activities like songwriting, screenwriting, or figuring out a user’s writing
style, it can generate, edit, and iterate with them.
Since the introduction of ChatGPT in November 2022, there has been a tsunami in the development of research and methodological
proposals for the integration of generative AI in society from different fields, as well as in the teaching–learning processes. Likewise,
alerts and criticisms have arisen to promote an ethical and responsible use of these types of generative AI since, among other things,
they allow the supplanting of human competences and skills while fostering plagiarism and passing their products off as self-generated
processes without sufficient credibility or adequate citation of their sources. These types of generative intelligences, especially those
developed by trained models such as ChatGPT, allow the generation of text from questions or the establishment of elaborative interventions in relation to text, such as summaries, outlines, or presentations from provided texts that allow the simulation and even
improvement of the human capacity to perform these tasks (Lund et al., 2023; Vrontis et al., 2022). In this way, the tool may restrict a
range of student skills and competencies, including critical thinking, the capacity for complex problem-solving, and professional
communication (Ali, 2023; Lim et al., 2023). Conversely, some contend that ChatGPT facilitates by offering individualized instruction
that caters to the needs of specific students (Benuyenah, 2023; Cano et al., 2023).
The scientific literature is beginning to analyze the influence of chatbot-assisted learning systems in different teaching models,
educational stages, and types of subjects since nowadays, a myriad of tools based on AI are emerging to generate text, images, and
digital products such as presentations, translations, and videos automatically. In this sense, different authors believe that generative AI
can provide higher degrees of personalization, instant feedback, and improved learning outcomes (Gupta & Chen, 2022). Nevertheless,
there is no doubt that its current use concerns more the generation of content, preventing the student from putting into practice
competences in searching, treating, and processing information, as well as related cognitive processes such as the ability to summarize,
detect main and secondary ideas, and organize their own argumentation based on different types of sources through a critical analysis
of them.
Therefore, the educational community, and especially teachers, are faced with diatribes of whether to integrate or ban generative
AIs in teaching–learning processes because of problems regarding cheating, dishonesty, being misled, and manipulation (Dwivedi
et al., 2023; Gregorcic & Pendrill, 2023; Lim et al., 2023; Tlili et al., 2023). To this end, a regulatory framework must first be developed
to determine the scope of AI and its application in educational processes. Second, methodological and evaluative models substantially
different from those currently in use are required, as well as proposals for didactic integration and the assessment of generative AIs so
that their use leads to the performance of competencies and skills that will improve student learning. Third, it is necessary to establish
protocols for the sustainable, ethical, and respectful use of these tools with respect to copyright and the sources used.
Different studies have been published in which ChatGPT obtained better or similar results compared to those obtained by students
in different contexts and official exams. In this regard, Christian Terwiesch, a professor at the Mack Institute for Innovation Management at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania, showed that an AI chatbot tool recently passed law exams in four
courses at the University of Minnesota and another exam at the University of Pennsylvania’s Wharton School of Business. After
completing 95 multiple choice questions and 12 essay questions, the bot performed on average at the level of a C+ student, achieving a
low but passing grade in all four courses (Terwiesch, 2023).
Likewise, with respect to official examinations, ChatGPT is obtaining significant results in different tests (Varanasi, 2023). For
example, in “The Uniform Bar Examination,” designed to test knowledge and skills that every lawyer should be able to demonstrate
prior to becoming licensed to practice law, the version GPT-4 scored in the 90th percentile with a score of 298 out of 400 (Koetsier,
2023). In the “SAT,” an exam which concerns school subjects covered in school such as reading, math, and writing and language,

2.2. ChatGPT and text generation
Language models like ChatGPT and Bing AI can generate human-like text and analyze natural language text (Else, 2023). The use of
language models in education by students through generative AIs such as ChatGPT is generating a wide social debate among teachers
at different educational stages (Reich, 2022). Among the main concerns are the reliability of the generated material (Waters, 2023),
problems related to the evaluation of students to avoid cheating (Weale, 2023), and the total reliance students are placing on the
answers generated by these AIs (Heyward, 2022). The use of these AI-based language models is increasingly accessible through search
engines such as Bing or Google, among others, and through integration in apps and webpage extensions. One of the main functionalities of these generative AIs, such as ChatGPT, is that through prompts, people can simulate different contexts and situations and
delimit the complexity and character of the texts depending on the situation and the objective of the work to be done. The scientific
literature also highlights that not all students perceive this type of practice as plagiarism (Dawson et al., 2020; Heyward, 2022), and
that when it is perceived as such, students begin to paraphrase and hide the fact that the generated text has been created by an AI by
using other free AI tools that are becoming more and more widespread such as https://quillbot.com/, among others. Also, many of the
tools that are emerging to detect plagiarism generated by text-generating AIs are proving to be unreliable (Cotton et al., 2023; Farrokhnia et al., 2023; Fowler, 2023).
Still, the competence of a trained adult may be greater than that generated by an intelligence such as ChatGPT in certain contexts of
written text production (novels, scientific articles, etc.) (Huh, 2023; Moser et al., 2022); nevertheless, such intelligences could exceed
the competence of pre-university and university students in the development of certain competencies associated with both information
gathering and the filtering and production of written texts. Therefore, it is still not very clear how to integrate didactically and
methodologically generative AIs to favor greater learning instead of inhibiting the development of certain transversal and essential
competences such as the interpretation and production of texts from a critical and contrasted individual perspective through the
analysis of different sources (Heyward, 2022). There is no doubt that new didactic and methodological systems must be introduced in
teaching that allow the use of generative AI to be competently integrated for the development and enhancement of these competencies
(van Dis et al., 2023).
Furthermore, it should be taken into account that these tools also have a series of potentialities and functionalities that can
represent substantial improvements, for example in the treatment of functional diversity for students who present difficulties due to
dyslexia or disorders associated with language by means of inestimable help in generating texts and e-mails and correcting those
orthographic and morphosyntactic aspects that are more difficult to handle in the construction and design of these messages (Mitchell,
2022).
3. Method
The main objective of this study is to analyze whether there are significant differences in the evaluation of a summary generated by
ChatGPT that was camouflaged among those of a standard class of 15-year-old students and corrected by 30 Spanish language teachers
of different genders, ages, and levels of professional experience.
Thus, this study is based on the following research hypothesis: “The score obtained by ChatGPT when performing summaries is
much higher than that obtained by the best pre-university students.”
The correction of open tests such as summaries has several variables that could condition the score assigned to them. Three of these
variables are related to sociodemographic variables associated with the correctors who perform the evaluation. The scientific literature
has identified three fundamental variables that can affect the correction depending on the person who performs it, they are called
“proximal” variables: age, sex and years of experience (Lopes Toffoli et al., 2015; Mahmood et al., 2017).
Based on these sociodemographic variables of the teachers, two research questions are established:
1.- Are there significant differences related to the age, sex and years of experience of the teachers when evaluating a summary
generated by ChatGPT?
2.- Are there significant differences between the grade obtained by ChatGPT and that of real students?
Taking into consideration the results of the first two research questions, a third one is formulated:
1.- What didactic and methodological implications can influence creativity and critical thinking when using ChatGPT in preuniversity education?
3.1. Research design
This study presents an investigation that evaluated the grade that a summary made by ChatGPT of a reading comprehension text

given in the PISA international tests obtained. For this purpose, the text was camouflaged among 30 other summaries made by real 15year-old Spanish students from several educational centers in Spain that were then evaluated by 30 Spanish language teachers of
different ages, levels of professional experience, and genders who were unaware that one of the texts was made by an AI.
The design of the investigation was developed in four phases: (1) selection of the student sample and their writing of the summaries;
(2) ChatGPT’s generation of the summary and conversion of the resulting digital text to a handwritten text by a 15-year-old student not
participating in the study; (3) selection of the sample of teachers with different socio-professional profiles; and (4) the final correction
of the test according to a rubric.
During the first phase, 30 Spanish students aged 14–16 (16 females, 14 males) at five secondary school centers were recruited. The
sample was randomly selected, with the teachers requested to conduct the summary test in class. Prior consent was sought from the
students and their families, and the tests were taken by students whose number on the class list ranged from 5 to 20. Each test was
coded according to the day of the test and the sex and age of the participant in order to generate an anonymous identity number (e.g.,
20/02/M/15).
In the second phase, 51 Spanish language teachers were selected from five regions across Spain (Madrid, Castilla-La Mancha,
Andalucía, Extremadura, and Asturias). The teachers were selected at random. Of the 51 corrections returned, 21 were rejected (5
because the teacher neglected to give informed consent for participation in the study and a further 16 in order to generate a homogeneous sample that reflected the study variables of sex, age, and years of teaching experience). The final sample consisted of 30
teachers (16 women, 14 men). When the corrected texts were returned, the teachers were asked for explicit consent for participation in
the study and were informed of the analysis process (anonymous comparison of their scoring and correction of the text against other
teachers’, and the computation of the three variables of sex, age, and years of teaching experience).
For the third phase, the students were given a reading comprehension test from PISA 2009 (OECD, 2015). The text was 385 words
long, and there was also an explanatory introduction. The students had to summarize this text in no more than 125 words. The use in
this research of an international written text that is part of the PISA assessment process could facilitate the replication of the study in
different socio-educational contexts, meaning that other researchers could compare possible differences in other countries related to
different students and teachers’ profiles.
Finally, to ensure that the evaluation was homogeneous, the teachers were provided with a rubric for the assessment of the
summaries according to two dimensions, “content” and “style,” with 5 indicators for each dimension (Table 1).

3.2. Data analysis
For the data analysis we used descriptive and inferential statistical tools. The descriptive statistical analysis was developed to
determine the demographic characteristics of the sample and calculate the differences between ChatGPT and the students’ style scores
given by each teacher. The inferential statistical tools used included the Student’s t-test and the one-factor ANOVA. The Student’s t-test
is a method that tells us how significant the differences can be between different groups, i.e., between male and female in this case. The
one-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) is a generalization of the two-sample t-test. The two-sample t-test is used to decide whether
two groups (levels) of a factor have the same mean. One-way analysis of variance generalizes this to levels where k, the number of
levels, is greater than or equal to 2. We also used the Shapiro–Wilk test, which is a test of normality that assesses whether a sample is
likely to originate from a normal distribution, to examine the normality of the data. Previously, we also used Levene’s test for the

fundamental skills such as the elaboration of content and summaries from reliable sources and from a critical and contrasting
perspective in a context in which AI can generate better results than the students themselves. As pointed out by different authors,
relying solely on language models for factual and relevant information adversely impacts student learning (Gonzalez et al., 2022;
Tapalova & Zhiyenbayeva, 2020). It is essential for the data to be contrasted and the information’s contents to be critically analyzed. If
not, the growth of the teaching–learning processes as well as the performance of the individual in both his or her personal and professional life will be significantly constrained (Ryan & Aasetre, 2021; Zapalska et al., 2018).
In this sense, generative AIs can help provide data at a higher speed than possible for any human (Luckin & Cukurova, 2019), but
the ability and competence to articulate one’s own discourse cannot be replaced by an AI as it would limit human capabilities in regard
to essential aspects of thinking and becoming a person (Techatassanasoontorn et al., 2023). When given instructions to elaborate texts
and cite the authors to whom it has made reference, ChatGPT frequently invents the references, making a connection between the text
generated and the authors cited impossible. This is one of the main issues that ChatGPT has demonstrated up to this point (Cooper,
2023; Gregorcic & Pendrill, 2023).
Another aspect that greatly limits its application in learning is the lack of integration of irony, sarcasm, as well as double meanings,
imagination, and creativity in the interpretation of responses (Dwivedi et al., 2023). Therefore, we must treat with great caution the
answers and products generated by AI tools in certain contexts and take the proposals as drafts or schemes of work to which we can
apply techniques of critical analysis, reflection, and contrasting information (Rosé et al., 2019; Stokel-Walker, 2022). Students will not
miss the opportunity provided by these types of tools since they make it much easier for them to carry out certain tasks; the challenge of
a productive and functional use of them must be assumed, first of all, by teachers who must propose tasks based on competencies that
require a critical use of generative AIs, through which students could learn to detect errors, omissions, biases, and develop the
necessary competencies to solve these problems (Luckin & Cukurova, 2019).
In this sense, institutions are beginning to emphasize the regulation and prohibition of the use of this generative AI in education
(Cotton et al., 2023), but this is like putting gates on the sea, and we have already experienced this in other moments with the
emergence of calculators, computers, word processors, etc. The proposal should be different and not merely a ban or the development
of tools to detect plagiarism or the fraudulent use of these tools. Instead, it should focus on raising awareness regarding the use of these
tools while encouraging the critical and creative use of it that allows students to integrate them into their learning processes and the
development of their skills in an analytical and productive way for their personal academic and professional development (Eaton &
Mindzak, 2021; Kasneci et al., 2023; Mohdzaini, 2023). In this sense, a critical and analytical use of these generative AIs to determine
their functionality and applicability to different academic and professional contexts will also be a fundamental competence in the
future professional development of students (Thontirawong & Chinchanachokchai, 2021).
For this reason, it is necessary that didactic models for teaching contemplate the student’s ability to make critical use of these
generative AIs and develop critical thinking skills that allow them to delimit the veracity of the information obtained, proceed to
contrast it, and determine which aspects of the texts generated by these tools can serve as a basis for constructing their own text that
avoids biases and reflects the thinking and knowledge of the person who generates it (Penkauskienė et al., 2019; Vázquez-Cano, 2021).
The results of this study have shown how ChatGPT can generate an excellent summary. This should make us reflect on the possibility of integrating it didactically into the teaching–learning processes as a virtual learning agent with which to promote formative

develop self-regulated learning (Gregorcic & Pendrill, 2023; Hunter, 2023). In this sense, learners could work at their own pace with
the help of AI-powered language learning platforms that repeat and highlight concepts they struggle with, engage them with tasks they
excel at, cater to their interests, and take into consideration aspects like their cultural backgrounds. In this line of research, the use of
technologies for language learning has been recommended by the European Commission and the Council of Europe, as also highlighted
in the Council Recommendation for a comprehensive approach to the teaching and learning of languages (Official Journal of the
European Union, 2019).
In this sense, teachers can use these models to generate questions and prompts that encourage students to think critically about
what they are reading or writing and can also provide summaries of more complex information automatically. They can also be very
useful in the step-by-step explanation of certain processes associated with the construction of texts and thus help to improve students’
reasoning and provide them with strategies for the argumentative construction of texts (Kasneci et al., 2023; Mengxiao & Liu, 2020).
Therefore, teachers can integrate at different educational stages and teaching models (face-to-face, online, blended learning) these
types of tools with specific proposals that allow the student to self-correct and advance in the development of his/her competencies
(Cheng et al., 2021; Farrokhnia et al., 2023). From this perspective, we can use generative AIs to increase the value of teachers inside
and outside the classroom and be able to develop activities that allow for the individualization of the learning process and feedback,
which are two of the processes that produce the greatest effect in improving learning (Hattie, 2008). To make this truly effective and
possible, generative AIs such as ChatGPT need to identify those extracts of their information that have been generated without sufficient consistency and merely intuitively, as well as information that it presents without properly identifying the source from which it
has been taken.
What is beyond doubt is that it is very complicated for a pre-university teacher to distinguish whether a summary such as the one
presented in this research has been produced by an AI or by a real student (Elkins & Chun, 2020; Gao et al., 2022; Nassim, 2021). It also
does not seem reasonable that teachers should have to become police officers investigating whether students’ production is their own
or from third parties as the process would need a huge amount of time to verify that each text or parts of it have been generated by
generative AIs. Therefore, we believe that teachers should be trained to generate learning environments in which generative AIs are
integrated from a critical and analytical perspective that allows for their use not in limiting or supplanting student competencies, but in
contextualizing competencies in the technological context of each moment.
The objective, therefore, must be faced from a methodological approach and not so much from the evaluation. The performance of
tasks based on summarizing texts and information is very common in the primary and secondary education stages and allows
determining that the student is acquiring communicative, linguistic and strategic competences applied to each communicative situation and to different topics. These competencies are essential to favor the increase of students’ critical thinking and creativity. In this
sense, robust methodological approaches are needed to allow the student to reflect and contrast his/her summary (written expression)
with the possibilities provided by ChatGPT (Anderson et al., 2023). In this sense, different studies have shown positive effects when
chatbots are used to assist in the elaboration of argumentative essays (Lin & Chang, 2020; Guo et al., 2022). The didactic use of
ChatGPT can be integrated at different stages with different prompts to enhance and contrast content elements (Su et al., 2023).
Different proposals can be generated for the improvement of the summaries in different areas: (1) content: requesting the identification of the fundamental blocks of content of the text; (2) grammatical structures: requiring proposals of grammatical structures
that guide the development of the different parts of the summary; (3) lexical, requesting the proposal of synonyms and antonyms that
allow the student to enrich his/her vocabulary applied to the specific context of the subject of the summary, (4) discursive, requesting
the generation of different summaries acting from different profiles (academic, scientific, informative, etc.) to detect similarities and
differences in the lexical and syntactic structures, and (5) social interaction; for example, requesting different types of summaries to be
generated for different types of social networks, in tweet format 280 characters, an Instagram post (1000 characters) or if we need a
script for a short 1 min video on TikTok). In this way, we are encouraging the student to increase their creativity and critical thinking
when learning to create and improve their own productions.
Therefore, the greater or lesser potential for learning ChatGPT lies in the methodological and didactic proposals used inside and
outside the classroom. It is the teacher who must first establish the design of activities based on ChatGPT in a virtual tutor format (Park
et al., 2021). Through the design of initial prompts that allow improving aspects of style and content in the summaries in order to later
contrast and highlight the changes made and the reasons for them from a critical perspective that allows expanding the creativity of the
answers and not copying them.
6. Conclusions
The main contribution of this study is to provide a rigorous analysis of how ChatGPT can make a summary and get the best score
regardless of the type of teacher who performs the correction according to the three variables of age, gender and years of experience.
Generative AIs are beginning to mark a turning point in three fundamental aspects of education: ethical use, methodology, and
evaluation. The effective integration of tools such as ChatGPT in education must start from a methodological debate determining how
to use these tools in a productive, responsible, critical, and analytical way in support of learning and the academic and professional
development of both students and individuals in general. To this end, strategies and protocols are needed at the institutional level to
guide the effective integration of these tools in education. Therefore, the results show that ChatGPT can be a double-edged sword; on
the one hand, it can inhibit and limit fundamental competencies that a pre-university student should acquire, such as the ability to
understand, synthesize and elaborate information from a critical and personal perspective. On the other hand, a methodological
approach that integrates the didactic use of ChatGPT in the classroom can favor the improvement of these competencies. From our

point of view, the proposal cannot reside in the prohibition of this type of tools, but in their methodological integration, since they can
help students - by adopting the role of virtual tutor - in the improvement of essential tasks such as the elaboration of summaries, so

generative AI in education, a number of additional questions are raised. (1) How can we integrate language models to increase students’ competences in relation to critical thinking, creativity, fact-checking, etc.? (2) How can generative AI affect students’ motivation and engagement in learning? (3) What constitutes cheating when using generative AI in generating assessed work?
Nevertheless, mastering AI will probably be the most important skill of the 21st century.
7. Limitations and future research
The study has focused on the Spanish language and on students at the secondary school stage because it is a crucial time to start
using emerging technologies for the development of teaching-learning processes. The study could be replicated in other contexts with
other languages to determine whether the generation of summaries produces similar or divergent results. Furthermore, it would also be
desirable to conduct studies to test the effectiveness of ChatGPT in other areas such as translation, text analysis and text completion.
