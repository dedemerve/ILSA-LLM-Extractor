Achievement motivation is fundamental for human flourishing. While numerous adaptive motivational constructs have been proposed, they are often examined in isolation without considering their shared contextual
roots. To identify the contextual factors underlying different forms of adaptive achievement motivation, we
conducted comprehensive analyses by integrating a global student assessment dataset (n = 77,068 middle-school
students across 19 countries, Mage = 15.79). We conducted a literature review and identified 27 potential predictors theoretically and empirically related to achievement motivation, including immediate contextual factors
available in the dataset and distal contextual factors available from varying sources. Results from machine
learning analyses showed convergent patterns of the contextual predictors for adaptive motivation (self-efficacy,
learning goals, and task mastery orientation). Specifically, the optimal environment for adaptive motivation is
characterized by the collective positive influence of parents, teachers, and peers, rather than depending on one
exclusively. In comparison, the pattern of other less adaptive motivation constructs (fixed mindsets, performance
goals, and fear of failure) is idiosyncratic. These findings provide synthesized evidence consolidating achievement motivation research, highlighting the shared contextual foundations for various adaptive motivations. This
integrative approach clarifies that the optimal motivational contexts involve the collective social support of
parents, teachers, and peers.

Achievement motivation, a social-cognitive construct concerning
one’s motivational beliefs, goals, and attitudes in relation to competence
and achievement, is an influential psychological factor with widespread
consequences for individuals, educational institutions, and societies
(Elliot et al., 2017). Substantial evidence shows that adaptive motivation serves as a critical precursor to academic success and social wellbeing (Covington, 2000). Achievement motivation involves multifaced
processes, and accordingly, numerous derived constructs have been
proposed and well-examined in the literature. This includes, but is not
limited to, self-efficacy (Bandura, 2006), learning goal orientation
(Elliot & Murayama, 2008; see also Guo et al., 2023), and task mastery
orientation (Helmreich et al., 1980). The definitions and examples for
each concept are depicted in Fig. 1. In essence, adaptive motivation
reflects positive attitudes, beliefs, and orientations toward competence

development, which positively impact students’ academic engagement,
performance, and social well-being.
Despite the centrality of achievement motivation in various areas of
educational research, this field has faced criticism for lacking efforts to
consolidate the collective knowledge regarding what constitutes a
flourishing environment for different adaptive motivations (Anderman,
2020; Guay et al., 2022; Hattie et al., 2020; Pekrun, 2024). For example,
different motivational theories highlight the importance of the learning
environment, but due to the siloed investigations into different motivational theories, it is unclear if these different motivational factors (e.
g., self-efficacy, learning goal, and task mastery orientation) share
similar contextual roots and whether the contextual influences are
distinct from other types of less adaptive motivation (e.g., fixed mindset,
performance goals, and fear of failure). Although these academic

consolidate and create a more systematic and coherent knowledge for

To bring insights into this important question, the current study aims
to identify the shared contextual roots by considering a wide range of
potential contextual predictors. Drawing from the socio-ecological
framework (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2007; Skinner et al., 2022) and
cultural psychology of motivation (Heine et al., 2001; King & McInerney, 2014; Markus, 2016), recent work has highlighted that human
motivation is also linked to more distal, macro-level contexts – societal
and economic conditions and cultural values, in which people and their
proximal contexts are embedded (Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). This
research integrated previous research findings on a variety of contextual
factors to identify the conditions conducive to a flourishing (thwarting)
environment for adaptive motivation. Specifically, we tested a wide
range of potential contextual predictors, encompassing immediate
contextual predictors (e.g., the roles of teachers, parents, and peers;
Collie et al., 2017) and more distal larger societal contextual predictors
(e.g., economic development), which are often neglected in the relevant
literature (cf. Skinner et al., 2022).
Capturing the complex social ecologies of achievement motivation is
challenging because there are many motivational factors and even more
contextual factors. Answering this question requires a large dataset that
includes different motivation outcomes and contextual factors. Another
challenge is that null hypothesis testing using conventional regression
analyses can often lead to overfitting models when including many
predictors, resulting in inaccurate predictor comparisons (Collie et al.,
2017). Relatedly, previous studies on achievement motivation rarely
employed analytic methods that can consider multiple outcomes, which
hinders the exploration of flourishing (and thwarting) environments
conducive to multiple forms of adaptive motivation simultaneously. To
overcome these challenges, we employed a machine learning approach
and leveraged data from the Programme for International Student
Assessment (PISA; OECD, 2019) along with various societal indexes.

1. Identifying motivational constructs
Using an integrated dataset with large-scale assessments of an international sample of 77,068 middle schoolers from 19 countries, we
explored what contextual factors, considering different levels, can substantially and consistently predict adaptive motivation. Middle school
students are ideal for addressing the current research question because
adaptive motivation tends to decline during the transition from
elementary and secondary education, making it crucial to understand
the contextual influences at play during this time (Gottfried et al., 2009;
Lepper et al., 2005; Muenks et al., 2018).
1.1. Adaptive Motivation: Self-Efficacy, Learning Goals, and Task
Mastery Orientation
The PISA survey included a rich set of achievement and competencerelated motivation outcomes (see Fig. 1), including self-efficacy,
learning goals, and task mastery orientation. Although this dataset did
not cover all motivational constructs, they are representative, covering
some widely discussed forms of adaptive motivation in education. These
three motivational constructs have important implications for fostering
optimal conditions for students to develop competence and well-being
(Bandura, 2006; Elliot & Murayama, 2008; Guo et al., 2023). More
importantly, Self-efficacy, learning goals, and task mastery orientation
are conceptually related yet distinct constructs, each relating to different
aspects of adaptive motivation: ability beliefs, goals, and attitudes,
respectively.
Self-efficacy refers to learners’ beliefs in their ability to learn and
perform tasks successfully (Bandura, 2006), and it encompasses
learners’ perceptions of their ability to overcome difficult tasks (Schunk
& Pajares, 2002). For instance, enhancing students’ self-efficacy can
improve students’ task performance by fostering greater effort and
persistence and reducing anxiety (Huang et al., 2020). Distinct from selfefficacy, learning goals, rooted in achievement goals, prioritize the
acquisition of knowledge and competence (Elliot & Murayama, 2008).
Students with a strong learning goal are intrinsically motivated to learn
new things and apply their learning, rather than solely focusing on
external validation or grades (Guo et al., 2023). This focus on the
learning process differentiates learning goals from self-efficacy, which

1.4. Immediate, Proximal Contextual Factors
Students’ achievement motivation is highly sensitive to the change in
immediate social environments and interactions with social partners
(Anderman, 2020). For example, students with more interpersonal
support, such as emotional encouragement and collaboration, tend to
develop higher levels of self-efficacy over time (Peura et al., 2021;
Schweder & Raufelder, 2022). In contrast, when social agents adopt
controlling behaviors and provide limited support, it can undermine
learners’ achievement motivation (Connell & Wellborn, 1991; Lerner
et al., 2022; Ryan & Deci, 2020). Some immediate social factors, such as
peer competition, on motivation, are inconsistent: some studies suggested that it leads to increased performance goals (Kilday & Ryan,
2022; Murayama & Elliot, 2012), while others found that it may foster
learning goals (King et al., 2012; Plass et al., 2013). Despite these inconsistencies, it is evident that the nature of students’ social interactions
shapes their motivation. This also suggests that competitive peer environments may have mixed effects on motivation and other social factors
may interact with peer environment. Therefore, understanding the dynamics is essential for fostering environments that support growth.
In addition to peers, teachers and parents are important sources of
social support, and they directly influence learners’ motivation and
achievement (Barger et al., 2019; Kilday & Ryan, 2022; Martin & Collie,
2019; Wentzel et al., 2021). Although previous studies have identified
the impact of each social support on learners’ achievement motivation,
less is known about the collective influences of these social relationships. Skinner et al. (2022) argued that students’ achievement motivation is not just a product of one social partner, but rather simultaneously
shaped by various socialized agents. In other words, collective influences, referring to the impacts of various immediate environments
that work jointly, are essential in shaping students’ performance and
growth (Skinner et al., 2022).
In this study, by evaluating the role of multiple sources of support
simultaneously, we hope to examine the complexity of learners’
microsystems. The immediate contextual factors from the PISA data
(OECD, 2019) included family-related factors (parent emotional support, family economic and social status, home educational resources,
home language used, and cultural possession at home), teachers/school
factors (teacher feedback, teacher’s adaptive instruction, teacher
enthusiasm, career information, and learning time at school), and peer
factors (peer competition, peer cooperation, and experience of peer
bullying).

1.2. Less Adaptive Motivation: Fixed Mindsets, Fear of Failure, and
Performance Goals
Although the study primarily aims to identify the shared contextual
roots for adaptive motivation, we also validate the results with other
types of motivation that are considered less adaptive (or sometimes
mixed findings) in the literature and are available in the same PISA
dataset. These less adaptive motivational constructs include fixed
mindsets (Dweck et al., 2019), fear of failure (Martin & Marsh, 2003),
and performance goals (Helmreich et al., 1980). These motivational
constructs are considered less adaptive because they are found to
potentially undermine learning and well-being (Covington, 2000;
Huang, 2020; Krou et al., 2021; Van Yperen et al., 2014). Including these
factors can help us better understand whether the context for adaptive
motivation is unique.
Fixed mindsets refer to the belief that intelligence cannot be changed
(Dweck & Yeager, 2019). Previous meta-analysis and large-scale studies
showed that fixed mindsets are linked to lower performance and wellbeing, although their relation was inconsistent across contexts
(Burnette et al., 2023; Lou & Li, 2023a). Performance goals refer to the
motive to outperform others and gain external validation. Although
performance goals may seem adaptive in a competitive context and lead
to positive performance outcomes (see Murayama & Elliot, 2012), we do
not consider performance goals as adaptive because they are also linked
to negative emotional and interpersonal consequences (Song et al.,
2015; Olivier et al., 2023). Finally, fear of failure refers to the desire or
tendency to avoid mistakes or challenges (Covington, 2000; Martin &
Marsh, 2003). Some studies found that students who are motivated to
avoid failures may have positive academic outcomes (Lou & Li, 2023b),
while others found that it is negatively associated with performance (e.
g., Bergold & Steinmayr, 2016; Deneault et al., 2020) and well-being
outcomes. In summary, unlike adaptive motivation (the focus of this
study), fixed mindsets, fear of failure, and performance goals can
potentially lead to negative performance and/or psychological
outcomes.

1.5. Distal Contextual Factors

1.3. Identifying contextual factors

Building on the socio-ecological framework (Bronfenbrenner &
Morris, 2007; Skinner et al., 2022) and cultural psychology of motivation (Heine et al., 2001; Markus, 2016; Stoet & Geary, 2018), surging
research highlights the importance of broader, macro-level contexts in
influencing human motivation and well-being. The macro context, in
which individuals and their immediate surroundings are situated, plays
a crucial role in fostering human functioning, including their motivation
(Lou & Li, 2023b; Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). Following these rationales, we identified different societal and cultural indexes that were
suggested to be related to individual motivation in previous studies (see
Table S3 for definitions and review).
Previous research suggests some societal factors related to students’
achievement motivation, including the country’s gross domestic product
(GDP; e.g., Dekker & Fischer, 2008), social mobility (e.g., Zhang et al.,
2020), social equity (GINI; e.g., Sommet et al., 2019), and cultural
values (e.g., individualism, power distance, masculinity, uncertainty
avoidance, long term orientation, and indulgence; e.g., Guo et al., 2023;
King, 2010; Taras et al., 2010). In general, regions that are wealthier and
more socially equal provide greater affordance that supports learners’
motivation (Chiu & Khoo, 2005; Dekker & Fischer, 2008; Sommet et al.,
2019). Many cultural values were found to be linked to achievement

1.6. Research questions
The primary goal of this study is to explore whether there are proximal and distal contextual factors that consistently predict various types
of adaptive motivation, including self-efficacy, learning goals, and task
mastery orientation. Additionally, we aim to explore whether these
contextual predictors are consistent across less adaptive forms of motivation, including fixed mindsets, fear of failure, and performance goals.
The study was not preregistered. Previous studies mostly examined the
effect of a limited number of contextual factors on a single form of
achievement motivation, providing insufficient insight to formulate
hypotheses when considering the effect of multiple contextual factors on
multiple adaptive forms of achievement motivation simultaneously. As a
result, we did not propose specific hypotheses regarding which specific
contextual factors are most influential for different forms of motivation.

2.3. Analytic plan
2.3.1. Model Construction Overview
Machine learning (ML) has gained increasing prominence across a
wide range of fields. Different from conventional statistical methods that
are often performed on a group base in a univariate manner, ML techniques are well known for being able to perform multivariate pattern
analyses, establish predictive models at the individual level, and
generalize to unseen data through training–testing protocols (Grimmer
et al., 2021; Jordan & Mitchel, 2015). In other words, unlike conventional approaches in psychological research, which focus on explaining
the outcome, ML focuses on predicting the outcome (Yarkoni & Westfall,
2017). These abilities enable researchers to employ ML techniques to
gain deeper insights into the human mind and behaviors based on
empirical data, making ML techniques a promising tool in the fields of
psychological and social science (e.g., Shaw et al., 2022). In this study,
we employed a classic ML technique, partial least squares (PLS)
regression (de Jong, 1993; Wold, 1975), to build a prediction model to
identify the shared essential contextual predictors for multiple motivation outcomes. Given a set of predictors and a set of outcome variables,
PLS regression seeks a linear regression model that maximizes the
covariance between the components derived by projecting predictors
and outcome variables to new spaces. The primary merits of PLS
regression are as follows: 1) address multicollinearity among predictors;
2) handle multiple predictors and outcome variables in one model; 3)
explore latent structures behind predictors and outcome variables such
that the obtained components achieve maximum covariance; and 4)
result in a linear model that is transparent and facilitates interpretation.
The above merits make PLS regression a promising tool for psychological and behavioral studies (e.g., Sawatsky et al., 2015). To improve
generalizability to unseen data, the models were trained and tested
using the repeated 10-fold cross-validation (CV) framework (N = 100),
incorporating feature selection and a hold-out validation process for
hyperparameter tuning nested in each fold. The overall model construction pipeline is presented in Fig. 2.

2. Methods
2.1. Participants
We used the dataset of PISA 2018 (OECD, 2019), which adopted
rigorous approaches for recruiting representative student samples across
societies and ensuring scale validation across societies. The original
dataset consists of self-report data from 15-year-old students across 80
societies. However, because not all data collection sites included all
measures and the scores of distal contextual predictors were only
available for some of these societies – the final dataset comprised responses from 77,068 diverse students (Mage = 15.79, SD = 0.29, range:
15.08 – 16.33; 51.90 % girls and 48.10 % boys) across 19 societies.
These samples provided complete data for all the target motivation
outcomes and potential contextual predictors. These societies include
Albania, Austria, Brazil, Bulgaria, Croatia, Germany, Greece, Hungary,
Iceland, Ireland, Kazakhstan, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Serbia, Slovak
Republic, Slovenia, Spain, and Thailand. This research used secondary
data (PISA 2018: https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/), which complied
with the ethics guidelines of the data collection sites. No ethics approval
from a local ethics board is required.
2.2. Key variables
As indicated in Tables S1 and S3 (in Supplementary Material), this
study encompasses 27 predictors that were theoretically and empirically
related to achievement motivation based on previous studies. Of these
predictors, we also included factors (immigration status, gender, age,

2.3.2. Detailed Implementation of Model Construction
As shown in Fig. 2, at the beginning of each repetition of the repeated
10-fold CV, the original dataset was randomly divided into 10 even
subsets. Subsequently, the 10 folds iterated through the 10 subsets, each
utilizing one subset for model testing and the remaining nine subsets for
model training. Within each fold, a hold-out validation protocol was
employed for tuning the model hyperparameter, i.e., the number of PLS
components (ncomp). More specifically, 30 % of the training data in the
current fold were held out as a validation set, while the remaining 70 %
of the training data were used to train a series of PLS regression models
under different ncomp settings. During the training procedure, the predictors and the outcome variables were standardized as Z-scores at first.
A feature selection process was then performed to identify predictors

PLS regression model was built using each ncomp setting in the list of {1,

were measured on the validation set, with the performance metric
defined as the total variance explained for all outcomes. For each ncomp
setting, a performance gain ratio was computed as the ratio of performance gain to the increment in ncomp. According to the elbow method
(Thorndike, 1953), the ncomp setting with the maximum performance
gain ratio was considered as the optimum (ncompbest). Using ncompbest,
the above steps of data standardization, feature selection, and model
training were repeated on all the training data to obtain the final model
in the current fold.
Subsequently, the final model made predictions on the testing data.
Note that during each prediction, the standardization of the predictors
and the restored scaling of the outcome variables were performed using
the means and the standard deviations of the training data. After
completing all 10 folds, we obtained predicted values for the outcome
variables across the entire dataset. The predicted-empirical correlation

(r) and the relative mean square error (RMSE) were then calculated for
each outcome variable as the measures of prediction performance. The
above 10-fold CV was repeated 100 independent times to avoid bias
caused by random data partition. The average results across all repetitions were reported for subsequent analyses.
To confirm the effectiveness of the prediction models, a permutation
test (N = 5000) was performed (Lindgren et al., 1996; Ojala & Garriga,
2010). The null hypothesis of the permutation test was the ineffectiveness of regression models, i.e., its prediction performance was driven by
the noise in the PISA dataset but not the actual predictor-outcome
relationship. More specifically, the permutation test permutated the
values of outcome variables at random and repeated the model construction pipeline. A total of 5,000 repetitions were carried out, and the
prediction performance of each repetition was compared with the
original model. The fraction of repetitions that performed worse than
the original model could be considered as the confidence level for
rejecting the null hypothesis.

prediction model at two levels. At the first level, a VIP score was

3.3. Prediction model results
Fig. 4 displays the separate and the overall variable importance in
projection (VIP) scores for the predictors concerning (4a) the various
forms of adaptive motivation outcomes and (4b) the other less adaptive
motivation outcomes. According to the VIP scores, the importance of the
predictors highly aligned across different adaptive motivation outcomes
(Fig. 4a), with the pairwise correlations all beyond 0.98 (ps < 0.001).
However, as shown in Fig. 4b, such alignment was not necessarily the
case for the less adaptive motivation outcomes, which shows that the
pairwise correlations between the predictors’ VIPs for different less
adaptive motivation outcomes ranged from 0.74 to 0.87 (ps < 0.001). In
addition, the small error bars in Fig. 4 indicate that the contributions of
predictors were steady throughout the repeated CV procedure.
Regarding the adaptive motivation outcomes, the top important
predictors (all four VIP scores higher than one) included parent
emotional support, peer cooperation, teacher enthusiasm, teacher’s
adaptive instruction, teacher feedback, and peer competition (in
descending order). Notably, these predictors were all from the proximal
context (parents, teachers, and peers). Fig. 5a further tabulates the
positive relationships between these predictors and adaptive motivation
outcomes, showing that the increment in each of these predictors was
associated with an increase in all three adaptive motivation outcomes,
but at different ratios.
In contrast to the consistent patterns observed in adaptive motivation outcomes, distinct and idiosyncratic patterns emerged from the
three less adaptive motivation outcomes. The top important predictors
(all four VIP scores higher than one) were gender, peer competition,
societal long-term orientation, students’ experience of being bullied,
and parent emotional support. Importantly, as shown in Fig. 5b, only
peer competition and societal long-term orientation exhibited consistent
relationships with all three less adaptive motivation outcomes (all positive and all negative, respectively). Conversely, diversified gender effects were observed (boys = 1; girls = 0), in which boys reported higher
scores in fixed mindsets and performance goals but lower scores in fear
of failure than girls. Students’ experiences of being bullied were positively associated with fixed mindsets and fear of failure but negatively
associated with performance goals. Finally, parent emotional support
was positively associated with performance goals but negatively associated with fixed mindsets and fear of failure.

2.3.4. Model Validation Analysis
For model validation, we performed the following four analyses.
First, the fold number of CV was known to affect the modeling results
(James et al., 2021). For validation purposes, we repeated the modeling
procedure with the fold number set at two and five, respectively. Second, the modeling procedure was reiterated, with each outcome variable
modeled separately. This approach ensured that the grouping operation
of the adaptive outcome variables would not distort our main findings
but rather enhance the exposure of the underlying common structure.
Finally, the employed PLS regression assumed linear predictor-outcome
relationships. To assess the validity of this linear assumption, we
replaced PLS regression with three well-known non-linear multivariate
multiple modeling methods, i.e., regression tree (RT; Hastie et al.,
2009), random forest (RF; Breiman, 2001), and feedforward artificial
neural network (ANN) (Schmidhuber, 2015). Detailed results and figures of these validation analyses are presented in the Supplementary
Material.
3. Results
3.1. Preliminary Analyses Results
Figure S1 displays the pairwise relationship between predictors and
motivation outcomes. Pearson’s correlation coefficients showed that
part of the ordinal predictors was weakly correlated with the motivation
outcomes (|r|s < 0.30, ps < 0.05). The one-way analysis of variance

and probably in the motivation outcome variables as well (Donath et al.,
2012; Vatcheva et al., 2016).

4. Discussion
Achievement motivation is important in human flourishing and is
inextricably connected with the learning environment. For decades,
scholars have faced challenges when empirically investigating the patterns of complex ecosystems of achievement motivation despite theoretical advancements aimed at moving beyond studying one contextual
factor or motivational construct at a time (Guay, 2022; Hattie et al.,
2020; Martin, 2023; Pekrun, 2024). To bridge this critical gap, we
leveraged machine learning to explore the predictive power of 27
contextual factors with an international dataset on six achievement
motivations. We found that self-efficacy, learning goals, and task
mastery orientation –three types of adaptive motivation – shared similar
roots in the immediate social context, including support and connections
from parents, teachers, and peers. In contrast, fixed mindsets, performance goals, and fear of failure – the other three less adaptive forms of

4.1. Theoretical implications
This study offers insights for advancing the theoretical development
of the ecological framework in achievement motivation by modeling
which and how the contextual factors can simultaneously predict
distinct aspects of adaptive motivation and less adaptive motivation.
There is merit in the traditional perspective on motivation by understanding predictions of each motivational process in silos, but examining
the complex socio-ecological contexts in an overarching motivational
framework may help lead to a more parsimony and bigger picture of the
field. Models built by machine learning (ML) techniques are able to
perform multivariate pattern analyses and make individual-level predictions. Moreover, the training–testing protocol used to build ML
models endows the generalizability of the obtained model to unseen
data. These advantages of ML models are essential to facilitate a robust
evaluation of the previous findings obtained in single studies of

Concerning the roles of 27 immediate and distal contextual factors

relative importance of distal factors observed in the current study, their

processes through the immediate context, thereby having weaker direct
effects than immediate contexts, and could shape people’s motivational
responses (e.g., Heine et al., 2001; King et al., 2018; Lou & Li, 2023b;
Skinner, 2022).

4.1.1. Adaptive Motivation: The Importance of Collective Influences of
Parents, Teachers, and Peers
Although self-efficacy, learning goal, and task mastery orientation
are conceptually distinct (Bong, 2001; Liem et al., 2008), the current
findings suggest they have something in common. Specifically, support
from the immediate environments – primarily consisting of parents,
teachers, and peers – consistently emerges as the most powerful predictors of these motivational factors. The VIP scores (Fig. 4a), the partial
dependent plots (Fig. 5a), and the PLS components (Fig. 6a) all revealed
consistent results, in which parent’s emotional support, peer cooperation, and teacher enthusiasm were identified to be the most important
shared contextual roots for three forms of adaptive motivation. Indeed,
the strongest predicting effect on adaptive motivation is the combination
of the three immediate socializing agents (parents, teachers, and peers)
with whom students interact in everyday life; not one or the other. This
result is compatible with the framework of collective influences in academic development (Skinner et al., 2022), in which different socializing agents work together to provide students with recourses and

teachers, and peers, may be more important (with higher VIP scores for
adaptive motivation). Second, there is a high cost of introducing competitions, as suggested by its greater importance in predicting less

VIP scores).

4.1.2. Less Adaptive Motivation: Inconsistent Contextual Influences
By contrast, we observe less consistent contextual effects for fixed
mindsets, performance goals, and fear of failure; different analyses
jointly suggested idiosyncratic patterns for different forms of less
adaptive motivation. Few contextual predictors with VIP scores higher
than one (Fig. 4b) were identified for the three forms of less adaptive
motivation. Importantly, the partial dependent plots (Fig. 5b) revealed
the varying nature of the relationship between the identified important
contextual predictors with each form of less adaptive motivation. The
PLS components (Fig. 6b) also failed to demonstrate a strongly coherent
structural component for the three forms of less adaptive motivation.
Altogether, we failed to identify the shared contextual roots from the 27
potential predictors for the less adaptive motivation. This result was
consistent with previous work considering a smaller range of predictors,
which showed limited predictive power of contextual factors (e.g., home
support and community support; Collie et al., 2017).
Two speculations were made for shared contextual antecedents
found only among the adaptative motivation. First, there might be more
nuances about the less adaptive motivation in this current study. For
instance, previous work proposed negatively and positively valenced
dimensions, i.e., approach- vs. avoidance-orientation, to understand
performance goals, one form of less adaptive motivation (Murayama &
Elliot, 2012). Similarly, fear of failure may undermine or facilitate
learning depending on context (Martin & Marsh, 2003; Lou & Li,
2023b). As such, there may be more social and individual reasons that
could explain why students endorse certain less adaptive motivation.
Second, the more unifying contextual pattern for the adaptive (vs. less
adaptive) motivation selected in the study may reflect the fact that social
ecologies are more likely to reinforce positive attributes (Alves et al.,
2017). In other words, social ecologies may provide a greater affordance
for the development of positive attributes, including adaptive motivation, leading to a greater likelihood of detecting the shared contextual
roots for more adaptive motivation. Future studies are needed to evaluate these speculations.
Based on these results, although it may be achievable to integrate
adaptive motivation by identifying their shared contextual roots, it is
necessary for a multidimensional approach to motivation (Liem &
Senko, 2022; Martin, 2007). Adaptive and maladaptive motivations are
not merely opposite ends of the same continuum, given that they have
different contextual predictors. Different types of less adaptive motivation may independently arise and encompass a diverse set of psychological processes that do not simply mirror the more adaptive
motivation. This notion was consistent with some previous theoretical
and empirical work. For instance, some students may endorse both
adaptive and less adaptive motivation at the same time (Liem & Senko,
2022; Lou et al., 2022). Considering the nuance of less adaptive motivation is an important consideration when attempting to unify
achievement motivation theories.

4.2. Practical Implications
Motivated students tend to believe in their ability, enjoy the learning
process, and focus on their learning, rather than believe that their ability
is limited, focus on the outcome and comparison, and worry about
challenges (Guay, 2022; Wentzel & Skinner, 2022). A key objective of
education is to elicit and sustain students’ motivation to grow. However,
promoting adaptive motivations goes beyond just changing individuals’
beliefs and attitudes (i.e., the basis for most interventions). Rather, these
adaptive motivational beliefs and attitudes are highly contextualized,
especially in the immediate relational contexts (Wentzel & Skinner,
2022). This is especially important because many classroom environments and educational practices appear to fail to sustain adaptive
motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2020). This aligns with the recent movement
advocating that educational interventions should pay attention to
contextual affordance (Walton et al., 2023; Hecht et al., 2023).
But what are the characteristics of a thriving context for learners? We
found that the three forms of adaptive motivation in this study are
consistently linked to students’ perceptions of their teachers displaying
interest in students’ learning (enthusiastic) and providing supportive
feedback to students’ learning, which highlights the importance of
autonomy-supportive teachers in learning (Cheon et al., 2023; Robinson, 2023). Additionally, our findings underscore the need to consider
practices beyond the classroom. Collaborative peers and supportive
parents are also suggested to take crucial roles in a motivating environment for adaptive motivation. These findings align with previous
studies highlighting the effectiveness of school-home collaborative interventions in addressing a variety of problems among students, such as
attention deficits and obesity (e.g., Pfiffner et al., 2013; Sgambato et al.,
2019). Therefore, future interventions should consider these interpersonal affordances and evaluate their joint benefit in multiple adaptive
motivation outcomes simultaneously. It is possible that the effort of
fostering different forms of adaptive motivation can be similar,
providing implications that move beyond promoting a single
motivation.
This study’s findings on the varied contextual patterns associated
with less adaptive motivation suggest that achieving the dual goals of
enhancing adaptive motivation while reducing maladaptive motivation
is challenging. Less adaptive motivational drives can emerge independently from one another and involve a wide range of psychological
processes that are not merely the opposite of adaptive motivation. The
idiosyncratic contextual predictors on various forms of less adaptive
motivation indicate that tailored approaches are necessary when
designing interventions to address different types of maladaptive
motivation.

4.1.3. The Double-Edged Sword Effect of Competition
Our findings also revealed the double-edged sword effect of
competition in achievement motivation. Although competition at school
is a key contributor to less adaptive motivation (with the second-highest
VIP score among the 27 predictors), it also consistently contributes to
the three forms of adaptive motivation (with the fifth-highest VIP score).
This effect is consistent with previous studies showing peer competition
could prompt performance goals (Kilday & Ryan, 2022; Murayama &
Elliot, 2012) but also lead to mastery goals (King et al., 2012; Plass et al.,
2013). The positive effect of competitions could be attributed to individuals’ higher level of enjoyment in completion (Plass et al., 2013).
As such, a motivating environment may be competitive but also emphasizes support and collaboration. While competition is inevitably
embedded in many parts of the educational system, and the present
findings show evidence for its potential positive effect on adaptive
motivation, we would like to highlight two points. First, competition

4.3. Limitations and Future Directions
This study explored the general contextual foundation for achievement motivation, providing implications for future research to address
how specific practices or policies predict different forms of motivation.
Future studies may extend to address the questions below to further
understand the generalizability of the current findings. First, the
selected motivational constructs, while representative, do not
completely encompass the phenomena of either adaptive or maladaptive
motivation. For example, the dataset did not include constructs such as
growth mindsets (it only includes an item for fixed mindsets). In addition, as we focus on general motivational constructs in this study, some
domain-specific motivational constructs (e.g., reading interest) were not
11

N.M. Lou et al.

Contemporary Educational Psychology 79 (2024) 102323

included. Future studies should examine the contextual underpinnings
of other motivations tied to key achievement motivation theories, such
as self-determination theory, expectancy-value theory, attribution theory, and achievement goal theory (see Anderman, 2020; Wigfield &
Koenka, 2020 for a summary).
Second, while we acknowledge the person × environment interaction in motivation and learning (Lou & Li, 2023b; Van Vianen, 2018),
this is not the goal of this study of generalizing the current results to all
contexts. Given the availability of the variables in the dataset, we could
not include other contextual factors suggested in previous studies. For
instance, other microsystem factors, such as virtual learning experiences
and neighborhoods, can be considered in future studies.
Third, all participants were about 15 years old; future studies are
required to evaluate the generalizability of the current findings to other
age groups of students. Future studies could also explore how other individual differences may also play a role and how the environment may
provide different affordances to students with different characteristics.
Fourth, the final dataset only included data from 19 societies due to
the missing value issue, with most of the data from European societies.
This restricted range of societal characteristics may account for the
relatively weak effect of distal contextual predictors observed in the
present study. Previous studies have shown that students’ learning
processes can vary significantly across societies with different cultural
characteristics (e.g., Lou & Li, 2023a, 2023b; Wang & Li, 2024). Thus,
future studies should assess the generalizability of the current findings to
societies with more diverse characteristics.
Fifth, considering the limitation of current cross-sectional correlational data, future research should use longitudinal designs to examine
the temporal precedence of contextual features and the developmental
trajectory of different motivation outcomes.
Finally, although the prediction models achieved statistically significant positive empirical-predicted correlations with only small-tomoderate effect sizes, the results are considered effective, consistent
with many previous psychological studies using machine learning (e.g.,
Pan & Cutumisu, 2022; Pavlovic et al., 2022). The permutation test
further confirmed the effectiveness of the obtained PLS regression
models. The small-to-moderate magnitude may be because more factors,
including other contextual factors as explained previously and factors
beyond contextual ones like personality traits and experiences (e.g.,
Jach et al., 2023), can influence individual achievement motivation,
especially for the less adaptive motivation outcomes. Future studies may
consider involving more factors, both personal and contextual, to
improve prediction performance.
To continue to advance the theoretical understanding of ecological
models of achievement motivation, future research may build upon the
current findings to continue to address how restricted and specific
conditions predict different forms of motivation. Researchers may
further study how the social and interpersonal processes – what teachers
do (feedback), how different parental support, and how peers interact –
independently and collectively predict each of these motivational constructs (Kilday & Ryan, 2022; Wentzel & Skinner, 2022). For instance,
examining other kinds of parental support (e.g., autonomy, competence,
home-based involvement; Lerner et al., 2022) in addition to emotional
support (in this current study) could provide additional theoretical implications for the ecological framework. In addition, future research may
expand to understand the mediation pathways and the socialpsychological processes (e.g., Schunk & DiBenedetto, 2020). For
example, it is possible that parent support may shape teacher or peer
support, which may then go on to predict student motivation outcomes
(Skinner et al., 2022). Addressing this could provide not only a bigger
picture but also a more in-depth, precise understanding of the ecological
theories in achievement motivation. Future experimental studies can
further reveal the causal effect of different contextual factors (Hecht
et al., 2023). We also resonate with the call for conducting more studies
on less adaptive motivation, as most previous intervention studies
focused on exploring how to enhance adaptive motivation (e.g., Collie &

Martin, 2017). The inconsistent relationships between contextual factors and various forms of less adaptive motivation suggested substantial
differences in these motivational constructs. Future work needs to
further investigate the nature of maladaptive motivation by considering
a greater variety of contextual factors. These findings may provide
important insights into whether diverse representations of different
forms of maladaptive motivation are required.
5. Conclusion
This study, representing one of the largest and most integrative approaches for understanding the contextual antecedents of achievement
motivation, provides answers to a question that concerns many educators and policymakers: How is the learning environment systematically
associated with students’ achievement motivation? Our results consolidate the current understanding of achievement motivation and support
the idea that the most powerful contextual predictors of adaptive
motivation (self-efficacy, learning goals, and task mastery orientation)
were consistently related to the collective influences of immediate
contexts, specifically teachers’ enthusiasm, parent’s emotional support,
and peer’s cooperation. This suggests that there may be a shared, similar
contextual foundation for adaptive motivation. However, the contextual
predictors of the less adaptive motivation (fixed mindset, performance
goal, fear of failure) vary. This provides an innovative perspective for
future theory integration for achievement motivation, in which adaptive
motivation may be converged into a higher-order factor, while a more
nuanced model may be needed to capture the diverse representations of
maladaptive motivation in terms of ecological affordance.
Our results also urge that motivation researchers need to move
beyond the classroom environments to fully understand the contextual
impact on achievement motivation. This data-driven integrative
framework based on the current findings sheds light on a more unified
field of achievement motivation and provides implications for future
research and educational interventions. Theory development based on
machine learning-driven exploration is an ongoing research area. Future
studies are required to evaluate the generalizability of the current
findings to different motivational constructs, contexts, and age groups.
We also encourage more large-scale studies to better entangle these
interrelated motivational theories in the future.
