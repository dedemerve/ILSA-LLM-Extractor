ABSTRACT
Rapid technological change and educational expansion raise fundamental questions about educational mismatch and efficient human capital allocation. This study employs an OECD-administered survey to identify
educational mismatch using self-assessment measures and to investigate its wage effects across 20 countries.
The results show that overeducated workers earn 14% less than their adequately matched counterparts,
whereas undereducated workers earn 10% more. Furthermore, gender-disaggregated analysis reveals that
women experience larger penalties for overeducation and smaller premiums for undereducation than men.
Propensity score matching estimates indicate substantial cross-country heterogeneity in wage effects, with
particularly large penalties in Spain, Korea, and Japan, implying that rigid labor market regulation with limited
occupational mobility yield larger penalties, while countries with well-developed vocational systems exhibit
smaller effects. This study provides novel comparative evidence on the causal wage effects of educational
mismatch and underscores the importance of institution-specific policies linking education quality, labor
market flexibility, and lifelong learning.

1. Introduction
Educational mismatch has emerged as a defining labor market challenge in developed economies, where the supply of tertiary-educated
workers has grown substantially faster than the availability of jobs
requiring advanced credentials (Chevalier and Lindley, 2006; Kyui,
2016; Ordine and Rose, 2011). Human capital theory predicts that
educational investments yield proportional wage returns through productivity enhancement (Becker, 1985; Mincer and Polachek, 1974),
implying consistent returns to schooling regardless of job characteristics. However, substantial empirical evidence reveals that returns to
years of schooling vary significantly with match status: the returns to
surplus or deficit years of education differ from the returns to required
years of schooling. This disconnect between educational credentials and
wages raises fundamental questions about the efficiency of human capital allocation and the private and social returns to education expansion.
Provided that highly educated workers accept positions below their
qualification levels, the misallocation imposes welfare costs through
foregone productivity, underutilized skills, and inefficient educational

investments (Groot and Van Den Brink, 2000; Leuven and Oosterbeek, 2011). Conversely, when workers occupy positions exceeding
their formal credentials, the premiums they earn suggest either that
employers value unobserved productivity attributes not captured by educational attainment or that credentialism creates artificial barriers to
appropriately skilled workers. Educational mismatch manifests in two
empirically distinct forms. Overeducation occurs when workers possess
educational credentials exceeding the formal requirements or typical
attainment levels for their occupations, while undereducation describes
situations where workers hold qualifications below the educational
standards required for their positions. The existing literature documents that overeducated workers experience significant wage penalties
while undereducated workers receive wage premiums compared to
their appropriately matched counterparts (Quintini and Martin, 2006;
McGuinness, 2008; Sloane et al., 2020). These wage differentials persist across diverse institutional contexts, educational systems, and labor market structures, indicating that educational mismatch represents a fundamental feature of contemporary labor markets rather
than a country-specific or transitional phenomenon. The problem has

Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)
member countries provide an ideal setting for examining educational
mismatch wage effects through systematic cross-national comparison.
These predominantly high-income, market-oriented economies share
fundamental institutional features including democratic governance,
market-based resource allocation, and substantial public investment
in education, yet exhibit considerable heterogeneity in educational
systems, labor market structures, and employment protection regimes.
Over recent decades, educational mismatch has expanded dramatically
across OECD countries, particularly for overeducation. The prevalence
of overeducation in advanced economies reflects multiple structural
factors operating simultaneously. First, credential inflation driven by
rapid tertiary enrollment expansion has outpaced labor market absorption capacity in many OECD countries, generating substantial populations of highly educated workers in positions below their qualification
levels (Waibel et al., 2018). This educational expansion creates positional competition where individuals pursue higher education primarily
to maintain relative standing in job queues rather than for productive
skill acquisition, thereby intensifying aggregate overeducation even
when individual returns remain positive. Second, rigid labor market
institutions including strict employment protection, extensive occupational licensing, and credential-based hiring norms systematically
constrain efficient matching throughout careers. Particularly in many
East Asian and Southern European economies, these institutional rigidities tend to lock workers into initial mismatched positions, limiting
opportunities for upward or lateral mobility. Third, education systems have been slow to adjust curricula to the pace of technological
change and industrial restructuring. Traditional academic programs fail
to provide occupation-specific skills demanded in emerging sectors,
creating temporal mismatches between educational supply and labor
market demands. Notably, OECD countries demonstrate substantial
cross-national variation in educational mismatch prevalence. Spain
exhibits higher overeducation prevalence attributable to rigid labor
markets and industrial structures (Nieto and Ramos, 2017), whereas
the Netherlands demonstrates lower prevalence owing to strong vocational systems (de Bustillo Llorente et al., 2018). Poland demonstrates
age-related asymmetries where younger workers face disproportionate overeducation while older cohorts experience higher undereducation (Kiersztyn, 2013); meanwhile, women suffer strikingly higher
overeducation levels than men (Davia et al., 2017). These patterns reflect varying educational expansion trajectories and institutional design
across countries.
Rigorous cross-national research on educational mismatch requires
both accurate measurement and cross-country comparability. The International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) dataset provide
harmonized data collection across OECD countries (Van der Velden
and Bijlsma, 2019; Brunello and Wruuck, 2021), which provides standardized measures of educational attainment, detailed employment
characteristics, and wages, facilitating rigorous comparative research
while minimizing measurement inconsistencies that compromise
cross-national analyses using disparate national surveys (Pellizzari and
Fichen, 2017; Abdulla, 2025). The transformed Mincer wage equation
provides the standard framework for estimating educational mismatch
wage effects (Duncan and Hoffman, 1981; Verdugo and Verdugo,
1989). However, non-random selection into mismatch generates potentially severe endogeneity bias, as workers select jobs based on
both observable characteristics and unobserved heterogeneity that
also influence wages directly. Previous research has employed various
approaches to address this selection problem, including instrumental
variables estimation and fixed-effects panel models controlling for
time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity (Korpi and Tåhlin, 2009).
Yet these methods face substantial limitations in the educational mismatch context. Valid instruments are rare, as most factors influencing
job-education match also plausibly affect wages through alternative

channels. Panel approaches, while addressing time-invariant heterogeneity, generate biased estimates when workers frequently change
jobs or when mismatch effects vary over careers (Leuven and Oosterbeek, 2011). Moreover, fixed effects cannot address time-varying
unobservables correlated with both mismatch transitions and wage
growth.
To address selection bias from unobserved heterogeneity, propensity
score matching (PSM) provides a quasi-experimental framework that
constructs comparable treatment and control groups matched on the
conditional probability of experiencing educational mismatch (Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983). PSM approximates randomized experiments
by balancing covariate distributions between groups, but relies on the
conditional independence assumption (unconfoundedness) requiring
that selection operates solely through observables. Consequently, PSM
estimates remain sensitive to unobserved heterogeneity, with effectiveness depending critically on including comprehensive controls (Jiang
and Guo, 2022; Pearl, 2009). Hence, in addition to demographic background and job characteristics, I incorporate PIAAC’s direct cognitive
skill assessments and parental education into propensity score estimation, explicitly controlling for ability heterogeneity to mitigate residual
selection bias. This study investigates three principal research questions. First, what are the wage effects of educational mismatch in
pooled cross-national observations, and how do these effects differ by
gender? Second, what are the causal wage penalties for overeducation
and premiums for undereducation through PSM, and how do these
effects vary across countries? Third, how sensitive are estimated treatment effects to alternative matching specifications, and what does this
sensitivity reveal about unobserved heterogeneity bias in conventional
estimates?
This study contributes to the existing literature in two principal
ways. First, I provide the cross-national comparative analysis of educational mismatch wage effects across 20 OECD countries. In doing so,
I raise the critical issue of potential endogeneity arising from workers’
selection into educational mismatch based on unobservable characteristics, particularly unobserved ability and skill heterogeneity. To address
this concern, I employ PSM to correct for unobserved heterogeneity
bias and explore the extent to which such bias varies systematically
across diverse labor market institutions. While previous studies have
applied PSM to educational mismatch in individual countries, there is
little empirical evidence implementing PSM comparatively across multiple OECD countries using standardized data. Furthermore, our comprehensive sensitivity analyses across multiple matching algorithms
reveal substantial cross-country heterogeneity in bias correction magnitudes, demonstrating that institutional arrangements fundamentally
shape both the selection process into mismatch and the estimated
wage effects. In addition, to the best of our knowledge, this study
represents one of the first attempts to extend PSM analysis to genderdisaggregated comparison of educational mismatch wage effects from
a cross-national comparative perspective. Second, I address fundamental PSM limitations by exploiting PIAAC’s direct skill assessments
to distinguish genuine from apparent educational mismatch, thereby
eliminating individual skill heterogeneity within same education background and correcting measurement-induced endogeneity. King and
Nielsen (2019) suggest that PSM estimators are more model-dependent
and less efficient, raising concerns about the robustness of existing
causal estimates. I therefore adopt a complementary approach that
uses PIAAC’s standardized skill measures to decompose overeducation
into genuine and apparent forms. This decomposition addresses a fundamental identification challenge in educational mismatch research:
whether educational mismatch and skill mismatch occur simultaneously, or equivalently, whether observed wage effects reflect innate
ability differentials rather than genuine human capital underutilization.
This skill-based refinement substantially improves causal identification
by validating that observed educational mismatch represents genuine
productivity-job misalignment, justifying more credible estimates of
wage effects.

The empirical findings of this study are as follows. First, for pooled
samples, overeducation exerts a wage penalty of 13.8%, while undereducation yields a 9.9% wage premium. Second, PSM estimates reveal
substantial cross-country heterogeneity in wage effects, with particularly large penalties in Britain, Cyprus, and Japan. Moreover, sensitivity
checks demonstrate significant cross-country variation in heterogeneity
bias correction magnitudes, ranging from minimal adjustment in Nordic
countries to considerable downward revisions in Southern European
countries. Third, gender-disaggregated regressions reveals that women
experience larger overeducation penalties in the majority of countries,
with disparities particularly in Continental Europe. Fourth, mediation analysis indicates that occupational sorting accounts for 5.2% of
overeducation penalties and 10.4% of undereducation premiums. In
addition, ICT skills and influencing skills play crucial roles as transmission channels. Fifth, apparent overeducation is more prevalent and
imposes wage penalties approximately 1.5 times lower than genuine
overeducation on average.

From the dynamic perspective, several theories extend these foundational frameworks by incorporating temporal dimensions and technological change processes. Career mobility theory reconceptualizes educational mismatch as potentially optimal investment behavior, where
workers accept overeducated positions as stepping stones to better
matches (Sicherman and Galor, 1990; Baert et al., 2013), particularly
in internal labor markets offering promotion opportunities (Arranz
and García-Serrano, 2025), thereby underscoring that overeducation
represents a temporary phenomenon at the individual level (Dekker
et al., 2002). This framework predicts that initial wage penalties should
be compensated by higher future wage growth rates, with mismatch
effects varying systematically across career stages and institutional
contexts (Büchel and Mertens, 2004). Skill-biased technological change
theory argues that technological innovations complement highly educated workers while substituting for less educated ones, increasing
relative demand for skilled labor (Card and DiNardo, 2002; Wang et al.,
2021). In this context, educational expansion that fails to keep pace
with technological progress generates skill shortages rather than overeducation, suggesting that apparent mismatch may reflect measurement
error or transitional adjustment processes (Vera-Toscano and Meroni,
2021). Routine-biased technological change theory refines this perspective by emphasizing that automation primarily affects routine tasks
concentrated in middle-skill occupations, creating labor market polarization rather than uniform skill upgrading (Hutter and Weber, 2021;
Minardi, 2025). This polarization generates complex mismatch patterns
where educational expansion may simultaneously create shortages in
high-skill positions and surpluses in middle-skill categories, explaining
heterogeneous wage effects across education levels and occupational
categories.
Sociological and institutional perspectives challenge the human
capital foundation underlying economic approaches by emphasizing
education’s non-productive functions and the structural constraints that
impede efficient labor market adjustment (Bürmann, 2023). Credential
inflation theory argues that educational expansion occurs for cultural,
political, and status-related reasons beyond technical skill requirements
— including legitimizing social stratification (Van de Werfhorst and
Andersen, 2005), providing cultural currency for occupational access
(Brown, 2001), and serving as symbols of prestige (Zusman, 2017)
— leading employers to raise hiring standards without corresponding
increases in job complexity (Araki, 2020; Araki and Kariya, 2022).
This process generates systematic overeducation as educational requirements escalate independently of productivity needs, creating deadweight losses through excessive credentialing. Social capital theory
contributes additional insights by emphasizing how differential access
to social networks and relationships affects labor market outcomes,
where individuals with superior social capital obtain better job information and opportunities, enabling them to secure positions that match
their educational qualifications while others face systematic mismatch
despite equivalent credentials (Kalfa and Piracha, 2018). Social closure
theory extends this analysis by examining how privileged groups use
educational credentials to restrict access to desirable occupations, creating artificial scarcity that maintains wage premiums despite potential
skill oversupply (Weeden, 2002; Capsada-Munsech, 2017). Rather than
testing these theories against each other to disentangle the underlying
educational mismatch mechanisms, I leverage this diverse theoretical
framework to compare how educational mismatch affects wages across
different national contexts.

2.1. Theoretical review
No single theory can adequately account for the phenomenon of
educational mismatch across countries (Wiedner, 2024). This review
examines a comprehensive theoretical framework drawing from economics, education, and sociology to understand the multifaceted nature
of educational mismatch and its wage consequences. It is worth noting
that relevant theories can be categorized into two mainstream scenarios: voluntary temporary choice and involuntary permanent choice
(Mavromaras et al., 2013). Human capital theory posits that education
enhances worker productivity through skill acquisition (Becker and
Tomes, 1986), predicting that overeducated workers should eventually transition to matched positions as labor markets adjust to utilize
available human capital efficiently (Guironnet and Peypoch, 2007;
Somers et al., 2019). Thus, educational mismatch represents a temporary disequilibrium where workers with equivalent marginal productivity receive similar wages once market adjustments occur (Caroleo
and Pastore, 2018). However, signaling theory suggests that education
primarily signals unobservable worker characteristics rather than enhancing productivity directly (Spence, 1973). Consequently, employers
rely on educational credentials and previous job titles as screening
devices to infer worker quality amid information asymmetries (Shi
and Wang, 2022), generating persistent overeducation penalties even
when workers demonstrate adequate job performance. Job competition theory provides a structural perspective, arguing that employers
rank candidates in hiring queues based on perceived trainability, with
jobs characterized by fixed productivity levels determined by their
requirements rather than worker characteristics (Thurow, 1975). As
a positional good, educational expansion triggers credentialing races
to preserve relative standing, generating widespread overqualification
insofar as hiring standards escalate (Horowitz, 2018; Wiedner, 2024).
Assignment theory synthesizes these perspectives by emphasizing that
wages depend on the quality of worker-job matches, where both individual human capital and job characteristics jointly determine productivity and earnings outcomes (Sattinger, 1993). In mismatched positions, workers may not fully utilize their acquired skills and knowledge,
leading to constrained labor productivity and reduced wages (Montt,
2017; Tran et al., 2025).

challenges (Korpi and Tåhlin, 2009; Lee et al., 2016). Consequently,
PSM has emerged as the preferred alternative for addressing selection
bias (Ordine and Rose, 2015; Wu and Wang, 2018; Jiang and Guo,
2022). Using PSM, McGuinness and Sloane (2011) confirms substantial
overeducation penalties in the UK that remain robust to selection
bias concerns. Focusing on doctorate holders in Korea, Park (2022)
provides evidence of negative overeducation wage effects. PascualSáez and Lanza-Leon (2022) finds substantial wage penalties averaging
20% across multiple PSM specifications in Spain. However, most PSM
studies focus on single countries, limiting both result generalizability
and cross-national comparison validity.

2.2. Empirical review
2.2.1. The prevalence and wage effects of educational mismatch
Educational mismatch reflects the broader phenomenon of educational expansion driven by governments’ efforts to upgrade human
capital and provide remedial training for adult workers in response to
skill deficits generated by technological change, globalization, and political development strategies (Sevilla and Farías, 2020). This expansion
has created systematic imbalances between supply and demand for educated workers, where educational supply growth in developed countries
frequently outpaces demand growth due to firms’ adjustment lags, wage
rigidities, and labor market frictions (Davia et al., 2017). Ghignoni
and Verashchagina (2014) demonstrate that significant cross-country
differences in educational mismatch patterns stem from variations in
labor market institutions and policies, including employment protection legislation, vocational training systems, and active labor market
programs. The empirical literature has extensively documented educational mismatch effects across diverse labor market outcomes (Clark
et al., 2017; Sloane et al., 2020; McGuinness et al., 2017), consistently finding that returns to surplus education are substantially lower
than returns to required education (Levels et al., 2014; Li and Miller,
2015; Rubb, 2014). This pattern indicates systematic underutilization
of human capital, with educational mismatch generating lower employment rates, reduced wages for overeducated workers (Fredriksson
et al., 2018; Tang and Wang, 2021; Tran et al., 2025), and decreased
productivity due to skill underutilization (Cervantes and Cooper, 2022).
However, the existing literature presents conflicting theoretical perspectives regarding educational mismatch persistence: while traditional
human capital theory characterizes it as temporary disequilibrium that
markets eventually correct (Grunau and Pecoraro, 2017; Arranz and
García-Serrano, 2025), several empirical evidence suggests persistence
throughout working careers, contradicting career mobility theory’s prediction (Kupets, 2016; Korpi and Tåhlin, 2009; Charalambidou and
McIntosh, 2021).
The existing empirical evidence on educational mismatch demonstrates substantial methodological evolution and variation in wage
effect estimates (Chevalier and Lindley, 2006; Sattinger and Hartog, 2013). Given overeducation’s negative impacts on both allocative efficiency and individual welfare (Tsai, 2010; Sáez et al., 2016),
the literature has focused disproportionately on this form of mismatch, particularly as it may exacerbate inequalities across demographic groups (Aleksynska and Tritah, 2013). Although most OECD

2.2.2. Gender differences and alternative measure of educational mismatch
The wage effects of educational mismatch exhibit substantial heterogeneity across genders in OECD countries, shaped profoundly by cultural and institutional factors. While women are more prone to overeducation than men (Capsada-Munsech, 2019), the gender overeducation
gap narrows among those with flexible work options, such as remote
work (Santiago-Vela and Mergener, 2022). Overeducation potentially
exacerbates existing gender wage disparities (Park, 2021), with women
typically experiencing greater wage penalties than men (McGuinness
and Sloane, 2011; Pascual-Sáez and Lanza-Leon, 2022), particularly
in skilled occupational markets (Didier, 2024). However, mixed empirical evidence also exists, with some studies reporting similar wage
penalties across genders (De Santis et al., 2022; Didier, 2024) or lower
overeducation penalties for women (Nordin et al., 2010; Boll and
Leppin, 2016), indicating complex interactions between institutional
structures, societal norms, and labor market dynamics. Sun and Kim
(2022) demonstrate that women suffer 43% higher wage penalty than
men in Korea; meanwhile, high-ability men suffer lower wage penalties
than their low-ability counterparts, while women exhibit an inverted Ushaped pattern across ability distributions. Gender-specific educational
choices, substantially shaped by cultural norms, disproportionately
allocate women to lower-remuneration sectors such as education, arts,

An important strand of literature emphasizes the conceptual and
empirical distinction between educational mismatch and skills mismatch, challenging the traditional human capital assumption that educational attainment reliably indicates worker capability (Rim and Kim,
2023). While human capital theory treats education as a proxy for
skills, screening theory suggests that skill levels are differentially distributed within educational categories, with employers assigning varying weights to educational credentials and actual competencies (Park
and Arce, 2019). This theoretical distinction has empirical support,
with Badillo-Amador and Vila (2013) demonstrating weak correlations
between educational and skill mismatches in Spanish labor markets,
where both forms significantly affect wages. Generally, overskilling
wage penalties are lower than overeducation penalties (McGuinness
et al., 2018); meanwhile, Mavromaras et al. (2013) find that combined overeducation and overskilling generate the most severe negative
labor market outcomes using Australian HILDA data. The literature
has developed refined measurement approaches distinguishing between
genuine and apparent forms of mismatch based on skill alignment and
job satisfaction dimensions. Chevalier (2003) pioneered an alternative
classification method using job satisfaction as a reflection of skill utilization, categorizing overeducation combined with low job satisfaction
as genuine overeducation and overeducation with high satisfaction
as apparent overeducation. This methodological innovation has been
widely adopted, with subsequent studies (Flisi et al., 2017; Caroleo
and Pastore, 2018; Gaeta et al., 2023) demonstrating that genuine and
apparent overeducation exert distinctly different wage effects. Longitudinal evidence from Mavromaras et al. (2013) analyzing Australian
university graduates (2001–2007) reveals that wage penalties are statistically significant only when overeducation and overskilling occur
concurrently, while Pecoraro (2016) using Swiss household survey data
(1999–2004) finds that only genuine overeducation imposes sizable
wage penalties on the working-age population. Contemporary empirical
evidence reveals substantial heterogeneity in mismatch combinations,
with Cultrera et al. (2025) documenting that among PhD holders, 43%
experience apparent overeducation while 36% face genuine overeducation. Furthermore, Cultrera et al. (2022) and Serikbayeva and Abdulla
(2022) demonstrate that workers experiencing multiple simultaneous
mismatches face the highest wage penalties, confirming that penalty
magnitudes vary systematically across different mismatch combinations. These findings suggest that accounting for skill mismatch is
beneficial for eliminating unobserved confounders in estimating educational mismatch effects on wages, as the distinction between genuine
and apparent mismatch provides a more precise identification strategy
that controls for worker heterogeneity in skills and preferences.

3.2. Propensity score matching (PSM)
Individual heterogeneity constitutes a critical identification challenge when estimating the causal effect of educational mismatch on
wages (Bauer, 2002; Korpi and Tåhlin, 2009; Nielsen et al., 2011).
Workers select into mismatch status based on both observable characteristics (preferences, ability, job search strategies) and unobservable
attributes (motivation, risk tolerance, information quality), generating
correlation between mismatch and wage determinants that confounds
ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates. Propensity score matching addresses this selection problem by constructing comparable treatment
and control groups with similar observable characteristics, approximating a randomized experiment where assignment to mismatch status
is independent of potential outcomes conditional on covariates. PSM

3.3. Potential mechanisms and mediation framework
To identify the causal pathways through which educational mismatch affects wages, I employ the classical three-step mediation framework developed by Baron and Kenny (1986) to quantify the role
of intermediary variables linking mismatch status to earnings outcomes. The mediation approach addresses a fundamental question: to
what extent does the effect of educational mismatch on wages operate indirectly through observable labor market characteristics versus
directly through unobserved channels? The Baron–Kenny framework
decomposes total effects by estimating how mismatch affects mediators,
how mediators influence wages conditional on mismatch, and how
these relationships produce indirect effects. Given potential sample

Baron–Kenny framework. Beyond Eq. (1), the three-step procedure

reaches 1.09 years in Italy and 0.99 years in France, compared to
0.46 years across the full sample. These countries share strong vocational training traditions and credentialing systems that enable workers
to access skilled positions through apprenticeships, on-the-job training,
and professional certification pathways rather than formal tertiary
credentials. The prevalence of undereducation in these contexts suggests that job requirements increasingly demand formal qualifications
that existing workers obtained through alternative routes, potentially
reflecting credential inflation or changing employer screening practices rather than genuine skill deficits. This cross-national variation
in mismatch patterns motivates our comparative institutional analysis,
as it indicates that educational systems, labor market regulations, and
credentialing norms fundamentally shape how workers sort into jobs
relative to their formal qualifications.

4.2. Variable definitions and summary statistics
Table 1 exhibits descriptive statistics disaggregated by educational
matching status. The sample shows substantial mismatch prevalence:
53% of workers are appropriately matched, while 31% experience
overeducation and 16% undereducation. This distribution indicates
widespread credential-job misalignment across OECD labor markets,
with nearly half of workers in positions either above or below their
educational qualifications. It is noteworthy that wage differentials follow a monotonic pattern across mismatch categories. Overeducated
workers earn substantially lower wages than matched counterparts,
who in turn earn less than undereducated workers. The total wage gap
between overeducated and undereducated groups reaches 32%, providing preliminary evidence consistent with assignment theory predictions
that workers in positions below their educational level face significant
earnings penalties while those in positions above their credentials receive premiums. Furthermore, demographic characteristics differ significantly across groups. Women comprise 53% of overeducated workers
compared to 49% of matched and 45% of undereducated workers,
suggesting substantial gender disparities in educational mismatch that
may reflect occupational segregation, labor market discrimination, or
differential sorting into job types. Marriage rates increase monotonically from 67% among overeducated workers to 74% among matched
workers and 78% among undereducated workers. One possible explanation involves age and cohort effects, as undereducated workers tend
to be older and therefore more likely to be married while simultaneously belonging to cohorts with lower average educational attainment
relative to current job requirements. Another plausible explanation
concerns differential career trajectories, where workers who remain
with employers long enough to accumulate experience compensating
for credential deficits also have greater opportunity to form stable
partnerships. Additionally, married workers may exhibit different job
search and mobility patterns, potentially being more willing to accept
positions above their formal qualifications for job security or due to
geographic constraints related to dual-career households. Moreover,
substantial differences emerge in labor market experience and job
tenure. Undereducated workers average 22.1 years of total experience and 12.7 years with their current employer, nearly double the
15.1 years of experience and 6.7 years of tenure for overeducated
workers. This pattern suggests that undereducated workers have accumulated substantial firm-specific and general human capital compensating for formal credential deficits, consistent with employer learning
models where productivity signals emerge through demonstrated job
performance over time rather than initial educational screening. In
addition, cognitive skills measured through PIAAC assessments differ
modestly across groups. However, these skill differentials are relatively small compared to the large differences in formal education,
suggesting that skill heterogeneity alone cannot fully explain educational mismatch patterns and that credential-based sorting plays an
independent role. Finally, firm size, industry, and sectoral distributions
vary significantly across mismatch groups, with overeducated workers
disproportionately concentrated in small firms and private sector employment. These patterns indicate that educational mismatch correlates
with multiple observable characteristics simultaneously, motivating our
matching approach to address potential selection bias in causal wage
effect estimation.

5. Empirical findings
5.1. Wage effects of educational mismatch in benchmark models
Table 3 presents baseline estimates of educational mismatch wage
effects using the augmented Mincer specification with Heckman twostep correction for sample selection bias. Importantly, considering the
different mechanisms of labor market allocation of men and women (Di
Stasio et al., 2016), I ran analyses separately by gender. The statistically
significant inverse Mills ratios across all specifications confirm that
non-random selection into employment generates bias in conventional
wage regressions, with larger selection effects for women than men
and the pooled sample. This pattern suggests that unobserved characteristics influencing employment probability correlate more strongly
with potential wages for women, potentially reflecting greater labor
force participation selectivity among female workers related to family responsibilities, reservation wages, or occupational preferences.
The central findings confirm substantial wage penalties for overeducation and premiums for undereducation across all specifications.
In the pooled sample, overeducated workers earn 13.8% less than
appropriately matched counterparts with equivalent educational attainment, while undereducated workers earn 9.9% more than matched
workers in similar positions. Furthermore, gender-disaggregated estimates reveal systematic differences in mismatch penalties. Women
experience larger overeducation penalties (−14.3%) compared to men
(−12.6%), a differential of 1.7 percentage points representing a 13%
larger relative penalty. Conversely, women receive smaller undereducation premiums (7.5%) than men (11.3%), a gap of 3.8 percentage
points. These asymmetric gender differences suggest that labor market mechanisms generating mismatch wage effects operate differently
for men and women, potentially reflecting occupational segregation,

5.3. Potential mechanisms
A central question in educational mismatch research concerns the
channels through which overeducation generates wage penalties. Understanding these transmission mechanisms is crucial for distinguishing between competing theoretical explanations, specifically whether
penalties reflect genuine productivity differences, labor market sorting processes, or employer discrimination, and for designing targeted
policy interventions accordingly. To investigate these potential mechanisms, I implement mediation analysis following Baron and Kenny
(1986), decomposing total wage effects into direct components and indirect effects operating through seven specific channels spanning three
conceptual domains. Job characteristics encompass occupational sorting and supervisory positions, representing employer screening mechanisms where mismatched workers face restricted access to desirable
employment features. Occupational sorting captures the job competition channel where overeducated workers are systematically crowded
into lower-skill occupational categories due to insufficient high-skill
positions, reflecting the labor queue model. I measure this mechanism
through ISCO skill classification, categorizing workers into four levels

5.6. Robustness checks: bandwidth selection and key variable sensitivity

There is no consensus in the literature regarding the most precise
PSM estimator, particularly given the notable variation in the distribution and sample sizes between treatment and control groups across
countries. PSM estimates can be sensitive to bandwidth specification in
KM, particularly given substantial variation in sample sizes and propensity score distributions across countries. To assess robustness, I conduct
sensitivity analysis comparing KM estimates across three bandwidth
specifications. Different bandwidth settings within the Kernel model
influence ATT estimates, so I verify the robustness of our findings
across multiple bandwidths. Wider bandwidths incorporate more control observations in constructing counterfactual outcomes, increasing
efficiency but potentially introducing bias if distant matches differ systematically from treated units. Narrower bandwidths restrict matching
to very similar observations, reducing bias but potentially increasing variance, particularly in smaller samples or with limited common
support. Table 9 presents the Kernel estimates of the wage effects
of educational mismatch under two distinct bandwidth settings (0.5
and 0.01), compared with the common rule-of-thumb setting (0.2 ×
standard error). Results show significant variability in the wage penalty
for overeducation across different bandwidth choices. For overeducation, wider bandwidths generally yield larger absolute penalties in
most countries, with effects increasing by 5–8 percentage points in
Belgium, Denmark, Netherlands, and Norway compared to our main
specification. This pattern suggests that more distant matched controls
have lower wages on average, pulling estimated treatment effects toward more negative values. Conversely, the narrowest bandwidth often
generates smaller penalties, as very close matches likely share more unobserved characteristics with treated units, reducing estimated effects.
Notable exceptions include Britain and Spain, where the narrowest
bandwidth produces larger overeducation penalties than wider specifications, possibly reflecting nonlinear relationships between propensity
scores and wages where very close matches reveal stronger negative effects. For undereducation, bandwidth variation generates larger
magnitude changes, with estimates at bandwidth 0.01 typically exceeding those at 0.5 by 5–10 percentage points across most countries. This sensitivity likely reflects smaller undereducated samples
limiting common support at narrow bandwidths, forcing matches that
differ more substantially on unobservables. Japan and Chile show
particularly pronounced differences, with undereducation premiums
increasing from 3.1% and 6.4% at wide bandwidths to 14.5% and
22.7% at narrow bandwidths respectively, suggesting highly localized treatment effects concentrated among workers with very specific
propensity score ranges. Despite these magnitude variations, our main
substantive conclusions remain robust across specifications. Overeducation generates significant negative wage effects in all countries
except Chile and Greece regardless of bandwidth, while undereducation
produces positive premiums in 15–19 countries depending on specification. The relative cross-country ranking of effect magnitudes also
remains largely stable, with Britain, Korea, and Netherlands consistently exhibiting large overeducation penalties and Chile, Britain, and
Japan showing large undereducation premiums across all bandwidths.
This consistency provides confidence that our main findings reflect
genuine treatment effects rather than arbitrary specification choices.
I retain the 0.2 standard deviation bandwidth for main results as it
balances bias–variance tradeoffs while following established guidelines
in the matching literature.

6. Conclusion and policy implications
Educational mismatch represents a critical labor market phenomenon affecting worker productivity, earnings, and human capital utilization across developed economies. Despite extensive research documenting mismatch prevalence and associated wage differentials, the
causal relationship between educational mismatch and wages remains
contested due to selection bias from unobserved heterogeneity. Workers
sorting into mismatched positions may differ systematically in ability,
motivation, or preferences from those achieving appropriate matches,
confounding observational comparisons. This study addresses these
identification challenges by implementing propensity score matching
across 20 OECD countries using PIAAC data, explicitly controlling for
cognitive skills and family background as ability proxies that traditional
credential-based approaches cannot capture. Our analysis contributes
to the literature by providing rigorous causal evidence on mismatch
wage effects, systematically examining cross-national institutional variation, decomposing aggregate effects by gender and mismatch type, and
assessing robustness across multiple matching algorithms and specifications.
The main empirical results are summarized as follows. First, baseline Heckman two-step estimates addressing sample selection bias
demonstrate that educational mismatch generates substantial wage
effects in pooled cross-country samples. Overeducation imposes significant wage penalties approximately -14%, while undereducation produces wage premiums around 10% relative to appropriately matched
workers. Gender-disaggregated analysis reveals that overeducated workers of both genders suffer significant wage losses; however, women
experience overeducation penalties 13% larger than men on average, while simultaneously receiving smaller undereducation premiums,
indicating significant gender asymmetry in how mismatches affect
wages. Second, PSM estimates addressing selection on observables
reveal that accounting for unobserved heterogeneity through cognitive skill controls and matching substantially alters estimated effects across countries. Britain, Korea, and Japan exhibit the largest
overeducation penalties (−36.6%, −21.9%, and −19.6% respectively),
while Slovakia demonstrates the smallest (−7.2%). Northern and Western European countries exhibit within-region heterogeneity ranging

These findings suggest that policymakers should prioritize facilitating occupational mobility and skill redeployment through targeted
upskilling programs, portable certification systems, and structured
internal promotion pathways.
I am aware of several limitations in this study’s scope and design. First, measurement remains central to mismatch research, yet
our self-assessment approach may introduce bias through subjective
evaluations and ambiguous reference points. Such bias may be amplified in cross-national comparisons given institutional diversity. Future
studies should develop refined cross-nationally comparable measurement strategies incorporating multiple data sources. Second, due to
data limitations, our analysis omits cultural factors and non-cognitive

skills that affect mismatch and wages, limiting our ability to eliminate unobservable heterogeneity and conduct robust cross-national
comparisons (Bühler et al., 2020). Future research should incorporate comprehensive skill measures and enhanced survey designs to
strengthen causal inference.
