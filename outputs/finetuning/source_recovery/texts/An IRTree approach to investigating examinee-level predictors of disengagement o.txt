ABSTRACT

Low test-taking effort remains a persistent problem in low-stakes
assessments as it introduces construct-irrelevant variance into the
scores which can compromise the validity interpretations made with
these scores. This study applies the IRTree Model for Disengagement
to jointly estimate disengagement and science ability in the U.S.A.
sample of the 2022 Programme for International Student Assessment.
We examined how disengagement relates to student-level characteristics, including gender, grade level, whether the language spoken at
home is different from that of the test, socioeconomic status, growth
mindset, sense of belonging, family support, and experiences of bullying. Results indicated that male students and those in higher grade
levels were significantly more likely to disengage. Language background also exhibited a credible effect, with students speaking
English at home showing higher disengagement. Socioeconomic status, sense of belonging, and family support showed directional tendencies that provided moderate evidence of association with
disengagement, whereas growth mindset, bullying, and educational
expectations exhibited no significant effects. A strong negative latent
correlation between science ability and disengagement confirmed
that lower-performing students were substantially more likely to disengage. The study underscores the importance of accounting for individual differences in test-taking motivation interventions and
highlights the utility of IRTree models for producing more valid inferences from low-stakes assessment data.

Introduction
Assessment plays a central role in how institutions of education function, with test
scores widely used to gauge learning progress and evaluate program effectiveness. A
fundamental assumption underlying such assessments is that a student’s test score
accurately reflects their position on an ability continuum (Wise & Kong, 2005).
However, extensive research has demonstrated that test scores can be significantly
influenced by various extraneous and irrelevant factors, introducing irrelevant variance
into the scores (AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014). One particularly concerning factor is
low test-taking effort, especially common in low-stakes contexts, where test results
carry minimal personal consequences or benefits for examinees. In such cases, scores

may fail to represent true ability, leading to biased estimates of item parameters and
student proficiency (Debeer et al., 2014; Wise & DeMars, 2005). This, in turn, can
distort interpretations of test results.
Low effort is a critical issue across multiple testing contexts, from group-based
accountability systems that rank institutions based on achievement gains (Wise &
DeMars, 2005) to large-scale assessments used to compare performance at the school,
district, state, or national level (e.g., Goldhammer et al., 2016; Rios & Soland, 2021).
Understanding the conditions under which low effort occurs, and the factors associated
with it, is therefore essential for mitigating its impact and ensuring the validity of
test-based inferences. Prior research has examined these issues within a specific theoretical framework and with different modeling approaches. In the present study, we
build upon this foundation (which we describe next) and introduce distinctive elements
that set the particularity of the present study.
A theory of examinee effort
Motivation significantly influences the achievement-related choices examinees make
during testing, such as whether to persist or exert effort (Pintrich & Schunk, 2008).
From a cognitive perspective, motivated and engaged examinees rely on internal beliefs
developed over time through interactions with socializers (Barron & Hulleman, 2015;
Pintrich & Schunk, 2008). Expectancy-Value Theory (EVT), developed by Eccles and
Wigfield (2002), offers a widely accepted framework for understanding how these
beliefs influence engagement in achievement contexts. EVT identifies two central
cognitive components that shape effort and performance: expectancy for success (“Can
I do this task?”) and subjective task value (“Why should I engage in this task?”).
In testing contexts, EVT has frequently been used to explain differences in test-taking
motivation (Barron & Hulleman, 2015; Conley, 2012; Flake et al., 2015). Specifically,
it explains how students’ beliefs about their likelihood of success and the value they
assign to a test affect how much effort they are willing to invest. When both expectancy and value are low, examinees are less likely to engage meaningfully, resulting in
low effort and scores that may not reflect their true abilities. This introduces
construct-irrelevant variance, threatening the validity of inferences drawn from test
scores (AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014). Such low expectancy-value beliefs are particularly
common in testing environments where students face little to no consequences for
their performance. One such testing environment is the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA), an international large-scale assessment that measures students’ knowledge and skills at the end of compulsory education, and provides governments and policymakers with valuable data on how students in their country or
economy compare to those in other participating systems (OECD, 2023a). The PISA,
however, does not report individual test scores to students, teachers, or school staff
(OECD, 2023b). As a result, while the stakes can be high for education systems and
national policy, they are essentially nonexistent for the students taking the test
(Goldhammer et al., 2016; Rios & Soland, 2021), creating a context where student
motivation to perform well may be substantially diminished. Although related to
broader constructs like academic engagement, test-taking effort is a distinct, situational
phenomenon. Academic engagement refers to students’ ongoing behavioral, emotional,

and cognitive involvement in learning across contexts and over time (Fredricks et al.,
2004), whereas test-taking disengagement describes an often temporary withdrawal of
effort during an assessment, often evident through low-effort responses (Wise &
DeMars, 2005). This distinction highlights that examinees may generally be engaged
learners yet still disengage in low-stakes testing situations, leading to score outcomes
that do not reflect their true abilities.
Within the EVT framework, researchers have sought to operationalize examinee
effort at both the test and item levels. However, test-level measures often fall short in
capturing the dynamic fluctuations in effort that can occur within a single testing
session, as students may respond with high engagement to some items while disengaging on others (Pastor et al., 2019). This limitation has led to the development of
item-level approaches that allow for more granular assessments of effort. Crucially, the
adoption of technology-based testing has enabled the collection of response process
data, such as response times, which form the basis of most these item-level approaches
in literature.
Wise and Kong (2005), building on the earlier work of Schnipke and Scrams (1997),
first proposed using item-level response times to classify responses into two categories:
rapid guesses, responses made so quickly that the examinee likely did not exhibit solution behavior (i.e., read or consider the item), and non-rapid responses, that exceed a
minimal time threshold and likely reflect some sort of solution behavior. Of note, using
response time to infer effort has been shown to be valid mostly in low-stakes settings
where students have little personal incentive to perform well (Pastor et al. 2019; Wise
& Kingsbury, 2016; Wise & Kong, 2005). That contrasts with high-stakes contexts, where
short response times may instead signal speededness, where external time pressure,
rather than low motivation, drives rapid responding (Schnipke & Scrams, 1997).
Building on this foundation, several methods have been proposed to establish the
response time threshold that distinguishes rapid guesses from non-rapid responses
likely translating effortful responses. The most straightforward approach involves applying a fixed time cutoff across all items, such as classifying any response made in under
5 s as a rapid guess. Wise and DeMars (2005), for example, applied a 3-s threshold
to identify rapid guesses on the Measures of Academic Progress (MAP) assessments.
Given the computerized adaptive format and large item pool of MAP, a fixed threshold
offered a practical and scalable solution. However, this k-seconds approach has tradeoffs:
overly long thresholds may result in false positives (classifying effortful responses as
rapid guesses), while overly short thresholds risk false negatives (failing to detect
non-solution behavior responses).
To address these limitations, alternative methods have leveraged the assumption that
response times for individual items may follow a bimodal distribution (DeMars, 2007;
Pastor et al., 2019). In such cases, one distribution (the one with the lower mode)
reflects rapid guessing, and the second, solution behavior. Thresholds are typically set
at the point of lowest frequency between the two modes. Some researchers determine
this intersection visually by inspecting item-level response time histograms, while
others apply more formal methods such as Mixture Log-Normal (MLN) modeling to
estimate the cutoff objectively (Ulitzsch et al., 2020). Although conceptually appealing,
the bimodal approach has practical limitations. Response time distributions are often
not cleanly separable, with substantial overlap between the two modes, leading to

potential misclassification. Furthermore, items with inherently short response demands
or those exhibiting unimodal or J-shaped distributions challenge the core assumption
of bimodality and complicate threshold estimation (Wise, 2017).
Another approach to setting response time thresholds combines response time with
response accuracy, based on the non-effortful random response assumption, the idea
that the probability of a correct response without effort should not exceed the chance
level of correct response. Under that assumption, Guo et al. (2016) proposed the
Cumulative Proportion Correct (CUMP) method, which identifies the threshold as the
response time at which the cumulative proportion correct first increases to chance
level. However, these methods also have limitations, particularly for difficult items,
where even effortful responses may fall below chance, making it hard to identify a
meaningful threshold. Moreover, Wise (2017) found that rapid guessers in his study
selected the second answer option 34% of the time, well above the expected 25% in
a four-choice item, suggesting that non-effortful behavior may yield a proportion
correct beyond chance level.
A further category of methods called the Normative Thresholds (NT; Wise &
Ma, 2012) offered a practical and relatively robust alternative for identifying
non-effortful responses. In this approach, the threshold is defined as a fixed percentage (e.g., 10%) of the average response time for a given item across all test
takers. When this value exceeds a set upper limit (e.g., 10 s), the threshold is capped
at that upper limit. Compared to the common k-second rule, NTs have been shown
to reduce false positives and false negatives (Wise & Ma, 2012). They are also highly
practical in operational contexts, including computerized adaptive testing and large
item banks, where item-level visual inspection or fitting mixture models may be
less practical.
Once rapid guesses are identified, a common strategy for adjusting scores is to filter
out examinees with a high proportion of such responses. This is often done using the
Response Time Effort (RTE) index, which quantifies the proportion of items for which
an examinee exhibited solution behavior. Examinees falling below a set RTE threshold
(e.g., 0.8 or 0.9) are excluded from analyses (Kong et al., 2007; Sundre & Wise, 2003).
While straightforward and practical, this method risks removing valuable item-level
data that examinees removed did not rapid-guess on and may artificially inflate
sample-level mean scores (Rios et al., 2017). To address these issues, several alternative
approaches have been proposed. These include treating rapid guesses as incorrect
responses (Wright, 2019), classifying them as missing and applying Effort-Moderated
IRT (Wise & DeMars, 2006), or imputing them using Effort-Moderated Imputation
(Hauser & Kingsbury, 2009). However, a common limitation across these methods is
the assumption that ability and effort are independent, a premise that has been increasingly challenged (Rios & Soland, 2021).
More recently, Leventhal and Pastor (2024) introduced the IRTree model for disengagement, a latent variable approach that avoids the problematic assumption of
independence between ability and effort. In this model, disengagement is the latent
variable that underlies the observed pattern of effortful and non-effortful responses,
a conceptualization supported by Liu et al. (2019). By modeling disengagement alongside ability, the IRTree framework allows for a more accurate estimation of both traits,
as well as item parameters, especially in datasets with high rates of rapid guessing

(Leventhal & Pastor, 2024). In the present study, we examine the extent to which
examinee-level variables predict disengagement as conceptualized within the IRTree
framework.
Examinee-level predictors of effort and rationale of the study
Several studies have explored examinee-level factors influencing test taking effort.
Using the 26-item HEIghten Critical Thinking Assessment, Rios and Guo (2020)
found gender to be a statistically significant predictor of test-taking effort in two of
the three countries studied, with males exhibiting significantly more non-effortful
responses than females. These results were corroborated by DeMars et al. (2013)
findings which showed that male students had lower average response times than
females. Further, Rios and Soland (2022) showed, using the PISA 2018 dataset, that
male students were more prone to engaging in rapid guessing behavior compared
to their female peers. Wise et al. (2009) was the only study that used rapid guessing
as an indication of low test-taking effort and that concluded a non-significant effect
of gender.
Additional factors contributing to disengagement include the year in school, language
background, socioeconomic status, prior ability, and self-reported test difficulty. For
example, Rios and Guo (2020) observed that junior and senior students exhibited
higher levels of disengagement than freshmen and sophomores. They also noted that
test takers who perceived the test as too difficult tended to provide more non-effortful
responses than those who considered it average or not difficult. Prior ability, measured
through college admission scores, predicted non-effortful responses in two of the three
countries analyzed by Rios and Guo (2020). On the PISA 2018, Rios and Soland (2022)
found that students who most often spoke a language at home that differed from the
test language (language background) were more likely to engage in rapid guessing or
provide non effortful responses. Likewise, they found socioeconomic status to be
negatively correlated with rapid guessing behavior.
However, none of these studies accounted for the potential relationship between
test-taking effort and underlying ability in their operationalization of effort, as they
explored its correlates. For instance, Rios and Soland (2022) modeled rapid guessing
as a binary outcome using a probit link, incorporating country-, item-, and examinee-level
predictors. Rios and Guo (2020) used the number of non-effortful responses as a
continuous dependent variable in a regression framework, ignoring examinee ability.
While informative, these approaches do not model effort and ability as interrelated
latent traits, leaving open the possibility of biased inferences. In contrast, the present
study applies an IRTree model for disengagement that jointly estimates both latent
variables. Through this simultaneous estimation, the model enables the trait of interest
to inform the estimation of disengagement and vice versa. This improves the precision
and validity of disengagement estimates, leading to more accurate inferences about its
correlates. This study also expands our understanding of disengagement by exploring
additional predictors unique to the 8th and most recent cycle (2022) of the PISA.
Finally, the 2022 PISA cognitive dataset offers a unique opportunity to explore disengagement post pandemic in a context where performance saw an unprecedented decline
across OECD countries (OECD, 2023a).

Methods
Measure description
The PISA is a triennial evaluation aimed at gauging the abilities and skills of students
approaching the end of compulsory education in solving real-world challenges (OECD,
2023a). Participating students from various countries are tested in three core disciplines:
Mathematics, Science, and Reading, with an innovative discipline every cycle (e.g.,
critical thinking).
In our study, we focus on the science core discipline since about 2 out of every 5 students received two science-based clusters. Moreover, the item pool was relatively large, 115
items organized in units of 1 to 5 items. A given 30 min science cluster is made of five to
six units which amounts to a total of 38 to 40 items per examinee. These items are either
simple multiple choice, complex multiple choice or constructed responses. Most items, 105
out of 115 were dichotomously scored (1 = correct; 0 = incorrect) and the other 10 items
were polytomously scored (0 = no-credit, 1 = partial credit, 2 = full credit).
Sample
In this study, we considered examinees from the United States of America (U.S.A.).
In the U.S.A. a total of 4,552 students representing 154 schools were tested. Of these,
2,201 students were assigned forms with two science clusters. Not all students were
assigned the same two science clusters, which means different groups of students
received different sets of items. Since these were planned missing data, we assumed
the missingness to be Missing at Random (MAR, Enders, 2010). Also, not all students
had complete responses on the predictors selected for the analysis. To assess the extent
and nature of missing data, we conducted bivariate correlations between predictors
missingness indicators and all other model variables, including total science score and
RTE. These correlations were small (|r| < .12), though some reached statistical significance, suggesting that the assumption of Missing Completely at Random (MCAR)
may not fully hold. Therefore, we implemented model-based imputation within the
Bayesian estimation framework using the factorized equations method (Enders, 2010).
This assumes the missingness to be MAR and uses all available observed information
to estimate missing values as part of the model-fitting process.
Model specification
Disengagement is conceptualized as the latent variable manifesting as rapid-guessing
during testing. Intuitively, one might model these dichotomous rapid guesses using a
logistic regression (e.g., Rios & Guo, 2020), which treats disengagement as independent
from students’ science ability. However, this can be problematic because ability and
disengagement are empirically linked as discussed above. The IRTree Model of
Disengagement (Leventhal & Pastor, 2024) allows for the joint estimation of the two
latent variables, science ability and disengagement, within a single framework.
The IRTree framework models item responses as the outcome of a sequential
decision-making process that an examinee follows when responding to a test item. In
Leventhal and Pastor (2024) model, examinee i interacting with item j undergoes two

Predictors in the model
Gender (GENDER): This dummy coded variable (Male = 0, Female = 1) was obtained
with the PISA question “Are you male or female?”. Previous studies (DeMars et al.,
2013; Rios & Guo, 2020; Rios & Soland, 2022) have identified gender as a significant
predictor of test-taking effort with males displaying higher tendencies to disengage
compared to females.
Language background (LANG): This variable determines if the language spoken
predominantly at home differs from the test language (language of the test = 0, other
language = 1). The question on the PISA questionnaire was “What language do you
speak at home most of the time?” That variable was further re-coded to create the
variable of interest (language background) equal to 1 when the language of the test
was the language selected by the student and 0 otherwise. Rios and Soland (2022)
found this to significantly explain more than any other variable in their model the
probability of rapid guessing on the PISA science 2018. Also, the language of the test
was English as we used a sample of U.S.A. examinees.
Economic, Social, and Cultural Status (ESCS) Index: The PISA ESCS is internationally recognized as a gold standard measure of socioeconomic status (SES, OECD,
2023a, p.183). The index is calculated for each student based on their answers to
questions related to mother’s and father’s, or guardian’s education, their occupation
and their home possessions, and the student’s own sense of socioeconomic status called
subjective SES. Practically speaking, the final ESCS score for each student was calculated as the arithmetic sum of the standardized highest parental occupation status
(Ganzeboom & Treiman, 2003), highest education of parents in years and home possessions (IRT scaled) score which was subsequently standardized to have a mean of
0 and a standard deviation of 1 across the OECD countries. In the PISA science 2018,
Higher SES examinees were found to have lower probability of rapid guessing than
low SES examinees (Rios & Soland, 2022).
International Grade (IG): In the U.S.A. PISA 2022 science sample, students ranged
from Grade 9 to Grade 12 (dummy coded). The modal grade for students aged 15 years

and 3 months to 16 years and 2 months, the target population for PISA in the U.S.A.,
was Grade 10 (sophomore year of high school). Nonetheless, variation in grade placement exists due to factors such as grade retention, delayed school entry, or interruptions
in schooling, which may result in students being placed in a lower grade, and early
school entry or grade acceleration, which may result in students placed in a higher
grade. Given that only five students were in Grade 12, Grades 11 and 12 were combined into a single category. Rios and Guo (2020), in a study of college students,
found that more junior students exhibited lower levels of disengagement compared to
those in more advanced years (freshmen and sophomores vs. juniors and seniors).
Although this variable has not been examined in the PISA dataset for high school
students, we hypothesize a similar pattern for PISA 2022, with lower grade levels
associated with reduced disengagement.
Growth Mindset (GROW): This variable captures students’ beliefs about the malleability of intelligence, derived from IRT scaling of their responses to items such as
“Your intelligence is something about you that you cannot change very much” and “Some
people are just not good at mathematics, no matter how hard they study.” Higher scores
reflect stronger growth mindset beliefs. Although growth mindset has been examined
primarily in classroom learning and performance setbacks (e.g., Yeager & Dweck,
2020), a difficult, evaluative test is itself an academic challenge, so the same mechanisms should generalize to test taking (Hulleman et al., 2017). Theoretically, growth
mindset fosters mastery goals that are linked to higher intrinsic task value and persistence (Wigfield & Cambria, 2010), and within EVT, the intrinsic valuing of the task
and expectancies for success directly drive effort (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002). Accordingly,
we anticipate that students with stronger growth-mindset beliefs will exhibit greater
test-taking effort and lower likelihood of disengagement.
Highest expected educational level (EXP_ED): Coded using the 2011 version of the
International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED), this variable ranges from
ISCED 2 (Lower secondary education) to ISCED 8 (Doctoral or equivalent). Research
has established that expectations are self-fulfilling prophecies, meaning that students
who expect to attain higher levels of education are more likely to act in ways that
make those expectations a reality (Fishman, 2022). Within a test-taking context, such
expectations can translate into greater test-taking effort: students who envision continuing their education have stronger incentives to demonstrate competence and maintain self-consistency with their future goals. Conversely, lower educational expectations
may reduce the perceived value of performing well on a test, leading to disengagement.
This mechanism aligns with Expectancy–Value Theory, in which perceived utility and
attainment value drive the willingness to invest effort in academic tasks.
Sense of belonging (BELONG): This scale consists of six polytomous items that
included statements such as: “Agree/disagree: I feel like an outsider (or left out of things)
at school”, “Agree/disagree: I make friends easily at school.”, “Agree/disagree: I feel like
I belong at school.”. The scores were obtained using IRT scaling from the ratings of
students to these polytomous items across all countries/economies. Meta-analyses on
school sense of belonging (Allen et al., 2018; Korpershoek et al., 2020) have shown
that it is linked to a wide range of educational outcomes, including academic motivation, engagement, and achievement. Because test-taking effort represents a specific

International Journal of Testing

91

form of academic engagement under evaluative conditions, students with a stronger
sense of belonging are likely to invest more sustained effort during testing situations.
Family support (FAMSUP): This scale includes polytomous items like “How often
parents/family member: Discuss how well you are doing at school.”., How often parents/
family member: Spend time just talking with you.”, “How often parents/family member:
Ask you what you did in school that day.”. The scores were obtained using IRT scaling
from the ratings of students to these polytomous items across all countries/economies.
Family support has also been shown by research to facilitate student academic engagement (Roksa & Kinsley, 2019).
Being bullied (BULLIED): This variable was derived from students’ responses to
polytomous items such as “In the past 12 months, how often: I got hit or pushed around
by other students,” scaled using IRT based on responses across all countries/economies.
The scores reflect students’ frequency of exposure to bullying at school. Research shows
that experiences of bullying are negatively associated with academic engagement,
including students’ emotional connection to school, sense of safety, and motivation to

Model calibration
We fit the model to the data using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedure
in SAS, retaining 150,000 iterations after burning in 10000 and thinning by a factor
of 15. In the Bayesian framework, priors must be specified for each parameter to
define the space from which sampled values are drawn. In this analysis, all priors
were essentially noninformative (see Table 1). For item and ability parameters, including
difficulty, step, ability, and correlation parameters, we followed the same specification
as Leventhal and Pastor (2024), who employed noninformative priors. Similarly, noninformative priors were specified for the predictor weight parameters.

Following the assessment of convergence, the absolute fit of the IRTree Model for
Disengagement was evaluated using posterior predictive model checking (PPMC), a
Bayesian model assessment technique that compares observed data to data simulated
from the model’s posterior distribution (Sinharay, 2005). By determining whether the
model can reproduce key features of the observed data, PPMC provides an empirical
basis for evaluating model adequacy. This approach is particularly advantageous in
complex modeling contexts, such as the present study, where conventional goodness-offit tests are impractical (Sinharay et al., 2006).
To implement our PPMC, we drew 1000 random samples from the approximated joint
posterior distribution of the model parameters. For each sampled parameter set, a complete
dataset was simulated using the observed predictor values for each examinee, preserving
the structure of the original data. Specifically, simulated responses were generated only for
items that each examinee was observed to answer, thereby maintaining the missingness
pattern arising from science cluster assignment. The simulated datasets included two variables of interest: (a) rapid guessing indicators (binary: 1 = rapid guess, 0 = not) and (b) item
scores (binary for dichotomous items; 0, 1, or 2 for polytomous items).
Absolute model fit was then assessed using six discrepancy statistics Drrep, for each
replicated dataset r = 1,…,1000: (1) the mean number of rapid guesses across examinees,
(2) the standard deviation of number of rapid guesses, (3) the mean total score across
examinees, (4) the standard deviation of total score, (5) the mean Response Time
Effort (RTE) across examinees and (6) the standard deviation of RTE. These discrepancy statistics were chosen for their relevance to the model’s purpose (Gelman et al.,
1996; Sinharay et al., 2006), specifically detecting disengagement patterns and improving
score estimation in the presence of rapid guessing.

Subsequently, we evaluated the significance of predictors by examining the posterior
distributions of their regression coefficients. This involved reporting the Expected A
Posteriori (EAP) estimates, posterior standard deviations, and 95% Highest Posterior
Density (HPD) intervals. In line with conventional Bayesian reporting practices (Gelman
et al., 2013; Kruschke, 2015), coefficients whose 95% HPD intervals excluded zero
were interpreted as showing strong evidence of a non-zero effect. For parameters whose
95% HPD intervals included zero, we implemented an additional probabilistic criterion
based on posterior tail probabilities. Specifically, we computed the posterior probability
that each coefficient was greater than or less than zero, that is, P ( parameter > 0 | data)
and P ( parameter < 0 | data). If either of these probabilities exceeded .75, the parameter
was interpreted as showing moderate evidence of a directional effect (SAS, 2014;
Wagenmakers et al., 2010). A probability of .75 corresponds to odds of 3:1 in favor
of the hypothesis, which is noteworthy because this ratio aligns with the Bayes factor
cutoff of 3:1 commonly cited as moderate evidence, under the classification scheme
of Kass and Raftery (1995). As such, this two-tiered approach provides a useful compromise between overly conservative interpretations (e.g., requiring 95% certainty) and
excessive sensitivity to minor fluctuations (Lee & Wagenmakers, 2014), supporting a
graded interpretation of evidence.

Results
Descriptive statistics
Out of the 2,201 U.S.A. students who received science test forms, three in four (n = 1,683;
76.4%) engaged exclusively in solution behavior. The remaining 518 (23.6%) students
showed some rapid guessing: 183 (8.3%) on one item, 110 (5.0%) on two items, 50
(2.3%) on three items, and 175 (7.9%) on four or more items.
In terms of demographics, the sample was approximately balanced by gender (1,109;
50.4% females and 1 missing). Most examinees were in 10th grade (n = 1,648; 74.9%),
followed by 11th grade (n = 378; 17.2%) and 9th grade (n = 175; 7.9%). For students’
language background, 1,697 (77.1%) examinees took the test in English, 409 (18.6%)
in another language, and 95 (4.3%) were missing. Regarding educational expectations,
866 (39.4%) expected to complete at least a bachelor’s degree, 629 (28.6%) expected
less than a bachelor’s degree (ISCED 5 or lower), and 706 (32.1%) did not respond.

Discussion
This study examined student-level predictors of disengagement using the 2022 PISA
U.S.A. science sample by employing an IRTree that jointly modeled science ability and
disengagement. Unlike approaches that model disengagement with logistic regression
based only on rapid-guessing, the IRTree estimates both constructs simultaneously,
allowing each to inform the other. The IRTree embeds the inference about disengagement from response behaviors within a psychometric model that also accounts for the
science ability, yielding more precise and valid estimates of both processes, and thereby
more defensible conclusions about the correlates of disengagement.
Among the predictors examined, gender emerged as a robust correlate of disengagement, with female students being less likely to engage in rapid guessing as compared to male students, echoing prior work on gender differences in self-regulatory
behaviors and task investment (Finn, 2021; Rios & Soland, 2022). This pattern

98

J. M. MEHOU ET AL.

underscores the value of gender-sensitive supports that promote sustained effort in
low-stakes contexts. Grade level also showed clear differences. Tenth grade students
were markedly less likely to disengage than 11th graders, aligning with findings from
Rios and Guo (2020) who found that more junior college students exhibited lower
levels of disengagement compared to those in more advanced years (first-years and
sophomores vs. juniors and seniors). However, ninth graders were more likely to disengage than students in 11th grade. To help explain why this pattern may deviate
from prior research, we must recall that the PISA tests students at the end of compulsory education. As 9th graders are three years from leaving high school, the content
of the PISA may be too advanced for their skillset. Therefore, they might have shown
more disengagement because the items were more difficult, which would have made
them more likely to exhibit rapid guessing (e.g., Wise, 2006; Wolf et al., 1995).
Notably, whether or not students spoke English, the language used in the test, at
home had a significant impact. Those who indicated that English was the language
they spoke the most at home tended to disengage more often than students who spoke
a different language at home most of the time. This finding contrasts with prior evidence linking limited familiarity with the test language to greater disengagement (e.g.,
Rios & Soland, 2022) and likely reflects the composition of our sample. Approximately
18.58% of students (n = 409) spoke a non-English language at home, with 74.57%
(n = 306) of these students speaking Spanish and the remaining “another language” not
reported. Importantly, within this non-English language at home group, the majority
were second-generation (56.72%) or first-generation (19.07%) immigrants, and only
13.44% were native-born students (the 10.75% remaining were missing on their immigration status). Prior research has documented high educational motivation and aspirations among immigrant families (Suárez-Orozco & Suárez-Orozco, 1995), and OECD
(2006, p. 90) similarly reported that first- and second-generation students tend to
exhibit higher interest and enjoyment in learning compared with native-born peers.
Interest and enjoyment are key aspects that influence motivation as explained in the
EVT since one component of the theory is intrinsic motivation. Such motivational
profiles may contribute to lower disengagement among students who speak a non-English
language at home. Furthermore, whereas Rios and Soland (2022) analyzed data aggregated across all OECD countries, the present study focused exclusively on U.S.A.
examinees, whose demographic and linguistic contexts likely shape these patterns
differently.
Students reporting a stronger sense of belonging showed modestly lower odds of
rapid guessing. This result is consistent with evidence that school connectedness supports academic motivation and performance (Allen et al., 2018). Socioeconomic status
was also modestly related to lower disengagement, though the magnitude was small.
By contrast, family support showed a small positive relation with disengagement. Given
its size and uncertainty, we interpret this cautiously as it may capture heterogeneous
forms of “support” (e.g., pressure or control vs. encouragement, Yau et al., 2022).
Growth mindset and bullying showed no associations after accounting for other
predictors.
Methodologically, embedding disengagement within an IRTree that jointly estimates
science ability and effort strengthened inference. The strong negative latent correlation
between disengagement and science ability corroborates the theoretical dependence of

these processes. This also highlights the value of joint modeling the latent traits when
interpreting test scores subject to variable effort.
Limitations and future research
Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First,
the study focused exclusively on examinees from the U.S.A. This choice was made to
avoid overgeneralization, as some predictors used in our analysis, such as the ESCS
index, have been criticized for lacking cross-national comparability in PISA data
(OECD, 2023). For instance, the ESCS measure assumes a traditional nuclear family
structure and provides limited flexibility for students from nontraditional households
(e.g., same-sex parents, multi-generational families, or households with multiple guardians). Future research could extend this framework to cross-country comparisons to
assess whether similar patterns of disengagement hold internationally.
Second, we did not examine potential interactions among predictors. This decision reflects
the absence of clear theoretical justification for such interactions in the present study. Third,
although the current study found no association between growth mindset and disengagement, this result should be interpreted with caution. The 4-item growth mindset measure
used in PISA captures only the growth mindset part of the construct leaving out the fixed
mindset part as developed in the original eight-item scale by Dweck (1999, 2006). Finally,
given the modest magnitude of most non-demographic effects and the unexpected direction
of family support, the PISA developers should refine the construct measurement (e.g.,
distinguishing types of “family support”) to allow researchers to replicate these findings
using multi-country datasets within a joint modeling framework.
Practical implications
The findings suggest that disengagement in large-scale, low-stakes testing follows systematic
patterns by grade level and gender, with smaller but notable influences from school belongingness, socioeconomic context, and language of test. Interventions might therefore provide
targeted support for male students and those in higher grade levels. Schools should also
foster a stronger sense of belonging as a potential preventative factor.
On the methodological front, this study extends the work of Leventhal and Pastor (2024)
by applying the IRTree framework to improve the measurement of disengagement in a
large-scale, low-stakes testing context. Although ability was not the focal construct in this
analysis, our approach builds directly on the core hypothesis of the original model: the
simultaneous estimation of disengagement and ability yields more accurate and valid estimates of both. By modeling these processes jointly, this study reinforces the value of IRTree
models in educational assessment and contributes to ongoing efforts to more precisely
capture the interplay between effort, motivation, and performance.
