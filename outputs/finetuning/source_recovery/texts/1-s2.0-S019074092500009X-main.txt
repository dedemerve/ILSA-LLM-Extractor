Parental involvement has been shown to be correlated with children’s academic performance. This study divided
parental involvement into behavioral involvement and emotional involvement to explore the correlation between parental involvement and digital reading performance. Data on 83,043 15-year-old students were
extracted from the latest reading-themed cycle of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA
2018). Three-level hierarchical linear models (HLMs) were subsequently constructed to analyze the data. The
findings demonstrated that 1) parental emotional involvement was positively correlated with adolescents’ digital
reading performance; 2) previous parental behavioral support was positively correlated with adolescents’ digital
reading performance, whereas unexpectedly, current parental behavioral support and school policies for parental
behavioral involvement had reverse associations with the performance; and 3) the interaction was related to
adolescents’ digital reading performance.

1. Introduction
Adolescence, a transitional phase marked by rapid physical,
emotional, cognitive, and social development, presents unique challenges for young people (Cao et al., 2024); however, for some of them,
their academic performance often declines (Barber & Olsen, 2004).
Within this context, previous research has confirmed that parents have a
strong influence on their children (e.g., Hill & Tyson, 2009; Jeynes,
2007; Kemethofer et al., 2023), and that this influence could be positive
or negative. Therefore, it is very important to identify the extent to
which parental involvement in education is positively related to adolescents’ academic performance and which types of involvement are
most effective.
Parental involvement is defined as parents’ interactions with schools
and with their children to promote academic success (Hill et al., 2004;
Hill & Tyson, 2009). However, given the different frameworks and aspects of parental involvement reported in previous studies, the relationships between parental involvement and adolescents’ academic
performance are inconsistent (e.g., Castro et al., 2015; Cheung & Pomerantz, 2012; Wang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). In previous studies
related to adolescents, parental involvement has been mainly characterized in two ways (Grolnick & Slowiaczek, 1994; Hill & Tyson, 2009).

Grolnick and Slowiaczek (1994) developed a three-pronged framework
for parental involvement, containing personal involvement which focuses on parental interactions with adolescents about school, cognitiveintellectual involvement which exposes adolescents to cognitively
stimulating materials (e.g., books); and behavioral involvement, where
parents may model the importance of school when children experience
their behaviors. Hill and Tyson (2009) proposed a three-dimensional
framework including home-based involvement, school-based involvement, and academic socialization. While the two frameworks highlight
both parental behaviors, such as engaging in conversations with children, and the emotional support parents provide, which can impact
children’s motivation and attitudes, they overlook the significance of
differentiating between behavioral involvement and emotional
involvement and analyzing the interaction of the two types of involvement. In addition, several important factors were not considered in
previous research. For example, Torppa et al. (2022) reported that early
parental involvement could have a positive effect on children
throughout adolescence. However, few studies have focused on the
relationship between early parental involvement and adolescents’ academic performance. Therefore, more attention should be given to
creating a completer and more unified framework. Additionally, in
previous studies, the sampling frame was limited to national or regional

In recent years, as information and communication technology (ICT)
becomes increasingly accessible to the general public, digital reading
has become a mainstream method of obtaining information. In today’s
digitalized world, it is crucial for adolescents to improve their digital
reading literacy to effectively participate in academic, social, and professional communication (OECD, 2019). Thus, the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018, which was established by
the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD),
has revised and expanded the reading framework to incorporate the
essential reading skills with digital texts. Digital reading is defined as the
act of reading textual content presented in digital formats, and performance in digital reading is evaluated through a computer-based
assessment format, which involves the display of texts on a computer
screen (OECD, 2019). Previous research has confirmed that adolescents’
reading performance has a positive relationship with their parents’
involvement (e.g., You et al., 2015); however, the functional mechanism
of parental involvement needs to be further explored. To extend the
findings of previous research and achieve robust results, we investigated
the correlation between parental involvement and adolescents’ digital
reading performance in 12 countries from the dimensions of behavioral
involvement, emotional involvement, and their interaction by analyzing
the data extracted from the latest reading-themed cycle of the PISA
(Fig. 1).

Previous research on adolescents has focused mainly on parental
behavioral involvement at school (Lahti et al., 2019; Sy et al., 2013) and
parental behavioral support within the family (Hampden-Thompson
et al., 2013; Reparaz & Sotés-Elizalde, 2019). First, parental cooperative
behaviors embodied within schools were considered advantageous for
complementing teachers’ instruction, fostering a disciplined school atmosphere and securing additional resources (Antony-Newman, 2024). It
is highly important for schools to establish policies or programs
regarding parental behavioral involvement, which were highly correlated with parental behavioral involvement (Lahti et al., 2019; Sy et al.,
2013). Lahti et al. (2019) reported that participation in home-visiting
programs promoted by educational institutions was positively associated with higher scores in students’ academic performance. Sy et al.
(2013) verified the effectiveness and long-term impact of a school-based
parental involvement program over three consecutive years. Since
parental behavioral involvement at school involves both parents and
schools, it is meaningful to measure value from parents’ perspectives.
However, most of the previous research focused merely on school reports of their policies (e.g., Antony-Newman, 2024; Niehaus & Adelson,
2014), which may lead to inaccurate results because of the error
generated by the potential low efficiency of the implementation of
school policies. It would be more accurate to measure parental perceptions of school policies related to parental behavioral involvement.
Second, parental behavioral involvement at home was found to have
both positive and negative relationships with adolescents’ reading performance. With respect to direct instructions, researchers have reported
an inverse correlation between parental assistance with homework and
children’s academic achievement (Reparaz & Sotés-Elizalde, 2019). It
could be attributed to the evocative effect, where the children who
needed extra support because of problems in learning would be more
likely to get the support from their parents. In this way, the relatively
low-level performance in reading should not be attributed to parents’
teaching their children, whilst it might manifest a negative relationship
between children’s performance and parental involvement. However,
without direct teaching measures, parental companionship in reading
can positively predict children’s reading outcomes. Hampden-Thompson et al. (2013) reported a positive correlation between increased social
and cultural communication with parents and higher levels of students’
reading literacy.
However, less attention has been given to both behavioral support
within the family and at school during adolescence, whereas previous
investigations have predominantly concentrated on preschool or
elementary students. Kloosterman et al. (2011) reported that parental
reading socialization (parental reading examples), parental reading
guidance and parental school involvement positively influenced children’s performance. A longitudinal study conducted in Tanzania
examined the simultaneous impact of teacher–parent communication
and parental involvement in homework and at-home reading. The

examined the relationship from a broad perspective which included
parental emotional support, parental enjoyment of reading and parental
expectations.

2.2. Parental emotional involvement in reading
Studies regarding parental emotional involvement vary among the
topics of parental emotional support (Burns et al., 2022; Froiland, 2015;
Kim & Yu, 2022; Villiger et al., 2014), parental enjoyment of reading
(Clavel & Mediavilla, 2020), and parental expectations (Mistry et al.,
2009; Wood et al., 2007). In this study, we took all of the three aspects
into consideration.
In this study, parental emotional support refers to parental encouragement and advocacy. Villiger et al. (2014) considered parental
emotional support to be a mediator between their children’s reading
motivation and reading score, but found little effect of parental
emotional support. Froiland (2015) described parental emotional support as helping children find interesting characteristics, aims, and
meaning in their schoolwork, and discovered that it increased their
children’s motivation and lifelong love of learning. Andersen and Nielsen (2016) found that students’ reading performance improved after
their parents were taught to support their children by praising their
effort. Burns et al. (2022) noted that high parental emotional support
may compensate for lower or average intrapersonal resources to support
adolescents’ academic achievement. Kim and Yu (2022) proposed that
parental warmth in Hispanic and Asian immigrant families had a positive relationship with children’s better reading performance. Peng et al.
(2023) supported that parental emotional support played a critical role
in fostering students’ relational component of school connectedness. In
contrast to prior research, this study directly analyzed how parental
emotional support correlated with adolescents’ reading performance.
Concerning parental enjoyment of reading, as parents often serve as
children’s models, their reading attitudes were considered to be positively correlated with students’ performance (Clavel & Mediavilla,
2020; Sonnenschein et al., 2016). In other words, parents’ enthusiasm or
disinterest in reading can affect their children’s perceptions, motivations, and then had an impact on children’s reading performance.

2.3. Interaction of parental behavioral involvement and emotional
involvement
Scholars have been concerned about both behavioral and emotional
parental involvement and have compared them.
These previous studies described parental involvement in a more
comprehensive way. For example, Xu et al. (2010) analyzed parental
involvement in six factors, among which parental education expectations, school involvement, and homework help were shown to have
stronger influence on reading achievement. Jeynes (2010) discussed
three forms of parental involvement that were beneficial for students’
achievement—strong parental expectations, high-quality parent–child
communication, and supportive parenting—and emphasized the
meaningful role of more subtle aspects of parental involvement in
fostering positive students’ outcomes. A framework was developed to
delineate parental involvement into parental beliefs and parental practices (von Otter, 2014), while parental practices exhibited the stronger
relation to children’s performance. Benner et al. (2016) analyzed three
types of parental involvement, namely, behavioral involvement at
home, behavioral involvement at school, and academic socialization (e.
g., parental expectations), and reported that greater parental behavioral
involvement within schools and parental expectations were related to
higher educational attainment. Choe (2020) described parental
involvement as parental academic behavioral involvement and
emotional involvement, and found that parental academic behavioral
involvement positively predicted adolescents’ academic performance,
whereas emotional involvement did not show significant association
with adolescents’ outcomes.
The interaction between different dimensions of parental involvement has also been considered in previous research. The positive effect

3. Methods
3.1. Data
The data for this study were extracted from the latest reading-themed
cycle of the PISA 20181 and the information and communication technology development index (IDI).2 The PISA measured the digital reading
performance of adolescents through standardized testing methods.
Assessment questions about digital texts ranged from fundamental
comprehension items (e.g., pinpointing specific information and
deducing conclusions) to more complicated items (e.g., integrating
multiple texts and corroborating information across texts) (OECD,
2019). In addition, in the PISA, student and parent surveys were
administered to obtain a detailed picture of student and family characteristics; these data were used in this study. The research was restricted
to countries that voluntarily administered the student questionnaire and
parent questionnaire because of the focus on parental involvement.
Consequently, each adolescent’s data were directly correlated with their
corresponding parental information. The student questionnaires were
completed at school in approximately 35 min under the instructions of
test administrators, while the parent questionnaires were sent to all the
parents whose children had taken part in the assessment by school coordinators 1–2 weeks before the students’ assessments and were
collected before the assessment date (OECD, 2022). For each variable in
the parent questionnaire that would be used in this research, the percentage of missing values was less than 30 %, which met the criteria for
imputation (Hu & Wang, 2022). In terms of sample selection, the process
included all 15-year-old students, with particular exclusions, which
were often for special education needs or language differences. The
national project managers selected the sample schools randomly at first
and then randomly selected the student samples for the assessment
(OECD, 2022). Therefore, a hierarchical structure emerged, with schools
nested within each country and students nested within each school. The
final sample included 83,043 adolescents from 3,342 schools in 12
countries. Details about the sample of the study were shown in Table 1.

2.4. The present study
Empirically, inconsistent and insufficient frameworks have produced
divergent results. Therefore, while other studies have already linked
parental involvement with reading performance, our study is unique in
three aspects. First, most researchers have focused on parental behavioral involvement (e.g., Hampden-Thompson et al., 2013) and
emotional involvement (e.g., Bowen et al., 2012) separately. These
studies have ignored the diversity of parental involvement when they
have simply focused on lesser aspects or activities. In this study, we
investigated not only parental behavioral involvement and emotional
involvement but also the interaction of these factors. Second, most of the
previous studies concentrated on the reading performance of preschool
children and primary school students (e.g., Malhi et al., 2018; Manolitsis
et al., 2013; Rey-Guerra et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2017), while few
focused on secondary school students. Thus, the relationship over longer
periods remains to be further examined. Third, previous studies have
focused mainly on a single country or region (e.g., Hillier et al., 2022;
You et al., 2015), whereas little attention has been given to a wider
range of countries or regions worldwide. In addition, few researchers
have applied large-scale databases to analyze the role of parental
involvement in students’ academic achievement; instead, they have
conducted research on only small samples (e.g., Akkermans-Rutgers
et al., 2021; Grolnick, 2015; Kim et al., 2018), which may affect the
generalization of the results. Therefore, the present study focused on
parental behavioral and emotional involvement and their interaction on
adolescents’ digital reading performance to test three research hypotheses by analyzing a large-scale cross-national database:
H1: The three forms of parental behavioral involvement in reading
(previous parental support in reading-related activities, current parental
support for learning at home, and school policies for parental involvement) positively correlate with adolescents’ digital reading
performance.
H2: The three forms of parental emotional involvement in reading
(parental emotional support, parental enjoyment of reading, and
parental expectations) positively correlate with adolescents’ digital
reading performance.
H3: The interaction of parental behavioral involvement and
emotional involvement positively correlate with adolescents’ digital
reading performance.
The present study is therefore guided by three research questions
(RQs):
RQ 1. To what degree do the three forms of parental behavioral
involvement in reading correlate with adolescents’ digital reading
performance?
RQ 2. To what degree do the three forms of parental emotional
involvement in reading correlate with adolescents’ digital reading
performance?
RQ 3. To what degree do the interaction of parental behavioral and
emotional involvement correlate with adolescents’ digital reading
performance?

Interaction terms were calculated by the product of continuous
variables from behavioral involvement and emotional involvement,
such as “PRECUPP × EMOSUPP”. Given that “EXPECTAP” was a categorical variable, it was not included in the calculation of these interaction terms. Thus, six interaction terms were added to the model.
3.2.2. Dependent variable
Adolescents’ digital reading performance was reported with 10
plausible values (PVs) in the PISA 2018. PVs are randomly generated
from posterior distributions to mitigate biased inferences occurring as a
result of measuring an unobservable underlying ability through a test
using a relatively small number of items (OECD, 2009). In other words,
PVs represent the range of abilities that a student might reasonably have
with a probability distribution. In accordance with previous research
(Hu & Wang, 2022; Hu & Yu, 2021) and official instructions from the
PISA (OECD, 2009), the current study treated the 10 PVs as dependent
variables simultaneously. In this way, the analysis was conducted ten
times, each time using a corresponding PV. The final result was the
average of the results of ten analyses.
3.2.3. Control variables
Previous research has revealed that socioeconomic background
significantly correlated with students’ academic performance (Sirin,
2005), indicating that it should be accounted for in this study. In
accordance with previous PISA-related studies (e.g., Cairns & Dickson,
2021; Zheng et al., 2024), this study considered background factors at
the student, school, and country levels. Most of them were extracted
from PISA database. Gender of the students (GENDER), an index of
students’ economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS), ICT resources
(ICTRES) served as control variables at the student level. School location
(SCHLOC), school type (SCHTYPE), number of available computers per
student at modal grade (RATCMP1), and proportion of computers connected to the Internet at school (RATCMP2) served as control variables
at the school level. With regard to country-level control variables, in
accordance with previous research (Skryabin et al., 2015), the IDI was
obtained from the International Communication Union (ITU) to capture
the status of the national development of ICT, and this index encompasses three indicators, i.e., national ICT access, ICT use, and ICT skills.

3.3. Data analysis
The data were analyzed via IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 and HLM 8
(License ID: 19760). Before establishing the HLM, the issue of missing
values across all variables were addressed. We assessed the percentage
of missing values and found that all variables, except for EXPECTAP_4,
had less than 30 % missing values, which met the criteria for imputation
(Hu & Wang, 2022). Consequently, we opted to exclude EXPECTAP_4
from our analysis and employed multiple imputation (MI) to handle the
remaining missing data. The MI data sets were matched with the corresponding PVs (e.g., MI1 with PV1, MI2 with PV2, etc.) and were
analyzed separately. Following Rubin’s rules (Rubin, 1987), the final
results were computed. The continuous variables were subsequently
subjected to z-score standardization, while the categorical variables
(SCHLOC and SCHTYPE) were subsequently transformed into dummy
variables to enable interpretation and comparison. In addition, the
student weight (W_FSTUWT) was normalized to promote unbiased estimates (OECD, 2009).
Next, a three-level HLM was prepared sequentially to analyze the
nested structure (country-school-student) of the PISA data (Hox, 2010;
Raudenbush et al., 2019). First, we constructed an intercept-only model
(Model 1) to determine whether hierarchical linear model was necessary
to conduct by calculating the the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC).
Then, we put all the control variables into the model (Model 2) and

5.1.1. Previous parental support in reading-related activities
In this study, we found that previous parental support in readingrelated activities was positively related to adolescents’ digital reading
performance, which corresponds to the previous findings (Nievar et al.,
2018; Sy et al., 2013; Torppa et al., 2022). A potential reason might be
that previous parental support could help children to develop reading
motivation which has lasting benefits for their reading literacy. Researchers have found that previous parental support, especially shared
reading, appeared to be effective to trigger children’s intrinsic motivation for reading during their school years and promote vocabulary or
linguistic skills and reading comprehension skills in subsequent years (e.
g., Baker et al., 1997; Sénéchal, 2006; Torppaet al., 2022). It has been
proved that reading motivation would positively predict reading literacy
(e.g., Froiland & Oros, 2014; Hebbecker et al., 2019). The results were
also consistent with previous analyses of large-scale database (Al Jefri &
Areepattamannil, 2019), which mainly focused on primary school students. In this study, we concerned about secondary school students and
reached similar results. Therefore, it is explicable that previous parental
support might generate a lasting influence on children.

5.1.2. Current parental support for learning at home
The relationship between current parental support and adolescents’
performance varied in previous research. Research investigating
involvement in academic instruction during adolescence has primarily
concentrated on providing academic assistance. While it seems that
current parental involvement should be a positive predictor as some
researchers found (Hirsh et al., 2019; Li & Li, 2022), our study reached
an unexpectedly different answer. However, some researchers have
found a negative correlation between parental assistance and students’
performance (Cooper et al., 2000; Hill & Tyson, 2009; Milne et al., 1986;
Shumow & Miller, 2001). Nevertheless, the process behind has not been
examined. In this study, three potential explanations were put forward
to account for the negative correlation.
First, parental behavioral involvement can be affected by students’
academic performance, which is known as “evocative effect” (Scarr &
McCartney, 1983; Silinskas et al., 2015). While parental involvement
has been considered as an important factor on children’s academic

performance (Kloosterman et al., 2011), children’s characteristics could
also have an impact on parental behaviors. For instance, it could be
supposed that children’s ongoing success or failure in academic work
may facilitate or discourage parental involvement related to children’s
learning. Children’s academic performance has already been shown to
activate parental involvement (e.g., Núñez et al., 2017; Silinskas et al.,
2015). Gershoff et al. (2009) discovered that parents provide less direct
guidance when children’s academic performance was outstanding,
while parents were more likely to offer increased instruction when
children achieve low levels of reading performance. In other words,
parental support could be considered as a rescue measure for dealing
with poor performance. It is plausible that parental behaviors may increase when children gain low scores, which leads to the negative correlation discovered in this study.
Second, different methods of assessing behavioral involvement could
cause distinct results (Yang & Zhao, 2020). In accordance with previous
research (Froiland, 2015; Smetana, 2017; Van Petegem et al., 2019),

when parents used a psychologically controlling communication style,
involving tactics such as threats of love withdrawal and guilt induction,
children were more likely to consider that parents should not regulate
their behavior. Dumont et al. (2014) found that students with low levels
of performance reported more parental control, which in turn led to
lower functioning. Gao et al. (2020) pointed out that parental psychological control was negatively associated with children’s achievement.
Xiong et al. (2021) noted that only parental practices that encouraged
adolescents’ active engagement in their academic work at school were
linked to higher academic achievement, while other forms may hinder
adolescents’ development. This approach provides implications for
parents to concern about children’s features and highlight the most
suitable measures of behavioral involvement.
Third, as children advance in both grades and development, parents
may encounter increasing capacity limitations to assist technically,
psychologically, and informatively (Hill & Tyson, 2009). Previous
research suggested that once formal school starts (Bus et al., 1995;
Scarborough & Dobrich, 1994), the effects of parents would gradually
diminish. A meta-analysis revealed a differentiated pattern of correlation
between parental involvement and students’ academic performance at
different school stages (Tan et al., 2020). Compared to students from
higher grade levels, kindergartners benefited more from parental education. In other words, even though parents provide similar levels of
behavioral involvement to their children, it might not meet their children’s actual needs.

are struggling and unmotivated to read. This suggests that schools
should teach better to improve students’ reading skills, which would
further facilitate parental emotional support to have a positive impact
on children’s academic performance.
5.2.2. Parental enjoyment of reading
Parents could exert a significant impact on their children’s emotions
(e.g., Else-Quest et al., 2008; Moè et al., 2018). Previous research has
highlighted the emotional contagion from parents to children and the
impact of emotions on students’ performance. Nalipay et al. (2020)
suggested that parental reading emotion, particularly the enjoyment
derived from reading, was related to the emotional experience of children and their subsequent reading achievement. When parents actively
engage in activities due to their perceived importance, they are more
likely to ask about and be aware of their children’s academic experience.
These forms of involvement have strong association with academic
outcomes (Grolnick, 2015), which is in agreement with the findings of
this study. In addition, reading enjoyment has been found to be reciprocally related with reading capacity (van Bergen et al., 2023). In other
words, those who have high-level reading capacity would be more likely
to have high-level reading enjoyment, and vice versa. Therefore, parents
with low-level reading enjoyment might have low-level reading capacity, which could become an inter-generational transmission towards
their children and lead to the low-level reading performance of their
children.

5.1.3. School policies for parental behavioral involvement
To our knowledge, the variable of school policy for parental
involvement has not been examined within context of the PISA 2018
context; thus, this study extended previous findings of parent-school
relation (Benner et al., 2021; Clinton & Hattie, 2013). Park et al.
(2017) discovered a positive association between extensive social
network among parents, which could then form a platform for schoolparent communication, and children’s reading performance. However,
we found a negative correlation between adolescents’ performance and
school policies for parental involvement. There might be two potential
reasons for this. First, school policies for parental involvement are
regarded as a remedial measure. Parents whose children perform worse
in reading may tend to play a more active role in school affairs and
contact teachers more frequently. It is probably attributed to higherachieving adolescents having stronger self-regulation processes, which
results in less parental intrusion in their school lives. It is possible that
parents whose children have low levels of performance tend to regard
their children to have lower self-regulation, and may resort more to
cooperation with schools. The other possible explanation is that there
might be a curve-linear relationship between school policies and students’ performance in digital reading. In other words, only a moderate
measure positively affects students’ performance, while excessive measurement might have side effects. Further research is necessary to gain a
deeper understanding of the causal directions and other important relationships pertaining to interventions in this context.

5.2.3. Parental expectations
Most of the categorical variables of parental expectations were
significantly positively related to adolescents’ digital reading performance, which corresponds with the results of previous studies (Mistry
et al., 2009; Rimkute et al., 2012; Singh et al., 1995; Wood et al., 2007).
Specifically, the coefficient of EXPECTAP_5A6 was the highest. One
possible reason for this finding might be that when parents have high
educational expectations for their children, their children may perform
better in digital reading assessments. Areepattamannil & Lee (2014)
found that when parents recognized the importance of education and
maintain an optimistic outlook regarding their children’s future prospects, they tended to exhibit a profound concern for their children’s
academic performance and do their utmost to support their children in
achieving better performance. Also, children with relatively low-level
academic performance for a long time may gradually make their parents lose confidence, which might cause parents to lower their expectations during children’s adolescence. In addition, the negative
relationship between EXPECTAP_3BC and adolescents’ digital reading
performance may be related to the fact that low parental expectations
might hinder adolescents from achieving better scores.

6. Conclusion
The present study analyzed parental involvement in relation to
behavioral involvement, emotional involvement, and their interaction
to explore the correlation between parental involvement and adolescents’ digital reading performance. After analyzing the PISA 2018
database with a three-level HLM, the results demonstrated the significant association between parental emotional involvement for adolescents’ digital reading performance. Unexpectedly, only previous
parental behavioral support in reading-related activities was positively
correlated to the performance, while current parental behavioral support and school policies for parental behavioral involvement were
negatively related to the performance.
Nevertheless, this study has some limitations and could provide
suggestions for future studies. First, the main data only came from
parents, which might influence the reliability of the answers. Because
answers that are solely from parents may not always represent the real
situation, future studies should include additional indices that are based
on the views of teachers and children. For instance, parents might
overestimate their involvement due to social desirability. Second, the
data source was still not comprehensive. Although the study included
countries and regions from America, Asia and Europe, the results still
need to be further confirmed across a wider range due to the lack of
attendance of participation in the PISA 2018 parental questionnaire.
