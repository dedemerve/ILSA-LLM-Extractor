A B S T R A C T  
The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) is the world’s most extensive 
assessment of student’s scholastic and general abilities, featuring assessments in over 70 coun­
tries. In the program’s 2015 cycle, the assessment included a computerized measure of collabo­
rative problem-solving skills (CPS) using virtual agents. Studies on the validity of this approach 
are sparse, however. To firstly investigate whether this computer-supported approach was validly 
assessing CPS, we obtained the original tasks from the Organization for Economic Co-operation 
and Development (OECD). We identified the extent to which the assessment is related to exist­
ing collaboration measures, including subjective self-rated collaboration, external teacher-rated 
collaboration, co-player collaboration, as well as reasoning and reading. The results of struc­
tural equation modeling and response surface analysis (N ¼ 483) showed that self-rated collab­
oration, teacher-rated collaboration, co-player collaboration, and reasoning were moderately 
related to PISA 2015 CPS performance, even after controlling for individual differences in reading 
performance.   
Today, students often collaborate with classmates in group learning tasks (e.g., Interactive Multi-Media Exercises; M. M. Cooper 
et al., 2008), and workers collaborate with colleagues to combine their expertise, with examples of remote surgery or remote case 
assessments in international law firms (Rummel & Spada, 2005). Beyond the workplace, we also interact with others to solve problems 
in our private lives, be it with our families, sports teammates, or physicians. In all settings, collaborative problem-solving (CPS) re­
quires that the people involved have sufficient cognitive problem solving and social collaboration skills (Graesser et al., 2018). This 
omnipresence increases the relevance of CPS proficiency in the eyes of educators and governments—in particular, given the current 
global societal challenges people face in the contexts of globalization, technological advancement, and political divisions (e.g., 
Newland, 2016). Therefore, educators and governments worldwide have recognized the importance of the ability to effectively solve 
problems through social collaboration with others in pursuit of a common goal (Griffin & Care, 2015b; OECD, 2013), and have come to 
view CPS as a critical 21st-century skill (Griffin & Care, 2015a; von Davier & Halpin, 2013). 
The assessment of individual CPS, as a basis of systematic fostering, is not trivial, though (Andrews-Todd & Forsyth, 2020). To limit

Century Learning sponsored by Cisco, Intel, and Microsoft (ATC21S; Griffin & Care, 2015b), developed computer-supported tools 
providing controlled environments for the collaboration. Among them, probably the most influential one is the Programme for In­
ternational Student Assessment (PISA) by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). PISA is a triennial 
survey of over 500,000 15-year-old students around the world (more than 70 countries and economies) that assesses the extent to 
which they have acquired crucial knowledge and skills essential for full participation in social and economic life (OECD, 2013). Due to 
the increasing general relevance of CPS for societal integration, the OECD integrated the assessment of CPS into the 2015 cycle in order 
to evaluate students’ current CPS levels as outcomes of the educational systems of various countries. PISA results are compared be­
tween countries worldwide and usually have an enormous impact on educational policies across the globe. The CPS assessment in PISA 
2015 was based on a state-of-the-art computer-based approach that was criticized for assessing CPS using computer-simulated group 
partners (human-to-agent; H-A) rather than other students (human-to-human; H–H). The PISA 2015 CPS assessment required students 
to collaborate with computer agents in dynamic virtual collaborative environments that tracked their CPS performance (OECD, 2013). 
A group of experts developed the approach based on various theoretical definitions and existing assessments (OECD, 2013), such as the 
ATC21S (Griffin & Care, 2015b). In their analysis of the tasks, Zehner et al. (2019) argued that the assessment was psychometrically 
sound but noted that future studies would need to investigate its external validity. In line with this, critics have questioned whether the 
assessment using the H-A approach validly captured the real dynamics of H–H interactions. Thus, empirical evidence for the validity of 
the assessment’s approach and results are of paramount importance but are not yet available (for first results see Stadler, Herborn, 
Mustafic, & Greiff, 2019; Herborn, Stadler, Mustafic, & Greiff, 2020). 
Given that the published PISA 2015 CPS results will most likely have a significant impact on policy changes and school reforms 
across the globe, in the current study, we aimed to investigate the validity of the H-A assessment approach. To do so, we tested for the 
very first time the relations between a CPS assessment based on the H-A approach on the one hand and H–H measures of CPS on the 
other hand. In a first step, we addressed construct validity by identifying the relations between PISA 2015 CPS performance and 
students’ subjective self-reported collaboration, external teacher-reported collaboration, and reasoning performance. Moreover, we 
controlled for reading skills so that we could also assess discriminant validity. In a second step, we investigated predictive validity by 
directly contrasting individual performance in the H-A tasks and co-player H-A tasks in which students tried to solve the tasks with a 
partner. 
1. The impact of PISA results 
Every three years, PISA results are compared between countries worldwide to evaluate educational systems and potentially adopt 
educational policies and improve student education so that students can move toward integration into society. In previous PISA cycles, 
for instance, representatives and PISA Governing Board (PGB) members from Austria and South Korea used PISA results as agenda 
setters "for policy dialogues among policy-makers and experts" (Breakspear, 2012, p. 13). Representatives from the UK even used PISA 
results as evidence for the effect of direct school reforms (Breakspear, 2012). PISA results have the power to steer how government 
policies are oriented (Figazzolo, 2009) and to induce all sorts of global policy changes, be it revisions to school curricula, the setting, 
and monitoring of performance targets, or increasing the proportion of proficient or top performers (Breakspear, 2012; Grek, 2009). 
An analysis of the PISA 2000, 2003, and 2006 results found that the impact of PISA results on policy changes has been increasing 
over time (Hopkins et al., 2008). Therefore, with the official publication of the special policy report on the PISA 2015 CPS assessment 
results commissioned by the OECD in November 2017, changes to educational policies regarding CPS proficiency can be expected to 
happen. One chapter in the report explicitly stated implications for educational policies. For instance, the PISA CPS data show that girls 
outperform boys in every education system resulting in calls for more substantial support for the development of boys’ CPS skills 
(OECD, 2017). The report also suggests that schools can foster CPS skills through instructional practice in existing school subjects and 
courses (OECD, 2017). Subjects such as science, reading, and mathematics provide opportunities to teach and practice collaboration 
skills (OECD, 2017). 
2. Theoretical background of CPS 
In psychological research, CPS defines the conjoint skill of solving problems and working toward a shared goal while a group of 
individuals collaborates socially with each other (e.g., O’Neil et al., 2010). However, as CPS is still a relatively new construct, different 
definitions and underlying theoretical frameworks of CPS have been proposed, none of which are universally accepted. For example, 
ATC21S combined the CPS literature from different fields and defined CPS as "a joint activity where dyads or small groups execute 
several steps in order to transform a current state into a desired goal state" (Hesse et al., 2015). ATC21S assessed CPS by testing 
students on 18 newly developed CPS subskills that comprise separate social and cognitive skills underlying CPS, such as interaction, 
self-evaluation, and goal setting (Hesse et al., 2015). 
For PISA 2015 the OECD combined existing theoretical frameworks (e.g., the ATC21S framework) in order to propose a definition 
of CPS as the skill " (…) to effectively engage in a process whereby two or more agents attempt to solve a problem by sharing the 
understanding and effort required to come to a solution and pooling their knowledge, skills, and efforts to reach that solution" (OECD, 
2017, p. 32). The definition was conceptualized based on an underlying CPS framework, the so-called 12-Cell Matrix, which describes 
CPS as comprised of 12 specific CPS subskills. The CPS skills derive from the four PISA 2012 individual problem-solving processes (i.e., 
A: exploring and understanding, B: representing and formulating, C: planning and executing, D: monitoring and reflecting) and three

action to solve the problem, 3: establishing and maintaining team organization) as illustrated in the 12-Cell Matrix in Table 1 (OECD, 
2017). CPS is, thus, considered a complex interaction between problem-solving and social collaboration. 
In PISA 2015, a newly developed computer-based approach was developed to assess the 12 individual CPS skills. Educators 
generally place importance on students’ sufficient CPS skills in virtual environments as CPS is increasingly performed over technical 
devices in modern workplaces (e.g., email or teleconferencing). Therefore, computer-based CPS assessments seem to be gaining in 
popularity as they simulate complex and varying CPS situations and can offer realistic virtual 21st century CPS environments. 
A new CPS assessment approach (i.e., the one applied in PISA 2015) was developed based on existing assessments by the expert 
group, the OECD (2013). The assessment required students to engage in social collaboration with computer-simulated agents (H-A 
approach) while solving diverse cognitive problems. Each PISA 2015 CPS task was employed to assess students’ CPS performance 
(OECD, 2013) by measuring students’ active collaboration with the agents in the form of communication via predefined chat messages 
while solving different problems based on realistic situations. The problem scenarios and group of collaboration partners varied in each 
PISA 2015 CPS task. To provide an example, in the CPS task Xandar depicted in Fig. 1, students had to communicate with the computer 
agents Alice and Zach to participate in a contest in which they had to work together to answer various questions about a fictional 
country called Xandar (for more detailed information about the task see OECD, 2017). On the left side of the task space, students 
collaborated with the computer agents by selecting one of a set of predefined messages to send as their reply to the group chat. In the 
PISA 2015 assessment, the agents possessed varying degrees of CPS proficiency (i.e., skills and abilities), the group sizes were varied, 
and the test-takers assumed different group roles (e.g., leader, coordinator). Communication was only possible via predefined chat 
communication. Open chat communication, in which test-takers type messages themselves, was not possible. Students received points 
for each correctly chosen predefined message as well as each correctly performed problem-solving action. 
PISA did not officially publish results on the validity and reliability of the assessment from the field trial approach. However, they 
claimed that their computer-based CPS assessment approach using computer-simulated agents was valid: 
While it is acknowledged that the PISA 2015 assessment does not directly test students working with other students, the agent- 
based approach permits controlled testing of the skills that are required for collaboration. By targeting these skills under controlled 
situations, the use of agents provides a sufficiently valid approach to measurement to allow generalizations about the critical 
collaboration skills.” (OECD, 2013, p. 22, p. 22). 
However, what sufficiently valid" means and to what extent the PISA 2015 CPS results reflect students’ real skills in solving 
problems in collaboration with others remains an open question. To date, there is hardly any empirical data available on the validity of 
H-A based CPS assessments in general (Lai, 2011; Rosen, 2015; von Davier & Halpin, 2013) and none specifically on the PISA 2015 CPS 
assessment. 
Moreover, several researchers criticized H-A assessment approaches that allow only predefined chat communication for creating 
unrealistic collaboration environments and limiting natural collaboration conversations (Scoular & Care, 2019). For example, Rosen 
and Foltz (2014) generally support the use of computer agents in CPS assessments. However, they agree that H-A collaboration can 
capture the dynamics between two humans only to a certain extent because CPS in H-A settings deviates from natural human 
communication delivery" (Rosen & Foltz, 2014, pp. 393–394). 
3. Aim and research questions 
Given that the publishing of the PISA 2015 CPS results will likely have a significant impact on policy changes and school reforms 
across the globe, empirical evidence on the validity of the assessment’s approach and results are of paramount importance but are not 
yet available. To fill this gap, we, therefore, assessed the validity of the original PISA 2015 CPS assessment approach by employing the 
H-A approach as used in PISA 2015. 
More specifically, we conducted a study to identify whether CPS performance, as assessed in PISA 2015, would be found to be 
related to performance in other previously validated collaboration measures. In a first step, we sought to identify the relations between

To assess construct validity, we asked the following research question: 
Research Question 1a: How is H-A CPS performance related to subjective self-rated and external teacher-rated collaboration? 
To assess discriminant validity, we asked the following research question: 
Research Question 1 b: How is H-A performance related to subjective self-rated and external teacher-rated collaboration after 
including reasoning and controlling for reading as a discriminant factor? 
In a second step, we aimed to assess the measure’s predictive validity by identifying whether CPS performance in PISA 2015 would 
predict co-player collaboration on a structurally similar task. To reiterate the definition of CPS in PISA, students with high CPS should 
be able " (…) to effectively engage in a process whereby two or more agents attempt to solve a problem by sharing the understanding 
and effort required to come to a solution and pooling their knowledge, skills, and efforts to reach that solution" (OECD, 2017, p. 32). 
To investigate whether CPS scores as assessed by the PISA 2015 CPS tasks predicted this ability, we had students cooperate directly 
with a partner in solving an additional, previously unknown PISA 2015 CPS task (note that this task was also an H-A task). Students 
with higher individual CPS scores should do well on the new task on their own, as the tasks were structurally identical. Given their 
assumed ability to pool their knowledge, skills, and efforts, they should doeven better when paired with equally able students, though 
(D. J. Cooper & Kagel, 2005). In this case, both students’ high CPS ability should allow them to maximize their shared results. Students 
with low individual CPS scores should still do poorly when paired with students of equally low ability, as they should not be able to 
benefit from each other’s knowledge. Correspondingly, we expected a complex interaction between students’ individual ability and the 
shared performance for pairs with differing levels of ability that goes beyond the mere addition of students’ individual knowledge (see 
the truth-wins model; Larson, 2010). We asked the following research question: 
Research Question 2: What is the relation between individual H-A performance and H-A performance when working co-player with 
a partner?

4.2. Measures 
PISA CPS. Students completed a total of four of the seven original PISA 2015 CPS tasks that required them to collaborate with 
computer agents and solve diverse problems. Each original PISA 2015 CPS task represented a realistic problem (e.g., answering 
questions about the geography, people, and economy of a country called Xandar based on written material) that required the test- 
takers to collaborate with the computer agents to solve the problem. Each task consisted of several parts related to the same prob­
lem scenario that needed to be solved consecutively. In each task, students collaborated with a minimum of one to a maximum of three 
agents by exchanging predefined messages in a chatbox on the left side of the task, as illustrated in Fig. 1 above. Students selected 
predefined messages from the list of messages provided and sent them to the group chat. Students could always scroll back to previous 
messages, but they could not type their own individually generated messages (known as open chat communication). On the right side 
of the task, students solved diverse cognitive problems. 
For each PISA 2015 CPS task, specific predefined messages (usually one per task, but in rare cases, two) and actions were scored as 
either being indicative of CPS skills or not based on the scoring guide provided by the developers of the assessment units. Full credit (1 
point; in some cases, 2 points) was provided (for more information on scoring, please see the technical report on PISA 2015 CPS; OECD, 
2017 as well as He et al., 2017). In all other cases, students did not receive any points (0) because their messages or actions did not 
reflect the particular CPS skill tested. To obtain a parsimonious CPS measurement model and to reduce the number of parameter 
estimates (110 items) in our latent models, we aggregated the points for each CPS task resulting in a total of four indicators (i.e., one 
score for each of the four PISA 2015 CPS tasks). The reliability of the latent PISA CPS measure with four indicators was estimated by 
applying McDonald’s Omega (ω; McDonald, 1970) and resulted in ω ¼ 0.97. The original PISA study did not have to aggregate these 
indicators due to the large sample size, but we do feel our results will be comparable, given the very good reliability. 
Self-reported collaboration. Students subjectively reported their collaboration skills using the paper-based teamwork and 
collaboration questionnaire for high-school students by Zhuang et al. (2008). The scale consists of 30 items capturing the three di­
mensions of cooperation (12 items), advocate/guide (9 items), and negotiation (9 items) in a 6-point Likert-scale format (1 ¼ never, 6 
¼ always). The cooperation dimension assesses students’ interpersonal skills, such as merging ideas, seeking solutions, and providing 
feedback (e.g., "I share ideas with others to accomplish a task"). The advocate/guide dimension assesses students’ skills in directing and 
persuading team partners, as well as providing suggestions and criticism to them (e.g., "I am comfortable providing constructive 
criticism"). The negotiation dimension assesses students’ skills in listening to fellow team members and approaching team conflicts (e. 
g., "I am open to varying opinions"). Three researchers with excellent English and German proficiency translated the scale from the 
original English to German. The reliability of the three latent factors was estimated and resulted in values of ω ¼ 0.99 (cooperation), ω 
¼ 0.99 (advocate/guide), and ω ¼ 0.98 (negotiation). 
Teacher-reported collaboration. Class teachers completed the teacher-report teamwork questionnaire by Zhuang et al. (2008) for 
each student. The paper-based teamwork questionnaire in the teacher-report format is an excerpt from the full self-report question­
naire for assessing students’ academic and social skills from their teacher’s perspective with five items by Zhuang et al. (2008). 
Teachers taught students for 4 h and 26 min on average per week (SD ¼ 1 h 25 min, n ¼ 64 missing values) and had known the students 
for at least six months (i.e., data collection in the middle of the academic year). The scale consists of five items evaluating students’ 
behavior (e.g., "The student works effectively with others") on a 5-point Likert scale (1 ¼ below average, 5 ¼ truly exceptional). The 
reliability of the latent factor was ω ¼ 0.99. 
Reasoning. Students completed the paper-based Intelligence Structure Test-Screening (IST-Screening; Liepmann et al., 2012) in 
approximately 30 min. This test screens verbal, numerical, and figural intelligence with a total of 60 items (20 items per dimension). 
Each item was scored dichotomously such that students received full credit (1) for correct answers and no credit (0) otherwise. We 
calculated sum scores for each dimension (i.e., numerical, verbal, and figural intelligence). The reliability was good, with a value of ω 
¼ 0.91. Reasoning was included as a latent factor defined by three indicators.

4.3. Procedure 
On the assessment days, all assessments followed a standardized procedure under the supervision of two trained administrators. 
Throughout the assessment days, students were given regular breaks in between the tests and questionnaires to avoid cognitive fatigue 
in students during our long test sessions (Ackerman & Kanfer, 2009; Sievertsen et al., 2016). Students always completed the PISA 2015 
CPS tasks first during approximately the first 2 h of each test session. Due to time restrictions, only five tasks (four individual and one 
face to face), rather than all seven tasks used in PISA 2015, were administered. 
After completing the individual PISA 2015 CPS tasks, students completed the remaining questionnaires and performance tests in 
booklets. If teachers did not have enough time to complete the questionnaires on the test day, they were allowed to send the completed 
questionnaires after data collection via postal mail within a given time. 
After students completed the paper-based performance tests and questionnaires, they were administered the fifth original PISA 
2015 CPS task in the co-player format along with a randomly assigned classmate. The task lasted approximately 20–30 min and was 
assessed after students had completed the paper-based performance tests and questionnaires to minimize the influence of their pre­
vious PISA 2015 CPS task experience on their test performance. Groups of two students were randomly assigned to a particular 
computer and were seated together in front of it. All types of communication, including finger-pointing, real communication, and facial 
expressions, were allowed. Notably, as the co-player collaboration also employed an original PISA 2015 CPS task, both students 
achieved identical scores for the unit resulting in N ¼ 228 observed individual scores. 
4.4. Data analysis 
To address RQ1a and RQ1b, we used a structural equation modeling (SEM; Bollen, 1989) approach in MPlus Version 7.0 (Muthen & 
Muthen, 2007-2012) to evaluate construct validity based on the latent correlations between subjective self-rated collaboration, 
teacher-rated collaboration, and reasoning (Model A). In a further step, for RQ1, we controlled for reading performance as a potential 
factor of discriminant validity in the analysis (Model B). Both Models, A and B, considered the hierarchical structure of our nested data 
(i.e., students nested in 32 different classes) by adjusting the standard errors using the "type ¼ complex" command.3 The ULSMV 
estimator was used for all analyses to account for the categorical nature of the indicators (Savalei & Rhemtulla, 2013). Model fit was 
evaluated according to standard fit indices and cut-off values, namely, the comparative fit index (CFI; cut-off CFI > 0.95 for a good fit), 
Tucker-Lewis index (TLI; cut-off TLI > 0.95 for a good fit), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; cut-off RMSEA < 0.05 for 
a good fit), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR; cut-off SRMR < 0.05 for a good fit). To control for reading performance 
as a potential factor of influence (Model B), the factors were residualized to control for common variance (e.g., Wüstenberg, Greiff, & 
Funke, 2012). 
To address RQ2, this study employed response surface analysis (Khuri & Cornell, 2018; Sch€onbrodt, 2016) using R 3.4.0 (R Core 
Team, 2020) and the RSA Package (Sch€onbrodt, 2015) to identify the validity of the PISA 2015 CPS performance in predicting 
co-player collaboration (Model C). Response surface analysis describes a set of regression models as well as their graphical repre­
sentation as a three-dimensional surface (Edwards, 2002) to identify nonlinear interactions.

¼ 0.064). Due to a similar wording between the advocate/guide Items 13 and 16, as well as Items 20 and 21, error correlations were 
allowed between these items. The latent model for teacher-reported collaboration showed a good model fit (teacher-reported 
collaboration was defined by five indicators: χ2 ¼ 11.237, df ¼ 4, p < .01, CFI ¼ 0.989, TLI ¼ 0.973, RMSEA ¼ 0.065). The co-player 
PISA CPS and reading measures were included as manifest scores in the models. Table 2 presents the correlations of the measures. 
The Relations between H-A Performance and Subjective Self-Rated and External Teacher-Rated Collaboration to Identify Construct 
Validity (RQ1a). 
For Research Question 1a, we assessed construct validity in the PISA 2015 CPS approach by correlating CPS performance with 
subjective self-rated collaboration and external teacher-rated collaboration. In Model A, self-rated collaboration and external teacher- 
rated collaboration were used as predictors of PISA 2015 CPS performance (Model A: χ2 ¼ 778.649, df ¼ 655, p < .01, CFI ¼ 0.914, 
TLI ¼ 0.908, RMSEA ¼ 0.020). The results for Model A showed that students’ individual CPS performance was moderately predicted by 
subjective self-rated collaboration (latent r ¼ 0.25, p < .001) and teacher-rated collaboration (latent r ¼ 0.33, p < .001). 
The Relations after Including Reasoning as a further Predictor and Reading as a Discriminant Factor (RQ1b). 
We tested whether the relations from Model A would hold when reasoning was integrated as a further predictor and reading 
performance was controlled for. To do so, Model B included self-reported collaboration, external teacher-reported collaboration, and 
students’ reasoning as predictors of their PISA 2015 CPS performance while reading performance was controlled for (Model B: χ2 ¼
928.850, df ¼ 807, p < .01, CFI ¼ 0.918; TLI ¼ 0.912; RMSEA ¼ 0.018). As illustrated in Fig. 2, the results showed that students’ 
individual CPS performance was moderately predicted by subjective self-rated collaboration (latent r ¼ 0.29, p < .001), teacher-rated 
collaboration (latent r ¼ 0.20, p < .05), and reasoning (latent r ¼ 0.38, p < .001). The relation between CPS and reading performance 
was moderate (latent r ¼ 0.29, p < .001). 
The Relation between H-A Performance and Co-player Collaboration between Students to identify Predictive Validity (RQ2). 
For Research Question 2, we investigated the extent to which CPS performance in PISA 2015 was valid for predicting co-player 
collaboration (Model C). We controlled for the dependency of the test-takers’ CPS on the partners’ performance because a partner’s 
CPS proficiency can have a substantial impact on the other partner’s CPS performance. To note again, as the co-player collaboration 
employed an original PISA 2015 CPS task, both students achieved identical scores for the unit. To avoid scoring errors in the analyses, 
we only integrated one of the two partners from each group in our analyses that resulted in a sample size of N ¼ 228 (see the Procedure 
section above). A regression was defined to model student’s joint co-player CPS performance in the co-player format based on their CPS 
performance as well as their partner’s CPS performance. The data were best described (AIC ¼ 2975.41; CFI ¼ 1.00) by a rotated 
squared difference model (RSQD; Sch€onbrodt, 2016), allowing for both linear and nonlinear terms in the regression and explaining 
30.2% of the variance in students’ performance in the co-player task. This model was superior to both a null model (AIC ¼ 3026.059; 
CFI ¼ 0.00), a linear interaction model (AIC ¼ 2980.692; CFI ¼ 0.90), and a full polynomial model (AIC ¼ 2977.643; CFI ¼ 1.00). In 
the RSQD model, the linear main effects of the students’ (β ¼ 0.31; p < .001) and their partners’ CPS performance (β ¼ 0.26; p < .001), 
as well as the interaction effect (β ¼ -.12; p ¼ .038) and the squared effect of students’ CPS performance (β ¼ -.11; p ¼ .020) contributed 
significantly to students’ performance in the co-player task. The squared effect of the partners’ CPS performance was not significant (β 
¼ -.08; p ¼ .078). 
To ease the interpretation of these effects, Fig. 3 graphically illustrates the results of the response surface analysis. The bagplot 
(black borders) emphasizes the interpretable region (Rousseeuw et al., 1999). Based on Fig. 3, it becomes evident that students’ joint 
co-player CPS performance in the co-player format depends on both students’ individual CPS performance. Pairs with two partners of 
high individual CPS performance were most successful, and pairs with two partners of low CPS performance were least successful. 
However, even a partner with a deficient individual CPS performance could not drag the joint performance down very far, as suggested 
by the curvature of the surface area.

6. Discussion 
As Zehner et al. (2019)argued, the implementation of the H-A approach to assessing CPS in PISA 2015 was innovative and psy­
chometrically sound. They noted, however, that future studies needed to assess the approach’s validity more thoroughly to be valuable 
for educational research practice and decision making. This study aimed to provide the first answers to this question by investigating 
the validity of the H-A approach of assessing CPS based on the PISA 2015 tasks. To do so, we obtained the original PISA 2015 CPS tasks 
from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), which are not publicly available. 
In RQ1a and b, we explored construct validity by identifying how PISA 2015 CPS performance was related to subjective self- 
reported collaboration skills and external evaluations by teachers (Model A). In this model, the relation between CPS and the 
external teacher-rated collaboration was the strongest. The teacher reports exclusively focused on collaboration in the classroom and 
represented a face-valid criterion for evaluating students’ collaboration skills in school as previously recommended by scholars (e.g., 
Dillenbourg, 1999; Lai, 2011). Reasoning was included as a further predictor and reading was included as a discriminant factor, as 
reasoning is part of the theoretical construct of CPS and the PISA 2015 CPS tasks have a general high reading load. The relations 
changed such that reasoning showed the most robust relation with CPS, followed by subjective self-collaboration and reading. The high 
relation with reasoning can be explained by the theoretical conceptualization of the underlying CPS construct (Table 1). The PISA 2015 
framework defines CPS as a construct that is located somewhere between social collaboration and individual problem solving, thus 
considering, individual problem solving as a facet of general reasoning. We assume that the change in the relation between CPS and the 
teacher reports occurred because students’ general reasoning performance biased the teachers (Saracho, 1991). In the self-reports, 
participating students rated their general collaboration behavior in different settings (i.e., at home and school), thus providing ac­
curate reflections of students’ general collaboration skills. The self-rated cooperation and negotiation dimensions had the highest

factor loadings. In fact, in the PISA 2015 CPS matrix (Table 1), for the three underlying collaboration dimensions (1: establishing and 
maintaining a shared understanding, 2: taking appropriate action to solve the problem, 3: establishing and maintaining team orga­
nization), actions involving cooperation and negotiation are given more weight than advocating/guiding aspects. In our view, only the 
underlying CPS skill C3 includes aspects of advocating and guiding behavior (C3: Following rules of engagement, e.g., prompting other 
team members to perform their tasks). Overall, the moderate relations between CPS and the external collaboration measures of 
self-rated and teacher-rated collaboration in both models add validity to the PISA 2015 CPS assessment. 
Regarding RQ2, we found support for the predictive validity of the CPS measures by identifying whether individual PISA 2015 CPS 
performance could predict students’ co-player collaboration performance. In order to consider the effects of group composition on co- 
player CPS performance, we identified the extent to which the test-takers’ performance depended on the performance of their partners. 
If PISA 2015 CPS scores validly capture students’ ability to pool their knowledge, skills, and efforts (OECD, 2017), the collaborative 
performance should not only reflect the stronger student’s ability but rather be an interaction between both students abilities (D. J. 
Cooper & Kagel, 2005). As expected, we found a complex interaction between students’ individual CPS performance and their 
partners’ individual CPS performance in predicting the pairs’ joint co-player performance. Pairs in which both students had done well 
in the individual tasks did best, which provides further evidence for the validity of the individual CPS measures. Those students 
cooperated particularly well, resulting in a problem-solving performance they could not have achieved individually (Larson, 2010). 
Further support comes from the observation that pairs of students where only one partner had shown a strong individual CPS 
performance performed well but not as well as pairs where both students had shown strong individual CPS performances. These pairs 
were not able to cooperate well enough to exceed the strong partner’s individual CPS performance. Finally, the pairs consisting of two 
students with low individual CPS performance also showed the lowest co-player performance. This pattern of results provides evidence 
for the individual CPS scores’ predictive validity. 
Taken together, the results on both research questions investigated in this manuscript provide substantial support for the validity of 
the H-A approach to measure students’ CPS ability employed in PISA 2015. Students’ CPS performance in the H-A tasks exhibited 
relatively consistent associations with the subjective self-reported and external teacher-reported evaluations before and after 
reasoning was included and reading was controlled for. Furthermore, individual CPS scores were predictive of the students’ interaction 
quality in the co-player collaboration. With these results, we gained empirical evidence on the relations between performance in the H- 
A CPS assessment and other collaboration measures for the very first time. Moreover, these results contribute knowledge to CPS 
research by showing to what extent H-A assessment approaches can capture the dynamics of natural communication, which has been 
questioned by scholars (Karaoglan Yilmaz & Yilmaz, 2019). 
Changes to international educational policies regarding CPS will likely happen soon as a result of the publication of the special 
policy report on the PISA 2015 CPS assessment commissioned by the OECD (Gauvain, 2018). The PISA 2015 CPS results may have a 
noticeable impact in diverse areas, including revisions to school curricula and changes to educational performance targets in 
participating countries. Therefore, empirical evidence of the validity of the PISA 2015 CPS assessment and results is crucial so that the 
PISA 2015 CPS instrument and its results can be adequately evaluated and so that sensible educational and political policy changes can 
subsequently be implemented. Because other political and educational programs are currently considering the inclusion of CPS, such as 
the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP: Graesser et al., 2018) in the US, CPS can are likely to remain relevant in the 
educational context well into the future (Rosen et al., 2020). Empirical evidence, as provided by this study, of the validity of the PISA 
2015 CPS assessment approach and its results are therefore of paramount importance. 
6.1. Limitations 
Because the underlying empirical evidence is often lacking or insufficient, many skeptics within and outside of academia have 
questioned the validity of the PISA assessments and similar programs. The current study, therefore, aimed to provide evidence that can 
inform current debates regarding the PISA 2015 CPS assessment. Although the findings are associative, and thus, do not allow for 
causal inferences, our findings are nevertheless essential for evaluating the PISA 2015 CPS assessment and its results. The results 
combined traditional (i.e., subjective self-rated collaboration, external teacher-rated collaboration, and reasoning) and new meth­
odological approaches (i.e., computer-based CPS assessment using simulated agents). However, to support future CPS research, some 
main limitations of the design of this study should be highlighted so that researchers can avoid them in future studies. 
First, we should address the limitations of the co-player CPS measure. The task we employed was a PISA 2015 CPS task that shared 
the same underlying methodology, and therefore, its relation to the PISA CPS factor was naturally strong. Also, we did not record the 
actual interactions between the students, thus, limiting our understanding of the social processes that lead to the final performance. 
However, we argue that the interaction between partners’ individual CPS scores in the prediction of their co-player performance was in 
line with the expected pattern of results, supporting the CPS scores’ validity. Future studies should include more distal CPS tasks and 
monitor the social dimension of CPS more closely to corroborate or contrast our findings. 
We should also address limitations that affect the extent to which our study allows us to draw implications for PISA. In this study, 
we assessed only four of the seven original PISA CPS tasks, and our sample was much smaller than is typical in PISA studies. Further, 
due to the brief amount of time allotted for conducting this study, the data were collected only in Germany using the PISA 2015 CPS 
units in German. Finally, the expectations and stakes associated with our study’s results are likely substantially lower than in the actual 
deployment of PISA. As the PISA 2015 CPS tasks aimed to be culturally independent to the highest possible extent, the results and 
implications are, theoretically, generalizable to the other cultures that participated in the PISA 2015 assessment. However, this should 
also be investigated empirically in future studies. 
Finally, the scoring employed in the PISA 2015 CPS assessment is not without limitations. As with all "expert ratings," the scoring

7. Conclusion 
Collaborative problem solving (CPS) has been receiving increasing international attention because much of the intricate work in 
the modern world is performed by teams (Fiore, Graesser, & Greiff, 2018). To better understand the assessment of CPS, our study 
investigated the validity of the H-A approach from different perspectives by identifying the relations between CPS performance based 
on the PISA 2015 CPS approach and existing measures of co-player, self-reported, and teacher-reported collaboration. We found that 
performance in H-A tasks was moderately related to more conventional assessments of individual CPS skills. Moreover, the individual 
CPS scores, as based on the H-A tasks, predicted the performance of students working with partners on structurally similar tasks. The 
results supported the PISA 2015 CPS assessment as well as the underlying CPS framework by identifying the extent of predictive, 
construct, and discriminant validity. Collaboration with computerized agents seems to be able to evoke similar dynamics as the 
collaboration with human partners.
