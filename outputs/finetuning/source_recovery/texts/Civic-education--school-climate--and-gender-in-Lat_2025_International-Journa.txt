A B S T R A C T

Keywords:
Gender
Latin America
ICCS
Civic education
Open classroom climate
Multilevel path analysis

This multilevel path analysis of data collected from the International Civic and Citizenship Study
investigates how differing civic education experiences between girls and boys predict notions of
citizenship across five Latin American countries. Findings show statistically significant differences
by gender. Generally, classroom activities, including the value of civic learning in the classroom,
willingness to participate in classroom activities, and an open classroom climate were positively
related to engagement, while student experience with abuse had a negative relationship. Additionally, evidence suggests that civic learning at school had a stronger impact on male students.
This finding varies between countries and notions of citizenship. Teachers can increase civic
learning with participation opportunities and monitor their interactions with girls and boys to
ensure equity.

1. Introduction
In 1948 the United Nations declared education a universal right, regardless of nationality or gender (United Nations, 1948).
However, gender disparities continue to produce uneven educational experiences for girls and boys.1 More recently, UNESCO UNESCO
(2021a) found that girls currently account for two-thirds of the globally illiterate population. UNESCO UNESCO (2021b) further
clarifies that, unlike absent and illiterate boys, girls are less likely to have access to education due to early marriage or pregnancy,
minority status, traditional social roles, bias against women in STEM fields, and gender-based violence. To address this, the United
Nations (UN) (2016) and Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC) (2016) have issued position statements
calling for greater efforts in gender mainstreaming and gender equality in Latin America. Policy initiatives and systems’ reforms have
improved access to education and UNICEF’s (2022) current report shows that over the past 20 years more students–of both genders–are attending school.
Even with the global efforts at gender parity, quantitative analyses show that gender continues to be a strong predictor of
achievement in subjects such as literacy, math, science, and civic education (Barber & Torney-Purta, 2009; Lee & Al Otaiba, 2015;
Mullis et al., 2020; Ortega et al., 2021). Looking even further down the line, gender has been found to frequently interact with predictors of achievement in civic education, such as school climate (Quintelier & Hooghe, 2013) and civic participation at school

Discussions on improving civic education and citizenship typically begin with building a democratic ethos in the classroom that
centers deliberation and practicing aspects of democratic civic engagement that students will enact as adults (Pace, 2019, 2021;
Levinson & Fay, 2019; Lo, 2022). However, an insufficient amount of international and comparative research examines how girls and
boys perceive, and are affected, differently (Knowles et al., 2018). Therefore, the specific focus of this study is the interaction of civic
education and gender among Grade 8 students. The findings from this analysis will inform the academic literature by centering differences by gender. By breaking down analyses into aspects of identity, educators can gain insights that will help adapt civic education
to meet the needs of all students.
To explore the differing implications of civic education for girls and boys, we ask three research questions. First, do students’
perceptions of civic engagement vary by gender? Which aspects of civic education have a differing relationship with various notions of
citizenship for girls and boys? Finally, is there a gender difference between preference for notions of citizenship that include personally
responsible, conventional, and social-movement citizenship? Addressing these questions will assist educators in making changes to
schools that aspire to make civic education more equitable for girls and boys. This is accomplished using data from the ICCS that
contains nationally representative samples of schools across five countries (Malak-Minkiewicz & Torney-Purta, 2021). Understanding
the connection between gender, education, and citizenship provides insight into the future of civic education in individual countries.
2. Literature review
The goal of this study is to better understand how girls and boys experience contemporary civic education in five Latin American
countries. Additionally, we suggest school factors that will potentially increase achievement in civic education, promote an open
classroom climate, and ultimately engage students in future political participation as citizens in their individual countries. To
accomplish this, we review various notions of citizenship and aspects of civic education, with special consideration of the Latin
American context.
2.1. Citizenship education
The International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS) was designed by the International Education Association to
investigate how students in their eighth year of schooling, across a wide range of countries, are prepared for their roles as adult citizens.
As part of this study, data were collected on students’ civic knowledge, civic attitudes, and civic engagement. The study measured
factors related to students’ home and peer contexts and school and classroom environments, as well as factors related to wider
community contexts and the education system. The resulting data regarding civic knowledge, attitudes, and engagement and their
various interpretations permit further analyses (Blaskó et al., 2019; Schulz et al., 2016). Barber and Torney-Purta (2009) posit that
schools are laboratories where students practice citizenship and develop values needed to maintain democratic societies. In schools,
students practice democracy by participating in social institutions like student councils and classroom debates. Schools are integral in
developing the upcoming generation’s political values, especially in regions where the older generation’s political values came from a
different system (Torney-Purta & Barber, 2005).
An open classroom climate has been the center of research on international comparative research for decades (Torney-Purta, 1991).
More recently, an open classroom climate has come to include a positive school environment with norms, values and expectations that
foster positive development and learning (Cohen et al., 2009; Thapa et al., 2013). Supporting this assertion, Halpin and Croft (1963)
and Thomas (1976) characterize an open school climate by energy, active progress toward organizational goals, and a focus on the social
needs of teachers and students. Additionally, other aspects of civic engagement in school including student teacher relations, civic
participation in school, willingness to participate in school have garnered increased attention. For example, Maurissen et al. (2018)
found that good student-teacher relations and value of participation were all associated with students’ sense of an open classroom
climate.
Civic education focusing on discussion and disagreeing about salient ideas in an open climate has demonstrated a positive relationship with students’ civic knowledge, civic self-efficacy, and expected future political participation (Knowles et al., 2018). For
example, Torney-Purta and Barber (2005) found a significant association between learning about voting and students’ expectations
that they will vote in school elections. Furthermore, their analysis showed a high correlation between learning about voting and
holding norms about conventional political participation. This data supports the previously held beliefs that more learning and
participation in democracy leads to more positive attitudes in the individual. Blaskó et al.’ (2019) study of non-cognitive ICCS civic
outcomes identified educational variables that predict positive civic self-efficacy and expected electoral participation among students
in several European countries. Despite variable factors, including region and family background, the authors aver that schools still
have a statistically significant impact on civic knowledge, in addition to non-cognitive behaviors, attitudes, and expectations. Within
the school, each classroom can foster its own open classroom climate which is characterized by instruction, activities, and norms to
promote individual expression and contributions of class members. Rapa et al. (2020) describe an open classroom climate as one that
cultivates critical consciousness. This, in turn, promotes socially just actions within the classroom and community at large. Campbell
(2008) suggests that a classroom with an open climate may increase the likelihood of political participation among disadvantaged
students. Cultivating a safe, diverse, and socially just atmosphere in the individual classroom and school community at large likely has
a positive impact on the future political participation of young adults.
Focusing on Latin America, Cox (2010) compared national citizenship curricula across several Latin American countries and

Civic education researchers in Latin America have also studied open classroom climate using large-scale data. In evaluating

latent class analysis of Chilean classrooms. Students in involved classes showed positive associations with these values: interest in and
discussion of politics, school involvement, and citizenship self-efficacy. While we have presented only a sampling of the research that
exists connecting open classroom climates and related measures to democratic civic education, the effects of these differences by
gender remain under examined.
2.2. What kind of citizen?
Citizenship can be defined in several ways. For example, Sant (2019) identified five discourses of democratic education including
liberal, deliberative, multiculturalist, participatory, critical, and agonistic. Attachment to these ideas reflects an individual’s ontological and epistemological assumptions. Research demonstrates that beliefs can influence how and what teachers teach (Gainous &
Martens, 2012; Knowles, 2019b; Reichert & Torney-Purta, 2019). These debates center around the various perceptions of citizenship
and their implications for educational curriculum (Westheimer & Kahne, 2004). Parker (1996) identified types of citizenship as
“traditional,” “progressive,” or “advanced.” Traditional prioritizes how the government functions, progressive prioritizes civic participation, and advanced draws “attention to the tensions between pluralism and assimilation” (Westheimer & Kahne, 2004, p. 239). Some
other perceptions of citizenship stem from the ideas of Freire (1990) and Shor (1992) on the left that encourage social change, to
Bennett et al. (1999) on the right who have conservative views of citizenship education and character.
From the various perceptions, the ICCS data contains items measuring three types of citizenship, which include personally
responsible citizenship, conventional citizenship, and social movement citizenship. Schools typically advocate for notions of democratic
citizenship associated with volunteerism, compliance, charitable contributions to society, lend a hand in their communities, obey local
laws, and nationalism (Camicia & Knowles, 2021). Westheimer and Kahne (2004) argued that citizenship should be more than this.
Good citizens are aware of public issues and grapple with the nuance of difficult and critical ideas. Expansive definitions of citizenship
have led to more scholarship on civic education and democracy that focuses on three different citizenship types: personal responsibility, conventional, and social movement related (Westheimer, 2008).
The International Civic and Citizenship Study measured three different notions of citizenship. Personal responsibility for citizenship
might be classified as “duty-based” (Hooghe & Oser, 2015; Irribara & Carrasco, 2021). Personally responsible citizens take a moral
orientation to citizenship. They believe that creating a “good” society is an individual responsibility and they often exhibit behaviors
that reflect this belief such as obeying laws, voting, picking up litter, and paying taxes. Conventional citizenship is often described as the
middle-ground. It has also been called “participatory” (Westheimer & Kahne, 2004), “mainstream” (Hooghe & Oser, 2015), and
“monitorial” (Irribara & Carrasco, 2021). Conventional citizenship prioritizes knowledge of government agencies and roles as well as
endorsement of peaceful protest, human rights, environmental preservation, and community activism. Conventional citizens believe
that building a good community is a collective responsibility and are likely to participate in a mix of government-sponsored and
non-institutional political parties. A third type, social movement related, also called “social justice oriented” (Westheimer & Kahne,
2004) and “socially engaged” (Irribara & Carrasco, 2021). These citizens critically evaluate social and political institutions and look for
inequities. They are likely to endorse systemic change and actively seek solutions as part of their quest for social justice. They focus on
ways citizenship can affect others and strive to find new ways to protect marginalized populations and the environment. These three
types of citizenship–responsible, conventional, and social movement–align with constructs used to analyze the ICCS 2016 data
(Westheimer & Kahne, 2004). Our research utilizes these constructs in a path analysis to predict the relationship between citizenship
type and civic education factors.
2.3. Gender and citizenship
Research from international and comparative education provides ample evidence that girls and boys have significant differences in
citizenship behavior and affect, stating, “In every country and in every cycle of ICCS, females tend to be more tolerant, empathetic, and
to participate more than males” (Sandoval-Hernández et al., 2018; Sandoval-Hernández & Miranda, 2021). This assertion is borne out
by Knowles and McCafferty-Wright (2015), whose mediation path analysis found that girls are more likely to participate in social
movement citizenship. Likewise, Knowles et al. (2018) conducted a review of research of student social and citizenship attitudes
reflected in international civic education data. They reported that girls were more supportive of women’s rights, more likely to endorse
social justice causes, more likely to excel in reasoning about issues regarding human rights, and more positive and supportive of
marginalized groups, including immigrants.

Historically, citizenship education and civic activities were male-dominated (Mayer & Schmidt, 2004). This may be related to
social deficit models in which females have fewer resources and lack power (Hooghe & Stolle, 2004; Barber & Torney-Purta, 2009).
More recently however, Hahn and Quaynor (2012) report no differences between girls and boys in U.S. social studies classrooms, and
Bohan (2017) reports that more North American girls take AP social studies courses than boys. Internationally, Barber and
Torney-Purta (2009) note that the gender gap in civic knowledge has closed in most countries, and that an open classroom climate
further helps reduce a gender gap in equal rights for women. Yet despite this upward positive trend toward gender equality, gender in
social studies education tends to be a marginalized topic in research (Bohan, 2017). In certain regions, gender inequality reflected in
politics, attitudes, and student data continues to be a concern. Along with these scholars, Seffner (2022) points out that in Brazil,
gender continues to be politicized. He asserts that school is “a laboratory to prepare for life in the full public space” and calls for explicit
democratic education to address perpetuating gender gaps and to create a new dialogue for up-and-coming student citizens.
Because the interaction between gender and civic education varies by region, any study of these topics must be situated regionally.
Torney-Purta and Barber (2011) suggest large scale data analysis may illuminate differences between regions, allowing different
countries and cultures to dialogue about effective policy and practices. Cultivating the global conversation is important, but the needs
of individual students remain a more immediate concern. The International Education Association’s International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS) and the Civic Education Study (CIVED) data provide insight into civic knowledge and beliefs in
different world regions. Malak-Minkiewicz and Torney-Purta (2021) caution against shallow descriptive analyses, comparing one
region’s achievements to another. They assert that local school systems and education are produced within the context of local culture,
geography, history, and politics. Thus, an analysis of civic education data must consider unique systems and histories of each region.
Such focused analysis will likely produce fruitful findings for educators, administrators, and researchers who seek to understand and
improve civic education in their regions.
2.4. Theoretical framework for understanding civic engagement among youth
We now turn our discussion to understanding civic education and the role that stakeholders play in promoting civic engagement
among youth. Torney-Purta et al. (2010) combined social theories of learning to create a framework for generating research questions
and appropriate methods of assessing civic engagement. Among the dependent variables they considered were meaning, identity, efficacy, agency, and experience. Specifically, civic engagement research seeks to impact civic meaning, identity, efficacy, agency, and
experience. The researchers suggested that the variables which might affect civic engagement among youth include three domains.
Personhood includes an individual’s gender, ethnicity, age, immigrant status, family background, and educational expectations.
Context involves country, region, neighborhood, economic situation, curriculum, climate for discussion, participation opportunities,
out-of-school groups, and family. Finally, process includes observational learning, apprenticeship, and scaffolding by adults.
While schools cannot make changes to a student’s personhood, they can affect the context and process of civic learning. Schools can
improve curriculum, classroom climate, and participation, as well as providing learning experiences, apprenticeship experiences, and
scaffolding by adults. As a theoretical framework, Torney-Purta et al. (2010) provide validation for the present study, which seeks to
illuminate different aspects of civic education affected by schools and how these factors are influenced by students’ gender.
Many institutions (family, neighborhoods, local churches, and government) promote citizenship and gender equality (Torney-Purta
& Barber, 2011; Torney-Purta et al., 2010). The central question of this study focuses on how civic education can be perceived
differently by girls and boys and how it has different effects on each. Understanding this connection can support women’s participation
in politics in Latin America, which are variable, conflicting, and highly correlated to gender (Morgan & Buice, 2013).
This project informs the research literature on civic education in a variety of ways. While large-scale international data sets have
shown the potential impact that civic education can have, differing relationships between girls and boys have been under analyzed
(Knowles et al., 2018). We assess how girls and boys perceive seven different domains of civic education differently. Data presented by
Schulz et al. (2018) from ICCS 2016 supports our assertion that student experiences in civic education differ by region and are
impacted by perceptions of gender equality. The report (Schulz et al., 2018) provides a cross-country comparison of student perceptions on a variety of social issues including perceptions of democracy and citizenship, equal rights for ethnic and racial groups,
terrorism, climate change, and trust in political institutions. While each social issue merits further investigation, we focus on international ratings of endorsement of gender equality. Five of the 21 countries included in the 2016 report are Latin American: Chile,
Colombia, the Dominican Republic, Mexico, and Peru. Students in Chile endorsed gender equality significantly higher than the ICCS
2016 average score for all countries. Students in the four remaining Latin American countries all rated gender equality significantly
lower than the ICCS 2016 average. In fact, student endorsement of gender equality in Mexico and the Dominican Republic scored more
than three points below the average, among the lowest scores by any country in the survey. Considering historical evidence showing
that girls and boys have different access to education (Permanyer & Boertien, 2019) and experience education differently (Samuelsson
& Samuelsson, 2016), and noting trends in recent literature that beliefs about gender inequality persist in some regions (Schulz et al.,
2018), we analyzed ICCS school climate data from the five Latin American countries for interactions with gender.
3. Methods
The International Civic and Citizenship Education Study (ICCS) was designed to investigate how students in their eighth year of
schooling, across a wide range of countries, are prepared for their roles as adult citizens. In addition, we test how a subset of these
domains relates to various notions of citizenship and how different types of civic education may have different relationships for girls
versus boys. The endorsement of gender equality scores from Chile within the ICCS international report demonstrates a unique

outcome comparative to other countries in its geographic region (Schulz et al., 2018). Still, this study demonstrates the effectiveness of
a large-scale statistical analysis in order to understand how gender and school factors might enable or constrain students’ engagement
in certain types of citizenship. To accomplish this goal, we use data measuring civic knowledge, attitudes, and engagement. This
information allows for the analysis of civic knowledge, attitudes, and engagement as well as their various interpretations (Schulz et al.,
2016).
One of the primary purposes of the ICCS is to explore how young people are preparing to become active citizens in a world where
the concept of democracy continues to evolve (Blaskó et al., 2019; IEA, 2009). As previously stated, five Latin American countries–Chile, Colombia, the Dominican Republic, Mexico, and Peru–participated in the ICCS 2016 study. Schulz et al. (2018) note that
each participating Latin American country places a high value on citizenship and civic education, while cautioning that the countries
have unique curricula, economic strengths, histories, and political contexts. Therefore, although we employ a collective term, Latin
America, to represent an ICCS data subset, our analysis presents general conclusions about the data for each country. The selection of
these five countries was based on visible trends in the data regarding gender equality reported by Schulz et al. (2018). The analysis
answers a call by Carvalho (2014) for additional scholarship focused on the intersection of education and gender in Latin America,
such as recent contributions by Sandoval-Hernández and Miranda (2021).
In addition to demographic variables and civic knowledge, the ICCS 2016 data sets contain summary variables measuring seven
factors of civic education and three notions of citizenship (see Appendix A). Each summary variable is a composite of several survey
questions which ask students to report on these school factors using a Likert Scale.
3.1. Measures
Using the student scores from the five Latin American countries (Chile, Colombia, the Dominican Republic, Mexico, and Peru), we
focus on seven school factors related to civic education (see Appendix A). Each of these are scaled by the developers of the ICCS from a
variety of measures using Rasch Item Response Theory modeling and included within the dataset. The individual items for each scale
are included in the appendix.
To assess different perspectives of civic education, the ICCS 2016 data set surveyed respondents on three different citizenship-type
categories: personal responsibility, conventional, and social movement related. Personal responsibility for citizenship might also be called
“duty-based” (Hooghe & Oser, 2015; Irribara & Carrasco, 2021). Personally responsible citizens take a moral orientation to citizenship
and believe that creating a “good” society is an individual responsibility. This perspective was measured by combining the following
seven survey items: working hard, always obeying the law, ensuring the economic welfare of their families, making personal efforts to
protect natural resources, respecting the rights of others to have their own opinions, supporting people who are worse off than you, and
engaging in activities to help people in less developed countries.
Conventional citizenship has also been called “participatory” (Westheimer & Kahne, 2004), “mainstream” (Hooghe & Oser, 2015),
and “monitorial” (Irribara & Carrasco, 2021). Conventional citizenship includes knowledge of government agencies and roles, as well
as community and social activism. Conventional citizens believe that building a good community is everyone’s responsibility. This
perspective was measured by combining the following six survey items: voting in every national election, joining a political party,
learning about the country’s history, following political issues in the newspaper, on the radio, on TV or on the Internet, showing respect
for government representatives, and engaging in political discussions.
Social movement related citizenship relates to “social justice oriented” (Westheimer & Kahne, 2004) and “socially engaged” (Irribara
& Carrasco, 2021). Socially engaged citizens critically evaluate social and political institutions and actively seek solutions as part of
their quest for social justice. This perspective was measured by combining the following four survey items: participating in peaceful
protests laws believed to be unjust, participating in activities to benefit people in the local community, taking part in activities promoting human rights, and taking part in activities to protect the environment.
Schulz et al. (2008) described ICCS items focused on types of civic education. Since 14-year-olds have few opportunities for
meaningful civic engagement, schools can apprentice students to become future adult citizens by practicing civic engagement
in-house. Seven types of civic education related to future engagement include student reports of civic learning at school, students’
willingness to participate in school activities, students’ perception of openness in classroom discussions, students’ perceptions of
student interaction at school, students’ experiences of physical and verbal abuse at school, students’ perceptions of teacher-student
relations at school, and students’ perception of the value of participation at school (see Appendix A). The ICCS dataset contains
pre-constructed scales for each of these aspects of civic education.
In addition to the key measures of the study, civic knowledge and student socio-economic status were included in the study as
relevant control variables. The ICCS 2016 contained 88 questions across four content and two cognitive domains that include civic
society and systems, civic principles, civic participation, civic identities, knowing, and finally reasoning and applying (Schulz et al.,
2016). Students’ socioeconomic status (SES) is consistently related to civic learning (Campbell, 2008; Knowles, 2019a), and the authors included SES as a variable to strengthen the model and control for this influence. The SES variable includes the internationally
normed measure provided within the dataset (derived from the occupational status of both parents, the highest educational level of
both parents, and the number of books in the home [Schulz et al., 2016]). Finally, gender was included as a key variable in the study
with a coding of 1=female and 0=male.
3.2. Analysis
A series of steps were taken to assess the connection between support for differing types of citizenship, civic education, and gender.

First, the means for girls and boys were calculated for each of the types of civic education. We then tested whether the differences
between those means were statistically significant. This step was completed using the SPSS complex data function to calculate means
with the appropriate replicate weights. This analysis will allow for an understanding of which types of civic education girls and boys
were perceiving differently. Upon completing this analysis, we chose five measures of civic education with the largest differences to
include in the next step.
Subsequently, a multilevel path analysis was conducted using types of civic education. This analysis was conducted in Mplus7. Both
the aforementioned means testing and the multilevel path analysis were conducted using the appropriate replicate weights as outlined
in the ICCS User Guide (Köhler et al., 2018). The analysis used students as the level 1 unit and classrooms (1 per school) were the level 2
unit, and the missing data estimation in Mplus7 was used to preserve as many cases as possible. This analysis used seven measures of
civic education, gender, civic knowledge, and SES to predict each type of citizenship within a single model. From the standardized path
coefficients, the strongest predictors were then used to create an interaction term between a measure of civic education and gender.
These interaction measures were then included in a separate model. To assess the quality of the models, various goodness of fit indices
are presented. We evaluated our models based on commonly used cutoffs, which include above 0.9 for the Comparative Fit Index (CFI),
Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) below 0.01 is excellent, 0.05 is good, 0.08 is mediocre, and the Standardized Root
Mean Square Residual (Standardized RMR) of below 0.08 for good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999). In addition, the multilevel modeling allows
for the calculation of the Standardized RMR at each level. Finally, a figure was produced based on the strongest interaction term to
visually demonstrate the differences between civic education and citizenship. The Mplus7 code used to produce this figure, as well as
the interaction models, is provided in the appendix.
Using path analysis allowed us to examine how each of these school factors predicts citizenship and how that varies across girls and
boys. We examined the standardized beta coefficients and chose the two strongest in each model to create an interaction variable with
gender. This allowed us to determine whether the interaction between school factors and gender produces a significantly different
support for each notion of citizenship. The appendix includes an example Mplus7 input for the path analyses with the interaction
graph. Once we understand how school factors and gender influence citizenship types, we can then attempt to increase a school’s
positive impact on students’ future civic participation.
4. Results

calculated for girls and boys (see Table 1). Each of the civic education measures were standardized with a mean of 50 and a standard
deviation of 10. Within Table 1, bolded numbers with asterisks indicate statistically significant differences between girls and boys.
Open classroom climate and experiences with abuse at school both demonstrated a significant difference between the genders across all
five countries. Boys indicated greater experience of physical and verbal abuse at school. However, girls had higher scores across the
remaining measures of civic education on all other statistically significant differences, with some exceptions in Chile. For example,
with respect to open classroom climate, girls had higher scores compared to boys. Upon calculating the average differences between
scores, the average difference between girls and boys are, in order from largest to smallest, 2.9 for experience with physical and verbal

student interaction at school. Based on these findings, we chose to include the five variables with the largest differences in the subsequent steps, which included abuse at school, perception of open classroom climate, civic learning at school, value of participation,
and willingness to participate in school activities.

5. Discussion
Gender equality issues impact all citizens, including the types of citizenship in which they choose to engage. Students are especially
impacted by the endorsement of, or lack of, gender equality. Education may appear equitable in many places, but researchers and
policy makers must monitor trends in learning experiences and achievements of girls and boys to verify or refute the appearance of
equity. While girls and boys may attend the same school and participate in the same lessons, they may not experience them in the same
way.
Schools play a major role in supporting citizenship among young people (Waghid, 2015; Waghid & Wong, 2020). To understand
which school factors impact civic education in five Latin American countries, we used ICCS 2016 survey data to construct models that
predicted different types of citizenship in youth. The differences across countries suggest ways in which civic education could be
adapted for country, region, and gender. This analysis contributes to a growing body of research on civic education in Latin American
countries as scholars explore the ways gender and context intersect in classroom and political spaces. Notions of global citizenship in
Latin American countries are ideologically problematic as “global” perspectives often advantage the economic West (Sant &
González-Valencia, 2018).
We acknowledge this disparity and our position as outsiders whose perspectives are not attuned to the nuances and differences in
Latin American communities and countries. Working with an international data set to make cross-country comparisons requires us to
assume more distal positions that may blur or obscure diversity and specifics. Nevertheless, exploring how students’ gender affects
civic learning and citizenship in Latin America contributes to the developing research base (Sandoval-Hernández & Miranda, 2021).
Based on the findings, we make three assertions that have relevance to the field of civic education.
First, we conclude that girls and boys experience civic education differently, as demonstrated by the data within in Table 1. Across
each of the domains of civic education, girl students tended to score higher than their boy classmates. This difference was most
consistent with perception of an open classroom climate, with boy students having a higher score in each country. Moreover, boy
students indicated more experience with verbal and physical abuse. Perhaps this aligns with the finding that girls continue to outscore
boys in the ICCS civic knowledge data, even as they demonstrate a lower factual knowledge and a lower overall perception of the
importance of all types of citizenship (Pereira et al., 2015; Schulz et al., 2018. Examining the ways that gender impacts civic education
is of interest to educational researchers who study political attitudes in children (Barber & Torney-Purta, 2009; Malin et al., 2015;
Morgan & Buice, 2013; Sampermans & Claes, 2018) and gendered political attitudes and participation in adults (cf. Hooghe & Stolle,
2004; Jennings, 2006). While the conclusions presented in this article are a step toward clarifying the different ways girls and boys
experience civic education in these Latin American countries, more research is needed to understand the reasons why.
Three school factors consistently promote citizenship in youth in five Latin American countries. One of these, open classroom
climate, has received considerable attention as a consistent predictor of positive civic outcomes (Campbell, 2008; Knowles &
8

International Journal of Educational Research 130 (2025) 102501

K.A. Chamberlain et al.

McCafferty-Wright, 2015). However, our results found that an open classroom climate was generally a weaker predictor of citizenship
than students’ willingness to participate in school activities school and students’ perception of the civic learning in the classroom. This
suggests that increasing student willingness to participate in school activities and emphasizing civic learning as an important part of
education will increase student engagement with citizenship. Considering that Maurissen et al. (2018) found relationships between
open classroom climate and other student-centered aspects of civic education, future research should assess how these measures relate
to, and potentially amplify, each other. From a theoretical standpoint, future research should consider civic engagement developmentally. For instance, does student willingness to participate contribute to an open classroom climate? Or does an open classroom
climate increase students’ willingness to participate in school activities? Understanding how these measures of civic engagement
develop sequentially could have an influence on curriculum, instruction, and public policy. For instance, teachers and administrators
might work together to incentivize and increase school activity participation or curriculum coordinators and writers might promote
open classroom climate in civic education classrooms. Increasing both would likely lead to greater civic engagement among young
people. More research is needed to understand how citizenship develops among these constructs. Additionally, for countries where
perceptions of physical and verbal abuse at school have a statistically significant relationship with decreased civic participation, school
reforms that promote safety might help to decrease instances of abuse. These are a few general ways local school and government
leaders can promote citizenship in the rising generation.
Secondly, the findings illuminate the role of civic education in promoting differing types of citizenship. For example, gender
predicts the likelihood that a person participates in personally responsible citizenship in the Dominican Republic and Mexico. Boys are
more likely to participate in personally responsible citizenship in the Dominican Republic than their female counterparts. Conversely,
girls in Mexico are more likely than boys to engage in personally responsible citizenship. The other three countries in our study,
however, showed no statistically significant relationship between gender and participation in personally responsible citizenship. Civic
knowledge showed statistically significant relationships to personally responsible citizenship in all five countries in our research,
supporting the extant literature (Knowles et al., 2018).
Conventional citizenship, unlike personally responsible or social movement related, has fewer defining characteristics. Those who
primarily practice conventional citizenship believe in collective responsibilities of communities. Gender influences participation in
conventional citizenship for two of the five countries. However, the countries of influence are not the same. Boys in Chile and Peru are
more likely to practice conventional citizenship than girls in those same countries. Civic knowledge showed the opposite effect on
conventional citizenship as it did on personally responsible and social movement related citizenship. As civic knowledge increases,
participation in conventional citizenship declines in four of the five countries. (Data on Mexico were not statistically significant.) This
movement away from duty-based, conventional citizenship toward more active citizenship may mirror Dalton’s (2008) description of
shifts in U.S. political engagement.
More active notions of citizenship, such as social-movement citizenship, are often positioned as essential to address inequities in
social and political institutions by actively seek to change those institutions (Westheimer & Kahne, 2004). These findings have implications for future activism within their societies. For example, boys in the Dominican Republic are more likely than girls to
participate in social movement related citizenship, while girls in Mexico are more likely than boys in Mexico to do so. This finding
supports Barber and Torney-Purta (2009), who noted that in developing countries, girls participate in social movements more than
boys. Once again, Chile, Colombia, and Peru showed no statistically significant relationship between gender and social movement
related citizenship. All five countries showed a statistically significant relationship between levels of civic knowledge and the likelihood of participating in social movement related citizenship. Civic education likely plays a part in fostering more personally
responsible and social movement related citizenship among youth.
Finally, the research provides some evidence that civic education can have different implications for girls versus boys. Civic
learning in the schools has a stronger relationship with civic notions. For the Dominican Republic, the interaction was found with
personally responsible, and social movement citizenship. The interaction was also found for Colombia with all three notions of citizenship. Civic learning in the schools had a stronger relationship with personal responsibility for male students. Finally, civic learning
had a stronger relationship with conventional citizenship in Peru. It is important to note that civic learning in the schools was measured
by how much students are taught to vote, how laws are introduced, how to protect the environment, how to contribute to solving
community problems, how to protect rights, understanding political issues, and how the economy works. These are all laudable goals
for civic education. However, the data provides evidence that an emphasis on these issues supports increased support for various
notions among boy students. Future research should continue assessing the existence of gender bias with civic education curriculum. It
is possible this could be due to teacher-student interactions that favor boys. The findings may align with the results of Pereira et al.
(2015) that found that male students had higher scores in factual knowledge, while female students excelled in reasoning/human
rights knowledge. Aligning with that study, Sandoval-Hernández and Miranda (2021) found that girls scored higher on ICCS items
measuring civic participation and tolerance (Sandoval-Hernández & Miranda, 2021). In other fields, Ortega et al. (2021) found that
mathematics instruction in Chile favored boys, as teachers were more likely to interact with boys. These findings suggest that either
curriculum, teacher behaviors, or teacher-student interactions might be more frequent or effective for boys in Latin American civics
classrooms. It is possible that slower growth of girls on these three types of citizenship are moderated by girls’ prior knowledge, affect,
and behavioral instincts.
In narrowing our discussion to five Latin American countries, we hoped to find regional trends that might inform policy or civic
curricula. With a few exceptions discussed above, our findings revealed the opposite, reinforcing the thesis of Crossley (2010) and
others (Lee & Al Otaiba, 2015). In education, context matters. Even among united countries, countries that have political, ideological,
and global alliances (Allianza del Pacífico, 2022), the variables and types of citizenship interact differently. We conclude that revisions
to improve civic education ought to be carried out by local and national leaders with a more complete understanding of a country’s
9

International Journal of Educational Research 130 (2025) 102501

lands, culture, indigenous history, and government. While there is much to be learned from comparative education studies and an
exposure to rich civic educational scholarship from all over the world, it is the local children in local schools whose local circumstances
matter. Educators would be wise to foster a deeper understanding of democracy and citizenship specific to their students and their
country (Sant & González-Valencia, 2018; Sears, 2016).
6. Implications and conclusion
The findings from this study bring up important new areas for research and practice. For example, girl students answered more
favorably to the notions of civic education, while boy students indicated a greater degree of experience with abuse at school. Given
this, there still was evidence that civic learning in the school had a stronger influence on boys. These seemingly contradictory findings
could be explored by assessing what contextual factors lead to gender being a statistically significant factor influencing notions of
citizenship. An example question for future research is: What contextual factors lead boys to be more likely to participate in personally
responsible citizenship in the Dominican Republic and girls to be more likely to do so in Mexico? Through continued research of the
confluence of education, types of citizenship, and unique contextual factors like traditional gender norms, perceptions of equality
among and between the genders, individual family values, religion, the political atmosphere, and nationwide goals of each country’s
leadership a clearer picture of what impacts personally responsible citizenship will develop.
Citizenship practices are dependent on contextual factors as well, including regional politics (Seffner, 2022), student socioeconomic status (Collado et al., 2015), and teacher ideology (Knowles & Castro, 2019). The political climate in each country could
promote or dissuade the proliferation of different types of citizenship (Morgan & Buice, 2013; Seffner, 2022). Our conclusions are
intended to promote positive civic outcomes and address gender inequities while raising awareness regarding imbalances in civic
education experiences for girls and boys. Most urgently, our results should provide support for addressing the unequitable impact of
civic learning for girl students and the degree of abuse at school by the boy students.
