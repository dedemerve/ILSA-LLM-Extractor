The relationship between education and subjective well-being has been a topic of interest for researchers and
educators alike. In this paper, we aim to explore the intricate interplay between educational performance and
subjective well-being, conducting a copula-based analysis of the PISA 2022 dataset while using factor analysis to
construct a novel multi-dimensional subjective well-being measure.
Copulas provide a flexible framework for the modeling of multivariate dependencies while allowing for the
independent consideration of marginal distributions. This approach can provide more nuanced analysis of variables under observation. The results of the analysis indicate that there is a weak relationship between educational performance on the PISA assessment and subjective well-being.
These findings have important implications for educators and policymakers, as they suggest that efforts to
improve educational performance and subjective well-being simultaneously may not be successful. Additionally,
the study highlights the relatively weak relationship between educational performance and life satisfaction
among adolescents.

2.2. Educational performance and subjective well-being
Concerning academic performance and SWB, previous studies
generally conclude that higher life satisfaction is positively associated
with higher academic performance whereas studies finding the reverse
(higher academic performance is positively associated with higher life
satisfaction) are more limited.
Gilman and Huebner (2006) conducted a study involving 485 participants from two Southeastern states in the United States, who
completed the Students’ Life Satisfaction Scale (SLSS) and the Behavior
Assessment System for Children (BASC). The average age of the participants was 14.45 years, and the findings revealed that individuals
reporting higher levels of life satisfaction were more likely to achieve a
higher self-reported grade-point average (GPA). Similarly, Proctor et al.
(2010) observed comparable results in a study primarily involving adolescents from the United Kingdom, also employing the SLSS. Participants in this study, aged between 16 and 18, who reported very high life
satisfaction, similarly reported better academic performance. Along the
same lines, in a study of Norwegian secondary school students, a correlation analysis identified a positive relationship between life satisfaction and self-reported academic performance (Diseth et al., 2012).
Consistent with these findings, in a longitudinal study of 197 Chinese
secondary school students, Wu et al. (2020) further demonstrated that
current life satisfaction, positive affect, and optimistic outlooks toward
future life were all positively linked to improved academic outcomes.
Using a different methodology, Cárdenas et al. (2022) applied machine
learning models to Australian data for students in grades 8 and 9. The
authors found that greater SWB among students was predictive of better
academic scores 7–8 months later. Overall, results indicated there was
evidence of an effect of SWB on academic performance but not the
reverse. Given the different data sets and countries of focus in previous
research, cultural differences and social perspectives on education may
yield varying results. However, the findings are generally consistent
suggesting the results are applicable across different cultures and
countries.
In a related yet distinct study, Crede et al. (2015) examined whether
parental educational attainment moderated the association between life
satisfaction and academic performance among German high school
students. The findings revealed that only maternal education served as a
moderating factor, and this effect was limited to students whose mothers

1.1. Research aims and objectives
The aim of this paper is to employ a copula-based analytical framework to comprehensively investigate and explore in detail the underlying relationship and dependence structures between PISA scores and a
SWB construct derived from a factor analysis using various questions
from the student and well-being questionnaires in the 2022 PISA
assessment. By integrating copula modeling techniques, this study aims
to enhance the precision and depth of understanding of the intricate
relationships between these dimensions. More specifically, the research
seeks to quantify the joint distribution of academic performance and the
SWB construct using copula models and identify the nature and strength
of the dependence patterns between these variables.

2.1. Education and subjective well-being
The relationship between education and SWB has been a subject of
research interest across many different research disciplines. The
component of life satisfaction, in particular, has been widely studied
through empirical research but the results tend to vary depending on the
countries being studied and the chosen methodology. With regards to
educational achievement and SWB, results of existing research generally
indicate that SWB is higher for individuals who have attained a higher

While many studies indicate that the relationship between SWB and
educational performance is unidirectional, other studies found evidence
of a reciprocal relationship (Kleinkorres et al., 2020; Ng et al., 2015;
Quinn & Duckworth, 2007) supporting self-determination theory. This
theory suggests that psychological needs such as competence are an
important input for SWB (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Strong academic performance contributes to fulfilling the psychological need for competence, thereby enhancing an individual’s SWB. In a prospective
longitudinal study of fifth graders, Quinn and Duckworth (2007) found
that SWB predicted academic performance and vice-versa when controlling for IQ. In a similar longitudinal study of 821 middle school
students from the southeastern United States, Ng et al. (2015) conducted
a multiple regression analysis which revealed evidence of positive
reciprocal causal relationships between students’ self-reported life
satisfaction and their academic grades. Specifically, Grade Point
Average (GPA) measured at Time 1 significantly predicted life satisfaction at Time 2, while life satisfaction at Time 1 significantly predicted
GPA at Time 2. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated that life satisfaction served as a stronger predictor of academic performance
compared to short-term affective experiences in school. Kleinkorres
et al. (2020) analyzed data from the National Educational Panel Survey
and found evidence of mainly positive reciprocal relationships between
academic achievement and different indicators of well-being.
Steinmayr et al. (2016) employed cross-lagged structural equation
modeling to analyze data collected over a one-year period from 290
German students, focusing on measures of SWB and test anxiety. GPA
was utilized as an indicator of academic achievement in their study.
Results indicated that higher GPA predicted an increase in life satisfaction; however, they did not find a reciprocal effect in contrast to the
reciprocal effect found by Ng et al. (2015). In general, the results
confirm the unidirectional relationship between SWB and educational
performance (Cárdenas et al., 2022; Crede et al., 2015; Diseth et al.,
2012; Gilman & Huebner, 2006; Proctor et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2020)
but in the opposite direction, indicating that the relationship between
the two is not without nuance.
While the precise mechanisms linking life satisfaction and academic
performance are complex, a notable tenet of higher academic performance is an individual’s level of determination and grit, also known as
resilience. These components exhibit a positive relationship with SWB
(Kwon, 2021) demonstrating that there are education-specific components of SWB that contribute to an individual’s academic performance in
addition to the extensively researched socioeconomic status.

2.4. Copula modeling and well-being
The use and application of copula models are prevalent in the fields
of finance and risk management. However, research applications of
copula models to social science data are still relatively novel with wideranging uses in terms of methodology. Nikoloulopoulos and Mentzakis
(2017) applied a copula-based Markov model to analyze structural relationships between components of SWB using data from the British
Household Panel Survey (BHPS). Contrary to prior research findings, the
results indicated that no income-related indicators were statistically
significant in predicting overall SWB. Similarly, gender and education
were also found to lack significant predictive power, suggesting the
presence of alternative underlying factors that influence an individual’s
SWB. D’Urso and Vitale (2021) applied non-parametric Bayesian networks based on the normal copula distribution to explore the relationships between SWB indicators from data from Italy. The authors found
that all dimensions of SWB are interdependent, highlighting the significant influence of the education system on labor market conditions and
its broader implications for economic growth. Looking slightly beyond
the domain of SWB, Luechinger et al. (2008) applied copula-based ordered probit models to analyze job satisfaction of public sector and
private sector workers in Germany. The authors identified statistical
evidence of self-selection bias, with public sector workers reporting
lower levels of job satisfaction. This finding underscores the importance
of accounting for heterogeneity, as failing to do so may result in biased
interpretations of the results. Hohberg et al. (2021) used a multivariate
copula generalized additive model to determine the interdependence of
poverty dimensions using data from the Indonesian Family Life Survey
(IFLS). The analysis revealed that higher levels of educational attainment are positively associated with increased income, with tertiary education demonstrating the most substantial impact. Additionally, urban
households were found to exhibit both higher income and greater
educational achievement compared to their rural counterparts.

2.3. Subjective well-being and PISA
Research utilizing the PISA 2018 dataset has provided significant
insights into factors influencing student SWB. Esidio et al. (2023) found
that girls report lower levels of SWB than boys, particularly in terms of
life satisfaction and emotional experiences, highlighting gender disparities in well-being. Similarly, Govorova et al. (2020) employed
network analysis to examine the role of school-related factors in students’ well-being and found that their overall influence is limited.
However, they identified a sense of belonging as a key positive
contributor to SWB, whereas fear of failure emerged as a central negative influence.

Arab Emirates, Ireland, Panama, Mexico, Spain, and Hong Kong, China,
found a significant decline in SWB over this period. Notably, student
health was identified as the most influential factor affecting well-being.

3.1. PISA data set
PISA is the OECD’s Program for International Student Assessment. It
measures 15-year-olds’ ability to use their reading, mathematics, and
science knowledge and skills to meet real-life challenges. PISA has been
conducted every 3 years since 2000. The planned assessment for 2021
was delayed until 2022 and the next assessment is planned for 2025 to
further reflect post-Covid difficulties (OECD, n.d.-b).
The 2022 PISA assessment included 81 participating countries and
economies, with results reported using scales. The average score is 500
with a standard deviation of 100 and separate scores are reported for
reading, mathematics, and science (OECD, n.d.-a). The assessment also
included a questionnaire about the respondents’ background. In the
2022 assessment, this questionnaire was comprised of 93 questions
designed to provide details concerning a student’s background,
schooling, and non-cognitive/metacognitive constructs (OECD, 2023a).

5. Results and discussion
As shown in Table 2, the results of fitting the PISA score and SWB
construct values (derived from factor scores) to normal, log-normal, and
gamma distributions indicate that the normal distribution provides the
best fit for the data according to AIC and BIC. Since p > 0.05 for the log
normal for both PISA score and the SWB construct variables, we fail to
reject the null hypothesis at the 5 % significance level, which states that
the distribution of the values follows the log-normal distribution.
To assess goodness-of-fit for the selected copulas, two tests were
conducted: the Cramér von Mises test and Scaillet test. Test statistics and
P-values were estimated using parametric bootstrap with 1000 iterations, employing the pseudo maximum likelihood method (see Table 3).
The Cramér-von Mises test is a rank-based goodness-of-fit test while the
Scaillet test is kernel-based with a fixed smoothing parameter. The null
hypothesis of the goodness-of-fit test posits that the chosen copula
adequately represents the dependence structure of the PISA scores and
life satisfaction variables.
The results of the Cramér-von Mises tests indicate that none of the
selected copulas are a good fit for the data. Therefore, we reject the null
hypothesis at the 5 % significance level as p < 0.05. However, the results
of the Scaillet goodness-of-fit test indicate that we fail to reject the null
hypothesis at the 5 % significance level as p > 0.05. As the goodness of fit
tests do not provide a conclusive result, the copula information criterion
(CIC) was calculated indicating that the Gaussian copula provided the
best fit. It has been noted that having the best rank according to the CIC
does not mean it will pass goodness-of-fit tests (Ko & Hjort, 2019).
After fitting the data to the Gaussian copula, the estimated parameter
value of 0.005 suggests that there is an observable weak dependence
between PISA scores and the SWB construct. While the parameter estimates of all the copulas are close to zero, they nonetheless indicate a
weak dependence between PISA scores and the SWB construct. This
relationship suggests that students with higher academic performance
tend to report higher levels of life satisfaction. However, the small effect
size suggests that SWB may not be the primary driver of academic
performance.

Gaussian copula captures most of the correlation structure (see Fig. 1).

6. Robustness check
To assess the robustness of the copula approach, a weighted least
squares (WLS) regression examining the relationship between PISA
scores and SWB, while controlling for the Index of economics, social and
cultural status (ESCS) and gender (ST004D01T). This approach served as
a validation check to determine whether the weak association identified
in the copula analysis holds under a different statistical framework.
Our regression model used student self-reported life satisfaction
measured on a 0–10 scale (ST016Q01NA) as the dependent variable
with independent variables using plausible values 1–10 for mathematics
(PVMATH), reading (PVREAD), and science (PVSCIE) scores, as well as
ESCS and gender, and using final adjusted student weight (W_FSTUWT).
This analysis was conducted using IEA IDB Analyzer 5.0 and SPSS
Version 27.

7. Conclusion
For the average 15-year-old in a developed economy, there is a weak
association between PISA performance and SWB. The results of applying
various copulas to analyze the PISA dataset using a novel multidimensional SWB construct suggest a weak dependence between PISA
scores and SWB. Extending the analysis to incorporate conventional
correlational analysis (Pearson) also yields a weak correlation coefficient (0.026) supporting the findings of this copula-based analysis.
Additionally, a robustness check using a WLS regression—incorporating
all ten separate plausible values for ‘Reading’, ‘Mathematics’, and ‘Science’—further corroborates these results.
These findings highlight the complexity and challenges faced with
accurately measuring SWB and its components due to its intricate and
multifaceted structure. While the relationship between PISA

10. Limitations
The research conducted has various limitations. The first is temporal
as this analysis only accounts for 2022. Additionally, the existence of
confounding variables may have affected the interpretation of the results. Another limitation is the cross-sectional nature of PISA data which
does not allow for causal assumptions. The findings of this research
paper are based on copulas and therefore any causality can only be
inferred from theoretical reasons. Additionally, the hierarchical and
nested structure of the PISA 2022 dataset is not accounted for in the
methodology. Therefore, there is an opportunity to refine this novel
approach to account for intra-cluster dependencies.

8. Implications for relevant stakeholders
Relevant stakeholders for this research are education and well-being
researchers as well as policy makers in national governments. The
findings from this paper could help education policy makers in national
governments to have a more comprehensive overview when it comes to
adjustments in national education policy because of their respective
country’s PISA scores. Policies targeting the performance on the PISA
assessment should potentially be treated as separate from policies
designed to increase SWB. However, it is important to note that not all
negative affect, which would lower SWB, is wholly negative. Negative
affect with high motivational intensity such as fear might increase an
individual’s focus and the resultant educational performance (Gable &
Harmon-Jones, 2010). More complex and detailed instruments that can
accurately measure the motivational intensity of negative affect could
help determine the trade-off between SWB and educational performance
and allow policy makers the opportunity to determine if specific interventions are worthwhile. As for education and well-being researchers, it is evident that this paper adds to the body of research
concerning the relationship between education and SWB.

Future research
This paper supports the area of research which is SWB and education,
more specifically, the relationship between SWB and educational performance. Firstly, it would be valuable to conduct a longitudinal study to
determine the long-term effects of educational performance on SWB.
While the age variable is by default a constant given the nature of the
data set, it would be interesting to see how the association between SWB
and educational performance or achievement may vary depending an
individual’s life stage or age group. By addressing these issues, future
research can contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of
subjective well-being and its underlying components.
