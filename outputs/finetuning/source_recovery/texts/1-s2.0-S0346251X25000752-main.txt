A B S T R A C T
Reading comprehension is crucial in education, serving as the foundation for acquiring knowl­
edge. In multilingual settings, children must develop these skills in multiple languages. However, 
there is limited understanding of comprehension levels across languages and of the role of 
influencing factors such as language exposure, non-verbal intelligence (NVI), socioeconomic 
status (SES), and extramural reading (ER). This study explores how these variables impact the 
reading comprehension of young learners (aged 10–11) in English (foreign language), Spanish 
(main language), and Basque (regional language for Basque-immersion participants). The par­
ticipants were divided into a high-intensity (HI) group (N = 118) and a low-intensity (LI) group 
(N = 81) within Basque-immersion programs. Results showed correlations among reading 
comprehension scores in Spanish, English, and Basque. While both groups demonstrated similar 
Spanish comprehension levels, the HI group excelled in English, particularly among high-NVI 
learners. LI learners scored lower in Basque compared to Spanish. Regression analyses indi­
cated that NVI and, to a lesser extent, SES influenced reading comprehension across languages. 
Also, HI learners engaged more in English ER, whereas LI learners favored Basque. However, ER 
did not have a significant impact on their reading scores. Based on these findings, pedagogical 
implications for multilingual education contexts will be discussed.
1. Introduction
Reading comprehension is a foundational skill in the educational development of children, essential for acquiring knowledge across 
various subjects. The importance of researching this skill has recently been highlighted by the alarming results from the 2022 Pro­
gramme for International Student Assessment (PISA), which revealed that around 25 % of EU students in secondary school do not reach 
a minimum proficiency level in reading in their mother tongue (European Commission, 2023). These concerning findings underscore 
the urgent need to improve reading skills and, in the case of the more and more common multilingual educational settings, this should 
be done across all languages of instruction. In fact, inadequate reading comprehension in the mother tongue might hinder students’ 
ability to engage with and understand content in foreign language settings, potentially affecting not only language acquisition but also

In the current educational context, it is becoming more and more common for children to be taught through the medium of a second 
or foreign language. In the case of teaching through a second language, which is present in the community, the term “immersion” has 
traditionally been used. This is the case, for instance, of French immersion in Canada, which has a long tradition (Swain, 2000). 
Especially in Europe, another term is often used interchangeably with immersion, i.e. Content and Language Integrated Learning or 
CLIL. While some authors claim that there are methodological differences between immersion and CLIL (Lasagabaster & Sierra, 2010), 
others have argued that CLIL is an “umbrella term” which includes immersion (Cenoz, 2015; Marsh, 2008; Mehisto et al., 2008). As 
immersion is more often used to refer to a situation where the medium of instruction is a (co-)official language, we will use it in 
reference to Basque, as it is a minority language in our context. When talking about English, on the other hand, we will use the term 
“CLIL”, as English is not commonly spoken outside of the classroom.
Regardless of the label used (immersion or CLIL), it is a fact that an increasing number of children are encountering the challenge of 
greater exposure to second or foreign languages at younger ages, especially where English is concerned. In spite of this, research 
focusing on this young population is relatively limited. It is important to investigate what happens at this age, as it represents the 
foundation and starting point for foreign language and content learning. In multilingual environments, such as schools with CLIL and 
immersion programmes, grasping the elements that affect reading comprehension is crucial (Lorenzo et al., 2010). However, there is 
limited understanding of reading comprehension levels across different languages (Merino & Lasagabaster, 2015) and of the factors 
influencing these levels (Soto-Corominas et al., 2023).
To fill this gap, this study aims to investigate various factors influencing reading comprehension among young learners in a 
multilingual context. Specifically, the research focuses on non-verbal intelligence (NVI), socio-economic status (SES), extramural 
reading (ER), and the intensity of exposure to a foreign language through CLIL lessons. Participants were learning English with varying 
degrees of CLIL in a region where Spanish is the majority language and Basque is the regional language. They were divided into two 
groups: one enrolled in Basque-immersion schools with low-intensity CLIL programs, and another in non-immersion schools (where 
Spanish was the vehicular language) with high-intensity CLIL programs.
2. Literature review
2.1. Reading comprehension in multilingual schools
In recent years, the educational landscape has seen a significant shift with the adoption of CLIL alongside traditional English as a 
foreign language instruction. CLIL is present in monolingual and, also, in L2-immersion programmes, where three or more languages 
share the school timetable.
Given the importance of reading skills for academic success (e.g.,Talwar et al., 2022), it is not surprising that reading compre­
hension, and the strategies used by competent readers, have received a great deal of attention from researchers, focusing both on L1 
and L2 reading skills (e.g., Guzm´an et al., 2021; Maghsoudi, 2022). According to Cummins’ (2000) “Interdependence Hypothesis”, 
learners can be expected to transfer certain skills from their L1 to their L2 (or L3). For reading, this hypothesis has been supported by 
several studies. For instance, Kim et al. (2022) found a relationship between L1 reading comprehension and L2 reading comprehension 
for university students of English in South-Korea. In the case of young learners, Proctor et al. (2010) also observed a correlation 
between reading comprehension scores in English and Spanish in bilingual American fourth graders.
2.2. Reading comprehension in immersion contexts
Research in immersion contexts has been mainly conducted in Canada by Swain and her colleages (see review in Genesee & Jared, 
2008), but also in other contexts such as Ireland (´O Duibhir & Cummins, 2012) and Wales (Gathercole & Thomas, 2009). The results 
consistently show that immersion learners display higher achievements in reading in the immersion language when compared to 
learners with less exposure and without detriment of reading comprehension in the L1 (see Parsons & Lyddy, 2015 for Irish; Swain & 
Lapkin, 1982 for French). Also, French immersion learners have been compared to native speakers of French and their reading 
comprehension has been reported to be similar, although when reading in the L1 and reading in the immersion language is compared, 
immersion learners display higher levels of comprehension in the L1 (Genesee & Jared, 2008).
In the case of Basque immersion, two studies comparing learners’ performance in the four skills in Basque, Spanish and English, 
have shown that students with different amounts of exposure to the three languages obtained similar results in Spanish reading 
comprehension, indicating that the majority language is not negatively affected by Basque immersion (Lasagabaster, 2000; Merino & 
Lasagabaster, 2018). At the same time, when comparing learners who are fully immersed in Basque (model D), to those who only study 
in Basque 50 per cent of the time (model B) and those who only study Basque for 4 h a week (model A), Lasagabaster (2000) observed 
that those with the greatest exposure (model D) significantly outperformed model B students in reading comprehension, while model B 
students in turn scored significantly higher than model A students.
As for official surveys, the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS, 2021) has shown that fourth-year primary 
students in Basque immersion schools achieve significantly lower scores in Basque (the immersion language) compared to their scores 
in reading comprehension in Spanish. Additionally, according to the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2022, 
conducted at the secondary school level, the average reading scores in Spanish and Basque in the region where this study was con­
ducted have experienced a marked decline that has raised concerns within both the educational community and society at large.

2.3. Reading comprehension in CLIL contexts
CLIL, which integrates content subjects with language learning, has been praised for providing a richer linguistic environment 
compared to the conventional English as a foreign language (EFL) approach. Dalton-Puffer (2008) argues that one key reason why CLIL 
students often excel in reading comprehension compared to their non-CLIL peers is that CLIL gives learners extra motivation for 
reading. However, while in CLIL programmes speaking and writing have been extensively studied, research on reading and listening 
remains scarce, especially in primary education settings (P´erez-Ca˜nado, 2012). This imbalance underscores the pressing need for more 
focused investigations into these receptive language skills, especially among young children (Merino & Lasagabaster, 2018).
In particular, reading competence, long considered a skill positively influenced by CLIL (Dalton-Puffer, 2007; Hidalgo & Villarreal, 
2024; Ruiz de Zarobe, 2011), shows mixed results in primary education (Soto-Corominas et al., 2023). For instance, Lorenzo et al. 
(2010) demonstrated that increased exposure through CLIL significantly enhances reading comprehension, with primary students in 
Andalusian bilingual sections showing notable improvements. Similarly, P´erez Ca˜nado (2018)reported that primary school students in 
CLIL programs displayed slightly better reading comprehension than their peers in traditional EFL programs. Expanding on this, 
Hidalgo and Villarreal (2024) compared low- and high-intensity CLIL programs alongside a standard EFL program in primary schools. 
Concentrating on the receptive skills of 11-year-old students, the results reveal that high-intensity CLIL programs lead to significant 
improvements in listening and reading proficiency compared to low-intensity ones. In contrast, no significant differences were 
observed between low-intensity CLIL and EFL programs.
Conversely, Nieto Moreno de Diezmas (2016) found that CLIL had minimal impact on reading comprehension when comparing 
4th-year primary education students in CLIL and EFL groups. In the multilingual context of Catalonia, Pladevall-Ballester and Vallbona 
(2016) conducted a two-year study on 5th and 6th graders, revealing that non-CLIL students outperformed their CLIL peers in listening 
skills, with no significant differences in reading competence. These findings suggest that the benefits of CLIL in primary education are 
not so clear-cut and that the most promising outcomes may only emerge with more prolonged and intensive exposure (Nieto Moreno de 
Diezmas, 2016; Pladevall-Ballester & Vallbona, 2016).
In fact, it is essential to acknowledge the potential limitations and challenges of CLIL. Many studies on CLIL tend to overlook the 
diverse contexts of its implementation and the varying exposure conditions of participants (Pladevall-Ballester & Vallbona, 2016). 
Bruton (2011) also questions the overly enthusiastic claims about CLIL’s superiority highlighting that methodological flaws in some 
studies might exaggerate the benefits. He emphasizes the need for robust comparative research that accounts for several confounding 
variables, such as SES, highlighting the importance of a balanced view that considers both the strengths and limitations of CLIL.
2.4. Non-verbal intelligence and reading comprehension
While NVI has been shown to significantly influence reading comprehension in one’s L1 within educational and psychological 
domains (e.g., Bouchefra et al., 2022; Hage & Stroud, 1959; Ribeiro et al., 2016), its relationship with reading comprehension in a 
foreign language remains a compelling but under-researched area. NVI, which involves understanding and analyzing visual infor­
mation and solving problems without the use of language, has often been overshadowed by the focus on vocabulary acquisition in 
language learning studies.
Although the limited research available has identified NVI as a factor influencing reading comprehension in a foreign language 
(L´azaro-Ibarrola, 2024a), its role is most effectively understood within the broader interaction of linguistic skills and cognitive 
abilities. Rather than acting as a direct determinant, NVI appears to contribute to reading comprehension by facilitating 
problem-solving, pattern recognition, and other domain-general skills. Evidence suggests that its effects are often mediated by lin­
guistic and broader cognitive factors, underscoring a complex and multifaceted relationship.
For instance, Morvay (2015) examined the combined effects of L1 reading ability, L2 proficiency, and NVI on the reading 
comprehension of 64 Hungarian secondary school EFL students. This study demonstrated that NVI is a key predictor of reading 
comprehension performance, highlighting its importance in the learning process. More recently, research by Ghonchepour and 
Moghaddam (2018) and Phan (2021) illustrated how NVI contributes indirectly to reading comprehension through its interaction with 
verbal intelligence, short-term memory, and broader cognitive capabilities. In particular, Ghonchepour and Moghaddam (2018)
investigated the impact of both verbal and non-verbal intelligence on the learning of 60 Iranian adolescent EFL students. Their findings 
revealed that NVI, when combined with linguistic skills, significantly contributes to the comprehension of grammar and reading texts. 
Phan (2021) explored the relationships between non-verbal reasoning, short-term memory, and foreign language learning among 30 
Vietnamese secondary school EFL learners, finding that strong NVI significantly enhances language comprehension and acquisition. 
While these studies underscore the importance of NVI, they also indicate that its impact is contingent on other factors, such as linguistic 
proficiency and cognitive strategies.
In addition, the relationship between NVI and reading comprehension varies across contexts, including language background and 
learner age. Four decades ago, Genesee and Hamayan (1980) already identified a correlation between NVI and language development, 
although this correlation was weaker among younger learners. Supporting these findings, Lasagabaster (2000) demonstrated that NVI 
exerted a stronger influence on adolescent learners compared to younger children, likely due to the increasing cognitive demands of 
language tasks in older learners. Similarly, Casaponsa et al., (2015) explored NVI’s role in L2 reading achievement and found that it 
interacted with proficiency levels and cross-language similarities, particularly through mechanisms such as the cognate facilitation 
effect. Kristiansen’s (1990) earlier research also identified a positive correlation between NVI and foreign language acquisition, 
including reading comprehension, across diverse linguistic and cultural contexts. While these studies’ findings remain relevant, they 
should be interpreted with caution, given the indirect nature of NVI’s contributions.

Therefore, the existing body of research suggests that while NVI alone does not determine reading comprehension, it contributes 
significantly as part of a broader cognitive framework, supporting the notion that cognitive skills beyond linguistic abilities may play a 
role in mastering a new language (Chomsky, 1972). To the best of our knowledge, the impact of NVI on reading comprehension in CLIL 
settings, among primary school students, and across three different languages has yet to be explored. This gap in the literature 
highlights a promising avenue for further research into how non-verbal cognitive skills may shape reading comprehension during the 
early stages of education that combine content learning with language instruction in multilingual contexts.
2.5. Extramural reading
Recently, researchers have turned their attention to the link between extramural English and language proficiency, recognizing that 
children’s diverse experiences with the target language outside of school can significantly influence their language learning (De Wilde 
et al., 2020; L´azaro-Ibarrola, 2024b; Soto-Corominas et al., 2023). As with NVI, while the bulk of studies have zeroed in on vocabulary 
acquisition, there is still a hazy and limited picture of how extramural English, particularly ER, affects learners’ reading skills, perhaps 
in part because children do not often engage in ER (De Wilde et al., 2020; Lindgren & Mu˜noz, 2013; Peters, 2018).
A study by De Wilde et al. (2020) involving Dutch-speaking children aged 10–12, who had no formal English instruction, found a 
positive correlation between reading English books and their listening, reading, and writing skills. However, regression analyses 
revealed that ER was not a significant predictor. Expanding the focus to Secondary Education, Lancaster (2018) and Rasc´on Moreno 
and Bretones Callejas (2018) examined the variables responsible for the differences between CLIL and EFL groups. Both studies 
revealed that CLIL students are more actively engaged in various extramural activities, such as reading books and magazines, 
compared to their non-CLIL peers, even though ER was not specifically examined. Additionally, they found that out-of-school exposure 
to English accounted for the differences in language attainment between the groups exclusively in Secondary Education. These findings 
support Mu˜noz’s (2015) assertion that the benefits of CLIL programs tend to become more pronounced over time.
Soto-Corominas et al. (2023) did examine the effects of ER on the overall performance of first graders from 14 different schools, 
measuring their progress at both the beginning and end of the school year. Consistent with the findings of Lancaster (2018) and Rasc´on 
Moreno and Bretones Callejas (2018), the study found no evidence that frequent English reading correlated with improved perfor­
mance by the end of the course. The authors suggest that this lack of correlation may be due to the young age of the sample group, who 
may not engage in reading activities frequently enough to produce significant results.
More promising results are reported by Wouters et al. (2024), who analyzed the relationship between extramural English and 
learners’ listening and reading comprehension across three age groups: grade 6 (ages 11–12), grade 8 (ages 13–14), and grade 10 (ages 
15–16). The study found that all three groups were regularly exposed to English during their free time and showed that reading English 
texts was the least popular activity among them. Notably, the results indicated that watching non-subtitled TV in English positively 
influenced both listening and reading proficiency.
2.6. Socio-economic status and reading comprehension
SES is another significant factor in educational outcomes, influencing students’ achievements across various domains. It encom­
passes aspects such as family income, parental education levels, material possessions, and occupational status, and it is widely used in 
educational research to identify and understand disparities among social classes (Butler & Le, 2018).
SES has been positively linked to the English performance of students in monolingual education systems (Rasc´on Moreno & 
Bretones Callejas, 2018). However, the impact of SES on immersion programs has not been extensively studied (let alone on reading 
comprehension in primary school), and existing research offers mixed results on general language performance (Lorenzo et al., 2021). 
For instance, Fern´andez-Sanjurjo et al. (2019) found significant SES-related differences. Specifically, CLIL students from disadvan­
taged backgrounds performed worse than their non-CLIL peers with similar socioeconomic status, indicating that SES significantly 
influences academic performance in both CLIL and non-CLIL settings. In contrast, Rasc´on Moreno and Bretones Callejas (2018) found 
that SES significantly influenced reading comprehension in non-CLIL settings, where primary schoolers from higher SES backgrounds 
consistently outperformed their lower SES counterparts. Conversely, SES did not significantly impact reading comprehension in CLIL 
contexts, suggesting that CLIL may mitigate some of the educational disparities associated with socioeconomic background. Although 
in a secondary school context, Lorenzo et al. (2021) arrived at the same conclusion. Their study showed that SES emerged as a major 
factor influencing reading skills in non-bilingual environments. However, this effect was much less pronounced in CLIL groups.
Recently, Soto-Corominas (2023) investigated maternal education as an indicator of SES and found that Grade 1 children with more 
highly educated mothers exhibited better outcomes across all four language skills. These findings align partially with those of Lindgren 
and Mu˜noz (2013), who observed that parents’ educational levels significantly impacted only the reading scores of fifth graders. Also, 
regarding parents’ educational level, Anghel et al. (2016) demonstrated that it did not have a significant effect on the reading ability of 
children finishing primary school, as this subject was taught in Spanish. However, for the subjects taught in English, students whose 
parents had less than upper secondary education showed poorer exam results. This suggests that bilingual education may present more 
challenges for these students compared to those whose parents have a higher educational level.
Research suggests a significant correlation between higher SES and increased participation in CLIL programs. Studies indicate that 
CLIL students often come from more affluent backgrounds and demonstrate higher levels of motivation and prior achievement (Caira 
et al., 2023; Dallinger et al., 2016). This trend is partly attributed to parental choice, as families from higher SES backgrounds tend to 
place greater value on multilingualism and actively seek out CLIL education for their children. Mediavilla et al. (2019) observed a 
“cream skimming” effect, where higher SES students concentrate in bilingual schools while their lower SES counterparts remain in

selective. Consequently, while CLIL aims to promote educational equity, it may inadvertently reinforce existing socioeconomic dis­

questions about educational access and the potential for these programs to exacerbate rather than mitigate social inequalities in 
education.
In our context, however, children do not have the option to choose whether to participate in CLIL programs. Their decision is 
limited to selecting a Spanish-medium school, which automatically includes CLIL instruction, or an ikastola (Basque-immersion 
school), where CLIL is not part of the curriculum. This distinction underscores that participation in CLIL is not a matter of individual or 
parental preference but rather a byproduct of the type of school chosen.
2.7. The study
As CLIL gains popularity, schools are implementing CLIL approaches earlier to enhance foreign language skills. However, these 
decisions are often not grounded in empirical research, which is particularly sparse for primary schools. Furthermore, research focused 
on reading comprehension is limited, frequently neglecting individual differences and the diversity of multilingual contexts. To 
address these gaps, we conducted a study examining the impact of intensity of exposure, ER, SES and NVI on the reading compre­
hension levels of primary school learners in English, Spanish, and Basque. With that goal in mind, this study aimed to answer the 
following research questions: 
1. Are there differences in the reading scores obtained in the English and Spanish tests between the HI and LI groups?
2. Do LI learners achieve similar scores in Spanish and Basque?
3. Is there a significant relationship between reading scores across different languages?
4. Are there differences in the amount of ER between the HI and LI groups?
5. Do ER, SES, and NVI influence reading scores?
3. Method
3.1. Participants
Our pool of participants consists of a total of 199 young EFL learners in primary school. They belong to five state-funded schools 
located in the same Spanish city.
Prior to the study, a background questionnaire was distributed to a larger sample, but some individuals were excluded from the 
study based on their answers. Only individuals who met the following criteria were included in the final sample: (i) The learners had 
begun learning English in school and no later than the first year of primary; (ii) they did not use English at home with parents, siblings, 
or friends; and (iii) they had not spent more than two weeks in an English-speaking country.
The two groups of participants differed in intensity of exposure to English in school and are labeled as high-intensity (HI) and low- 
intensity (LI). The HI group consists of 118 participants (66 boys and 56 girls) with a mean age of 10.3 years. These participants receive 
5 h of EFL lessons and 5–7 h of CLIL lessons per week, resulting in a total weekly exposure of 10–12 h to English, while the remaining 
hours are in Spanish. The accumulated exposure for this group is estimated to be around 2200 h, which is 700 h more than the LI group. 
The CLIL lessons include science, math and social sciences. On the other hand, the LI group comprises 81 participants with a mean age 
of 10.5 years. In this group there were 42 boys and 39 girls. The LI learners were in Basque-immersion schools and received all lessons 
in Basque except for 4 weekly hours of Spanish, 5 h of EFL lessons per week and a maximum of two weekly hours of CLIL devoted to art 
and craft. The total weekly exposure to English of this group is 5–7 h, and the accumulated exposure is estimated to be around 1500 h. 
Thus, the HI group does not receive any lessons in Basque, and we only investigate Basque reading comprehension in the LI group.

(both EFL and CLIL), and the cumulative exposure to language learning over time.

3.2. Instruments
The two groups completed a SES questionnaire, a NVI test, an ER questionnaire, and reading comprehension tests in English and 
Spanish, and in the case of the LI group, also in Basque.
3.2.1. SES questionnaire
The participants completed an official socioeconomic status (SES) questionnaire used by the regional government (Supplementary 
Material 2). The students took the questionnaires home on paper before completing them online at school to ensure they understood 
how to provide the required information. This test, with a maximum score of 30 points, included information on parental education, 
occupation, income, living space, and access to resources (books, culture, and technology). The resulting SES score is a continuous 
variable, allowing for a nuanced assessment of SES (that is, it captures a range of socioeconomic positions rather than categorizing 
participants into groups). This enables more precise comparisons and statistical analyses, as small differences in SES can be accounted 
for rather than being grouped into arbitrary categories.
3.2.2. Non-verbal intelligence test
The Raven’s Progressive Matrices (RPM) Test (version for children 5–12) was employed to measure NVI. All the participants in our 
sample completed this test and their answers complied with the test validation criteria. These validation criteria are very strict and 
state that only tests that show consistency across sets when compared to norms (with a maximum variation of +2/-2) and in which 
learners do not score higher on a later set than on an earlier one (reversals) are valid.
3.2.3. ER questionnaire
The questions on ER were extracted from a longer questionnaire on extramural English and Basque, including other activities such 
as watching tv or listening to music. However, for the aims of the present study, only the questions related to ER were considered 
relevant. In the ER questionnaire, the learners were asked about the amount of time they devoted to reading in English and Basque 
outside school (Supplementary material 3).
3.2.4. Reading comprehension tests
All the participants completed a reading comprehension test in English and Spanish (Supplementary material 4). The participants 
in the LI group also completed a reading comprehension test in the regional language (Basque). All the tests were administered online 
by the school teachers during classroom sessions. The students were given 50 min to complete each test.
The reading comprehension tests in Spanish and Basque consisted of four texts with a total score of 18 points (one point per correct 
answer). Selected from official language tests conducted by the regional government for learners of this age, both assessments were 
matched in structure, difficulty, and question typology to enable valid comparisons between regional and majority language per­
formance in the LI group. The tests included four diverse text types, accompanied by multiple-choice questions that assessed various 
reading abilities, including inferential comprehension, information synthesis, and detection of irony.
Given that English is a foreign language for our participants, the level of the English test is lower than that of the Spanish and 
Basque tests. The English test selected was an adaptation of the Cambridge Flyers test, set at an A2 level of proficiency according to the 
Common European Framework of Reference for Languages. It consisted of four sections with a total score of 28 points (one point per 
correct answer). 
1. Reading definitions and matching 10 words.
2. Reading and completing 5 gaps in a continuous dialogue.
3. A story and 6 questions about it.
4. A picture and 7 true/false statements about it.
3.3. Data analysis and statistics
The results from the reading tests, SES questionnaire, NVI tests, and ER questionnaire were coded for each student.
For the first research question, which examines differences in reading scores based on the intensity of instruction, independent 
samples t-tests were used to compare reading comprehension scores between groups.
To address our second research question, which involved comparing the Spanish and Basque scores obtained by learners in the LI 
group, we employed a paired samples t-test.
For the third research question, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were used to examine the relationships between language scores 
(Spanish, English, and Basque).
To answer our fourth research question, independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare the amount of ER reported by 
learners in both groups.
For our final research question, which aimed to determine whether ER, SES, and NVI influence reading scores, we first used in­
dependent samples t-tests to compare SES and NVI scores between groups. However, to compare Spanish and English reading scores 
divided into quartiles based on NVI scores, we used Wilcoxon tests due to the smaller sample sizes. We then applied multiple regression

4. Results
4.1. Reading scores
As Table 2 shows, the mean Spanish reading scores for both groups are extremely similar (HI 6.27 and LI 6.20) and so are the SDs 
(HI 1.77 and LI 1.86). The results of a t-test comparing both groups indicates that the small differences are not statistically significant 
(p = .348) and this is reinforced by a very small effect size (d = .135). On the contrary, there is a clear difference between groups in the 
reading scores obtained in English. The mean English reading score for the HI group (7.33) is notably higher than the LI group’s mean 
(6.27). This difference is statistically significant (p = .001) and it presents a moderate to large effect size (d = .548).
In the LI group, we conducted a comparison of the scores obtained in the majority language, Spanish, and the regional language, 
Basque. These scores are comparable due to the similar difficulty levels of both assessments. As shown in Table 3, the Basque scores 
(4.78) were notably lower than those obtained in Spanish (6.20). A t-test revealed that the scores in Basque were significantly lower 
than those in Spanish (p < .001; d = .612).
To determine whether students show consistent performance patterns across their languages, we examined associations between 
reading scores in Spanish and English in each group, that is, whether those who obtained strong, medium, or weak scores in one 
language also tended to obtain similar scores in the other. In the case of the LI group, correlations between Basque and English and 
between Basque and Spanish were also considered. The results of the Pearson correlation tests revealed clear relationships among the 
languages in both groups (see Table 4 and scatter plots in Figs. 1–4).
As Table 4 shows, in both groups, a positive and significant correlation was observed between reading in Spanish and reading in 
English (HI group: r (118) = .511, p < .001; LI group: r (81) = .330, p = .003). Within the LI group, positive correlations were also 
identified between reading in English and reading in Basque (r (81) = .374, p < .001), as well as between reading in Spanish and 
reading in Basque (r (81) = .407, p < .001).
4.2. Extramural reading
In the HI group, 82 learners (69.49 %) reported reading in English at home, compared to 40 learners (49.38 %) in the LI group. Not 
only did the HI group have a higher proportion of participants engaging in ER, but they also spent more time reading in English, with a 
mean of 2.4 h per week compared to 1.9 h per week in the LI group. An independent samples t-test revealed that this difference was 
significant (p < .001). The effect size (d = .61) indicated a moderate to large difference between the groups, as can be seen in Table 5.
As for potential associations between ER and the score obtained in the reading tests, a Pearson correlation test revealed a non- 
significant relationship between reading at home and reading scores in the HI (p = .187) group (Table 6). Interestingly, however, a 
small to moderate correlation was found between reading in English at home and SES (p = . 009), suggesting that higher SES is 
associated with a greater likelihood of reading in English outside of school.
In the LI group (Table 7), no significant correlation was found between reading in English at home and reading scores (p = .667). As 
for ER in Basque, the majority of learners in the LI group (72/81 = 88.9 %) reported reading in Basque at home, with only 9 students 
indicating they do not read in Basque at all outside school. Similar to the findings for ER in English, no association was observed 
between reading in Basque at home and the scores achieved in Basque reading (p = .782).
4.3. Socioeconomic status
The analyses of the questionnaires revealed that the groups had very comparable SES levels (Table 8; Fig. 5). HI learners had a 
slightly lower mean and greater variation in scores, but these differences were not statistically significant (p = .453). Lack of statistical 
significance was confirmed by a very low effect size (d = .147).
The analysis examining correlations between SES and reading scores revealed differences between groups (Table 9). In the HI 
group, moderate correlations were found with reading scores in both English (p = .008) and Spanish (p = .003). In contrast, no sig­
nificant correlations were observed in the LI group (p > .05 for Spanish; p = .409 for English; p = .748 for Basque).

Finally, and given the relevance of the variables analyzed, multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine the influence of 
the three variables under scrutiny (ER, NVI, and SES) on reading scores. These analyses aimed to measure the impact of these variables 
on reading performance across languages and groups. A summary of the results is provided in Table 14. Since ER did not show a 
significant impact, it is not included in the table (detailed tables for each language and group are available in Appendix 1).
The multiple regression analyses reinforced and expanded upon the findings from our correlation analyses. In the HI group, both 
NVI (β = .412, p < .001) and SES (β = .240, p = .005) significantly predicted reading performance in Spanish, explaining 24.1 % of the 
variance (R2 = .241, p < .001). A similar pattern emerged in English, where NVI (β = .344, p < .001) and SES (β = .195, p = .029) were 
also significant predictors, albeit with a lower explained variance (R2 = .180, p < .001).
In the LI group, only NVI was a significant predictor. In Spanish, NVI (β = .303, p = .007) accounted for 9.9 % of the variance (R2 =
.099, p = .045), while in English, it remained the sole significant predictor (β = .233, p = .039), though the explained variance was 
weaker (R2 = .065, p = .158). In Basque, the model was marginally significant (R2 = .079, p = .094), with NVI (β = .279, p = .014) as 
the only significant predictor.
ER (hours per week reading) did not significantly predict reading scores in any model (p > .05), further confirming the lack of 
association found in the correlation tests.
In sum, across all groups and languages, NVI consistently emerged as the strongest predictor of reading performance, while 
SES showed a moderate influence, mainly in the HI group. ER had no measurable impact on reading scores in any of the models.
5. Discussion
In this study, we have examined the impact of SES, NVI and ER on the reading comprehension levels of young children (aged 
10–11) learning English as a foreign language in high and low-intensity tracks. Their reading comprehension has been measured in 
English (the foreign language), Spanish (the main language) and, for participants enrolled in Basque-immersion schools, also in Basque 
(the regional language).
Regarding reading comprehension in English, our results have revealed a clear advantage in the HI group, offering a positive 
outcome amidst the limited and conflicting findings reported for primary school learners (Nieto Moreno de Diezmas, 2016; P´erez 
Ca˜nado, 2018; Pladevall-Ballester & Vallbona, 2016). Concerning Spanish reading comprehension, no differences were found between 
Basque-immersion learners and non-immersion learners, indicating that immersion in a minority language does not compromise 
reading comprehension in the majority language (Genesee & Jared, 2008; Lasagabaster, 2000; Merino & Lasagabaster, 2015; Parsons 
& Lyddy, 2015). In the case of the immersion language, Basque, scores on reading tests were significantly lower compared to scores in 
the Spanish test. This suggests that proficiency levels can vary depending on the intensity and quality of the immersion program. While 
previous studies have found that learners in immersion contexts can achieve native-like proficiency levels in receptive skills by the end 
of schooling (Swain, 2000), the children in the present study have clearly not yet attained this level, as they were only in primary 
education. Thereby, it would be necessary to investigate whether this disparity diminishes in secondary school and whether these 
proficiency levels are sufficient to ensure comprehension of content subjects.
Clear correlations between reading scores in different languages were found in both groups. Learners who excel in reading 
comprehension do so regardless of the language, supporting Cummins’ (2000) Interdependence Hypothesis. Additionally, our results 
indicated that reading in English outside of school did not affect scores but was relatively frequent in both groups, contradicting 
previous findings reporting that learners do not read in English in their free time (Lindgren & Mu˜noz, 2013). HI learners reported more 
frequent engagement (2.4 h per week vs. 1.9 per week), consistent with Lancaster (2018). It is plausible that LI learners engage less in 
English reading due to attending Basque-immersion schools and needing to allocate time to read in Basque at home. Moreover, HI 
learners might have reported more hours of English reading at home because it is part of their homework. However, these in­
terpretations remain speculative, as our data do not distinguish between reading for pleasure and reading for academic purposes. 
Future research should refine questionnaire designs to explicitly differentiate between voluntary and school-mandated reading

Regarding Basque, ER was very common, possibly as part of school activities, yet like ER in English, it did not correlate with 
learners’ scores. Although we did not specifically address ER in Spanish, including it in future research would be worthwhile.
When considering overall SES levels, the correlation analyses and multiple regression analyses indicated that its impact was 
relatively weak and only evident in the HI group, where students from higher SES backgrounds tended to achieve better scores. The 
absence of correlations in the LI group may be partly due to the smaller variability in SES scores within this group and the lower 
statistical power of the regression model. Regarding the SES correlations observed in the HI group, their weak nature aligns with 
previous research suggesting that SES has a minimal influence on CLIL outcomes in secondary education (Lorenzo et al., 2021). Future 
studies would benefit from comparing the impact of SES on CLIL programs at both the primary and secondary levels.
Regarding NVI, the correlation analyses and multiple regression analyses revealed significant correlations in both groups and 
across all languages. The strongest correlation was observed in the main language, Spanish. Future studies could explore the reasons 
for this phenomenon. One hypothesis is that Spanish, being more widely spoken and used in daily life compared to Basque and English, 
leads to greater familiarity with the language. This increased familiarity, in turn, may amplify the influence of NVI and SES on Spanish 
reading comprehension.
As for NVI in English, a correlation was found in both groups. A comparison of learners divided into upper and lower quartiles based 
on their NVI scores revealed compelling findings. When comparing students with low NVI scores between groups, the superiority of the 
HI learners mirrored that of the whole group. However, among students with high NVI scores, the HI learners displayed an even more 
pronounced advantage. This suggests that the influence of NVI particularly benefits students with strong cognitive abilities in high- 
intensity tracks. On the other hand, another interesting observation related to NVI was that students with lower NVI scores in the 
HI group obtained similar reading scores in English to those of higher NVI scorers in the LI group, suggesting that high-intensity 
instruction might help students with lower cognitive abilities achieve scores comparable to those of students with higher cognitive 
abilities in low-intensity tracks. All in all, these findings emphasize the relevance of NVI for first (e.g., Bouchefra et al., 2022; Hage & 
Stroud, 1959; Ribeiro et al., 2016) and L2 (e.g., Genesee & Hamayan, 1980) learners and, in particular, for young learners 
(Lasagabaster, 2000). They also reinforce the strong link between NVI and language acquisition, as suggested by limited previous 
research (Ghonchepour & Moghaddam, 2018; Morvay, 2015; Phan, 2021).
6. Conclusion
Several main conclusions can be drawn from our data. Firstly, our data indicate the positive impact of high-intensity tracks on 
reading comprehension in English, particularly for students with strong NVI test results. Secondly, they confirm the similarity in 
reading comprehension in the main language (Spanish) between L2-immersion and non-immersion students, while comprehension in 
the immersion language (Basque) is still lower at this age. Thirdly, they highlight the significant role of NVI and, to a lesser extent, SES 
as mediators in reading comprehension. This underscores the need for greater control of these variables in future research and for 
increased awareness among teachers and, in the case of SES, among educational authorities as well.
Of course, this study is not without limitations. For instance, the learners belong to different schools, which may introduce 
intervening factors that we cannot control. Examining other potential moderating factors, such as teaching methodology and student 
motivation, could provide a more complete understanding of these complex relationships.
Additionally, reading comprehension was measured by a single test in each language, and more extensive measures could provide a 
more comprehensive understanding of our findings. For instance, we did not include measures of specific aspects, such as vocabulary 
or morphosyntactic abilities, which would probably have an impact on the reading comprehension abilities of the participants. 
Furthermore, the information on ER was self-reported by the learners. Given their young age, this information may not be very ac­
curate. It may therefore be useful to involve the children’s families to ensure that the answers are reliable. Moreover, our questionnaire 
included only two items related to reading habits, which limits the reliability and depth of our findings. Expanding the number and 
scope of survey items would provide a more comprehensive understanding of reading behaviors and their potential impact on reading 
proficiency. Future ER questionnaires could include questions about different types of reading, such as online versus offline reading or 
reading with or without visual support. Consequently, while our results offer valuable insights, they should be interpreted with caution 
until further studies with more robust methodologies can validate these findings.
These findings have several important implications for educational practice and policy. First, the consistent significance of NVI 
across all contexts suggests that cognitive abilities are a crucial factor in reading achievement regardless of instructional intensity or 
language. Educational programs should therefore incorporate strategies to support cognitive development across all instructional

students’ overall reading skills. For instance, teachers could design interdisciplinary projects that integrate reading materials in 
multiple languages, allowing students to draw parallels between them. Regular coordination meetings among language teachers could 
help align teaching strategies, particularly in fostering transferable reading skills such as inference, summarization, and compre­
hension monitoring. Additionally, schools could implement shared professional development workshops focusing on multilingual 
literacy strategies, providing a platform for teachers to exchange methodologies and resources. For example, a Spanish language 
teacher could share effective ways to teach summarization that an English or Basque teacher might adapt to their context. Lastly, 
incorporating comparative language exercises in the curriculum, such as exploring cognates or engaging in translation activities, could 
explicitly teach students how to transfer skills across languages, thereby enhancing their reading comprehension in a holistic manner.
