A B S T R A C T
This study investigated the causal impact of gains in teacher self-efficacy (TSE) on educational outcomes within a 
Social Cognitive Theory framework. In a 3-wave longitudinal randomized control trial with 91 secondary 
teachers in Korea, the experimental group received skill-based training (in autonomy-supportive teaching). As 
expected, the intervention significantly improved both autonomy-supportive teaching and TSE at mid-year. 
Structural equation modelling showed that the mid-year gains in TSE predicted subsequent gains in job satis­
faction and student achievement. Findings suggest that gains in TSE lead to gains in valued teacher and student 
outcomes, though interpretations are cautious given sample size and contextual limitations.
Within a Social Cognitive Theory framework (Bandura, 1986, 1997), 
self-efficacy is considered the most influential personal factor shaping 
human behavior and influencing outcomes. It is defined as “beliefs in 
one’s capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required 
to produce given attainments” (Bandura, 1997, p. 3). These beliefs are 
domain-specific, meaning they are tied to in-domain functioning and 
outcomes. In the domain of classroom teaching, teacher self-efficacy 
(TSE) refers to a teacher’s beliefs in their capabilities to plan, orga­
nize, and implement instructional activities that can successfully foster 
student learning, even in challenging circumstances (Tschannen-Moran 
& Hoy, 2001).
Teachers with higher domain-specific TSE tend to invest more time 
and effort into teaching tasks, and they demonstrate increased resilience 
when faced with challenges and stressors (Klassen & Chiu, 2010; Zee & 
Koomen, 2016), which helps explain why these teachers tend to attain 
more

(Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001). These favorable outcomes include 
both teacher benefits, such as increased job satisfaction (Buri´c & Kim, 
2021; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2007), and student benefits, such as higher 
levels of student achievement (Klassen & Tze, 2014; Zee et al., 2018). As 
such, TSE may play a critical role in supporting both teacher well-being 
and student success. The present study builds on this foundation by 
investigating the causal status of TSE in shaping these outcomes.
1. Teacher self-efficacy (TSE) and favorable educational 
outcomes
Cross-sectional and longitudinal studies both suggest a relationship 
between TSE and multiple teacher benefits. In cross-sectional research, 
TSE is associated with high levels of positive teacher outcomes, such as 
motivation to teach (e.g., Berg et al., 2023), professional commitment 
(Ware & Kitsantas, 2007; Zee & Koomen, 2016), a sense of personal 
accomplishment (Berg et al., 2023; Zee & Koomen, 2016), job satisfac­
tion (Richter et al., 2022; Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2007, 2017; Zee & 
Koomen, 2016), well-being (e.g., Lee et al., 2024), and long-term 
retention (Richter et al., 2022). These associations suggest that teach­
ers who feel more capable in their teaching tasks are more likely to 
report satisfaction with their work and a stronger attachment to the 
profession. TSE is also found associated with low levels of negative 
teacher outcomes, such as teacher burnout, including emotional 
exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment 
(Aloe et al., 2014; Dicke, Parker, et al., 2015a; Skaalvik & Skaalvik,

Similarly, longitudinal investigations have demonstrated that TSE 
positively predicts key aspects of teacher functioning, including teach­
ers’ engagement and satisfaction (Granziera & Perera, 2019), overall 
well-being (Lee et al., 2024; Schwerdtfeger et al., 2008), and, to a lesser 
extent, instructional quality (Holzberger et al., 2013). TSE has also been 
found to negatively predict emotional exhaustion (Brouwers & Tomic, 
2000; Dicke et al., 2018) and perceived stress (Klassen & Durksen, 
2014), suggesting a buffering role against teacher ill-being. Neverthe­
less, some longitudinal studies have reported inconsistent or null find­
ings regarding the impact of TSE on favorable teacher outcomes (e.g., 
Buri´c & Kim, 2021; Caprara et al., 2006), underscoring the need for 
further investigation, particularly using longitudinal and experimental 
designs that can clarify the direction and strength of these effects.
For student outcomes, the correlational and longitudinal research 
evidence is less robust, at least as compared to TSE’s well-documented 
relationship with favorable teacher outcomes (Klassen et al., 2011). 
TSE has been positively correlated with teacher perceptions of student 
engagement (Ross et al., 1996). Longitudinal studies show that TSE at 
Time 1 predicts increased student enjoyment in learning mathematics at 
Time 2 (Lazarides & Schiefele, 2024). TSE has also been shown to be 
indirectly related to student interest through student-reported teaching 
practices (Lazarides et al., 2023). As to student achievement, 
meta-analyses indicate a small but statistically significant association 
between TSE and student achievement (r = .10, Kim & Seo, 2018; r =
.08, Klassen & Tze, 2014). More recent work showed that aggregated 
student-specific TSE was positively correlated with aggregated student 
achievement in mathematics, though this relationship does not consis­
tently extend to other domains such as reading (Zee et al., 2018). These 
findings suggest that the influence of TSE on student learning outcomes 
may depend on the TSE domain-specificity and the nature of the 
outcome being measured. However, longitudinal investigations into the 
impact of TSE on student outcomes remain limited (Holzberger et al., 
2013), and further research is needed to establish causal mechanisms 
and domain-specific effects.
Despite the frequently observed associations between TSE and 
educational outcomes, much of the existing research remains correla­
tional in nature. The causal status of TSE—that is, whether changes in 
TSE lead to changes in educational outcomes—has been questioned 
(Lauermann & Butler, 2021) and, in some cases, challenged (Buri´c & 
Kim, 2021; Jerrim et al., 2023). A key limitation is that many longitu­
dinal studies fail to account for baseline TSE, which weakens the internal 
validity of causal claims. For instance, Caprara et al. (2006) reported a 
significant longitudinal effect of Time 2 TSE on Time 3 student 
achievement but did not control for Time 1 TSE, making it difficult to 
rule out pre-existing efficacy differences as an alternative explanation. 
Such omissions raise concerns that the observed relationship might be 
correlational rather than causal. Particularly, it remains unclear 
whether increases in TSE produce improvements in important down­
stream outcomes, such as teacher job satisfaction or student academic 
achievement.

rigorous research design of an RCT with longitudinal assessments of all

condition participate in a skill-based intervention so that we could 
manipulate TSE to a high level. Our question was whether mid-year 
gains in TSE, enabled by teacher participation in the skill-based inter­
vention, would lead to end-of-year improvements in teacher job satis­
faction and students’ academic achievement.
2. TSE and skill-based interventions
Skill-based training has become a widely adopted approach for 
enhancing TSE in both teacher education and professional development 
programs (Huang et al., 2025). Numerous skill-based training in­
terventions that focused not only on the skill to be learned but on any 
corresponding increase in TSE as well (T¨aschner et al., 2024). This is 
because, according to Bandura (1997), sustained improvements in 
human functioning depend not only on the acquisition of skills but also 
on individuals’ beliefs in their personal efficacy in executing those skills. 
In their recent meta-analysis, T¨aschner et al. (2024) reviewed numerous 
skill-based interventions with teachers that assessed TSE and found that 
these interventions had an overall significant effect on positive changes 
in TSE (Hedges’ g = .47), indicating a moderate impact of skill-based 
interventions on TSE.
The targeted skills of these interventions range from foundational 
instructional abilities—such as classroom management (Dicke, Elling 
et al., 2015b; Garwood & Harris, 2020), planning and instruction (Mok 
& Staub, 2021)—to subject-specific capabilities such as English 
grammar and vocabulary (Chan et al., 2021). Other interventions have 
focused on more specialized competencies relevant to pedagogical 
effectiveness, including strategies for promoting intrinsic motivation 
(Jang & Reeve, 2021), behavior modification techniques (Hopman et al., 
2018), autonomy-supportive teaching practices (Cheon et al., 2014, 
2018, 2020; Tilga et al., 2019), anti-bullying practices (e.g., Espelage 
et al., 2023), and language support strategies for diverse learners (Heppt 
et al., 2022). The findings from these interventions suggest that as 
teachers acquired and refined specific instructional skills in the in­
terventions, they simultaneously developed a stronger belief in their 
ability to use those skills effectively. This co-development of skill and 
efficacy aligns with Bandura’s (1997) proposition that the internaliza­
tion of personal efficacy during skill development enhances the likeli­
hood of sustained improvements in performance. Collectively, the 
evidence underscores the adaptability and effectiveness of skill-based 
training programs in producing meaningful and enduring gains in TSE.
3. Present study
The present study aimed to investigate the causal impact of TSE on 
teacher and student outcomes by addressing the limitations identified in 
previous research. To establish causality, we applied two methodolog­
ical design features. First, we adopted an RCT design to manipulate TSE 
to a higher level. The employment of an experimental research design 
allowed us to control for baseline scores, covariates, and potential 
confounders so that we could be assured that it was the experimental 
condition that enhanced TSE. This is an important design feature 
because the evidence for the link between TSE and student outcomes 
sometimes diminishes or vanishes once confounding and preexisting 
factors are taken into account. For example, a recent analysis of the 
Trends in Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2015 data found no 
confirmed positive relationship between TSE and mathematics 
achievement after accounting for confounding factors, including class 
size, teacher experience, and teacher specialization in the subject 
(Jerrim et al., 2023). Second, we examined for the effects of 
intervention-enabled gains in TSE on the study outcomes by assessing all 
dependent measures longitudinally. Many teacher interventions treat 
changes in TSE as a proxy indicator of intervention success (see Baysal & 
Mutlu, 2021; T¨aschner et al., 2024), yet they rarely examine whether 
these changes in TSE translate into subsequent improvements in teach­
ing and learning outcomes. However, the nature of this relationship 
requires a closer examination and a deeper understating of how gains in 
TSE leads to improved teaching and learning outcomes (Hettinger et al., 
2024). Until then, it is premature to conclude that a teacher intervention 
is effective in enhancing teaching and learning because it increases TSE.
Two key educational outcomes—teacher job satisfaction and student 
achievement—were examined in this study for two reasons. First, they

are considered critical indicators of teacher well-being and student

As to the specific skill-based training intervention employed in the 
present study, we selected the autonomy-supportive teaching (AST) 
intervention outlined by Reeve and Cheon (2021). This is a 
well-developed and widely-used skill-based training program for 
teachers that was especially well-suited for our purposes because it has 
been shown to be able to enhance both the instructional skillset asso­
ciated with autonomy support (i.e., autonomy-supportive teaching 
practices, such as “take the students’ perspective”) and teachers’ con­
fidence in their ability to engage students effectively. Teachers who 
participate in AST intervention do (1) develop greater AST skills, as 
rated by their students (Aelterman et al., 2014; Cheon et al., 2014; Reeve 
& Cheon, 2016) and as scored by classroom observers/raters (Aelterman 
et al., 2014; Cheon et al., 2014) and (2) report stronger TSE beliefs in 
their efficacy to teach effectively (Cheon et al., 2014, 2018) and in a way 
that fosters greater student engagement (Cheon et al., 2020). Its com­
ponents likely support key sources of self-efficacy, including vicarious 
and mastery experiences. We describe the AST intervention in detail in 
the Method section.
Finally, we assessed TSE in a way that was both domain-specific and 
directly aligned with the intervention, using the student engagement 
subscale of the short-form Teachers’ Sense of Efficacy Scale (TSES; 
Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001). The TSES is one of the most widely 
used and psychometrically strong measures of TSE, and it includes the 
three domain-specific scales of classroom management, student 
engagement, and instructional strategies. Instructional strategies effi­
cacy reflects teachers’ confidence in their capability to design, adapt, 
and deliver effective instructions. Classroom management efficacy per­
tains to maintaining an organized and disruption-free learning envi­
ronment. Student engagement efficacy captures confidence to motivate 
and actively involve students in learning. We used the TSES because its 
wide usage facilitates comparisons with the broader body of research on 
TSE and its demonstrated relationship to favorable educational out­
comes. Additionally, the TSES is not confounded with similar but 
different constructs—such as teaching effectiveness and outcome 
expectations—which is a common limitation with many alternative TSE 
measures (Huang et al., 2025).
It was our a priori decision to use the student engagement subscale of 
the TSES in the main analyses because (1) Social Cognitive Theory 
emphasizes that domain-specific self-efficacy beliefs are most predictive 
of behavior in that they guide the choices teachers make, the effort they 
invest, and their persistence when facing difficulties (Bandura, 1997) 
and (2) the AST intervention’s explicit purpose is to enhance students’ 
motivation and engagement (Reeve et al., 2004, 2022). The AST inter­
vention is based on Self-Determination Theory principles and recom­
mendations on how to promote student engagement by supporting 
students’ psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and related­
ness (Ryan & Deci, 2017). That said, because student achievement was 
the student outcome of interest, we recognized that the other two TSES 
subscales (instructional strategies, classroom management) might also 
have been affected by the intervention and contributed to outcomes. To 
address this, we conducted supplemental analyses that tested the model 
(a) including all three TSES subscales simultaneously within a single 
model, and (b) separately with each subscale as the focal predictor. 
Given potential multicollinearity among the highly correlated subscales 
in the combined model, we emphasize the separate subscale analyses as 
more reliable tests. Full results are provided in the Supplemental 
Materials.

5. Method
5.1. Participants
We used intentional sampling and recruited our sample of teachers 
from the larger population of all physical education (PE) teachers 
throughout Korea who were participating in an annual national training 
program required by the Korean Ministry of Education. Of the several 
hundred PE teachers who attended the two-day training program and 
were invited to participate in the current study, 102 agreed to partici­
pate. These participants were all full-time ethnic Korean PE teachers 
with certification and experience. Each teacher came from a different 
school (102 teachers from 102 different schools) across twelve school 
districts throughout South Korea. That each participating teacher was 
from a different school was an important recruitment strategy because it 
not only allowed us to conduct a randomized control trial without the 
risk of cross-condition contamination, but ensured broad geographic 
diversity and minimized the likelihood of school-level clustering. During 
the transition to the new school year, 11 teachers were not able to 
maintain their participation in the study (the reasons for which are

5.2. Procedures
Ethics approval for this research was obtained from the University 
Research Ethics Committee of the authors’ affiliated university. The 
questionnaire began with a consent form, and we informed participants 
that their responses would be confidential and used only for research 
purposes. Fig. 2 shows the CONSORT flowchart for our year-long RCT 
intervention. As shown in Figs. 2 and 102 teachers were recruited and 
randomly assigned into either the experimental group (n = 51) or the 
control group (n = 51). The first wave of data collection (T1) occurred 
before Part 1 of the intervention, i.e., in August two weeks prior to the 
start of the Fall semester (Semester 2 of the school year in the Korean 
educational system). The second wave of data collection (T2) took place 
at the end of the Fall semester, and the third wave of data collection (T3) 
occurred at the end of the Spring semester (Semester 1 of the next school 
year). During these two semesters, teachers in the control group 
continued their usual classroom practices (i.e., “practice as usual”), 
while teachers in the experimental group received a three-part AST 
intervention described in the next section.
5.3. Autonomy-supportive teaching (AST) intervention
AST interventions are rooted in the principles of Self-Determination 
Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2017), which posits that the fulfillment of three 
basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—is 
essential for optimal functioning and well-being. Among these, auton­
omy refers to the experience of volition and choice in one’s actions. 
When students’ need for autonomy is satisfied, they are more likely to 
experience psychological need satisfaction, enhanced engagement, and 
improved academic outcomes. In AST interventions, teachers are trained 
to use autonomy-supportive instructional strategies, such as take stu­
dents’ perspective, invite students to pursue their interest, present 
learning activities in need satisfying ways, provide explanatory ratio­
nales, acknowledge students’ negative feeling, and rely on invitational 
language (Reeve & Cheon, 2021).
This study adopted an RCT design, with teachers randomly assigned 
to either an intervention or control group. The intervention group 
received training at the beginning of Fall semester; the control group did

were frequently divided into smaller groups of four to eight participants 
who shared similar teaching contexts (e.g., grade level taught). This

social validity were supported by prior research using the same protocol. 
These previous studies included rigorous manipulation checks, such as 
classroom observations conducted by trained raters, who assessed the 
extent to which teachers engaged in AST practices (Cheon et al., 2022; 
Zhang et al., 2020), and student-reported perceptions of teachers’ AST 
behaviors (Assor et al., 2018; Tilga et al., 2019). Furthermore, these 
studies collected social validity data indicating that participating 
teachers found the intervention useful, were satisfied with the recom­
mended teaching practices, believed it led to positive changes in their 
teaching, and would recommend it to colleagues (Cheon et al., 2024). 
Collectively, these findings consistently demonstrated that teachers in 
the experimental groups adopted autonomy-supportive practices to a 
significantly greater degree than those in control groups (Cheon et al., 
2022).
Additional procedural details and validation data for the AST inter­
vention can be found in Reeve et al. (2022).

5.5. Measures
All measures were in the Korean language. The Korean-language 
version of each measure had been previously translated and back- 
translated into Korean in previous publications (See Klassen et al., 
2009; Reeve & Cheon, 2016).
Autonomy Supportive Teaching (AST). To measure their autonomy 
supportive teaching, teachers completed the short form of Learning 
Climate Questionnaire (teacher version; Reeve & Cheon, 2016; Black & 
Deci, 2000). The questionnaire includes the following six items that 
assess the autonomy support of an individual teacher (Black & Deci, 
2000): “I provide my students with choices and options”, “I listen to how 
my students would like to do things”, “I convey confidence in my stu­
dents’ ability to do well in this course”, “I encourage my students to ask 
questions”, “I understand my students”, and “I try to understand how my 
students see things before suggesting a new way to do things”. It has 
often been used to measure teachers’ autonomy-supportive teaching (e. 
g., Cheon et al., 2018; Furtak & Kunter, 2012; Reeve & Cheon, 2016). 
The response scale for this measure was a 7-point Likert scale (1 =
Strongly Disagree; … 7 = Strongly Agree). In this study, the measure 
demonstrated reasonably good internal consistency (T1: α = .79; T2: α 
= .81).
Teacher Self-Efficacy (TSE). To measure TSE, we used the subscale of 
“Efficacy for Student Engagement” from the 12-item short-form 
Teachers’ Sense of Efficacy Scale (TSES; Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 
2001). The TSES’s Efficacy for Student Engagement is a 4-item subscale 
that includes the following items: “How much can you do to get students 
to believe they can do well in schoolwork?”, “How much can you do to 
help your students value learning?”, “How much can you do to motivate 
students who show low interest in schoolwork?”, and “How much can 
you assist student families in helping their kids do well in school?”. For 
this measure, we used the 9-point response scale recommended by its 
authors (Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001). The internal reliability of the 
4-item measure of Efficacy for Student Engagement in this study was 
good (T1: α = .82 and T2: α = .89).
Job Satisfaction. Teacher job satisfaction was measured using the 
following 4 items from Caprara et al. (2003): “I am fully satisfied with 
my job”, “I am satisfied with what I achieve at work”, “I am happy with 
the way my colleagues and superiors treat me”, and “I feel good at 
work”. The response scale for this measure was a 7-point Likert scale (1 
= Strongly Disagree; … 7 = Strongly Agree). The internal consistency for 
the four items was good (T1: α = .89; T2: α = .89; T3: α = .89).
Student Achievement. Student achievement data were provided by 
teachers using their students’ PE scores (course grade, scored on a 0–100 
scale) at three time points: before the start of Fall semester, at the end of 
Fall semester, and at the end of Spring semester. For T1 student 
achievement scores, we used the students’ PE scores from that teacher’s 
class from the previous semester (semester 1). In Korea, student per­
formance in PE is evaluated based on a fixed grading structure, with 80 
% of the final grade derived from in-class performance-based skill 
challenge assessments and the remaining 20 % from subjective criteria. 
All PE teachers implement a standardized national curriculum and uti­
lize a uniform assessment system across schools. The PE course structure 
is organized into sport-specific modules, each typically lasting three 
weeks or so. The third week within each module always features these 
skill assessments that contribute to the student’s grade. Some sports also 
incorporate an evaluation of technical execution or “good technique” as 
part of the scoring system. As such, the majority of a student’s PE grade 
is based on observable, standardized demonstrations of physical- 
education skill, conducted under consistent conditions across schools. 
In our study, the individual student scores were averaged to create a 
class mean for each teacher. Thus, the student achievement measure 
reflected the average achievement of the students in that teacher’s class 
across the three time waves.
5.6. Data analyses
All data were at the teacher level, including teacher-reported AST, 
TSE, job satisfaction, and class-average student achievement. Thus, the 
unit of analysis was the teacher.
Before testing the hypothesized model, we first evaluated the 
construct validity of each of the seven questionnaire-based variables 
included in the model (i.e., T1 and T2 AST; T1 and T2 TSE; and T1, T2, 
and T3 JS), using a scale-specific confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). 
Each CFA was conducted in Mplus 8.3 (Muth´en & Muth´en, 2019), with 
the maximum likelihood robust estimator (MLR), the full information 
likelihood estimation (FIML) procedure to treat missing data (1.1 % of 
the data), and standard goodness-of-fit indices (i.e., RMSEA ≤.06, SRMR 
≤.08, CFI ≥.90, and TLI ≥.95; Hu & Bentler, 1999). Each of the CFAs fit 
the data reasonably well, and the descriptives and CFA results appear in 
Supplemental Materials (Table S1–S2).
We next tested the hypothesized model by adding the experimental 
condition as a predictor (coded 1 = experimental, 0 = control), and the 
hypothesized paths and autoregressive effects shown in Fig. 1. Teacher 
gender (1 = male, 2 = female), grade level taught (1 = primary, 2 =
middle, 3 = high), and whether the teacher had students with special 
needs in the classroom (1 = yes, 0 = no) were included as statistical 
controls and standardized for all variables at T2 and T3. All variables 
were standardized before testing the hypothesized model shown in 
Fig. 1. To evaluate the model, we employed structural equation

To assess mediation, we tested whether T2 AST and T2 TSE mediated

6.1. Hypothesized model test
Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics and correlation matrix for 
the variables in the hypothesized model. Notably, the experimental 
condition significantly correlated (p < .05) with all T2 variables (AST: r 
= .23; TSE: r = .48; job satisfaction: r = .36; student achievement: r =

r = −.28; T2: r = −.20) and job satisfaction (T1: r = −.20; T3: r = −.25), 
while teaching students with special needs significantly correlated with 
T1 job satisfaction (r = −.29).
The hypothesized model fit the data reasonably well: χ2 (14) =
21.20, p = .10, RMSEA = .076, SRMR = .039, CFI = .982, and TLI =

6.2. Mediation analyses
In the T3 job satisfaction mediation analysis, the total indirect effect 
from the experimental condition to T3 job satisfaction was significant: β 
= .32, SE = .06, t = 5.34, p = .000, 95 % CI [.22, .43]. Importantly, T2 
TSE significantly mediated the otherwise direct effect from the experi­
mental condition to T3 job satisfaction (β = .09, p = .008, 95 % CI [.04, 
.15]), while T2 AST did not (β = −.01, p = .608, 95 % CI [-.03, .02]). The 
inclusion of the T2 TSE mediator reduced the direct effect of experi­
mental condition to non-significant (β = .08, SE = .05, t = 1.47, p = .142, 
95 % CI [-.01, .17]), confirming TSE’s full mediation.
In the T3 student achievement mediation analysis, the total indirect 
effect from the experimental condition to T3 student achievement was 
significant: β = .17, SE = .05, t = 3.23, p = .001, 95 % CI [.08, .25]. 
Importantly, T2 TSE significantly mediated the direct effect from the 
experimental condition to T3 student achievement (β = .11, p = .011, 95 
% CI [.04, .18]), while T2 AST did not (β = −.02, p = .353, 95 % CI 
[−.06, .02]). The inclusion of the T2 TSE mediator reduced the direct 
effect of experimental condition to non-significant (β = −.06, SE = .09, t 
= −.66, p = .508, 95 % CI [-.20, .08]), confirming TSE’s full mediation.

7. Discussion
The purpose of the present study was to investigate the causal impact 
of intervention-enabled gains in TSE on subsequent gains in favorable 
educational outcomes. By using a longitudinal RCT design of a skill- 
based intervention and controlling for confounders and covariates, 
this study provides stronger evidence of TSE’s causal influence on 
educational outcomes. We tested whether post-intervention gains in TSE 
would lead to follow-up gains in job satisfaction and student achieve­
ment. Although not all hypotheses were supported by the data, the re­
sults yielded important indications that TSE may function as a causal 
mechanism, particularly through its longitudinal associations with 
teacher and student outcomes. As expected, the intervention increased 
T2 TSE, which subsequently increased T3 outcomes and mediated the 
intervention effects on these outcomes. These findings suggest that the 
AST intervention improved T3 job satisfaction and student achievement 
by first enhancing teachers’ T2 efficacy for student engagement. This 
supports the proposition that increasing TSE through a skill-based 
intervention may translate into improved educational outcomes. How­
ever, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution given several 
important limitations, including the modest sample size, context- 
specific implementation, and the absence of direct measures of student 
engagement.
7.1. Skill-based intervention increased both skill and TSE
The manipulation of TSE in this study was shown effective. The AST 
intervention contributed to gains in both T2 AST and T2 TSE, even after 
accounting for baseline T1 AST and T1 TSE as potential confounds. This 
suggests that participation in a skill-based training may simultaneously 
improve teachers’ skill and their confidence in using the skill. These 
findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating the benefits of 
AST interventions (e.g., Aelterman et al., 2016; Cheon et al., 2018; 
Reeve & Cheon, 2021). The observed effects can likely be attributed to 
the skill-based nature of the training, which enabled teachers to acquire 
concrete instructional strategies for supporting students’ psychological 
needs, thereby facilitating greater student engagement (Reeve & Cheon, 
2021). Moreover, these results suggest that AST intervention aligns with 
Bandura’s (1997) call to integrate personal efficacy into skill develop­
ment for effective functioning.
The AST intervention strengthened teachers’ efficacy across all three 
domains of the TSES, likely because it engaged multiple sources of self- 
efficacy identified by Bandura (1997). For instance, in Part 1, teachers 
observed video models and live demonstrations of autonomy-supportive 
strategies by the trainer, engaging vicarious experience. In Part 2, they 
practiced the strategies in small groups while receiving encouragement 
from peers and the trainer, drawing on verbal persuasion. Between Parts 
2 and 3, teachers applied the strategies in their own classrooms, gaining 
real-classroom mastery experience. Finally, Part 3 facilitated collabo­
rative reflection and experience sharing, which may have further sup­
ported vicarious experience and enhanced emotional readiness by 
providing a supportive space to process classroom experiences. While 
the intervention’s explicit goal was to foster student engagement, these 
components may have also enhanced teachers’ broader sense of 
instructional and classroom management efficacy.
Specifically for our focused TSE domain of student engagement, the 
intervention was effective because AST strategies gave teachers practical 
tools for fostering engagement. For example, presenting learning ac­
tivities in ways that support students’ sense of competence helped 
teachers build students’ belief that they can do well in schoolwork. 
Providing explanatory rationales enabled teachers to help students see 
the value of learning. Additionally, strategies such as taking students’ 
perspectives and inviting students to pursue their personal interests 
equipped teachers to engage even less motivated learners. These same 
approaches also supported teachers in their work with families, by 
fostering empathy for parents’ perspectives.
By activating multiple pathways for efficacy development, the 
intervention was likely to have created a robust foundation for belief 
change and may explain the substantial increases in TSE observed in this 
study. However, it is worth noting that T¨aschner et al.’s (2024)
meta-analysis found no significant difference in effectiveness between 
interventions targeting only one source of self-efficacy and those tar­
geting multiple sources, suggesting that the quality and contextual 
alignment of the source(s) may be as important as their number.
7.2. Causal impact of TSE on educational outcomes
At the center of our investigation was the question of causality 
(Bollen & Pearl, 2013). This question was important for three reasons. 
First, correlation does not equal causation. While TSE is strongly 
correlated with various educational outcomes, correlation alone fails to 
disentangle whether TSE causes these outcomes, these outcomes cause 
TSE, or both are caused by some third variable (Jerrim et al., 2023). 
Second, manipulation is key to causation (Bollen & Pearl, 2013). 
Without manipulation, it is difficult to rule out alternative explan­
ations—such as reverse causation or confounding variables—for the 
observed relationship between TSE and outcome variables. To establish 
that TSE causes educational outcomes, stronger evidence was needed to 
show that changing TSE directly leads to changes in those outcomes. 
Third, inferring causality from correlation risks misguiding TSE research 
and practice. Without robust causal evidence, treating TSE as a direct 
cause of educational outcomes may overestimate its true impact or 
overlook other contributing factors.
The evidence for a causal effect of gains in TSE is supported by two 
key findings. First, the use of an RCT allowed for experimental manip­
ulation of TSE though a skill-based intervention. Participation in the 
intervention significantly increased teachers’ T2 TSE, providing evi­
dence that TSE can be effectively influenced. Second, the SEM analysis 
showed that this intervention-increased T2 TSE (for student engage­
ment) was significantly associated with subsequent gains in both T3 
educational outcomes: job satisfaction and student achievement. To 
address potential confounding, the analysis controlled for baseline levels 
of all variables, T2 educational outcomes, the other two TSE domains as 
in the supplemental test, covariates and intervention-targeted teaching 
skill—a factor that could independently influence both TSE and educa­
tional outcomes. These methodological controls strengthen the infer­
ence that it was the T1 to T2 gains in TSE for student engagement, rather 
than pre-existing differences in teaching skill or other confounders, that 
contributed to the subsequent T2 to T3 gains in educational outcomes.

The mediation analyses further supported this conclusion by 
showing that T2 TSE for student engagement significantly (and fully) 
mediated the relationship between the intervention and both T3 out­
comes. In other words, the increases in TSE for student engagement 
following the AST intervention were instrumental in producing the later 
gains in job satisfaction and student achievement, consistent with a 
causal interpretation (Pearl & Mackenzie, 2019). While the effect sizes 
may appear modest, the results support a causal interpretation, because 
these effects were estimated from a longitudinal SEM model that 
included statistical controls for baseline levels of all variables, concur­
rent outcomes at Time 2, and demographic covariates. In educational 
settings, where job satisfaction and student achievement are influenced 
by multiple interacting factors over time, these findings are particularly 
meaningful can have substantial practical implications when scaled 
across classrooms, schools, or systems (Hattie, 2008; Marsh et al., 2022).

The causal impact of TSE for student engagement on job satisfaction 
and student achievement may be explained through two interrelated 
pathways. First, teachers who gained greater confidence in effectively 
engaging students after the intervention may be more likely to adopt the 
learned AST practices in their classroom. These practices most likely 
promoted their students’ sense of volition to engage in learning, which 
can produce higher academic achievement (Reeve, 2013; Skinner et al., 
1998). Although student engagement was not directly measured in the 
present study, we conceptualized it as a proximal target of the AST 
intervention—and a mediating mechanism linking TSE to student 
achievement. This aligns with research indicating that engaged class­
rooms support deeper learning and improved academic outcomes 
(Fredricks et al., 2019; Ladd & Dinella, 2009). Second, teachers with 
higher self-efficacy are more resilient in the face of challenges. Rather 
than doubting their own capabilities and feeling incapable, efficacious 
teachers are more likely to invest time and effort to solve problems they 
face. This may help reduce their emotional exhaustion and increase job 
satisfaction (Dicke, Elling et al., 2015b; Dicke et al., 2018; Polat & 
˙Iskender, 2018). Their greater sense of efficacy enables them to interpret 
classroom challenges, such as student disengagement, as manageable 
and within their capability to address, which in turn contributes to 
sustained well-being and professional fulfillment.
Taken together, these findings suggest that TSE – particularly in the 
domain of student engagement – may function as a key psychological 
mechanism through which instructional interventions translate into 
both teacher and student benefits. Our focus on this domain was 
intentional, reflecting Social Cognitive Theory’s emphasis on domain- 
specific prediction and the AST intervention’s central aim of 
enhancing student engagement. Results from supplemental analyses 
showed that although the intervention increased all three TSE domains, 
only student engagement efficacy predicted later job satisfaction and 
student achievement. Importantly, the null effects for instructional 
strategies and classroom management are themselves informative: they 
suggest that while the intervention fostered a broad sense of teachers’ 
efficacy beliefs, only student engagement efficacy served as the unique 
mechanism linking the AST intervention to educational outcomes. This 
pattern both reinforces our a priori decision to highlight engagement 
efficacy in the main analyses and provides transparency by showing that 
alternative domains did not account for the observed improvements. 
Thus, the supplemental results strengthen confidence in the robustness 
of our main findings while allowing readers to evaluate whether the 
intervention produced more general shifts in teachers’ efficacy beliefs 
(Supplemental Materials: Table S3–S8).
While future studies should include direct measures of student 
engagement to test this proposed predictive pathway, the current evi­
dence 
supports 
the theoretical alignment 
between 
the 
AST 
intervention’s goals, the selected TSE measure, and the observed 
educational outcomes. However, it is important to acknowledge that 
TSE is a multidimensional construct, and recent research has shown that 
different TSE domains may vary in their effects on specific outcomes. For 
example, Chen et al. (2020) found that different dimensions of TSE in­
fluence different aspects of teaching practice: TSE for instructional 
strategies had a stronger impact on instructional clarity, while TSE for 
classroom management had a greater effect on classroom management 
practices. Similarly, Kim and Seo (2018) reported that instructional and 
classroom management efficacy were more closely linked to cognitive 
and behavioral outcomes than engagement efficacy. Therefore, future 
research should examine the distinct and interactive contributions of 
multiple TSE domains to better understand the full scope of their in­
fluence on educational outcomes.
In contrast, gains in T2 AST following the skill-based intervention did 
not directly lead to gains in job satisfaction and student achievement at 
T3, although we had expected them to. This could be due to the exclu­
sive reliance on teacher self-reports to assess AST, which may have 
introduced bias or limitations in accurately capturing changes in 
teaching skill. In comparison, previous AST intervention studies often 
used non-teacher-reported measures—such as raters’ reports of AST 
(Aelterman et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2020) or students’ perceptions of 
AST (Assor et al., 2018; Tilga et al., 2019)—which may offer more 
objective or valid indicators of teaching practice.
7.3. Limitations
Four important limitations needed to be acknowledged. First, the 
sample size (N = 91) was relatively small for SEM, with a ratio of sample 
size to estimated parameters below the commonly recommended 10:1 
threshold (Bentler & Chou, 1987). Although the manifest model allowed 
for reduced model complexity, the modest sample size may have limited 
statistical power, particularly for detecting small effects. As such, the 
results should be interpreted with caution and are in need of replication.
Second, the study was conducted exclusively with PE teachers in 
South Korea who had participated in a mandatory national level 
training, which may limit the generalizability of the findings in several 
important ways. For instance, PE teaching often involves unique inter­
personal dynamics (e.g., teamwork) and physical environments (e.g., 
open field) that may translate only loosely to other subject areas (Bevans 
et al., 2010). Moreover, the sociocultural context of South Kore­
a—particularly its educational norms, hierarchical structures, and pro­
fessional development systems—may shape teacher experiences and 
responses to interventions differently than in other national or regional 
settings. It remains uncertain whether similar effects would be observed 
with teachers from other subject areas, nations, or grade levels.
Third, the study relied primarily on teacher-reported measures. 
While appropriate given the study’s focus on teacher-centered con­
structs, such as AST, TSE, and job satisfaction, this approach may 
introduce biases and not fully capture the impact of the intervention on 
student outcomes (Klassen & Tze, 2014). Notably, student engagement 
was not assessed as an outcome, despite its conceptual relevance: TSE in 
this study was framed around efficacy for enhancing student engage­
ment, and the AST intervention was specifically designed to promote 
student motivation and involvement. The absence of student-level data 
constrains the ability to evaluate the intervention’s full impact on 
classroom 
dynamics 
and 
learning 
outcomes. 
Incorporating 
multi-informant measures (e.g., student ratings, classroom observa­
tions) may yield more nuanced results, as such approaches capture as­
pects of teaching efficacy that teacher self-reports along may not fully 
reflect.
Fourth, while we assessed and controlled for whether teachers had 
students with special needs in their classroom, we did not account for 
the presence of students’ emotional and behavioral problems. Prior 
research has shown that such challenges may influence teachers’ 
student-specific self-efficacy beliefs for instructional strategies, behavior

management, student engagement, and emotional support (e.g., Zee, 
Jong et al., 2016a; Zee, Jong et al., 2017).
7.4. Implications and future directions
The above-mentioned limitations of the present study offer impor­
tant directions to improve future research. First, to establish the 
robustness and broader applicability and generalizability of the SEM 
findings, future research should utilize samples and replicate the study 
across varied disciplines, cultural contexts, and educational systems. 
Second, future research should include student engagement as an 
outcome and would benefit from incorporating a broader range of stu­
dent outcomes, assessed through multiple sources such as student self- 
reports, peer evaluations, and independent classroom observations, to 
yield a more comprehensive and externally validated assessment of 
intervention effectiveness. Third, future studies should consider incor­
porating students’ social-emotional behaviors in class in the analysis. 
Doing so would improve the precision of statistical controls and provide 
a more comprehensive understanding of the contextual factors influ­
encing TSE and educational outcomes.
The observed causal status of TSE in this study also provides 
important implications for future research. First, TSE may serve as a 
critical psychological resource for addressing global educational chal­
lenges, including teacher shortages and variability in teaching effec­
tiveness. By contributing to job satisfaction and student achievement, 
TSE may improve teacher retention—particularly among beginning 
teachers, who are at risk of high attrition rates (Dicke, Parker, et al., 
2015a; Ingersoll, 2012). Skill-based teacher training programs present a 
potentially cost-effective strategy to enhance TSE. Equipping teachers 
with essential skills and enhancing their efficacy beliefs in future teacher 
training programs could help mitigate attrition, especially in 
under-resourced schools, nations, and regions that face acute challenges 
in delivering high-quality education.
Second, in the context of intervention design, future teacher devel­
opment programs may benefit more from placing greater emphasis on 
enhancing teachers’ efficacy beliefs about targeted teaching skills. Evi­
dence from the skill-based AST intervention highlights how gains in 
domain-specific TSE led to long-term improvements in favorable teacher 
and student outcomes. Similarly, Reeve and Cheon (2016) demonstrated 
that 
shifts 
in 
teachers’ 
“easy-to-do” 
beliefs 
fully 
explained 
post-intervention increases in their autonomy support for students. 
These findings underscore the pivotal role of fostering positive belief 
changes in achieving desired outcomes within teacher professional 
development initiatives.
Third, future research could focus on identifying and integrating 
effective strategies to explicitly enhance TSE. Despite its significance, 
TSE is often treated as a secondary benefit rather than a primary 
objective in professional development programs. A few notable exam­
ples of programs primarily targeting TSE, such as Ross and Bruce’s 
(2007) training for math teachers and Tschannen-Moran and McMas­
ter’s (2009) use of varied professional development formats, illustrate 
how targeted efforts can boost TSE by addressing its core sources. 
Additionally, mastery modelling approaches from non-educational 
contexts (e.g., Ozer & Bandura, 1990), further demonstrate the poten­
tial of these strategies.
Further, much of the existing research, including the present study, 
has focused on domain-specific TSE. However, an important avenue for 
future research lies in examining whether the current findings extend to 
student-specific or class-specific TSE, which captures teachers’ efficacy 
beliefs regarding their ability to support the learning of particular stu­
dents or class. Early evidence suggests this more detailed approach has 
value. For example, within-teacher variation in TSE across different 
students or contexts (intra-individual variation) is often greater than 
between-teacher differences (inter-individual variation) (Zee, Jong 
et al., 2016a; Zee, Koomen et al., 2016b).
8. Conclusion
This study examined the causal impact of TSE on educational out­
comes by investigating whether gains in TSE directly contributed to 
gains in favorable educational outcomes. Through teacher participation 
in a skill-based intervention, teachers gained greater teaching skill and 
TSE. These intervention-enabled gains in TSE for student engagement 
then significantly contributed to improved teacher job satisfaction and 
student achievement, even after controlling for concurrent gains in 
teaching skill. These findings add support to TSE’s causal status and 
highlight that enhancing TSE can improve educational outcomes.
