In today's digital society, it is important for young people to develop a metacognitive understanding of how to
react to and respond to messages from strangers. This study explores the relationship between reading
achievement, online reading activities, metacognitive skills, and socio-economic backgrounds. Analysis of PISA
2018 Nordic samples indicates a positive statistical association between metacognitive credibility-evaluation
skills and reading achievement. Reading achievement is also associated with student background variables.
Across the Nordic countries, the tested models explain between 17 % and 29 % of the variation in students’
metacognitive credibility evaluation skills within schools. However, not all students benefit equally as schoolbased digital skills instruction does not consistently support online metacognition. Addressing these disparities
is key to supporting inclusive digital learning and enhancing students’ ability to assess information credibility.

1. Introduction: the focus of the study
As society and education become increasingly digital, students need
to acquire vital content knowledge and digital literacy skills to navigate
this environment effectively. Online activities present a dual challenge
for students, who often make rapid decisions while grappling with
credibility assessments (Guo et al., 2017). Reading online demands the
development and application of specific metacognitive skills (Cromley,
2023; Dabarera et al., 2014; Organization for Economic Cooperation
and Development, 2021), such as evaluating online content critically,
recognizing scams disguised as offers or rewards, and protecting against
digital threats (Hu et al., 2018; Livingstone et al., 2008; Nygren & Guath,
2019). Furthermore, students must continuously adapt their pre-learned
metacognitive skills to successfully respond to the distinct demands of
various situations and process online content efficiently (Helsper, 2021;
Rodríguez-de-Dios et al., 2018).
Metacognition is a fundamental component essential for both
reading success and broader academic achievement (Baker & Brown,
1996; Koyuncu et al., 2022; Mokhtari & Reichard, 2002). This complex
process involves monitoring and self-regulating one's understanding and
integrating and synthesizing information successfully (Coiro, 2021;

Salmerón et al., 2018). Studies indicate that regular academic internet
use, such as information-seeking activities, fosters valuable metacognitive skills, thereby enhancing students’ reading literacy levels
(Gil-Flores et al., 2012; Lee & Wu, 2013; Zhou & Lam, 2019).
Given its critical role, teaching metacognitive skills significantly
enhances reading abilities (Cromley, 2023; Dabarera et al., 2014; Lai,
2011; Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2019a;
Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2021).
Instructional strategies designed to cultivate these skills are especially
important in digital environments, where students must consistently
confront challenges like filtering out irrelevant information and verifying the authenticity of sources. Moreover, skilled readers are at an
advantage as they adeptly employ online metacognitive strategies,
harnessing their self-regulation and critical thinking capabilities to
effectively evaluate the reliability of information (Muijselaar et al.,
2017; Sutiyatno, 2019). Thus, the necessity of metacognitive instruction
becomes evident as it effectively empowers students to navigate the
complexities of reading in both traditional and digital landscapes.

quality indicators like author identity, publisher, documentation, and
references (Breakstone et al., 2018; Breakstone et al., 2021; Chen et al.,

2.2. Metacognition for Assessing Source Credibility (MASC)
Metacognition, the ability to monitor and control cognitive processes, is crucial in developing literacy (Kuhn & Dean, 2004; Lai, 2011;
Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2019; Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2021). Due to
its absolute importance, the role of metacognitive skills in improving
reading achievement has been acknowledged in prior research, both
from theoretical and empirical perspectives (Cromley, 2023; Koyuncu
et al., 2022; Lee & Wu, 2013; Soto et al., 2020). Metacognition in digital
reading involves higher-order thinking skills like evaluating sources and
information and making meaning from multiple texts (Organization for
Economic Cooperation and Development, 2021). Digital literacy is
crucial, as online reading includes communication (e.g., email, chat) and
risks like phishing and scams. Thus, online safety and credibility
assessment skills are vital for digital reading literacy (Cho, 2014;
Dabarera et al., 2014). In this context, PISA defined metacognition as
“an individual’s ability to think about and control his or her reading and
comprehension” (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2021, p. 28). The PISA 2018 questionnaire included three
scenarios assessing students’ knowledge of strategies for “understanding
and remembering,” “summarizing,” and “assessing credibility.” All three
strongly predict a 15-year-old’s digital reading achievement. (Lim &
Jung, 2019; Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development,
2019b). Researchers have also explored the moderating and mediating
effects of meta-cognitive strategies and found the potential moderating
role of meta-cognitive strategies on digital reading (Lee & Wu, 2013)
and the mediation role of metacognition in the relationship between the
use of social media and digital reading (Chen et al., 2021a; Chen et al.,
2021b).
Instruction plays a crucial role in developing metacognitive skills,
improving reading proficiency, and enhancing online search abilities
(Cromley, 2023). Within the context of online reading, students engage
in essential activities such as using search engines, evaluating sources,
and synthesizing information. Lazonder and Rouet (2008) describe
reciprocal links between online search behaviors and metacognitive
abilities; accordingly, effective online searching and metacognitive skills
may be mutually associated. In our analyses, both online search activities and metacognitive skills show positive associations with reading
proficiency, but the cross-sectional design does not allow us to confirm
causal direction or reciprocal causation.
Proficient readers are likely to perform better in search tasks and
employ metacognitive strategies to critically question and synthesize
information (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development,
2021, p. 141). Moreover, as proficient readers become more aware of
their cognitive processes, they can adapt their reading strategies such as
skimming or rereading, according to the demands of the text, further
enhancing their comprehension and overall reading effectiveness
(Muijselaar et al., 2017).

2. Literature review
2.1. Online reading activity for information search (ORA)
Students frequently use online resources for learning and assignments, reflecting the growing importance of digital literacy in education. With easy Internet access, they encounter vast amounts of
unfiltered digital information (Drotner, 2022; European Commission,
2019; Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2021).
This exposure to diverse online content is significant, as frequent digital
reading is linked to improved digital reading achievement (Kong et al.,
2022) and the development of enhanced metacognitive skills, such as
assessing information credibility (Pérez et al., 2018). Furthermore,
studies indicate that online information search activities are strong
predictors of reading achievement (Lee & Wu, 2013; Wu, 2014; Zhou &
Lam, 2019). Importantly, online search behaviors are associated with
socioeconomic status and with reading performance, and they account
for part of the observed association between socioeconomic status and
reading outcomes (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2010; Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2021).
Although students are skilled at using digital technologies for information searches, they need to continuously improve their critical
evaluation skills in all areas, such as schoolwork, communication, or
social media content. Improving reading proficiency in the digital realm
relies more on the depth of online interactions than merely the frequency or duration of engagement (Petko et al., 2017). Research indicates a differentiation between online search activities conducted for
academic purposes and those for social engagement, each having
distinct relationships with reading achievement. Engaging in discussions
during online activities is associated with the development of metacognitive skills, particularly in evaluating information quality and
identifying biases or errors (Lazonder & Rouet, 2008), whereas frequent
participation in social online activities tends to have a less positive association with reading achievement (Lee & Wu, 2013; Zhou & Lam,
2019).

2.3. Reading in a digital world
Reading literacy has evolved rapidly, now encompassing traditional
print skills and advanced digital processing (Leu et al., 2018). Digital
platforms require adapting conventional reading skills to non-linear
paths and multimedia integration (Gil-Flores et al., 2012; Lim & Jung,
2019). According to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and
Development (2019b, p. 14), reading literacy in the PISA 2018 framework involves understanding, using, evaluating, reflecting on, and
engaging with texts to achieve goals, gain knowledge, and participate in
society. This includes accessing, retrieving, integrating, interpreting,
reflecting, and evaluating information (Organization for Economic
Cooperation and Development, 2019b). Effective reading strategies and
engagement are crucial for academic achievement. Students need skills
to assess information quality and credibility, corroborate facts, and
resolve discrepancies (Organization for Economic Cooperation and

3. Research aims
The study aims to validate the proposed MSEM model (Fig. 1) and
investigate the associations that is 1) between online reading activities
(ORA) and metacognitive skills (MASC) through an intermediary and 2)
between economic, social, and cultural status (ESCS) and reading
achievement (RA) through a third variable. The two research questions
(RQ) are as follows:
RQ1. How do online reading activities relate to students’ metacognitive skills, and whether that relationship can be partly explained by
students’ reading achievement?
Based on the premise that ORA provides students with valuable
experience and training in online reading and that higher RA contributes
to the enhancement of MASC our hypotheses (H) are:
H1a. Increased engagement in ORA is positively associated with
higher RA.
H1b. Students with higher RA will demonstrate better MASC than
those with lower RA.
The rationale for H1a is that engagement in online reading activities
(ORA) offers students valuable experiences and training that enhance
their online reading skills. For H1b, the reasoning is that reading
achievement (RA) contributes to the development of metacognitive
skills online (MASC). These insights motivate an investigation of indirect
associations in which RA may partly account for the relationship between ORA and MASC.

2.4. Socioeconomic status in relation to achievement
Socioeconomic status is significantly associated with academic outcomes, internet use, and online engagement, and these associations
present challenges for students from disadvantaged backgrounds
(Livingstone & Helsper, 2007; Mullis et al., 2017; Organization for
Economic Cooperation and Development, 2010; Sirin, 2005). Research
indicates that having ICT resources at home positively affects academic
achievement by fostering greater online reading engagement (Lee & Wu,
2013). Access to digital technology and opportunities for its use at home
are critical components of this issue, facilitating the development of
metacognitive skills through varied learning experiences (Koyuncu
et al., 2022; Lee & Wu, 2013).

H1c. Reading achievement (RA) is associated with both ORA and
MASC and thereby accounts for some of the ORA–MASC covariation.
RQ 2. How is socio-economic status associated with students' online
reading activities and subsequent reading achievement?
We posit that higher ESCS is positively associated with students’
ORA. Students from higher ESCS backgrounds generally report greater
access to digital resources, educational support, and diverse learning
experiences, factors that are associated with higher levels of reported
reading activity online.

2.5. Gender
Gender remains a crucial determinant of educational outcomes
(Freeman & Viarengo, 2014; Gubbels et al., 2020; Mullis et al., 2023;
Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2019). Girls
outperform boys in reading literacy (Mullis et al., 2017; Organization for
Economic Cooperation and Development, 2019). Gender is associated
with differences in metacognitive skills, with observed variation on tasks
such as summarizing and memorizing (Wu, 2014). Socialization plays a
role in these differences, affecting how boys and girls assess source
credibility online; girls are generally more cautious, while boys are often
encouraged to take risks (Espinoza & Strasser, 2020) and are better at
anti-phishing behaviors (Sun et al., 2016).

H2a. Higher ESCS is positively associated with students’ ORA.
H2c. Students from higher ESCS backgrounds will exhibit greater RA
than their peers from lower ESCS backgrounds, and we are examining
how the ESCS-RA association operates through ORA. H2b is similar to
H1a.
Hypothesis 2a posits a link between socio-economic status and
educational access in terms of resources. Students from higher ESCS
backgrounds commonly report greater access to enriched digital environments, and the observed associations suggest these differences
correspond with variation in reading skills. Recognizing these disparities
is crucial, as they lead to unequal academic outcomes, including
achievement, highlighting the need to address digital inequality for
equitable educational success.
This analysis investigates the association between an independent
variable (X) and a dependent variable (Y) and whether that association
is accounted for, in part, by a variable (M) that partly explains the association. Due to the cross-sectional design, we are examining “statistical mediation” when looking at the indirect association between
variables. An indirect association means that a third variable explains
how two variables are related. Within-country analyses were conducted
to assess the validity of the hypotheses in each Nordic country.

2.6. Acquiring Digital Literacy Skills and Strategies (ADSS)
Providing structured digital skills instruction in schools is crucial for
helping students evaluate digital information and maintain online safety
(Anastasiades & Vitalaki, 2011). While direct teaching of reading strategies is associated with improved reading comprehension, teaching
digital skills also significantly contributes to developing metacognitive
skills (Cromley, 2023; Organization for Economic Cooperation and
Development, 2021; Wu et al., 2019). This kind of instruction is crucial
for students from low socioeconomic background who may have limited
opportunities to learning these skills outside of the educational
environment.
Guided by prior research, we hypothesize that reading achievement
(RA) explains part of the association between ORA and MASC, and that
ORA contributes to the association between ESCS and RA.

4.1. The Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
The PISA 2018 test focused on reading achievement (RA), using a
computer-based, multistage test with multiple-choice and shortconstructed items in a two-hour format. Due to the focus on Reading
domain in 2018, the study also included an additional subscale for
metacognition “how to assess source credibility online (MASC) besides
self-report questions about online reading experiences (ORA),
(Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2019b, p.
222). The MASC factor contains five statements about different action
options for messages, and where there is a progression in the level of
reflection through the five action options (from following instructions in
the email to checking out the information in the email).

5.2. Evaluating measurement invariance
In this multi-country study, we also needed to test measurement
invariance across the five countries, to check that the construct operated
similarly across the five countries. Comparability between groups can be
examined statistically with measurement testing using MG CFA methodological approach. Ensuring that ORA is understood uniformly across
countries is essential for drawing accurate conclusions about its role in
the associations. Because the χ 2 statistic is highly sample-size dependent
and may detect negligible misfit, we avoided relying on χ 2 difference
tests when assessing nested model fit. Our primary focus was on CFI and
RMSEA changes to evaluate the influence of equality constraints.
For testing configural invariance we look at the fit indices and the
configural model indicated a good fit (thresholds: CFI should be ≥ 0.95,
RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and SRMR ≤ 0.08). Our results show CFI = 0.991,
RMSEA = 0.051 and SRMR = 0.016), confirming that the structure of
the factor is consistent across countries and meets configural invariance.
For testing the metric invariance within five countries, the model for
ORA, showed a good fit (with CFI = 0.989, RMSEA = 0.038, SRMR =
0.022). However, in this case we need to observe the change in the fit
indices from configural to metric. The change in fit indices showed ΔCFI
= 0.002, and ΔRMSEA = - 0.013. For 5 groups, cutoff values of Δ CFI
≤ 0.010, and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 are considered for establishing metric
invariance (Chen, 2007). Metric invariance is suitable for enabling valid
cross-country comparisons of correlations and regressions. Nevertheless,
we also tested scalar invariance (equal item intercepts) as a robustness
check and to see if comparing latent means would be possible. The
construct ORAs did not reach scalar invariance criteria of Δ RMSEA
≤ 0.03, but ADSS had Δ RMSEA = 0.024 (Appendix B). Full Metric
invariance was consistently met, but scalar invariance did not hold for
the key constructs.

6. Discussion
As the MSEM technique is well-suited for inferential purposes
(Byrne, 2013; Hox & Roberts, 2011), our study's contribution highlights
the fit of the model with data from the five countries, for the associations
between the variables. While taking the multi-national perspectives into
account, we attempt to identify the interplay between variables.

6.1.1. The role of the school
Schools play a key role in promoting responsible digital behavior and
teaching students to navigate the digital world safely (Anastasiades &
Vitalaki, 2011; Chen et al., 2022). Effective learning environments are
associated with students’ ability to identify fraud and phishing, but this
association between ADSS and MASC was statistically confirmed only in
Norway, suggesting cross-country heterogeneity in the observed
relationship.
Our finding that ADSS was not associated with MASC contrasts
research from Wu et al. (2019), which emphasized the benefits of metacognitive instruction for reading comprehension as effective instruction can amplify metacognitive benefits. Additionally, Breakstone et al.
(2021) stress the importance of teaching students to critically evaluate
online information, which is particularly crucial in a digitally diverse
landscape. Ensuring that students from low ESCS have access to
adequate digital learning opportunities is essential for developing their
metacognitive skills, as evidenced by Organization for Economic
Cooperation and Development (2021), which links relevant classroom
practices to MASC. Enhancing strategies to ensure access to ORAs is
crucial for improving both reading ability and MASC across diverse
contexts. Further research is needed to explore these mechanisms in
varying cultural settings.

6.1. ORA-RA-MASC interrelationships
Reading is the main subject in each third cycle of PISA. In 2018
reading competence was measured with a test, but there were also
specific statements related to reading activities and metacognitive approaches when getting online, written messages in the student
questionnaire.
Our study finds a positive link between online reading activities for
information search (ORAs) and reading achievement, suggesting that
frequent engagement in ORAs enhances reading comprehension (Kong
et al., 2022; Lee & Wu, 2013; Zhou & Lam, 2019). This finding aligns

Frequent and effective participation in ORAs, specifically social activities, was positively related to digital reading comprehension through
metacognitive strategies (Chen et al., 2021a; Chen et al., 2021b; Lee &
Wu, 2013; Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development,
2021).
The results also reveal a significantly positive relationship between
MASC and RA across all five countries, emphasizing the role of digital
reading strategies in education (Lim & Jung, 2019; Pérez et al., 2018).
This suggests that 15-year-olds engaging extensively in online reading
are likely to develop metacognitive abilities crucial for evaluating information credibility (Cho, 2014; Cromley, 2023; Dabarera et al., 2014;
Koyuncu et al., 2022). As students enhance their digital literacy through
Internet navigation, they also boost their ability to evaluate online
sources' credibility (Cho, 2014; Dabarera et al., 2014; Lim & Jung,
2019).

6.4. Limitations and future directions

The conditional associations observed in our study highlight the
necessity for a comprehensive approach to improving reading achievement that includes not just an emphasis on online reading but also addresses broader educational inequalities. Initiatives to enhance digital
literacy must consider the diverse socio-economic factors influencing
students’ learning experiences. Understanding these contextual factors
is vital for tailoring educational strategies that effectively leverage online reading activities. Considering digital diversity (Dolan, 2015; Van
Dijk, 2020), acquiring digital skills is especially vital for students from
low ESCS who depend on schools to access digital learning opportunities
and develop metacognitive skills, supported by evidence that relevant
classroom practices are linked to MASC (Organization for Economic
Cooperation and Development, 2021). Also, Breakstone et al. (2021)
point to the importance of teaching students to successfully evaluate the
quality of information found online.
Differences in access to digital resources could play a crucial role;
nations with better infrastructure and technology integration in education are likely to see a stronger association between ORA on MASC. By
recognizing that online reading activities are a piece of a larger puzzle,
educators and policymakers can create more effective, inclusive strategies that promote equitable educational success for all students.
Enhancing educational equity requires a comprehensive approach
integrating reading and digital literacy. Effective reading strategies are
positively associated with reading proficiency, and, given this association, it may be beneficial for educators to support students in developing
these strategies. Choi et al. (2017) and Fraillon et al. (2019) similarly
highlight links between digital and information literacy and indicators
of responsible online behavior.
Similarly, the difficulties students encounter in assessing the reliability of online content emphasize the need for targeted instructional
support in schools (Frønes, 2017; Nygren & Guath, 2019). However, this
does not imply that inclusion efforts are unsuccessful; the situation could
be worse without the proactive emphasis on equal opportunities in the
Nordic region.
Our analysis of ESCS reveals that, despite Nordic efforts to ensure
educational equality and digital literacy, socioeconomic disparities
persist due to unequal home conditions and school resources. This aligns
with Buchholtz et al. (2020) and Sandsør et al. (2023), who identify

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of
PISA data prevents causal inferences, making the findings exploratory
and in need of replication with longitudinal data. Second, the analysis
assumed linear, unidirectional relationships, which may oversimplify
complex dynamics.
We include ADSS as a background covariate. Although it is not a
fixed demographic characteristic, it reflects students’ perceived opportunities at school to acquire digital skills and strategies and thus serves
as a proxy for school-related preparation before the test.
We also acknowledge that ORA and MASC could influence each
other: indulgence in ORAs provide training, for developed MASC, while
higher metacognitive capacity can lead to more successful ORAs. Still,
we used a one-way model because we lack plausible external instruments to identify feedback effects. Future work should test these
two-way effects as our estimates are conditional on this assumption.
MASC scale focuses on a single process in the digital reading context
and relies on self-reports about actions taken, though many other strategies can be considered. More so, ORA focused on message frequency
rather than quality. Revising items could improve the scale’s reliability.
Future research could also include qualitative measures to strengthen
these constructs. Despite these limitations, this study sheds light on the
diversity in students’ perceptions of digital skills learned at school.
Additionally, ADSS used yes/no responses, offering limited detail on
teacher guidance or activity timing. Addressing these gaps would
improve the PISA questionnaire’s conceptualization of online strategy
instruction.
We recognize another limitation of our study: our analysis of indirect
associations was based exclusively on students’ self-perceptions. Incorporating teachers’ perspectives could have offered valuable insights and
supported students’ views. However, in the absence of a teacher questionnaire, the PISA dataset we used does not permit this addition.
Moreover, data from non-European countries could enhance our understanding of how students’ ORAs predict metacognition online across
different contexts.

Reading was a central focus of the PISA 2018 assessment. Our study
identified positive associations between online reading activities (ORAs)
and reading achievement (RA), as well as between RA and metacognitive skills for evaluating the credibility of information (MASC).
These findings are consistent with previous research highlighting the
role of ORAs in enhancing reading proficiency (Kong et al., 2022).
Moreover, students with higher reading achievement seem better
equipped to recognize unreliable information and navigate online risks
such as fraud and phishing (Zhou & Lam, 2019). However, the link
between ORAs and MASC varies across countries. Notable associations
were observed in Denmark, Finland and Iceland, but for Norway, and
Sweden, our results indicated cross-country variation in how digital
reading is associated with educational practices and cultural context.
When content is culturally relevant, ORAs are more effective;
conversely, misaligned materials can reduce student engagement and
limit cognitive benefits. Schools play a critical role in fostering responsible digital behavior and teaching students to navigate online spaces
safely (Chen et al., 2022).
Our finding of significant association through a third variable (ORA)
in the ESCS–RA relationship reinforces the notion that both ESCS and
ORA play a critical role in developing RA. Equitable access to digital
learning opportunities is essential for students from low ESCS, who often
rely on schools to develop metacognitive skills (Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2021). Digital literacy initiatives
must consider socio-economic disparities, as unequal home and school
resources persist even in egalitarian regions like the Nordics (Buchholtz
et al., 2020; Sandsør et al., 2023). Policymakers should integrate ORAs
into broader educational strategies to enhance RA and indirectly support
MASC development while addressing gaps in access to digital resources.
A multifaceted approach is needed to advance digital equity, supporting
the development of responsible online behavior and critical evaluation
skills across diverse contexts.
