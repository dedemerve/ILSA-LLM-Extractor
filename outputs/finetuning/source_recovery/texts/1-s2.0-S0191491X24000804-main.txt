When construct-irrelevant sources affect item difficulty, validity of the assessment is compromised. Using responses of 260000 students from 71 countries to the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA)
2018 mathematics assessment and cross-classified mixed effects models, we examined three validity concerns
associated with the construct-irrelevant factor, item format: whether the format influenced item difficulty,
whether item format’s impact on difficulty varied across countries, undermining PISA’s foundational goal of
meaningful country comparisons, and whether item format effects differed between genders, affecting assessment fairness. Item format contributed to a substantial average of 12 % of variance in item difficulties. The effect
of item format was non-uniform across countries, with 30 % of the variance in item difficulties being due to
format in lower-performing countries, and 10 % in higher-performing countries, challenging the comparability of
educational outcomes. The impact of gender on item format differences was minor. Implications for secondary
research and assessment design are discussed.

Contemporary assessments often incorporate both MC and CR formats, under the belief that this balanced approach mitigates any
disadvantage caused by a student’s (or a group of students with different
characteristics such as, for example, gender or reading ability) unfamiliarity or lack of skills in one particular format, thereby ensuring
fairness by equally offsetting potential biases (Hambleton, 2002). In
adopting this strategy, assessment designers acknowledge that specific
item formats may inherently disadvantage some individuals; however,
by balancing these formats, such biases are expected to be reduced.
Thus, the choice of item format is often treated as a nuisance factor, a
byproduct of assessment design that, while recognised, is managed
rather than eliminated.
However, this nuisance nature of the item format is not guaranteed;
instead, it can become an active factor that influences the item’s difficulty level for reasons unrelated to the assessed construct. For example,
some studies suggest that CR items may be more challenging than MC
due to the higher cognitive demands they place on test-takers (recall and
production for CR, compared to recognition and selection processes for
MC; Martinez, 1999). Consequently, CR items may disadvantage
test-takers with weaker skills in areas unrelated to the tested knowledge,
such as writing or organisation. Furthermore, considerations of
test-taking strategies and motivation highlight that MC items may afford
strategic advantages (Bridgeman, 1992; Katz, Friedman, Bennett, &
Berger, 1996) and lower cognitive loads (compared to CR items; Haladyna & Rodriguez, 2013), potentially making them less mentally taxing
and thus easier for test-takers. The compounded effects of these factors
—cognitive demands, test-taking strategies, and motivation— suggest
that item formats can influence item difficulties beyond factors relevant
to the target construct.
A common limitation of previous research on item format is its scope,
often restricted to reviewing the effect of item format within the singular, arguably homogeneous group (e.g., school exit examinations or
university admission within a single year’s cohort) or restricted to a
single geographical local, with the large number of studies originating in
the US. However, it is important to recognise that assessments do not
occur in a vacuum. Cross-cultural diversity (van de Vijver, Jude, &
Kuger, 2019), differences in instructional practices, varying test-taking
motivations (Barry, Horst, Finney, Brown, & Kopp, 2010; Debeer,
Buchholz, Hartig, & Janssen, 2014), and familiarity with test formats
(Hambleton, 2002) likely differ across countries. Therefore, it is realistic
to expect that the effects of item formats are not consistent or stable
across different cultural contexts, suggesting that differences between
formats observed in one country may not necessarily apply universally.
In turn, variability in how much item format affects item difficulty
across countries can introduce further layers of validity, fairness, and
comparability concerns, as an assessment administered across diverse
countries may yield differing outcomes purely based on item format. The
comparability across educational systems and countries is arguably
particularly crucial for the reliability of global performance leagues such
as those produced by international large-scale assessments like the
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA), which influence educational policy and practice worldwide (Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2016). When assessments fail to account for cross-country
differences (e.g., differences in teacher instruction, educational contexts, socioeconomic factors), they risk providing an oversimplified
basis for such rankings, which are often used to justify educational reforms and policy shifts (Grek, 2009), and can lead to ‘policy borrowing’,
where countries adopt educational strategies that have been successful
elsewhere based on these rankings (Phillips & Ochs, 2003;
Steiner-Khamsi, 2014). However, if the foundational comparability of
assessment data is compromised, these policy adaptations may be
misguided, leading to educational decisions that do not genuinely reflect
the needs or abilities of the local student populations. Thus, ensuring the

comparability of assessment outcomes, not just within but also across
countries, becomes a pivotal concern, directly influencing international
educational policy and strategic educational planning.
Therefore, the present study aims to quantify the aforementioned
validity concerns by examining to what extent item format determines
item difficulty on a global scale. We use the comprehensive and diverse
response data from the Programme for International Student Assessment
(PISA) 2018 mathematical literacy assessment as a working example to
address this aim. With its wide geographical coverage, including 79
countries, and its large item pool of over 80 items, the PISA 2018
response data offer a unique opportunity to explore the extent and implications of item format effects on a nearly global scale and assess the
generalisability of findings both across countries and across items.
Our research questions (RQ) are the following:
RQ: To what extent does the probability of a correct response systematically differ between MC and CR items in the PISA 2018
mathematics literacy assessment?
Furthermore, the potential variability in item format differences
across educational systems would inadvertently challenge the foundational principle of comparability in international assessments, thus not
only threatening the fairness of assessment but also raising questions
about its underlying equity on a global scale. Therefore, we extend our
main research question to:
RQ(a): To what extent does the above item format difference vary
across countries that participated in the PISA 2018?
Moreover, previous research also points to the role of student characteristics in moderating item format differences, with gender emerging
as a particularly critical factor. Studies indicate that the differences
between MC and CR items are not uniform across genders. Males,
generally more prone to risk-taking and guessing behaviours than females (Ben-Shakhar & Sinai, 1991; Gafni & Melamed, 1994), may find
MC items easier. In contrast, CR items could be more challenging due to
lower verbal abilities and a tendency towards less diligence, especially
in low-stakes situations (DeMars, Bashkov, & Socha, 2013). These factors could result in a larger discrepancy in the probability of a correct
response to MC versus CR items for males. The gender-based disparities
in responding to different item formats introduce yet another layer of
validity concerns, suggesting that item format differences may not only
be subject to variation across countries but also by gender within the
same educational context. In light of this, our study further incorporates
gender as an additional factor, and we aim to assess its interaction with
item format, leading to yet another extension of our main RQ:
RQ(b): Within each country, to what extent does the above item
format difference vary across students of different genders but with
the same mathematics ability?
Most critically, if the item format differences vary by gender, RQ(b),
and further, by country, RQ(a), we are confronted with a scenario that
challenges the very essence of comparability. Such a finding would
imply that the interaction between item format and gender by country
could lead to a highly unpredictable understanding of student abilities,
rendering cross-country comparisons far less straightforward and
reliable.
2. Method
The PISA 2018 included reading literacy as the major domain, while
mathematical and science literacy served as minor domains, assessing
students’ proficiencies covering multiple areas of knowledge (OECD,
2019). To optimise test efficiency and minimise student fatigue, PISA
2018 utilised a rotated-booklet design, in which each student was presented with a subset of the overall item pool, packaged in a booklet

2.2.2. Cross-Classified Mixed Effects Models
The present study estimated a series of cross-classified mixed effects
models following an explanatory item response theory (IRT) approach
(De Boeck & Wilson, 2004; Van den Noortgate, De Boeck, & Meulders,
2003), relating the probability of a correct response on a PISA 2018
mathematics literacy item to basic individual characteristics of both the
student (i.e., their mathematics ability and gender) as well as the item (i.
e., their difficulty and format).

probability of a correct response to a PISA 2018 mathematical literacy

2.1. Sample
PISA 2018 Countries, Schools, & Students. The PISA 2018 mathematical literacy assessment was administered in 79 countries and
economies (henceforth, ‘countries’).1 In 71 countries, students took the
computer-based version of the PISA assessment (CBA), and the paperbased version (PBA) was administered in eight countries. The present
study exclusively considered the response data from the CBAadministered countries to maintain consistency and comparability of
results. Adhering to the PISA’s sampling design, each participating
country aimed to sample approximately 5000 to 6000 15-year-old students. Only the students who received a booklet containing mathematics
items were eligible for our study. The resulting sample sizes varied from
822 students in Moscow region (Russia) to 14713 students in Spain
(with a median sample size of 3043; see Table A1, Appendix A). The
total sample size amounted to over 260000 students.
PISA 2018 Mathematical Literacy Items. The item pool for the CBA
mathematical literacy domain included 82 items, of which 35 were
presented in multiple-choice (MC) format and 47 in constructed
response (CR) format (see, Annex A in OECD, 2020). MC items included
simple single-selection questions (20 items) and multiple-selection
questions (15 items), such as a series of binary choices (yes/no, true/false). CR items required students to demonstrate their thought process
by outlining the steps taken or providing an extended written response.
The items were organised into non-overlapping, mutually exclusive
clusters (M01, M02, M03, M04, M05, and M06: M6A vs M6B).2 Each
cluster corresponded to a testing time of 30 min and included between
11 and 12 items. No item was included in more than one cluster. Each
student tested in mathematics was administered one to two mathematics
clusters.

Regarding the first two validity concerns, the design of assessment
items should ideally ensure that the influence of item format (and other
construct-irrelevant item characteristics) on item difficulty is minimal,
and it is instead the construct-relevant factors that primarily determine
item difficulty. Instead, incorporating multiple item formats in an
assessment is meant to be a benign feature that offers equal opportunity
of success to all test-takers, irrespective of their format preference
(OECD, 2019). Contrary to this ideal scenario, our findings indicate that
item format was a noteworthy difficulty determinant of the PISA 2018
mathematical literacy assessment items, accounting for 12 % of item
variance on average across countries.
Furthermore, the impact of item format on item difficulty was also
worryingly not uniform across countries; It was less pronounced in
higher-performing countries (e.g., 6 % in China) yet far more pronounced in lower-performing countries (e.g., 30 % in the Dominican
Republic). The pronounced impact of item format on students performance in lower-performing countries, in turn, suggests that these students may have faced additional challenges that were not related to the
core mathematics skills and knowledge being assessed by PISA, be it due
to varying reading and writing abilities (Haladyna & Rodriguez, 2013),
less familiarity with specific test formats (Hambleton, 2002), test-taking
motivation (Barry et al., 2010), or a multitude of other factors.
With respect to the difficulty of the two item formats, our findings
show a consistent pattern: CR items were more difficult than MC items
across all countries (i.e., the average odds of a correct response to an MC
item were three times the odds of a correct response on a CR item). Our
findings are consistent with prior research, also from contexts outside
international large-scale assessments, where CR items were found to be
more demanding, potentially because the test-taking strategies effective
for MC items do not apply to CR tasks (Bonner, 2013; Katz et al., 1996;

4. Discussion
The validity of assessment results can be compromised when
construct-irrelevant factors, such as item format, influence the difficulty
of assessment items (Whitely, 1983). Using the responses of over 260000
students from 71 countries to over 80 items of the PISA 2018 mathematics literacy assessment, the present study systematically examined
the influence of item format —multiple-choice (MC) and constructed
response (CR)— on item difficulty, and consequently on the probability
that students respond correctly to an assessment item.
Specifically, this study examined three validity concerns regarding
item format. The first concern pertained to whether the format of the
PISA mathematical literacy items influenced their difficulty, thereby
affecting the students’ probability of a correct response for reasons unrelated to their mathematics abilities. The second validity concern was
whether the impact of item format on item difficulty varied across
countries, undermining PISA’s fundamental goal to facilitate fair and
meaningful country comparisons. The third concern was the possibility
that the influence of item format on item difficulty might also vary between student genders within the same country, further threatening the
fairness of assessment results, as differences in item formats could
potentially skew the comparability of scores between genders within
individual countries, disadvantage certain groups of students, and
hinder our understanding of their mathematical literacy.

cross-country variability in item format differences relates to systemlevel covariates, such as exam culture, prevalent assessment formats,
and instructional practices. The differences, of course, could be due not
only to instructional approaches but also to varying levels of test-taking
effort and motivation in low-stakes settings (Debeer et al., 2014; Hambleton, 2002).
Regarding our third validity concern, our findings suggest that while
the differences in difficulties of items of different formats indeed varied
by student gender within the same country —with average differences in
the chances of a correct response between MC and CR format being
larger for male students as compared to female students with the same
mathematics ability— the overall moderating effect of gender was
consistently small across most countries. Our findings on the moderating
effect of gender on item format differences are also in line with the
presumed gender behavioural and cognitive tendencies: males may
engage more in risk-taking and guessing, which aids them in MC formats
(e.g., Ben-Shakhar & Sinai, 1991; Gafni & Melamed, 1994), whereas
females might excel in CR formats due to higher verbal abilities and
greater diligence (DeMars et al., 2013). Despite these differences, the
moderating effect was minor and less concerning than the more pronounced disparities observed across countries. Thus, while item format
does vary in its impact by gender within countries, the extent of this
variation is not as severe as to present a substantial threat to the fairness
of the assessment within countries.

Additionally, our findings suggest that CR items not only presented
higher difficulty but also showed greater heterogeneity in difficulty
levels compared to MC items, with no MC items in the PISA 2018
mathematics assessment designed, intentionally or unintentionally, to
address the higher difficulty levels. To mitigate the impact of item
format as a nuisance factor, balancing the distribution of item formats
across different difficulty levels could be an effective strategy (Kirk,
2013). While expanding the item pool to achieve this balance may raise
practical concerns, such as increased time and financial costs, the benefits of a more systematic assessment design outweigh these challenges.
Lastly, incorporating item-focused feedback from the PISA assessment could prove beneficial as it is directly relevant to school instruction. Currently, PISA country profiles focus primarily on student
characteristics, such as gender and socioeconomic status, as an example
to provide feedback on performance. However, extending this feedback
to include test results related to specific item formats could provide
educators and students with valuable insights into potential weaknesses,
encouraging targeted instructional improvements.
Implications for Secondary Research. For secondary researchers
using the PISA 2018 mathematics assessment results, it is crucial to
consider the validity concerns outlined in this study. Specifically, the
substantial role that item format played in lower-performing countries
could result in misleading interpretations regarding the actual mathematical abilities of students from these countries. Therefore, researchers
should exercise caution when drawing inferences from these results,
especially when making comparisons across countries, as the influence
of item format on student performance can undermine the validity of
such comparisons, suggesting that country outcomes may not solely
reflect the actual differences in mathematical proficiency across countries but also the differences in how these countries’ students interact
with the item formats. To enhance the accuracy of cross-country comparisons, we recommend directing analyses toward countries that
demonstrate similar patterns of item format differences. This approach
of ‘comparing what’s comparable’ sidesteps broad and unspecific comparisons that overlook the unique characteristics of each country (e.g.,
instructional practices, language, familiarity with test formats, curriculum, and exam culture), and by focusing on more closely aligned
contexts, the analyses may more faithfully represent the true educational achievement, minimising distortions introduced by differing item
formats.
The present article focused on the traditional item formats. As new
technology-enhanced item formats become increasingly prevalent in
assessments (Goldhammer, Scherer, & Greiff, 2020; Sireci & Zenisky,
2016), they introduce additional design factors (e.g., layout, level of
interactivity, visual aids) whose impacts are not yet fully understood.
Incorporation of these new factors into item design may raise validity
concerns similar to those discussed in this article, necessitating continuous refinement of our assessment designs to ensure that they effectively
facilitate fair and valid evaluations of student performance.

4.1. Implications for Secondary Research and Assessment Design
The broader implications of our findings are the following. The
impact of item format on the probability of a correct response to the
PISA 2018 mathematical literacy items challenges the validity of the
assessment, indicating that the PISA 2018 mathematical literacy
assessment might have measured additional, unintended skills (reading
or writing) or strategies (e.g., test-taking and response strategies)
beyond the intended construct of mathematical literacy. The differential
impact of item format by country presents a considerable challenge to
the meaningful comparability of global educational outcomes, a foundational goal of PISA. Addressing this issue may require a concerted
effort from the secondary researchers and policy-makers and from
assessment designers to understand and account for the underlying
factors contributing to these differences, ensuring that the assessment
can fulfil its objective of offering insights into educational systems
worldwide without the confounding influence of irrelevant factors
(American Educational Research Association; American Psychological
Association; National Council on Measurement in Education Eds., 2014).
Implications for Assessment Design. From an assessment design
perspective, our findings emphasise the need for test designers to carefully consider the item formats used to minimise their impact on item
difficulty levels (Mislevy, Steinberg, & Almond, 2003). The considerable
variation in item format differences between higher- and
lower-performing countries further points to a need for a more tailored
approach to assessment design that takes into account the unique
educational contexts, access to instructional resources, and typical
instructional practices prevalent in various countries, thus ensuring that
assessment items are accessible and fair for students from diverse
