Abstract
Evaluating differential item functioning (DIF) in assessments plays an important role in
achieving measurement fairness across different subgroups, such as gender and native
language. However, relying solely on the item response scores among traditional DIF
techniques poses challenges for researchers and practitioners in interpreting DIF.
Recently, response process data, which carry valuable information about examinees’
response behaviors, offer an opportunity to further interpret DIF items by examining
differences in response processes. This study aims to investigate the potential of
response process data features in improving the interpretability of DIF items, with a
focus on gender DIF using data from the Programme for International Assessment of
Adult Competencies (PIAAC) 2012 computer-based numeracy assessment. We
applied random forest and logistic regression with ridge regularization to investigate
the association between process data features and DIF items, evaluating the impor-
tant features to interpret DIF. In addition, we evaluated model performance across
varying percentages of DIF items to reflect practical scenarios with different percen-
tages of DIF items. The results demonstrate that the combination of timing features
and action-sequence features is informative to reveal the response process differ-
ences between groups, thereby enhancing DIF item interpretability. Overall, this
study introduces a feasible procedure to leverage response process data to under-
stand and interpret DIF items, shedding light on potential reasons for the low

Fairness is a foundational element for drawing valid and reliable assessment infer-
ences (American Educational Research Association [AERA] et al., 2014; Huang
et al., 2016). According to the Standards (AERA et al., 2014), a core element of fair-
ness is providing equal opportunities across various facets and stages, including test
design and administration, for diverse subgroups and individuals in measurement.
However, many challenges can arise in offering equal opportunities for all examinees
to demonstrate their standing on the construct being measured, which may compro-
mise measurement fairness. For large-scale assessments, such as the Programme for
International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) and the Programme for
International Student Assessment (PISA), test design should consider the diverse
characteristics of examinees (e.g., gender, race, countries, and languages) to ensure
fair opportunities to demonstrate their knowledge and skills. In addition, the transi-
tion of these large-scale assessments from paper-based to computer-based modes
may introduce irrelevant factors, as this shift may affect subgroups’ response beha-
viors and score differences.
Differential item functioning (DIF) occurs when different groups of examinees
with the same level of ability exhibit different probabilities of correctly answering an
item, raising fairness concerns among these groups. Researchers commonly evaluate
DIF to investigate potential sources that contributing to score differences among dif-
ferent groups in large-scale assessments. Nevertheless, detecting a DIF item does not
completely indicate the underlying sources of DIF (Shermis et al., 2017). Shermis
and St. George (1992) provided an example of misinterpretation of a detected DIF
item. They found that, with respect to the subgroups categorized by cultural back-
ground, the DIF item favored the reference cultural group over the focal cultural
group. Their interpretation suggested that the discrepancy arose from the reference
group’s greater exposure to a game related to the DIF item. Yet, Shermis et al. (2017)
later argued that this interpretation of the detected DIF item was incorrect, as both
groups had comparable experiences with the game.
Some researchers proposed to simultaneously explore multiple demographic vari-
ables for a better understanding and interpretation of detected DIF items in large-
scale assessments (e.g., Bauer et al., 2020; Finch & Herna´ndez Finch, 2013; Lin &
Lin, 2014; Oliveri et al., 2013; Wu & Ercikan, 2006), including a few studies particu-
larly focused on large-scale computer-based assessments (e.g., Belzak, 2023). They
evaluated the combination of multiple demographic variables (i.e., gender, age, coun-
try, and native language) to understand the occurrence of DIF based on the

correlational relationship between demographic variables and item scores. For exam-
ple, Belzak (2023) demonstrated that combining three variables—gender, age, and
native language—provided a more comprehensive interpretation of DIF than using a
single demographic variable.
However, previous studies rarely incorporated response behaviors and response
processes in interpreting DIF items. Analyzing response process differences could be
promising for DIF interpretation, offering additional insights beyond demographics
and item responses (Ercikan et al., 2020). Moreover, exploring the relationship
between response processes and detected DIF items is critical for enhancing interpre-
tation and assisting test designers in mitigating DIF. For instance, Ercikan et al.
(2020) found that Chinese and Japanese examinees had longer response times and
fewer actions than Canadians when responding to constructed-response items in the
PISA 2015 science assessment. These findings suggested that language DIF items
were associated with language spelling and input typing procedures, underscoring
the potential for additional insights through interpreting response process differences.
Such a comprehensive understanding of DIF items benefits researchers and test
designers, enabling them to adjust these items efficiently and purposefully.
Response process data, also termed as process data or log data, refer to the cumu-
lation of records of examinees’ clicks and keystrokes while they are taking
computer-based tests, containing numerous pieces of response process information,
and facilitating studies of differences with respect to response behaviors (He et al.,
2019; Kuang & Sahin, 2023). Recent research has revealed that response process
data can provide valuable information, such as (a) evaluating examinees’ proficiency
level (He et al., 2019) and (b) the correlations of response behaviors to item diffi-
culty and item discrimination under classical test theory and item response theory
(Goldhammer et al., 2017; Kuang & Sahin, 2023). In addition, several studies lever-
aged response process data to compare performance differences across subgroups
with various background characteristics (e.g., Eichmann et al., 2020; Ercikan et al.,
2020; He et al., 2019; Leng et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2019). For example, Leng et al.
(2024) found significant differences in response speed between boys and girls in the
mathematics assessment, with response speed negatively correlated with mathe-
matics ability.
Given the informative response process data, this study aims to evaluate the rela-
tionship between response process and DIF items to enhance the interpretability of
DIF items. Different from Ercikan et al.’s (2020) research, which used the total
response time (RT) and the total number of actions as separate indicators to detect
DIF items, we propose using multiple features—such as the timing of each action and
specific action features—to better interpret detected DIF items. In this study, we first
provide an illustrative example to demonstrate the process of identifying response
process data features. Subsequently, we detail the implementation of machine learn-
ing (ML) techniques to analyze the associations between response process data fea-
tures and items. Specifically, we applied random forest (RF) and logistic regression
(LR) to select features related to the detected DIF items, offering visualizable

Mantel–Haenszel Method to Detect DIF
DIF is a widely used method for identifying test items that may function differently
across groups of test-takers, bringing attention to potential item-level fairness con-
cerns (Perrone, 2006). Specifically, DIF methods evaluate whether the probabilities
of getting an item correct are different between two subgroups (i.e., the reference and
focal groups), conditioning on examinees’ ability.
The Mantel–Haenszel (MH; Holland & Thayer, 1988) method is commonly used
in DIF detection for dichotomously scoring items. It assumes the total test score is
the proxy for examinees’ ability. The MH DIF method estimates the odds ratios of
correctly answering an item between two subgroups, conditioning on examinees’
ability (Equation 1). It hypothesizes ^aMH = 1, indicating no probability differences in

Research findings indicated that the MH method exhibits stable performance in
detecting DIF with small type I errors and acceptable power when compared to other
DIF detection techniques, such as likelihood ratio test (Cohen et al., 1996; Finch,
2005). This stability is particularly notable in large-scale datasets (Cohen et al., 1996;
Finch, 2005). Also, the MH method is straightforward as it does not require strict
assumptions of the item parameter distribution and model fit (Fidalgo et al., 2007).
When applying the MH to detect DIF items, researchers recommend the iterative
purification MH method over the two-step purification and no-purification methods
(e.g., Fidalgo et al., 2000). In the no-purification MH DIF detection, the total test
score is used to match students in the reference and focal groups under the assump-
tion that the total test score does not contain DIF items. In the two-step purification
procedure, the MH method is reapplied using the purified test score that excludes the
DIF items detected in the first step rather than the total test score (Holland & Thayer,
1988). Likewise, the iterative purification procedure repeatedly detects DIF using the
purified test score that excludes DIF items detected at each step until the DIF out-
come stabilizes (Fidalgo et al., 2000). Fidalgo et al. (2000) found that the iterative
purification MH method achieved higher power and a lower type I error rate than the
two-step purification and no-purification methods, especially when the percentage of
DIF items was large (i.e., 15% and 30%).
Response Process Data and DIF
Response process data contain valuable information but are unstructured, where
lengths of action sequences for each examinee responding to an item vary, posing
challenges for analysis using traditional statistical methods (Tang et al., 2020).
Therefore, researchers endeavor to extract features from response process data to
generate structured data.
Timing Features and DIF
Timing features are the commonly identified ones, including the response time for an
item, the response time of the first response attempt, and the response time for each
action. Some researchers have used these timing features to explore the associations
between response time and examinees’ proficiency (e.g., De Boeck & Jeon, 2019;
Goldhammer & Kroehne, 2014). Ulitzsch et al. (2021) combined response time with
action subsequences from ‘‘n-gram’’ to identify common response actions within a
problem-solving item.
In addition, timing features support the evaluation of item measurement fairness.
Ercikan et al. (2020) used item response time as a DIF indicator to compare English
language learners (ELL) with non-ELL groups, conditioning on the total test score or
conditioning on the response time for the test. They found that ELL examinees took

787

longer to respond to an item than non-ELL examinees with the same ability. Ercikan
et al. (2020) concluded that timing features provided insight into examinees’ test
engagement and measurement fairness even when traditional DIF methods did not
detect DIF. Moreover, Molenaar and Feskens (2024) used the multiple indicator mul-
tiple cause model to evaluate the mediating effect of item response time on the direct
effect of the grouping variable on DIF items. Their study concluded that the sub-
group difference in item response time can interpret DIF items.
Action-Sequence Features and DIF
Action-sequence features, such as the total number of actions, subsequences, and
action transitions between latent states, are used to identify behavior differences
among examinees with different characteristics (e.g., He & von Davier, 2015), pre-
dict examinees’ responses (Tang et al., 2020), and estimate examinees’ ability and
performance motivation (Arieli-Attali et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). In addition,
the number of interactions and the number of exploration steps in responding to
problem-solving items differ by gender. The positive correlation between these fea-
tures and examinees’ scores explains the score differences between boys and girls in
solving complex problems (Eichmann et al., 2020).
Furthermore, Ercikan et al. (2020) used the total number of actions for an item as
an indicator to investigate language and cultural DIF items, demonstrating that such
an action-sequence feature reveals behavior differences between subgroups and thus
support the interpretation of DIF items.
Extracting Action-Sequence Features by Social Network Analysis
Given the valuable information of response process data, researchers have proposed
different methods to extract action-sequence features, such as hidden Markov model
(Arieli-Attali et al., 2019), ‘‘n-gram’’ (He & von Davier, 2015), multidimensional
scaling method (Tang et al., 2020), and the autoencoder algorithm (Tang et al.,
2021). However, there is no consensus on which approach is most effective for gen-
erating informative features to analyze the response process data.
Zhu et al. (2016) proposed to apply social network analysis (SNA) to extract
action-sequence features. SNA is a research methodology originally developed for
analyzing the structural relationships within a network. It involves mathematically
and visually exploring the interactions between nodes, which can be individuals,
groups, organizations, or other entities (Scott, 2012). Specifically, SNA uses graphi-
cal models to represent networks, where the nodes represent the entities (e.g., indi-
viduals, organizations) and the edges represent the relationships or interactions
between these entities. Through SNA, researchers can identify (a) the most influential
entities in a network, often termed as ‘‘central nodes’’; (b) subgroups within the net-
work as ‘‘clusters’’; and (c) patterns or structures in the relationships. In SNA, differ-
ent performance measures are used to assess different aspects of networks. Network
788

density, a measure reflecting the overall connectivity or cohesion within the network,
indicates how robustly the nodes are connected. A higher network density indicates a
longer action sequence to answer that item. Noticeably, network density is sensitive
to the number of actions (termed nodes in SNA) in a given action sequence (Scott,
2012). To support the network density as a comparative feature across items, it is cru-
cial to incorporate the number of nodes as an additional action-sequence feature
(Scott, 2012). In addition, betweenness centrality, a measure based on a node’s role
as a bridge or broker within the network, quantifies the importance or influence of
individual nodes (Ghali et al., 2012). A lower betweenness centrality implies that an
examinee executes each action equally, following a direct-action sequence leading
straight to the item’s answer without back-and-forth movement. In contrast, a higher
betweenness centrality indicates that an examinee relies more on the important
actions when responding to an item.
Zhu et al. (2016) extracted the network density and centrality from SNA to inves-
tigate each examinee’s response actions within a problem-solving item. Network den-
sity reflected the extent of an examinee’s exploration in transitioning from one action
to another within the problem-solving task. Centrality highlighted the most common
actions by examinees to solve the problem. Zhu et al. (2016) found that both network
density and centrality aligned with the scoring rubric for response efficiency and sys-
tematicity in problem-solving. In addition, these action-sequence features derived
from SNA revealed differences in response efficiency and systematicity across differ-
ent groups.
After that, Shin et al. (2024), which developed an engagement-aware predictive
model to assess examinees’ problem-solving performance, used SNA as the first layer
in their model. SNA was applied to extract action-sequence features, including net-
work density, centrality, and the total number of nodes, to understand the transitions
in response actions during problem-solving tasks.
Although SNA has not yet been widely used in extracting response process fea-
tures, it enhances the analysis of sequential interactions within action sequences
(Zhu et al., 2016). The main advantages of using SNA are that (a) SNA can visualize
hidden patterns in the response process, providing insights into each examinee’s
response behaviors and engagement, (b) existing SNA tools enable direct application,
and (c) the action-sequence features derived from SNA support item scoring equity
by elucidating differences in actions across groups (Shin et al., 2024; Zhu et al.,
2016).

Random Forest
RF is a nonparametric model commonly applied to classification tasks, developed by
Breiman (2001), which operates by constructing multiple decision trees (Probst
et al., 2019). In RF, each tree randomly samples a subset of features for each split
(Genuer et al., 2008). This strategy of using a subset, rather than the full set of fea-
tures, enhances the classification accuracy on the test set and mitigates the risk of
overfitting, thereby improving the model’s generalizability (James et al., 2013). In
addition, RF has the advantage of effectively handling noisy data (James et al.,
2013).
Researchers often choose RF for its ability to select important features, helping to
identify strong predictors of outcomes (Salles et al., 2020). Feature importance in RF
indicates the relative contribution of each feature in predicting the outcome within
the training data. There are two commonly used methods for feature selection. The
first method is the impurity-based method, which measures feature importance by
calculating the average decrease in node impurity caused by a specific feature Xm
during the splits within the RF (Breiman, 2001). The importance of feature Xm is
determined by summing of impurity reductions it generates across all trees in the for-
est, as defined in Equation 2. A larger mean decrease in impurity indicates higher
importance.

Empirical Study
In this section, we present an empirical study to exemplify the exploration of the
association between response process data features and DIF items, aiming to facili-
tate a deeper understanding and interpretation of the detected DIF items.
Data
The current study used the dataset from the computer-based PIAAC 2012 numeracy
assessment in the United States, which contained 49 items and 2,746 examinees. Out
of the 49 items, 31 items were in the open-ended response format, and 18 were in
the selected response format. We focused on the open-ended response item format in
this study for two reasons. First, different item formats can affect the examinees’ per-
formance (Liou & Bulut, 2020; Woodcock et al., 2020). Second, the numbers of
Li et al.
791

items for open-ended and the selected response formats were unbalanced, which
could hinder the comparison of scores across various groups. In addition, to mitigate
the influence of missing data on DIF item detection, we excluded 22 items with
response missing rates larger than 55% in the dataset (Robitzsch & Rupp, 2009).
Furthermore, we selected examinees who fulfilled specific criteria, such as being
recorded in the process data log file and providing responses to certain background
questions in the PIAAC U.S. Public Use File 2012. The details of background char-
acteristics and the reason for considering specific background characteristics are fur-
ther described in the ‘‘Obtaining Background Variables’’ section. The final dataset
included nine dichotomous items in the numeracy assessment, and contained 1,675
examinees, with 55.40% women and 44.60% men.
To address missing values issues, we used the Multivariate Imputation by
Chained Equations (MICE) method to replace missingness and structure the com-
pleted data before detecting DIF items. As recommended by L. M. Collins et al.
(2001), we conducted MICE with 10 imputations using the ‘‘mice’’ package (van
Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) in R (version 4.2.2). This generated 10
imputed datasets that were used to detect DIF items.
Method
To investigate response process data features that support DIF interpretation, this sec-
tion introduces the procedure for studying the association between response process
data features and the detected DIF items. The process consists of three main stages.
Stage 1 is to obtain the dependent variable for modeling by labeling DIF and DIF-
free items using the MH technique, as described in the ‘‘Detecting Gender DIF by
MH’’ section. Stage 2 focuses on extracting the independent variables (i.e., response
process data features) and accounting for confounders (i.e., background variables), as
described in the ‘‘Extracting Response Process Data Features’’ and ‘‘Obtaining
Background Variables’’ sections. Stage 3 involves applying RF and LR to identify
the important features associated with DIF items, as described in the ‘‘Classifier
Modeling: Investigating Association and Feature Selection’’ section. The interpreta-
tion of the identified features is presented in the ‘‘Results’’ section, rather than here.
In addition, this section introduces an evaluation procedure using varying percen-
tages of DIF items to examine whether the classifiers’ performance is sensitive to a
specific percentage of DIF items. The percentage of DIF items is one of the com-
monly used DIF conditions and is a variable we can vary and study using our empiri-
cal data.
Detecting Gender DIF by MH. Gender DIF is one of the common concerns across many
measurements, specifically in the subject-specific assessment (e.g., Cheema, 2019;
Cook, 2018). The existence of gender DIF items decreases the comparability of test
scores between groups, leading to a misinterpretation of examinees’ abilities. For
instance, Cook (2018) evaluated whether empowering women’s education decreased
792
Educational and Psychological Measurement 85(4)

gender differences in numeracy skills by analyzing the PIAAC 2012 numeracy
assessment. Contrary to other research that empowering women’s education was ben-
eficial to decrease gender difference, her findings indicated that women’s advance-
ment in education did not reduce gender differences in numeracy. The unexpected
conclusions could result from the presence of gender DIF items, which underesti-
mated women’s ability due to the less comparable test scores. Therefore, we used
gender DIF in the PIAAC numeracy assessment as an example to show a way of fur-
ther interpreting DIF items.
The MH method was applied to detect gender DIF among the nine items in the
numeracy assessment. We conducted the MH DIF method with iterative purification,
as it can achieve higher accuracy in DIF detection than the MH method with two-
step purification or no purification (Fidalgo et al., 2000). The detected DIF item was
labeled as 1, while the others were marked as 0, indicating that they were DIF-free
items. This process was executed using the ‘‘difR’’ package (Magis et al., 2010) in R
(version 4.2.2). We ultimately detected three out of nine items (33%) in the PIAAC
2012 numeracy assessment as DIF items, after applying the DIF detection procedure
to each of the 10 imputed datasets and labeling an item as DIF if it was detected in
more than 5 datasets. For more details about DIF item detection outcomes in each
imputed dataset, refer to Supplemental Appendix A. This DIF label criterion consid-
ers the stability and consistency of the detected DIF across the 10 imputed datasets.
Extracting Response Process Data Features. To obtain valuable information from
response process data, we extracted 11 features, including timing features, action-
sequence features, and item response scores, which aligned with prior literature high-
lighted that they provided information to elucidate individual’s abilities and evaluate
item fairness (e.g., Ercikan et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2016). Seven timing features were
extracted from the response process data, recording the time each examinee spent
within an item response sequence, which were (a) the RT for an item, (b) the timing
of the initial action in response to an item (termed ‘‘FIRST_TIMING’’ in the follow-
ing), (c) the total time length on the BUTTON action within an item, (d) the total time
length on the DOACTION action within an item, (e) the total time length on the
NEXT-BUTTON action within an item, (f) the total time length on the NEXT-
INQUIRY action within an item, and (g) the time length on CALLED ITEM-ENTRY
LOADING action. These timing features were computed based on the timestamps that
associated with each action in the response process data file. Three action-sequence
features were engineered by SNA for each item, which were (a) the betweenness cen-
trality, (b) network density, and (c) the number of nodes. SNA was conducted with
the ‘‘NetworkX’’ package (Hagberg et al., 2008) in Python (version 3.9).
Obtaining Background Variables. To account for potential confounding effects on
examinees’ response behaviors and scores, we included individual background vari-
ables as confounders. In the PIAAC 2012 numeracy assessment, item responses cor-
related with background variables, such as the match between the test language and
Li et al.
793

examinees’ native language and their highest level of formal education (Liao et al.,
2019; Wang et al., 2023). Liao et al. (2019) found that these background variables
distinguished differences in response behaviors between groups in the PIAAC 2012
data. Considering these confounders is crucial, as they may bias DIF detection results.
Given that the MH DIF technique relies on item response scores, we selected these
background variables based on prior studies (Liao et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023) and
incorporated them into the RF and LR modeling procedure. This allowed us to exam-
ine whether these confounders were strongly correlated with the detected DIF items.
If the confounders are found to be strongly associated with DIF, they should be
accounted for in the interpretation of DIF items. The details of the 17 background
variables are in Supplemental Appendix B.
Classifier Modeling: Investigating Association and Feature Selection. We hypothesized that
DIF items are associated with response process data features. This hypothesis is
grounded in the low agreement on DIF items between content expert reviews and sta-
tistical DIF outcomes (e.g., Cheng et al., 2020; Ercikan et al., 2004; Shermis et al.,
2017). Content experts may not consider the subgroup response behavior differences
that impact examinee response scores, particularly in developing computer-based
assessments. When features are not important, or their associations with DIF items
tend to be zero, these features are not correlated with DIF items, and vice versa.
Furthermore, when timing or action features associated with DIF items are more
important than response scores, it indicates that these features provide more valuable
information than response scores alone. These features can then be used to compare
subgroup response behavior differences, thus enhancing the understanding and inter-
pretation of DIF.
We applied RF and LR methods to explore the associations between the extracted
features and the DIF items using the ‘‘scikit-learn’’ package (Pedregosa et al., 2011)
in Python (version 3.9). The classification procedure contains two steps. First, the
data were divided into training and testing sets at a size ratio of 5:5. Specifically, we
used the stratified sampling method to ensure a consistent ratio of label classes (i.e., 0
= DIF-free items, 1 = DIF items) in both training and test datasets. Moreover, we used
the ‘‘SMOTE’’ package (Chawla et al., 2002) in Python to oversample the minority
class of the outcome labels (i.e., three DIF items vs six DIF-free items) to balance the
class sizes for the training data.
Second, we executed 10-fold cross-validation with the training data to locate the
optimal model. The recommended number of folds for cross-validation is typically
between 5 and 10 (Couronne´ et al., 2018; Probst et al., 2019). Given no consensus
on the superior performance of a specific number of folds for modeling data from
computer-based large-scale assessments, we chose to use 10-fold cross-validation.
This decision was made because our sample size was sufficiently large, ensuring that
each fold contained a minimum of 463 observations. Moreover, the optimal model,
characterized by a set of tuning parameters, was determined based on achieving the
smallest cross-validation error. For RF modeling, we experimented with two levels

of tuning for the number of trees: 500 and 600. In addition, we explored two levels
of the maximum depth of the trees: six, or with no imposed limitations. The LR was
conducted with the ridge regularization by tuning the penalized parameter in the
range of 0.01 to 10.
Investigating the Impact of Various Percentages of DIF Items on Classifiers’ Performance. To
evaluate the variability in classifier performance and ensure that the classifiers are
not overly sensitive to a specific DIF percentage, we evaluated whether their perfor-
mance was influenced by different percentages of DIF items. In practice, the percent-
age of DIF items can vary across different samples and empirical data.
We created a range of DIF percentages, from 0.14 to 0.75, by randomly sampling
different numbers of detected DIF and DIF-free items from the same dataset
described in the ‘‘Detecting Gender DIF by MH’’ section. This DIF condition design
follows previous research on traditional MH DIF simulation conditions (Magis & De
Boeck, 2012). The same classifier modeling procedure described in the ‘‘Classifier
Modeling: Investigating Association and Feature Selection’’ section was then applied
to evaluate the performance. For each condition, we conducted 100 iterations, where
the DIF and DIF-free items were randomly sampled without replacement from the
dataset. Finally, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess whether the clas-
sifiers’ performance metrics related to the percentage of DIF items. The ANOVA
models were conducted using Python (version 3.9).

Discussion
The purpose of this study was to propose a procedure for using response process data
to enhance the interpretation of DIF items. It is important to note that this method
examines correlational relationships, aiming to provide insights into why an item is
detected as exhibiting DIF. We do not claim that response behaviors are the causes of
DIF, but rather that they offer valuable context for understanding the factors associ-
ated with DIF detection. This method was illustrated by using data from the PIAAC
2012 numeracy assessment. We demonstrated the procedure for extracting response
process data features, including timing features, action-sequence features, response
scores, and background features. Then, we evaluated the variability of classifier

Supervised Classifier Selection: Modeling Association Between Response
Process Data Features and DIF
Our results indicated that RF and LR were comparable modeling capabilities in this
study, even with varying percentages of DIF items. Although prior studies (e.g., Qiao
& Jiao, 2018) have compared the performance among some ML algorithms (i.e., RF)
in analyzing response process data, to our knowledge, this study is the first study to
evaluate algorithms’ performance in modeling multiple response process data fea-
tures and DIF items for further interpreting DIF. The stable and comparable perfor-
mance of RF and LR across various percentages of DIF items shows the feasibility of
using a single classifier when modeling the association between response process data
features and DIF items. However, we recommend selecting both RF and LR in mod-
eling as the RF can provide information about the important features while the LR
describes the magnitudes of association. The agreement on RF and LR outcomes sup-
ports a more comprehensive understanding of the roles of important features in DIF
items.
Moreover, although we eventually reported the performance of RF and LR, we
also evaluated two other commonly used classifiers, K nearest neighbors (KNN) and
support vector machine (SVM). However, neither KNN nor SVM outperformed RF
and LR in terms of the five evaluation metrics. Additional performance details for
the SVM and KNN models can be found in Supplemental Appendix C. SVM even
required additional computational time when processing the PIAAC 2012 numeracy
assessment. Given the poor performance and large computational cost in KNN and
SVM, we recommend RF and LR in modeling the association between process data
features and DIF items for further interpretation of DIF.
Process Data Feature Selection and DIF Interpretation With Important
Features
The results demonstrated that the identified important features, which extend beyond
response scores, provide valuable information in interpreting DIF. Specifically, by
comparing subgroup behavior differences on DIF items using these identified impor-
tant features, the empirical data revealed that women with high ability responded to
gender DIF items more efficiently and with greater exploration than men of the same
ability level. This finding contributes additional evidence regarding the role of
response behaviors in DIF items, helping to reduce those misinterpretation of DIF
due to linking the detected DIF to the item content (e.g., Shermis et al., 2017).
Moreover, this finding offers a plausible explanation for the less than 50% agree-
ment on DIF outcomes between content expert reviews and statistical DIF outcomes
reported in prior studies (e.g., Cheng et al., 2020; Ercikan et al., 2004). For instance,
Li et al.
805

in international assessments, language DIF items are detected in statistical analyses,
whereas the items are not flagged as DIF by content experts. In our empirical study,
the identified features interpreted the gender DIF items with a consideration of
response efficiency and exploration preference. Response efficiency and exploration
preference are not the measured construct in the PIAAC numeracy assessment,
whereas response efficiency and exploration preference may impact examinees’
response scores due to time constraints during the assessment. These differences
reduce the comparability of test scores between subgroups, further underscoring the
importance of these features in DIF interpretation, especially in the case that response
factors, such as response efficiency and response exploration, are omitted by test
designers.
In addition, the identified important features can help evaluate DIF items that
manifest within specific ability ranges. For instance, our empirical study suggests
that gender DIF primarily occurs among high-ability women and men, rather than
across all ability levels. These findings are consistent with previous research which
demonstrates that response process data can provide valuable insights for interpreting
DIF items (e.g., Ercikan et al., 2020; Molenaar & Feskens, 2024). Furthermore, our
results reveal the specific ability levels at which DIF is most observable, whereas tra-
ditional DIF techniques typically aggregate ability across all levels in the sample.
These additional insights offer content experts a more nuanced understanding of the
factors contributing to statistical DIF outcomes, thereby improving their interpreta-
tion and evaluation of DIF.
This study also demonstrates that the combination of timing features and action-
sequence features is more informative than item response scores in interpreting DIF
items. Ercikan et al. (2020) suggested selecting the RT and the number of actions as
separate indicators to understand examinees’ response strategies and thereby evaluate
examinees’ engagement as evidence of DIF. Our study takes a further step toward
investigating examinees’ response strategies, engagement, and efficiency by involv-
ing more timing features and action-sequence features, such as action density and the
timing of each action. The results showed that the response time, the first timing of
response, action density, and the important action in one action sequence (between-
ness centrality) are beneficial to further understand and interpret DIF rather than
solely relying on one type of feature. This finding is consistent with prior studies,
which reveal the combination of response time and the number of actions is advanced
to examine examinees’ disengagement than the feature of response time (e.g., Kuang
& Sahin, 2023; Sahin & Colvin, 2020; Wise & Gao, 2017). The combination of
response time, the first timing of response, and action density demonstrate response
engagement and efficiency, explaining the additional reasons for probability differ-
ences of getting the item correct between groups in DIF interpretation beyond item
response scores. Moreover, the important action, which is derived from the action
with the highest frequency in connecting the other two actions (Scott, 2012), com-
bines with response time and action density, indicating the response process with

more or fewer exploration actions, consequently, providing another evidence to inter-
pret DIF.
The Impact of Percentages of DIF Items on Studying the Association Between
Process Data Features and DIF
Our results indicated that RF and LR performed with stable accuracy and AUC
scores across various percentages of DIF items in a test. Also, the accuracy, AUC,
and F1 scores from RF and LR were all higher than 0.7 when the percentage of DIF
was higher than 0.38. These findings illustrate the promise of applying RF and LR to
further understand and interpret the detected DIF by response process data, even if
researchers and practitioners obtain various percentages of DIF items, at least when
analyzing the PIAAC 2012 numeracy data.
Limitation and Future Research
The empirical study identified four response process data features (i.e., response den-
sities within an action sequence, the RT for an item, the timing of the initial action in
response to an item, and the important action that connects between actions for
exploring the answer to an item) to reveal subgroup behavior differences in response
efficiency and exploration, supporting DIF interpretation. However, these features
were derived from open-ended items in the PIAAC 2012 numeracy assessment,
which may not fully generalize to other assessments. We suggest future studies eval-
uate the use of process data features for DIF interpretation across different assess-
ment types and incorporate a broader range of response process features to validate
the wider applicability of our proposed method. It is important to note that student
response process features can vary depending on the assessment. Inadequate response
process features for an assessment may result in weak classifier models that fail to
support accurate DIF interpretation. An example is provided in Supplemental
Appendix D, which analyzes the PIAAC 2012 literacy assessment with inadequate
features. The response process features extracted from the selected-response item for-
mat may not fully capture crucial information about examinees’ literacy proficiencies
and response strategies. Future studies can explore a broader range of response pro-
cess features that provide valuable information for understanding DIF items, includ-
ing the features describing different item formats.
Another limitation of this study is its omission of item-level features, such as item
difficulty and item discrimination. Instead, we primarily focused on individual-level
features, such as response time, item response scores, and individual background
characteristics. If we incorporated item-level features into the classification proce-
dure, the item-level features would become the most important ones due to the small
number of items in the PIAAC 2012 numeracy assessment, consequently obscuring
information from individual-level features and constraining our ability to interpret
DIF items. However, item discrimination and item difficulty may help process data
Li et al.

features to interpret DIF when the number of items in the dataset is large enough.
Therefore, we recommend that future studies integrate item-level features for a more
comprehensive analysis of DIF items in assessments. This may involve leveraging
artificial intelligence (AI) technology, which is increasingly essential for ensuring
measurement fairness, as highlighted in recent literature (e.g., Huggins-Manley et al.,
2022).
Implications and Conclusions
In summary, this study proposes a method for further interpreting DIF items in
computer-based large-scale assessments by incorporating response process data.
Using the PIAAC 2012 numeracy assessment as an illustrative example, we demon-
strated how to implement this method. By using important response process features
that are more informative than response scores, we compared subgroup behavior dif-
ferences on gender DIF items to uncover underlying factors, such as response effi-
ciency and exploration, enhancing the understanding and interpretation of DIF.
In practice, if we use response process features to understand DIF in the way this
study demonstrated, one may be able to combine content expert review with response
process relationships to DIF to come to a more holistic discussion and evaluation of
DIF items. Specifically, when test designers and researchers overlook response strate-
gies (e.g., response efficiency and exploration) and response engagement that may
impact response scores, the proposed method offers valuable insights into subgroup
behavior differences in DIF reviews, helping to reduce inconsistencies between con-
tent expert reviews and statistical DIF results.
