Abstract
Are teachers and principals aligned in their perceptions of the core components of the
theory of Leadership for learning across countries, or are there subgroups of schools in
which there is misalignment? The purpose of this study is to examine the extent to which
a congruency-typology model of leadership for learning is distributed across
countries/economies using the TALIS 2018 dataset through examining the interaction of
significantly different subgroups of teacher and principal responders through using
multilevel latent class analysis (LCA) with a cross-level interaction. I analyse data from
lower secondary schools of n=152 635 teachers in 9 079 schools and their principals across
47 countries/economies. Currently in the research literature on school leadership,
leadership for learning has emerged as a framework to bring together managerial,
transformational, distributed, and instructional leadership. Yet little is known about
leadership for learning across national contexts. This study 1) maps the TALIS 2018 survey
items to the current literature and surveys for leadership for learning, 2) then details the
methods and analysis framework to examine if there are multiple significantly different
types of teachers, principals, and schools from a leadership for learning theory framework.
The final model 3) identifies a three-group teacher typology and a three-group principal
typology, linking these types to school context, covariates, as well as teacher and principal
training and experience. Results relate directly to the intersection of research, policy, and
practice for training and capacity of school leaders across 47 countries/economies globally.

Executive summary
School leadership is an important area of focus for researchers, policymakers, and
educators globally, as schools and schooling systems search for ways to help inform
instructional improvement and student achievement, improve teacher working conditions,
and help develop the teachers and leaders who can serve their local communities through
providing a strong educational context through their schools. Yet much remains to be
learned about how principals lead schools across different national contexts, how teachers
and principals agree or not on the central components of school leadership practice, and to
what extent educators perceive leadership in their schools as a single monolithic dimension,
or if there are significantly different types of school leadership, especially when
considering the extent to which teachers and principals are aligned (or not) in their
perceptions of leadership behaviours and frequency of activities. The purpose of this study
is to leverage the large Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018, which
includes responses of teachers and school leaders from across 47 countries/economies, to
examine the extent to which there are significantly different types of schools based on the
alignment or misalignment of teacher and principal perceptions around practices linked to
instructional leadership and leadership for learning theories: a congruency-typology model
of leadership for learning.
While multiple theories of school leadership and leadership styles abound (Hallinger,
2018[1]; Krüger and Scheerens, 2012[2]; Muijs et al., 2004[3]), instructional leadership has
become a central focus for school improvement across a wide variety of national contexts
in which school leadership is focused on 1) communicating goals, vision, and expectations;
2) coordinating and supervising curriculum and instruction; 3) overseeing, planning, and
participating in teacher professional development; 4) aligning resources to priorities, and
5) ensuring a supportive and orderly environment (Hallinger, 2011[4]; Robinson, Lloyd and
Rowe, 2008[5]). However, instructional leadership research has been critiqued as being
overly vague and lacking strong linkages to how to shift teacher and school leader practice
in ways known to improve instruction (Bush and Glover, 2014[6]; Neumerski, 2013[7];
Rigby, 2014[8]), as well as how to include teachers in the management and leadership of the
school, at times termed integrated leadership (Marks and Printy, 2003[9]; Printy, Marks and
Bowers, 2009[10]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]).
More recently, the concept of instructional leadership has been expanded to the theory of
leadership for learning to include human resource management and further adult
development and professional development components, as well as student and teacher
assessment and feedback (Boyce and Bowers, 2018[12]; Hallinger, 2011[4]; Hallinger and
Heck, 2010[13]; Murphy et al., 2007[14]; OECD, 2016[15]), resulting in a five domain model
of 1) focus on learning, 2) monitoring teaching and learning, 3) building nested learning
communities, 4) acquiring and allocating resources, and 5) maintaining a safe and effective
environment (Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]; Halverson, Kelley and Shaw, 2014[17]).
Recently, the OECD surveyed n=152 635 teachers in 9 079 lower secondary schools and
their principals across 47 countries/economies around these issues of school teacher and
leadership practice as part of the Teaching and Learning International Survey, TALIS 2018
(Ainley and Carstens, 2018[18]; OECD, 2019[19]). The goal of the present study is to leverage
this large global survey of teaching and leader practices to describe the extent to which
there are significantly different types of teacher and principal responders across the survey
in their perceptions of the practices around the five domains of leadership for learning in
their schools. Importantly, this study draws on a congruency-typology framework for

EDU/WKP(2020)7  7

school leadership for learning (Bowers et al., 2017[20]) to investigate the extent that
significantly different types of teacher responders across the survey are congruent or
incongruent with the perceptions of leadership for learning in their school. Schools in which
teachers and principals are aligned in their perceptions (either high or low) of leadership
for learning are postulated to be very different contexts for teacher and principal
professional development and instructional improvement in comparison to schools in
which teachers and principals are not aligned and disagree in their perceptions of leadership
for learning.
This study uses multilevel modelling to appropriately nest teacher responses within schools
and compare those responses to their principals, acknowledging the dependent nature of
the data, and uses recent innovations in mixture modelling to estimate a two-level latent
class analysis (LCA), estimating the extent to which there are multiple significantly
different types of teacher responders across the survey items (a typology) as well as
different types of principal responders. Importantly, the model includes a cross-level
interaction, such that I examine the extent to which the different types of schools are
identified through the proportions of the identified typology of teacher responders as well
as the typology of principal responders to items on TALIS 2018 that relate to leadership
for learning, providing an opportunity to examine the extent that teachers and principals
agree or disagree in their perceptions: a congruency-typology model of leadership for
learning. Using a reduced sample model of n=93,494 teachers across 5,831 schools (due to
missing data across eleven countries/economies), I also examine the relationship of a host
of teacher, principal, and school covariates in relation to the probability of a teacher or
principal being in one of the identified typologies.
In this study, I report three different types of findings: 1) a conceptual mapping of
leadership for learning to TALIS 2018 items; 2) the results of the two-level latent class
analysis congruency-typology model of leadership for learning, and 3) differences in the
model results across country/economy contexts. First, this study provides the first evidence
of a conceptual mapping of the components of the leadership for learning framework to the
TALIS 2018 items. Conducted by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD) since 2008, for issues concerning school leadership, TALIS has
historically been focused on theories of transformational and instructional leadership
(Ainley and Carstens, 2018[18]; OECD, 2009[21]; OECD, 2014[22]; OECD, 2016[15]).
However, recent school leadership surveys, especially in the US context, have begun to
examine principal and teacher practices through the lens of leadership for learning,
especially the recent survey the Comprehensive Assessment of Leadership for learning
(CALL) (Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]; Halverson, Kelley and Shaw, 2014[17]) using the
five domain model noted above. However, to date, no schools have reported results in
which teachers and principals provide data for both CALL and TALIS 2018. Thus, the first
outcome of the present study then is that I provide a detailed conceptual mapping of the
five CALL domains and all subdomains to specific TALIS 2018 survey items, to provide
a means to examine leadership for learning using TALIS 2018 items for teachers and
principals. This conceptual mapping resulted in 30 variables at the teacher level and
41 variables at the principal level. In this study I provide specifics on each of these items
and the evidence for the conceptual mapping to TALIS 2018 across the five domains of
leadership for learning from CALL.
Second, the results of the two-level latent class analysis congruency-typology model of
leadership for learning identified that there are at least three different types of teacher
responders at Level 1 (CW within), and three different types of principals/schools at
Level 2 (CB between). At the teacher level, the majority CW1 (52.4%) teachers were high
leadership for learning teachers, who reported high responses across the leadership for
learning domains. These teachers were more often female, held a Master’s degree, had the

Unclassified

highest job satisfaction, chose teaching as a first career choice, were more experienced, and
more often were full time employees. CW2, 19.9% of the teachers, as the smallest group
in the typology, reported a high focus on learning, a high climate of trust and community
engagement, and a high agreement on shared discipline expectations and climate in the
school, but reported low feedback on their teaching, low amounts of effective professional
development, and low collaboration on lessons. CW2 teachers had the lowest workload
stress, high job satisfaction, were the least experienced, and were the most likely to not be
full time employees. CW3 (27.7%) teachers reported moderate levels of feedback on their
teaching, with low climate of trust and community engagement, and the lowest stakeholder
engagement and shared discipline climate. CW3 teachers more often held Master’s degrees,
had higher workload stress, and lower job satisfaction.
At the principal/school level the three CB types included CB1 (37.8%) the high leadership
for learning schools. CB1 schools had a majority of CW1 high leadership for learning
teachers, with principals who generally had high perceptions of leadership for learning
across the domains, providing strong evidence for a level of congruency between the
teachers and principals in the CB1 school type. CB1 principals reported having some
teaching duties, were trained in instructional leadership, and reported higher levels on the
diversity beliefs and organisational innovativeness scales. CB1 leadership for learning
schools were less often private schools, had higher enrolments and lower migrant student
populations. CB2 (35.4%) schools had more even proportions of the three-teacher group
typology, and were typified with principals who reported more moderate perceptions across
the leadership for learning domains, with the lowest levels of teacher assessment and
feedback. CB2 principals held a PhD less often, had some teaching duties, were trained
more often in instructional leadership, and reported less training in school administration
and less autonomy in educational policies. CB2 schools had higher enrolment and higher
levels of students whose primary language differed from the instructional language. CB3
(27.0%) schools, as the smallest group in the typology, were typified as being the most
misaligned between teacher and principal responses, as 46.2% of the teachers in CB3
schools were the CW1 high leadership for learning teachers, yet principal responses were
low across multiple domains, and specifically around mentoring and induction with CB3
principals reporting that teacher mentoring and induction was low or not part of their
responsibilities in their school. Interestingly, CB3 principals provide a moderate level of
feedback to teachers, higher than CB2. CB3 principals had the least amount of teaching
duties, were less often trained in teacher education or instructional leadership, and reported
less autonomy in staffing, budgeting and instructional policy, as well as that a lack of
resources were more often a problem in their schools. CB3 principals are typified by noting
multiple challenges and issues in their schools and disagreeing that mentorship is a primary
responsibility of the principal.
Relying on these findings across the response patterns of the three-teacher typology and
the three principal/school typology, I provide evidence from the TALIS 2018 responses
that suggests expanding the number of leadership for learning domains from the five
domain model to an eight-domain model of 1) student assessment and feedback, 2) teacher
feedback, 3) teacher self-efficacy and a common set of beliefs about teaching and learning,
4) professional development and trust, 5) professional collaboration on lessons,
6) mentoring and induction, 7) stakeholder engagement, and 8) shared discipline climate. I
provide the individual TALIS 2018 items that correspond to each of these proposed eight
domains of leadership for learning.
And finally, the third main finding from this study are the proportions of the typology
across the 47 included countries/economies. Interestingly, a main finding of this study is
that while a large percentage of the research literature on instructional leadership and
leadership for learning comes from the US context, the results of this study indicate that

the United States has only two of the three types of schools identified here through the
congruency-typology model of leadership for learning. Replicating previous research
(Soncin, Bowers and Agasisti, 2018[23]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]), the majority of
schools in the United States TALIS 2018 sample are the high leadership for learning
schools, CB1, followed by CB2. Yet, the United States has virtually no CB3 schools. Thus,
a central finding of this study is that while such a large percentage of the school leadership
research is used globally as the foundation for instructional leadership and leadership for
learning theory, policy, principal training, and practice, and indeed for TALIS 2018, the
US context does not include one of the three types of schools identified here, CB3, which
is 27% of schools identified across 47 countries/economies. This finding replicates
previous research which has shown that there is disagreement across principals in some
countries on the extent that they agree with instructional leadership practices (Aas and
Brandmo, 2016[24]). Indeed, some of the central components of instructional leadership and
leadership for learning theories that concern mentoring do not acknowledge the contested
nature of the role of principal mentorship across different nations (Kemmis et al., 2014[25]),
as in countries such as Finland, mentorship is conceived more as teacher collaborative selfdevelopment than a role of school leadership. The findings of the present study mirror this
past research and provide a means through the congruency-typology model of leadership
for learning to identify these differences across the 47 countries/economies using
TALIS 2018.

Purpose and literature review

Description of research objectives
Are teachers and principals aligned in their perceptions of the core components of the
theory of leadership for learning across countries, or are there subgroups of schools in
which there is misalignment? The purpose of this research project is to examine the extent
to which a congruency-typology model of leadership for learning is distributed across
countries using the TALIS 2018 dataset through examining the interaction of significantly
different subgroups of teacher and principal responders through using multilevel latent
class analysis (LCA). Currently in the research literature on school leadership, leadership
for learning has emerged as a framework to bring together managerial, transformational,
and instructional leadership. Yet, little is known about leadership for learning across
national contexts. This study aims to 1) map the TALIS 2018 survey items to the current
literature and surveys for leadership for learning, 2) examine if there are multiple different
types of teachers, principals, and schools from a leadership for learning theory framework,
3) link these types to school context, equity, and teacher and principal training and
experience. Results relate directly to the intersection of research, policy, and practice for
training and capacity of school leaders in multiple contexts.

Introduction
Across the education research and practice literature, school leadership is continually noted
as one of the most influential school factors on student success, second only to quality
teachers and instruction (Hallinger, 2018[26]; Leithwood, Harris and Hopkins, 2008[27];
Waters, Robert Marzano and McNulty, 2003[28]). Yet, the question of how effective school
leadership operates in practice, its components and contexts, and how to train aspiring and
current school leaders in best practices, remains a highly active area of research and
development globally (Kovačević and Hallinger, 2019[29]; Muijs et al., 2004[3]; OECD,
2019[19]; Scheerens, 2012[30]; Wang, Bowers and Fikis, 2017[31]). Indeed, multiple theories
of school leadership action have been examined throughout the research, with some of the
most popular focusing on principals leading the core practices of instruction and developing
teacher instructional improvement practices in their schools (i.e. transformational and
instructional leadership theories), with many countries promoting leadership training and
professional development in these areas. However, there is not consensus on this issue.
As noted in a recent OECD (2019[19]) report on the TALIS 2018 dataset across
48 countries/economies, at the country level there are “different roles principals have in
different countries, whether they are pedagogical and administrative leaders or
administrative leaders only” (p.136). This question of the instructional leadership role
versus a strictly management role for effective school leader practice is also a recurring
question within the school leadership research literature (Grissom and Loeb, 2011[32];
Sebastian et al., 2019[33]) in which an ongoing debate continues as to the role in school
leadership practice of organisational management versus instructional-focused leadership
across specific contexts. In short, should a school leader focus on instruction, or focus on
the management of the organisation, or a combination of both?
In addition to these questions about the leader’s role, the teacher’s role in school leadership
and the overall instructional and professional development climate of a school is an active
area of research and development. Because schooling organisations are seen as fairly flat
hierarchically, leadership can be thought of as being stretched thinly across a school, and

EDU/WKP(2020)7  11

thus including the formal leader as well as the teachers (Diamond and Spillane, 2016[34];
Muijs and Harris, 2007[35]; Spillane, 2006[36]; Spillane, Halverson and Diamond, 2004[37]).
Thus, there are perennial questions from a policy perspective of how best to train school
leaders, which roles and actions are the most effective in which contexts, and how the work
of the school leader interacts with the teachers’ perceptions of leadership across a school.
Describing and understanding the different ways in which principals and teachers perceive
these practices across schools and global contexts as informed by current school leadership
theory can help to pinpoint areas of potential research, development, and practice.

Leadership for learning
Across the research and practice literature on school leadership, multiple theories have
emerged over the past 50 years to help describe and understand the multiple and often
conflicting demands on the work of school leaders, with four of the most popular being
transformational leadership, instructional leadership, integrated leadership, and leadership
for learning. Leadership for learning is a recently emerging domain of school leadership
theory that bridges between these other theories of transformational, instructional, and
integrated instructional theories of leadership (Boyce and Bowers, 2018[12]; Hallinger,
2011[4]; Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]; Murphy et al., 2007[14]). Transformational
leadership (Rost, 1993[38]) is a theory of a style of leadership applied to schools in which
school leaders focus the structures and mission of the organisation to encourage teachers
to build a community of practitioners in the school committed to engaging teachers in the
work of instructional improvement (Berkovich, 2016[39]; Leithwood and Jantzi, 1990[40];
Leithwood and Jantzi, 2006[41]; Printy, Marks and Bowers, 2009[10]; Thoonen et al.,
2011[42]). This in turn creates a culture and climate in the school of teacher collaboration,
dialogue, and improvement (Thoonen et al., 2011[42]). In contrast, instructional leadership
focuses on three aspects of the principalship of leading the vision and mission of the school,
managing curriculum and instruction, and creating a culture of instructional improvement
through providing resources and structuring the professional development of the school
(Hallinger, 2003[43]; Hallinger, 2005[44]; Hallinger and Murphy, 1985[45]; Neumerski,
2013[7]; Rigby, 2014[8]). As noted by Urick and Bowers (2014[11]) in reviewing the literature
on the differences between transformational and instructional leadership:
Transformational principals do not practice the guidance of curriculum and
instruction or the monitoring of student learning… transformational leaders spend
more time directly building community through support of the needs of teachers
and the community and through transfer of school goals to personal goals.
Instructional leaders build a positive climate through professional development
and coordination and attainment of instructional goals. However, unlike
transformational leaders, instructional leaders do not work to build a climate.
Instead, in instructional leadership, a positive climate is an indirect result of a
common and successful focus on instruction (p.101).
In a meta-analytic review of the effects of transformational versus instructional leadership
in schools across 27 studies, Robinson et al. (2008[5]) found that instructional leadership
practices significantly outperformed transformational leadership practices when examining
student and school test score outcomes (Robinson, Lloyd and Rowe, 2008[5]), with all but
two studies in the analysis focusing on the US context. Recent, as well as past instructional
leadership research has shown this mediating effect of the principal’s focus on instruction
on the academic and professional climate of the school through providing a strong set of
professional learning opportunities for teachers along with a clear vision and mission
(Bellibas and Liu, 2017[46]; Bellibas and Liu, 2018[47]; Boyce and Bowers, 2018[48]; Heck
and Hallinger, 2009[49]; Heck and Hallinger, 2014[50]; Heck, Larsen and Marcoulides,

1990[51]; Pietsch and Tulowitzki, 2017[52]; Price, 2012[53]; Sebastian and Allensworth,
2012[54]; Sebastian, Huang and Allensworth, 2017[55]) (Urick et al., 2018[56]; Urick and
Bowers, 2019[57]).
In contrast to the research comparing transformational and instructional leadership, the
theory of integrated instructional leadership postulates that leadership is multi-dimensional
rather than dichotomous, and so effective school leadership practice is a consideration of
the extent to which transformational and instructional leadership is practiced
simultaneously (Marks and Printy, 2003[9]; Printy, 2014[58]; Printy, Marks and Bowers,
2009[10]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]). Indeed, in their study of 24 restructured schools,
Marks and Printy (2003[9]) found that schools in their study with high instructional
leadership also had high transformational leadership, and that while they found schools
with high transformational leadership and low instructional leadership, they found no
schools with high instructional leadership and low transformational leadership. Thus, as a
core component of the integrated instructional leadership framework, Marks and Printy
(2003[9]) concluded that transformational leadership was necessary but insufficient for
instructional leadership (Printy, Marks and Bowers, 2009[10]). Accordingly, for the
integrated leadership framework, successful schools simultaneously practice
transformational leadership and instructional leadership. This finding was then more
recently replicated with the large nationally generalizable US Schools and Staffing Survey
(SASS) (Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]).
However, despite these positive findings across the literature in general, there is not
universal agreement around the concepts and practices of transformational, instructional,
or integrated leadership theories. For example, in surveying 149 Norwegian school leaders
across 24 items related to transformational and instructional leadership, Aas and Brandmo
(2016[24]) were unable to replicate the transformational, instructional, and integrated
leadership factor structure from US-focused studies, arguing that school leadership may
differ in Nordic countries in comparison to the United States. In a second example, in
referring to a specific issue within these school leadership theories, namely mentorship and
induction, Kemmis et al. (2014[25]) demonstrate how the concept of mentorship from the
school leaders’ perspective is a contested domain across national contexts. The authors
demonstrate how in three different national contexts, New South Wales in Australia,
Sweden, and Finland, the concept of the role of the leader in teacher mentorship is
fundamentally different, with mentorship in Australia as helping teachers move through
early teaching probation, traditional mentorship as support in Sweden, and mentorship that
is exclusively the domain of teachers across different schools in Finland as collaborative
peer-group development (Kemmis et al., 2014[25]).
At a conceptual level, both transformational leadership (Berkovich, 2016[39]) and
instructional leadership (Neumerski, 2013[7]; Rigby, 2014[8]) have been critiqued as rather
than being theories, that they are vague lists of behaviours that proponents encourage
schools to practice, with little evidence that specific components of the lists are related to
instructional improvement. Indeed, in one of the few randomised controlled experiments
in education leadership, Jacob et al. (2015[59]) tested the causal effect of providing the
Balanced Leadership professional development intervention in the United States , which is
based on the Waters, Marzano, and McNulty (2003[28]) framework. This framework of over
20 factors closely mirrors the lists of effective practices education leaders are encouraged
to engage in throughout their practice (Waters, Robert Marzano and McNulty, 2003[28]). In
the randomised controlled experiment of over 100 schools in the mid-west of the United
States, the authors found that principal perception of the efficacy of their leadership
increased due to the intervention, but that there was no effect on teacher perceptions or
student achievement, although they did find that principal and teacher turnover was lower
in the treatment schools (Jacob et al., 2015[59]).

A recent body of evidence has begun to suggest the important role of organisational
management in the principal’s work, in comparison to transformational and instructional
leadership. In an exploratory factor analysis study of principal surveys of leadership
practices, Grissom and Loeb (2011[32]) surveyed about 250 US school principals from the
state of Florida on 42 different items that included items related to instructional leadership,
organisational management, internal and external relations, and general school
administration (Grissom and Loeb, 2011[32]), finding that only organisational management
was connected to student achievement. When looking at principal time use in the same
United States Florida context, time spent on organisational management was related to
student achievement in comparison to instructional leadership activities (Horng, Klasik and
Loeb, 2010[60]). In discussing these findings, Grissom and Loeb (2011[32]) argue for a model
of school leadership more akin to Marks and Printy (2003[9]) in which they note that “we
suggest that a more holistic view of school leadership as necessitating skills across multiple
dimensions, in instruction but also in management of the school as an organization”
(p.1119), arguing that organisational management works in concert with instructional
leadership. Indeed, a recent study of about 260 principals from the city of Chicago in the
United States examined the extent to which principals practiced organisational
management practices versus instructional leadership, using a similar survey to Grissom
and Loeb (2011[32]) and latent class analysis (Sebastian et al., 2019[33]). The authors’ results
indicated evidence for a strong link between these two constructs, as while they found a
type of principal leadership with high perception of both organisational management and
instructional leadership, as well as a type with low in both, they did not identify principals
who were high in one and low in the other (Sebastian et al., 2019[33]). As stated by the
authors:
From the perspective of a practicing principal, the results of this study suggest that
if improvements in principal practice can be made to influence student
achievement, they need to be devoted to both instructional leadership and
organisational management. (p.15) (Sebastian et al., 2019[33])
Consequently, authors across the education administration research literature also point to
the managerial and human relations management domains as important for effective school
leadership practice, in addition to the work of transformational and instructional leadership
(Boyce and Bowers, 2018[12]; Grissom and Loeb, 2011[32]; Sebastian et al., 2019[33]). Recent
findings from a meta-narrative analytic review of 109 quantitative studies that used US
nationally generalizable SASS data provides evidence for the continued evolution of
integrated instructional leadership towards the concept of leadership for learning (Boyce
and Bowers, 2018[12]). In their study, Boyce and Bowers (2018[12]) found that effective
school leadership included the influence of the principal on individual teacher classroom,
school, and social motivations, but also included influence on leading teacher adult
development, shaping the school climate through professional development, as well as
leading the mission and vision of the school. The authors also note the equally important
influence of strong principal human resource management including maintaining high
teacher job satisfaction, teacher commitment, and teacher retention and hiring practices
(Boyce and Bowers, 2018[12]). In addition to these findings, a recurrent theme across the
instructional leadership literature is the influence of principals addressing the context of the
school (Edmonds, 1979[61]; Hallinger, 2011[4]; Hallinger, 2018[26]; Hallinger and Murphy,
1985[45]; Hallinger and Murphy, 1986[62]). For example, schools may “layer” their
leadership practice by focusing on different aspects of these leadership areas across
transformational and instructional leadership depending on the school context (Day, Gu
and Sammons, 2016[63]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]) or by focusing on resource allocation
to address specific needs (Urick, 2016[64]). Indeed, recent empirical analyses of large
numbers of articles and journals across the education leadership research literature have

shown not only the strong rise in these leadership topics over the past 30 years, but also the
connected aspects of the multiple dimensions of school leadership, as multiple authors
attempt to integrate and connect across each of these theoretical domains (Hallinger &
Kovačević, 2019 in press; Kovačević & Hallinger, 2019 in press; Oplatka, 2009; Wang,
2018; Wang & Bowers, 2016; Wang et al., 2017).
Thus, as noted by Boyce and Bowers (2018[12]), the literature on instructional leadership
continues to highlight these additional aspects of school leadership as it evolves towards
what has been termed “leadership for learning” (Hallinger, 2011[4]; Hallinger and Heck,
2010[13]; Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]; Murphy et al., 2007[14]). As noted by Hallinger
(Hallinger, 2011[4]): “While the term ‘instructional leadership’ originally focused on the
role of the principal, ‘leadership for learning’ suggests a broader conceptualization that
incorporates both a wider range of leadership sources as well as additional foci for action”
(p.126). In these additional areas, the principal works collaboratively across the school to
include teachers, parents, and the community. In the leadership for learning research
domain Hallinger (2011[4]) nominated five leadership areas based on Robinson et al.’s
(2008[5]) findings of establishing goals and expectations, strategic resourcing, planning
coordinating and evaluating teaching and the curriculum, promoting and taking part in
teacher learning, and ensuring an orderly and supportive environment. Murphy et al.
(2007[14]) proposed eight areas of leadership focus in leadership for learning, including
vision for learning, instructional programme, curricular programme, assessment
programme, communities of learning, resource acquisitions and use, organisational culture,
and social advocacy. Taking an empirical perspective, Boyce and Bowers (2018[48]) used
multilevel factor analysis and the 2011-2012 US nationally generalizable Schools and
Staffing Survey (including 34 850 teachers across 7 070 schools) to investigate the extent
that teacher factors relate to leadership for learning, and identified nine domains including
six domains at the individual level including classroom control, teacher commitment,
school influence, collegial climate, student attendance, neighbourhood context, and three
domains at the group or organisational level of instructional leadership, management, and
social environment (Boyce and Bowers, 2018[48]). Interestingly, recent work examining
principal perceptions of their work from n=260 principals in Chicago public schools in the
United States found that instructional leadership and school organisational management
were important and highly related aspects of effective leadership, including school
discipline and safety, budgeting, hiring, and managing staff (Sebastian et al., 2019[33]). This
finding provides additional empirical evidence for the above inclusion of human resources,
school resource allocation, and school safety and discipline domains within broader
conceptions of leadership, such as leadership for learning.
Recently, in working to measure leadership for learning directly, a team at the University
of Wisconsin-Madison in the United States has developed, tested and validated the
Comprehensive Assessment of Leadership for learning (CALL) survey (Blitz, Salisbury
and Kelley, 2014[65]; Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]; Halverson, Kelley and Shaw, 2014[17];
Kelley et al., 2012[66]). Synthesising the research across these previous studies, CALL
includes over 200 questions clustered into five main domains of leadership for learning
each with three to four subdomains including: 1) focus on learning, 2) monitoring teaching
and learning, 3) building nested learning communities, 4) acquiring and allocating
resources, and 5) maintaining a safe and effective learning environment (Halverson and
Kelley, 2017[16]). Thus, in the current literature, leadership for learning includes such areas
as promoting the vision and academic climate of the school, motivating and engaging
teachers in collaborative practice, and managing the curriculum. However, leadership for
learning also includes intentional focus on communities, resources, and safety and
discipline. As leadership in schools is an interaction between teachers and principals
(Bellibas and Liu, 2017[46]; Boyce and Bowers, 2018[48]; Hallinger and Heck, 2011[67];

of practices that happen daily in schools (Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]).

The issue of alignment of questions between teachers and principals on TALIS was recently
examined using TALIS 2008 and multilevel factor analysis (Urick and Bowers, 2019[57]).
Urick and Bowers (2019[57]) analysed data from n=59 152 teachers across n=3 788 schools
in 24 countries. They examined items related to instructional leadership, with a focus on
the extent to which teachers and principals across national contexts perceive of instructional
leadership in their schools. At the principal level the authors replicated the OECD factor
structure in which principals saw instructional leadership as three factors of communicating
school goals, promoting professional development, and supervision of instruction.
However, at the teacher level, teachers perceived of school leadership as a single
dimension. In summarising their study, Urick and Bowers (2019[57]) noted that:
First, teachers view instructional leadership as one main task. There is little
variation in how teachers respond across these items... Teachers perceive all of
these items as one main principal behaviour, whereas principals saw instructional
leadership as three main tasks: goals, professional development, and supervision
of instruction. Second, because of this difference in perceptions, when both
principals and teachers are included in a multilevel model of instructional
leadership, there are two main factors at the school level, principal perception
versus teacher perception. Even though teachers were asked some of the exact same

survey questions about instructional leadership as the principals, these items did
not load together at the school level, which suggests that principals and teachers
view them differently. (pp. 18-19)

across the literature and had urged for research designs to take into account not only the
multilevel structure of the data, but also the perceptions of both teachers and principals
(Leithwood and Jantzi, 2008[68]; Urick, 2016[64]; Urick, 2016[76]; Urick and Bowers,
2011[77]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[69]). Thus, both teacher and principal perceptions of
school leadership are important to consider when examining school leadership domains.

A congruency-typology framework model of school leadership
Thus, over the last 30 years, education administration and leadership research has
progressively built a research literature base across these multiple different styles of school
leadership, leading from transactional and transformational leadership, to instructional
leadership, and most recently leadership for learning (Boyce and Bowers, 2018[12]; Printy,
Marks and Bowers, 2009[10]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]). However, recent research has
begun to question the prevalence of these leadership styles as separate constructs, and
instead has postulated a typology theory of school leadership, in which there are
significantly different types of school leaders who practice different levels of
interdependent leadership styles (Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]). For instance, as noted above,
Marks and Printy (2003[9]) first provided evidence for the integrated leadership model, in
which transformational leadership may be necessary but insufficient for shared
instructional leadership (Marks and Printy, 2003[9]; Printy, Marks and Bowers, 2009[10]).
This finding was then replicated by Urick and Bowers (2014[11]) using the large nationally
generalizable US SASS sample (n=7,650 schools). In their study, Urick and Bowers
(2014[11]) postulated a typology theory of school leadership, in which they used latent class
analysis to identify the extent to which there were statistically significantly different types
of school leaders across the SASS data, finding evidence of a three-group typology,
Balkanising (22%), Controlling (24%), and integrated (54%) each of which included some
level of transformational and shared instructional leadership. Importantly, these types were
distributed first along transformational leadership and then shared instructional leadership,
replicating Marks and Printy (2003[9]), with the majority integrated leadership type having
the highest perceptions of both transformational and shared instructional leadership.
Additionally, as covariates can be modelled in LCA on the probability of a principal being
classified into one of the three groups, the authors showed that in comparison to the
Integrating leadership type, rural and small enrolment schools were more likely to have a
Balkanising principal, while both Balkanising and Controlling types were less likely to be
female principals, and have the school meet state or district accountability performance
goals. Urick (2016[64]) then followed up this work with a two-level latent class analysis of
the SASS data (n=35,560 teachers across 7,310 schools) finding that teacher turnover was
lowest for the integrated leadership type in comparison to the rest of the typology.
Thus, this literature provides a strong initial evidence base for a typology theory of school
leadership. Indeed, over the past decade there have been multiple school leadership
typology studies. One of these studies used cluster analysis to cluster principals based on
their time use (Goldring et al., 2008[78]) finding Eclectic leaders who spent time across
many activities in the school fairly evenly, instructional leaders who spent a large amount
of time in classrooms, and student-focused leaders who focused on student affairs. Also
using cluster analysis, with a focus on Nordic countries, Ólafsson (2016[79]) examined the

38a-f) focusing on “leadership practices, attitudes to power sharing, views on certain
potentially limiting factors to the principal’s effectiveness, actions following teacher
appraisal and attitudes to mentoring” (p 69) (Ólafsson, 2016[79]). Ólafsson found four

with a high discipline and reporting focus, 3) moderate instructional and collaborative
focus, and 4) focus on teachers’ responsibility and consequences for student outcomes.
However, as it is a descriptive technique, a central critique of cluster analysis is that there
is no agreed upon means to estimate statistically significantly different clusters (Bowers,
2010[80]; Romesburg, 1984[81]; Vermunt and Magidson, 2002[82]). To address this issue,
recent research using inferential statistics has begun to use the emerging domain of mixture
models in leadership typology studies.
In research that has thus focused on what has been termed “person-centred analysis”
(Collins and Lanza, 2010[83]), research has demonstrated that mixture models, and
specifically latent class analysis (LCA) models provide a strong means to estimate the
extent that there may be one or more than one significantly different subgroup of responders
across a survey dataset (Jung and Wickrama, 2008[84]; Masyn, 2013[85]; Muthén, 2004[86];
Samuelsen and Raczynski, 2013[87]). In education leadership research, the Urick and
Bowers (2014[11]) study noted above used LCA with nationally generalizable US data to
identify three significantly different types of principals. Using LCA and survey responses
of principals across Italy (n=1073), recent research (Agasisti, Bowers and Soncin, 2019[88])
identified three different types of principal responders, 1) educative leaders (49%) who
focus on developing teachers’ instructional skills, 2) leaders who teach (35%) who report
leading their schools but also serve in instructional roles in classrooms with students, and
3) transactional leaders (16%) who focus on the management of the school. The authors go
on to show that leaders who teach serve in lower performing Italian schools in the southern
region of Italy, which is correlated with higher levels of poverty.
Focusing on TALIS 2013 data, in a report titled “School Leadership for learning” (OECD,
2016[15]), the authors used LCA with data from principals of lower secondary schools across
36 countries/economies. The authors analysed TALIS 2013 principal survey responses in
four domains 1) the amount of decision making distributed to teachers, parents and students
(distributed leadership PDISLEADS scale), 2) facilitating co-operation among teachers,
ensuring teachers take responsibility for improving their teaching skills, and ensuring
teachers take responsibility for student learning outcomes (instructional leadership
PINSLEADS scale), 3) time spent on curriculum and teaching-related tasks and meetings
(TC2G19B), and 4) engagement with developing educational goals, programs and a
professional plan for the school (TC2G20A, TC2G20B). The authors identified a fourgroup typology across instructional and distributed leadership with a general pattern of
high-high (50%), high-low (17%), low-high (23%), and low-low (10%). The four-group
typology included integrated leadership (50%) with a strong focus on instructional and
distributed leadership, inclusive leadership (17%) with a strong focus on distributed
leadership but a weak focus on instructional leadership, educational leadership (23%) with
a strong focus on instructional leadership but a weak focus on distributed leadership, and
administrative leadership (10%) with a low focus on both instructional and distributed
leadership (OECD, 2016[15]). Additionally, at the national/country level, the authors of the
report found three groups of countries, the largest group were countries with mainly
integrated leaders, next were countries with mainly inclusive leaders, and the smallest
group included countries with mainly educational leaders. Interestingly, the smallest
country group of educational leaders includes Italy, which corresponds with the recent
Italian leadership LCA noted above which also found a high level of principals who either
teach directly or do not focus on instructional or distributed leadership to a high degree

across the majority of this work, the focus has been on principal perception of their own
leadership. To address this issue, recent research has called on researchers to model the
complexity of leadership in schools through using nested complex datasets in which teacher
perceptions of leadership are combined and modelled with the principal perceptions using
hierarchical modelling, nesting teachers within schools, along with including school
covariates and outcomes (Bowers and White, 2014[89]; Hallinger and Heck, 1996[90];
Hallinger and Heck, 2011[67]).
Thus, a core issue within the leadership typology research concerns the interaction of
teacher perceptions of school leadership with principal perceptions of leadership. As much
of the past research has either focused exclusively on teachers or principals (Leithwood
and Jantzi, 2008[68]; Urick, 2016[76]; Urick and Bowers, 2011[77]; Urick and Bowers,
2014[69]; Urick and Bowers, 2019[57]), there is a need to examine how teacher and principal
perceptions agree or disagree. Using the CALL survey validation dataset, researchers set
out to address these issues of the above school leadership typology research through using
a multilevel latent class analysis (LCA) framework (Bowers et al., 2017[20]). As noted
above, the CALL survey focuses on the core concepts of leadership for learning across five
main domains. The CALL validation dataset included n=3 367 teachers and 117 schools
and their principals in the United States. In their study, Bowers et al. (2017[20]) proposed a
“congruency-typology” model of school leadership, in which they proposed that there may
be a multiple group typology of teachers as well as school leaders (typology theory), and
that these two groups may agree or disagree in how they are aligned or misaligned across
the core aspects of leadership for learning (congruency theory). Thus, Bowers et al.
(2017[20]) postulated that there may be four types of leadership for learning schools as
measured through CALL, a low-low type in which teachers and principals agree that
leadership for learning practices do not occur often (congruency), a high-low type in which
teachers have high perceptions of leadership for learning but principals have low
perceptions (misalignment), a low-high type in which teachers are low but principals high
in their perceptions (misalignment), and a high-high type in which teachers and principal
agree that the practices happen often (congruency). Interestingly, (Bowers et al., 2017[20])
found a three-group typology of teachers, and a three-group typology of principals and their
schools, with evidence for the overall congruency-typology of only low-low, low-high, and
high-high. Missing was evidence of a school type in which teachers perceived high levels
of leadership for learning while principals perceived low levels. Additionally, while the
low-low schools employed more experienced teachers, they also had the least experienced
principals on average. Conversely, the high-high leadership for learning schools had the
least experienced teachers and most experienced leaders (Bowers et al., 2017[20]).

Framework and research questions
Thus, a congruency-typology model of leadership for learning using multilevel LCA
appears to be a productive means to address many of the critiques of the previous school

leadership research noted above. However, the only congruency-typology model of
leadership for learning to date used the validation sample of CALL (Bowers et al., 2017[20]).
While the validation sample for CALL provides a strong validity framework for the
leadership for learning constructs (Blitz, Salisbury and Kelley, 2014[65]; Halverson and
Kelley, 2017[16]; Kelley and Halverson, 2012[71]) it is not generalizable beyond the CALL
sample. Given the strong conceptual alignment between TALIS 2018 and CALL discussed
above in Tables 1 and 2 and Annex A, the TALIS 2018 dataset provides a unique
opportunity to test the congruency-typology model of leadership across a large transnationally generalizable sample of schools. Additionally, TALIS includes a range of
relevant covariates to include at the teacher and leader levels. Thus, using the leadership
for learning CALL conceptual mapping to TALIS 2018, the research questions for this
study include:
1. To what extent are there significantly different subgroups of teacher and leader
responses to TALIS 2018 (a typology of teachers and a typology of leaders)?
2. To what extent is the teacher typology and leader typology distributed across
schools and countries/economies?
3. To what extent is there alignment or misalignment between the teacher typology
and the leader typology when nesting teachers within schools?

Methods

Sample, data, and variables
This study is a secondary analysis of the TALIS 2018 dataset. As noted above, TALIS 2018
is a globally generalizable survey of the perceptions of teacher and school leader working
conditions, instructional and management priorities, and leadership across the participating
countries/economies. Building on the progress from TALIS 2008, and TALIS 2013,
TALIS 2018 includes a range of survey items for teachers and school leaders, with a focus
on lower secondary schools (ISCED 2) (Ainley and Carstens, 2018[18]; OECD, 2009[21];
OECD, 2014[22]; OECD, 2019[19]). The TALIS 2018 sampling framework surveyed about
20 teachers and the school leader in about 200 schools in each of the participating
countries/economies (Ainley and Carstens, 2018[18]). Focusing on the lower secondary
school sample (ISCED 2) for the present study, this equates to a teacher sample size of
about n= 152 635 teachers across 9 079 schools in 47 countries/economies (a slightly
reduced sample as the full TALIS 2018 dataset includes data from
48 countries/economies). This sample size represents the sample used for the majority of
the analyses in this study. However, due to missing data for the covariates in the covariate
analysis portion of the study, the final sample size for the covariate model described below
is n=93 494 teachers with n=5 931 Principals/Schools in 36 countries/economies.
Covariates are mainly missing across the eleven specific countries Argentina (Ciudad
Autónoma de Buenos Aires), Austria, Bulgaria, Spain, France, Israel, Italy, Netherlands,
Portugal, Sweden, and the United States, which are removed from the covariate analysis
using list-wise deletion. All remaining variables had low to no missing data (less than 5%),
and so following the recommendations from the analysis literature, I used FIML (Full
Information Maximum Likelihood) for data imputation in both the full model and the
covariate model. As TALIS 2018 is not a simple random sample, the below analysis
includes the teacher-level statistical weight TCHWGT and the school-level statistical
weight SCHWGT.
Drawing on the literature and the conceptual mapping of TALIS 2018 to the CALL survey
discussed above in Table 2 and Annex A, this study includes survey items that conceptually
align with the leadership for learning framework presented throughout this report. As noted
in Table 2, these items come from eight domains of TALIS, including teachers’
instructional practices, school leadership, teachers’ professional practices, teacher
education and initial preparation, teacher feedback and development, school climate,
teacher self-efficacy, and human resources. Importantly, as the model includes teachers and
principals, as noted in Table 2 from the conceptual mapping, teacher specific TALIS
domains include teachers’ instructional practices, teacher self-efficacy, and human
resources, while the principal specific domains include teacher education and initial
preparation. The remaining domains have at the minimum a partial conceptual alignment
with leadership for learning and similar survey questions at the teacher and principal levels,
as noted in Annex A.

Analysis
The purpose of this research study is to examine the extent to which a congruency-typology
model of leadership for learning is distributed across countries/economies using the
TALIS 2018 dataset through examining the interaction of significantly different subgroups
of teacher and principal responders using multilevel latent class analysis (LCA). Multilevel
LCA is an emerging analysis technique to examine not only the extent to which there is a
typology of responders across a survey, but also to appropriately nest the data within a
hierarchical model (Finch and French, 2014[91]; Henry and Muthén, 2010[92]; Park and Yu,
2018[93]; Vermunt, 2003[94]; Yu and Park, 2014[95]). A well-known issue in education
leadership research, given that teachers are nested within schools, this creates a dependency
structure in the data that can then be taken advantage of to examine the within-school
variance at the teacher level, between-school variance at the school level, and potential
cross-level interactions (Boyce and Bowers, 2018[12]; Hallinger and Heck, 1996[90];
Hallinger and Heck, 2011[67]; Hox, 2010[96]; Raudenbush and Bryk, 2001[97]). Multilevel
LCA extends the hierarchical framework to latent class analysis and mixture models,
through estimating a typology at Level 1 as well as a typology at Level 2, and allowing for
a cross-level interaction term that informs the Level 2 typology with the Level 1 types
(Henry and Muthén, 2010[92]). The analysis here is a non-parametric two-level random
effects latent class analysis. Multilevel LCA model estimation is a three-phase process
(Henry and Muthén, 2010[92]), and here I follow the recommendations from the previous
multilevel leadership for learning LCA from Bowers et al. (2017[20]) which also nested

Unclassified

EDU/WKP(2020)7  29

teachers within schools. In phase 1, a Level 1 only teacher within-school LCA model is
estimated.
In phase 1, only the top panel of Figure 1 (within school, teachers) model is estimated,
without considering the Level 2 structure. The enumeration model includes items from
each of the TALIS domains noted above. Following the recommendations of the LCA
literature (Masyn, 2013[85]; Nylund-Gibson and Masyn, 2016[98]; Nylund, Asparouhov and
Muthén, 2007[99]), modelling follows a single-level LCA process, of first fitting the
enumeration model by iteratively testing for the number of latent classes k, assessing model
fit, and interpreting the k-1 latent class model when the data no longer fits the model. Model
fit is assessed through the use of multiple fit statistics including the Lo-Mendel-Rubin
(LMR) test (Lo, Mendell and Rubin, 2001[100]), as well selecting the model with the lowest
Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Nylund-Gibson and Masyn, 2016[98]; Nylund,
Asparouhov and Muthén, 2007[99]; Park and Yu, 2018[93]; Vermunt, 2010[101]). The
identified Level 1 typology is used to inform the next phases of the analysis. Additionally,
the Level 1 LCA model may be of substantive interest on its own, and further modelling
using the three-step LCA could be of interest with possible distal outcomes (Bakk and
Vermunt, 2016[102]; Nylund-Gibson, Grimm and Masyn, 2019[103]). This process is then
repeated for the Level 2 data (Henry and Muthén, 2010[92]), here for principals/schools
(Figure 1, bottom half). The number of latent classes at Level 1 and Level 2 are then used
in the full multilevel model in phase 3. Covariates are included at this final step at phase 3.

Phase 3 of the multilevel LCA model is to estimate the full model shown in Figure 1. Here,
I have depicted the model with a three-group typology at Level 1 from phase 1, noted as
CW for latent classes C within. Note that the number of Level 1 latent classes is determined
as noted above from phase 1, and I use three here only as an example. Following the
recommendations from recent multilevel LCA research (Bowers et al., 2017[20]; Graves,
2019[104]), as show in Figure 1, the multilevel model includes three latent classes at Level 1
with a cross-level interaction in which teachers are nested within schools. The Level 2
between-school typology (CB) tests the extent that the Level 1 types are distributed evenly
across schools, or to what extent there are subgroups of schools (CB) at the Level 2
between-level as defined by different proportions of the Level 1 subgroups (Figure 1, filled
circles at Level 1 and CW#1 – CW#3 at Level 2) .
The full final multilevel LCA model (Figure 1) for phase 3 of the analysis nests teachers
within (CW) in schools (between or CB). Here, in the fully specified model, the
TALIS 2018 indicators noted above for teachers are specified as the indicators in the
Level 1 within enumeration, along with teacher demographic, experience, motivation, and
training covariates on the probability of being included into any one of the Level 1 latent
classes, here for example assumed to be three latent classes (CW). A cross-level interaction
is included in which the proportions of the Level 1 latent classes are indicators at Level 2
for the between part of the model (CB), in addition to principal Level 2 variables noted in
the conceptual framework for leadership for learning. Additionally, principal and school
covariates are included at Level 2 on the probability of a school being classified into one
of the three example between-level latent classes (CB). Thus, the estimation strategy is to
first estimate the teacher (Level 1 CW) only LCA model, then the principal/school (Level 2
CB) only LCA model. Once the number of latent classes at Level 1 and Level 2 are
identified, then I estimate the full two-level latent class analysis with the cross-level
interaction without covariates and report the results using the full TALIS 2018 sample.
Then, I estimate the full model with covariates using the reduced sample noted above given
missing covariate data across eleven countries/economies, and report the results.

In this results section I will first present the fit of the single-level models for Level 1
teachers, then Level 2, principals/schools in which, following the recommendations of the
literature, I fit iterative models with increasing latent classes to assess model fit. Once the
appropriate number of latent classes is determined at each level individually, I then present
the fit of the full two-level model without covariates, describe the teacher and principal
latent classes at levels 1 and 2, as well as the proportions of teacher latent classes across
Level 2 as the cross-level interaction. With the description of the latent classes at each level,
I will also present the findings on the covariates at each level. With the full model
described, I will then proceed to summarise the model indicators at Level 1 and 2, and
present the findings on the congruency-typology findings. Finally, I will then present the
findings on the cross-nation/economy analysis, describing the differences in the
congruency-typology model across the national contexts.
A three latent class model fit both the Level 1 teacher and Level 2 principal/school
individual level models well. As noted above, to determine the number of latent classes at
each level, following the recommendations of the literature (Collins and Lanza, 2010[83];
Nylund-Gibson, Grimm and Masyn, 2019[103]; Nylund-Gibson and Masyn, 2016[98];
Nylund, Asparouhov and Muthén, 2007[99]), I ran iterative sets of models of increasing
numbers of latent classes at each level, and once the model no longer fit the data,
interpreting the model with the best fit. TALIF or the single-level Level 1 teacher model,
Table 7 provides the fit statistics for the two class through nine class models. Up to nine
classes the lowest BIC was not determined. However, the three-class model had a
significant LMR p-value, while the four-class model did not. Thus, I decided to use the
more parsimonious three-class model. The model fit the data well with a -2 Log Likelihood
of -2220150.116, AIC 4440484.232, BIC 4441398.326, p<0.001, and an entropy of 0.831.

from two classes to eight latent classes, nor were any of the models significant by the LMR
criterion. However, entropy was high for all Level 2 models tested. While at three latent
classes the proportion of the classes was all over 10%, at four latent classes, a small
proportion class was identified at less than 3% of the sample. Additionally, model
identification for the four-class model was problematic as the indictor plots showed that for
this small fourth class the indicators were fixed to the threshold ceiling and floor values
(data not shown). As noted in the LCA literature, additional latent class proportions below
5% of the sample are an indication of a poor model fit. Thus, for model parsimony, I
decided on the three-class solution for Level 2. The three-class Level 2 principal/school
level model fit the data well with -2 log likelihood of -154029, AIC 308307.85, BIC
309194.41, and entropy of 0.901.

development (PD) and trust, 5) professional collaboration, 6) mentoring and induction,

For CB1 (Figure 9, left panel) the principal average responses are generally high across the
domains, either matching or are somewhat above CW1 responses. The exception is for the
two domains of stakeholder engagement and shared discipline climate. As noted in the
lower panel table in Figure 9, as well as Figure 6, in CB1 the majority of teachers are CW1
responder type teachers (62.6%). CW1 teachers are more often female, have a Master’s
degree, have higher work satisfaction, intentionally selected teaching as their career, and
are more often more experienced and full time employees (FTE). In CB1 schools, the
principals and teachers are generally aligned on average across the majority of the eight
domains proposed in the present study for leadership for learning. It is interesting however
that the principals and teachers on average are not aligned on stakeholder engagement and

Unclassified

EDU/WKP(2020)7  45

shared discipline climate, as the majority of teachers in the CB1 schools report a higher
perception on addressing these domains than the principal. At the covariate level CB1
schools are more often led by principals who have some teaching responsibility, have been
trained in instructional leadership and have higher diversity beliefs and organisational
innovativeness in schools that are less often private, more often high enrolment, and less
often serving significant migrant student populations.
CB2 schools are much more evenly split in the ratios of CW teachers. As demonstrated in
Figure 9 (middle panel), on average the principal perceptions of leadership for learning
across the eight-domain model lie between CW1 and CW2 teacher perceptions, with the
exception of mentoring and induction (principal perception far exceeds teachers), and are
aligned with CW3 teacher averages in stakeholder engagement and shared discipline
climate. Nevertheless, in CB2, 46.4% of the teachers are CW1 responders who report high
levels of teacher feedback, yet principal perception of teacher feedback in CB2 is more
closely aligned with CW3 teachers. Given that there is no majority CW teacher type in CB2
it is difficult to argue for alignment between principals and teachers in their perceptions of
leadership for learning in CB2 schools across the majority of domains. However, as the
leaders in CB2 lie between CW1 (46.4%) and CW2 (23.6%) teachers on the domains
teacher self-efficacy and a common set of beliefs about teaching and learning as well as
professional development and trust, and professional collaboration, this evidence suggests
alignment for these three domains in the congruency-typology of leadership for learning
framework for CB2 schools. Leaders in CB2 schools hold a PhD less often, have been
trained in instructional leadership, and report doing some teaching. However, these
principals report having been trained less often in school administration and having less
autonomy over educational policy. CB2 schools are more often higher enrolment and serve
learners whose first language is different from the instructional language.
In comparison to CB1 and CB2 schools, CB3 schools are the smallest percentage of schools
(27.0%). As with CB2 schools, CW1 teacher types are somewhat more evenly split than in
CB1 schools, as none are over 50% of teachers. However, CW1 teachers are 46.2% of
teachers in the CB3 schools, yet the principal responses across almost all of the leadership
for learning domains are lower than the CW1 teachers. CB3 schools are typified by having
principals who report either low levels of mentoring and induction or that these are not the
responsibilities of the principal. Interestingly principals in CB3 appear to align in their
perceptions with CW1 teachers on teacher self-efficacy and a common set of beliefs about
teaching and learning in the school, as well as professional collaboration on lessons.
However, principals are aligned with CW3 teachers on professional development and trust,
stakeholder engagement, and a shared discipline climate. CB3 schools have principals who
teach less often, and have less often been trained in teacher education and instructional
leadership. Principals report having less autonomy over staffing, budgeting, and
instructional policy, and they report that lack of resources is a problem. Overall, the CB3
school type is an intriguing result when considering the leadership for learning congruencytypology framework. The principals on average do not align with a specific teacher type,
and appear to perceive that the school may have many issues and challenges.
Thus, these findings lend supporting evidence for a congruency-typology model as
originally proposed by Bowers et al. (2017[20]), and here extended to the TALIS 2018 data.
The previous study found a congruency-typology of high agreement, low agreement, and
misalignment where principals had high perceptions of leadership for learning yet teachers
were moderate to low (Bowers et al., 2017[20]) using the validation sample for the CALL
survey in the United States. Here I find using the TALIS 2018 dataset that across
countries/economies a high alignment congruency type with principals agreeing with high
responder teachers (CB1), and two misalignment non-congruent school types, one with
principals perceiving moderate levels of leadership for learning (CB2), and the other (CB3)

with principals identifying challenges and needs that are quite different from the other two
school types. I will return to these results below in the discussion.

As a central finding of the present study, I argue here that while such a large amount of the
research that informs instructional leadership, leadership for learning, and the TALIS 2018

has only two of the three types of school leadership contexts globally as measured through
the two-level latent class analysis congruency-typology leadership for learning model. I
acknowledge that this model is a very specific analysis framework. Nevertheless, the US
results are striking, and suggest that the context from which such a large percentage of the
current theories in education leadership is based is missing one of the three types of school
leadership contexts. This leadership context that is missing, CB3, is typified by principals
who note the challenges and issues with their school, have generally lower perceptions of
leadership for learning, and see little role of the leader for mentoring and induction in
schools. The point that the US school leadership research is missing this important school
leadership context, which represents over a quarter of all schools globally (27.0%) who
participated in TALIS 2018, is a core finding of the present study, which I will return to
below. Interestingly, other countries/economies with this issue include Australia and
England, and to a lesser extent New Zealand for the majority English speaking countries,
but also Kazakhstan, Russia, Shanghai China, Singapore, and United Arab Emirates.
Returning to the research that examines the cross-national context in education leadership
noted above in the literature review, in Figure 11, the difference in the CB proportions
between United States and Norway are striking. While the United States has a majority of
schools in CB1 leadership for learning, Norway is almost evenly split between CB2 (low
feedback) and CB3 (low mentoring). As noted in the previous literature (Aas and Brandmo,
2016[24]), when attempting to fit an instructional leadership factor model to Norwegian
principal perceptions of leaders, Aas and Brandmo (2016[24]) were unable to fit such a
model. One interpretation of the Aas and Brandmo (2016[24]) results is that Norwegian
principals did not agree with the instructional leadership model from the US context. These
stark differences in CB proportions in Figure 11 lend further evidence to this finding. For
a second example, the difference between Australia, Sweden, and Finland is similarly
striking, as Australia has a majority of CB2 schools with a large percentage of CB1 schools,
while Sweden mostly has CB2 schools, while Finland has a majority of CB3 schools, the
low mentoring type. However, this is not unexpected given the results from Kemmis et al.
(2014[25]). In their study of the contested conceptions of mentoring across national contexts,
Kemmis et al. (2014[25]) demonstrated that Australian teacher mentoring has a strong role
for the principal and is focused on both support and evaluation, in Sweden mentoring has
a mix of support, evaluation, and collaborative self-development, while in Finland there is
little role for the principal as mentoring is the domain of a network of teachers as
professionals across the country as collaborative self-development. Here, Figure 11
provides evidence in support of these findings and importantly demonstrates that the
multilevel LCA congruency-typology model of leadership for learning using TALIS 2018
data can capture these differences across country/economy contexts. Importantly, as
Kemmis et al. (2014[25]) note that mentoring across global contexts in teaching is considered
a “contested” domain, Figure 11, as well as the CB figures (Figures 7 and 8) reflect this

Discussion

This report provides the results from a two-level latent class analysis model that tests a
congruency-typology model of leadership for learning with the TALIS 2018 dataset of
n=152 635 teachers, 9 079 principals/schools, and 47 countries/economies. Building on the
past research reviewed in the literature review above that maps instructional leadership and
leadership for learning frameworks to the TALIS 2018 items, with a focus on the five
CALL survey domains, I find evidence for a three-group typology of teachers (CW1 52.4%,
CW2 19.9%, CW3 27.7%), and a three-group typology of principals/schools, including a
cross-level interaction between the teacher and principal/school levels (CB1 37.6%
leadership for learning, CB2 35.4% low feedback, CB3 27.0% low mentoring). The
evidence in this report is correlational only, as is the vast majority of research in education
leadership and instructional leadership and leadership for learning specifically. Throughout
this study and this discussion below, I make no causal claims as each of the relationships
highlighted could be due to a variety of factors. The goal of this study is to surface and
highlight the relationships across the variables (both measured and latent in the model) as
they relate to the leadership for learning framework in an effort to understand and describe
the work globally around instructional leadership and leadership for learning as measured
through TALIS 2018 in participating countries/economies. As the model is fairly complex,
I encourage future research to first replicate the findings reported here, and then extend
them to further detail the framework and perceptions of leadership for learning globally.
The evidence from the indicator plots across these typologies at the teacher and leader
levels suggests moving from a five domain leadership for learning framework (Bowers
et al., 2017[20]; Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]; Halverson, Kelley and Shaw, 2014[17]) to
eight domains of 1) student assessment and feedback, 2) teacher assessment and feedback,
3) teacher self-efficacy and a common set of beliefs about learning, 4) professional
development and trust, 5) professional collaboration on lessons, 6) mentoring and
induction, 7) stakeholder engagement, and 8) a shared discipline climate. A reduced sample
covariate model suggests that CW1 (52.4%) teachers who respond with high perceptions
of leadership for learning are more often female, have Master’s degrees, higher job
satisfaction, are more experienced, and chose teaching as a career. CW2 (19.9%) teachers
who have high self-efficacy, shared beliefs about learning, professional development, trust,
engagement of stakeholders, and a shared discipline climate have the lowest stress as well
as high job satisfaction and are least experienced. CW3 (27.7%) who receive moderate
levels of feedback but have some of the lowest responses to leadership for learning have
Masters degrees more often than CW2 teachers, but higher workload stress, and lower job
satisfaction. At the principal/school level CB1 (37.8%) principals have the highest
perceptions of leadership for learning and the highest congruency with their teachers, as
CB1 schools have a majority of CW1 teachers. Principals in the CB1 leadership for learning
schools have some teaching responsibilities, are trained in instructional leadership, and
have high diversity beliefs and organisational innovativeness, with the schools being less
often private schools with higher enrolment and lower migrant populations. CB2 (35.4%)
principals have more moderate perceptions of leadership for learning with less congruency
with their teachers. CB2 principals less often have a PhD degree, and have less training in
school administration than CB3 principals. CB2 principals have received training in
instructional leadership and have some teaching responsibilities. CB2 principals report
more often having less autonomy over educational policies than CB3 principals. CB2
schools are somewhat higher enrolment and have higher levels of language learners that
differ from the instructional language. CB3 (27.0%) principals have the low perceptions of

52  EDU/WKP(2020)7
leadership for learning and are also less congruent with their teachers in comparison to CB1
schools. CB3 principals have the least teaching responsibilities, and are less often trained
in teacher education and instructional leadership, and report lower levels of autonomy in
staffing, budgeting, and instructional policy. CB3 principals also report more often that
lack of resources are a problem in their school.
A central finding of this study is that across the TALIS 2018 data, not only are the majority
of the teachers the CW1 type (52.4%) but that these teachers are more often in the CB1
schools, which are the highest proportion of schools (37.6%). The CB1 schools thus have
the highest alignment in leadership for learning given the high prevalence of CW1 teachers.
Combined with the covariate findings, this alignment, or “high-high” leadership for
learning school type, suggests that these schools have highly trained, autonomous,
experienced, and satisfied educational professionals that converge in similar notions of
school leadership. An intriguing finding is that the principals of these schools report having
been trained in instructional leadership more often, and have the highest diversity and
organisational innovativeness beliefs. As the analysis here is correlational only, one
interpretation may be that schools that have a high proportion of high leadership for
learning CW1 teachers are able to recruit or promote high leadership for learning principals.
On the other hand, it may be that high leadership for learning CB1 principals create a
schooling environment, which promotes and sustains the development of high CW1
leadership for learning teachers. Or perhaps across schools and countries/economies it is a
mix of these. However, it is striking that in examining the covariate results, as principals
and teachers report lower levels of education, lower autonomy, and less interaction in the
classroom there is an increase in CW2 (least experienced) and CW3 (least satisfied)
teachers, and the alignment between leaders and teacher perceptions of leadership for
learning decreases. Perhaps more experienced, satisfied, and well-trained professionals
seek each other out across schools. However, at the policy level, this finding is intriguing
as a means of examining possible directions for school improvement. These results suggest
that perhaps the CW1/CB1 high leadership for learning alignment school may provide an
interesting case of principal and teacher collaborative work, and so further study within
each national context of these schools could provide useful directions for school
improvement policy. Additionally, methods similar to the one used here could be used with
TALIS 2018 survey items to identify these different school leadership for learning contexts
within a nation or policy region. As noted in the previous research on using such methods
to informatively cluster schools for cross-school professional development (Bowers,
2015[108]; Bowers, Ni and Esswein, 2018[109]), school personnel may benefit from structured
professional development in which they are matched across schools either with similar
schools identified through these methods, or perhaps pairing strongly aligned schools with
those that wish to align more in leadership for learning.
While past research on transformational and instructional leadership has demonstrated a
strong three factor model to support instructional leadership theory (Hallinger, 2011[4];
Robinson, Lloyd and Rowe, 2008[5]; Urick and Bowers, 2019[57]), past research on the five
domains of leadership for learning has to date mostly been conceptual and theory focused
(Boyce and Bowers, 2018[12]; Halverson and Kelley, 2017[16]; Halverson, Kelley and Shaw,
2014[17]). In this study I first worked to conceptually map the TALIS 2018 survey to the
five CALL survey domains to provide a means to begin to understand leadership for
learning globally through TALIS 2018. Nevertheless, this mapping is conceptual only.
Additionally, as CALL survey items are focused on actions in schools, TALIS 2018 items
have a mix of questions that ask how frequently a task occurs along with perception and
dispositional items. As there are currently no datasets in which teachers and leaders
responded to both CALL and TALIS 2018, it is not possible to statistically estimate the
alignment of TALIS 2018 items to the five domains of leadership for learning. However, I

Unclassified

EDU/WKP(2020)7  53

argue here that this conceptual alignment provided in this study provides a unique
opportunity to leverage the large global sample of TALIS 2018 to begin to describe and
understand how teachers and school leaders perceive their schools through a leadership for
learning lens. As TALIS 2018 includes many questions beyond instructional leadership
that align with leadership for learning, including human resource questions, adult
development, and mentoring and induction, the present study is meant to be a first step in
working to understand how the different aspects of leadership for learning may be enacted
globally. Here, the results of the two-level LCA model suggest shifting from a five domain
model of leadership for learning, to an eight-domain model, as the three-group typologies
at the teacher and principal levels suggest that respondents may see three of the five original
domains as each being two separate domains. These include separating a) monitoring
teaching and learning to student assessment and feedback and teacher assessment and
feedback, b) focus on learning to teacher self-efficacy and a common set of beliefs about
teaching and learning as well as professional collaboration on lessons, and c) building
nested learning communities to professional development and trust as well as mentoring
and induction. Stakeholder engagement and a shared discipline climate for safe and
effective schools remain from the original five-domain model. Thus, this expanded
eight-domain model provides a means to identify the average differences across a complex
survey such as TALIS 2018. I encourage future research to investigate further to what
extent these proposed eight domains of leadership for learning are helpful in understanding
TALIS 2018 response patterns.
A central finding of this study is that the congruency-typology framework is a useful means
to capture the leadership climate across schools. This study builds upon the previous
congruency-typology model examining leadership for learning with the CALL data in the
United States (Bowers et al., 2017[20]), and demonstrates that the congruency-typology
framework is able to capture not only interesting differences within and between schools,
but also across countries/economies globally. Previous leadership typology research that
has used latent class analysis with US samples and TALIS 2013 has shown that the most
prevalent type of principal responder for instructional leadership is the responder type that
responds highly across both transformational and instructional leadership survey items
(Soncin, Bowers and Agasisti, 2018[23]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]). This result was
replicated here and extended to the leadership for learning framework as the results showed
that for the US context, the most prevalent responder is the high perception responder type,
CB1, here extending to the congruency-typology framework with the majority of the
teachers agreeing with the principal as CW1 teachers with high leadership for learning
perceptions.
Continuing with a focus on the US results, it is intriguing that although the research that
supports both theories of instructional leadership and leadership for learning, and the
TALIS 2018 items, depends to a large extent on research from the US context, the results
of this study suggest that the United States has only two of the three types globally of
leadership for learning schools. A caveat is of course that these three types of leadership
for learning schools are empirically defined through a very specific selection of variables
from TALIS 2018 and the two-level latent class analysis congruency-typology model.
Nevertheless, given the global conversation on both leadership for learning as well as
policy in many nations attempting to implement instructional leadership theories and ideas
(Aas and Paulsen, 2019[105]), this finding that the United States is missing one of the three
types of schools is intriguing. I will note, that I am not arguing here that the US research is
wrong, but rather that it may be incomplete, as US researchers have not had access in their
context to this third school type in the typology. Indeed, in the original study using the
CALL validation sample to survey leadership for learning in the United States and using a
two-level latent class analysis congruency-typology model (Bowers et al., 2017[20]), while

Unclassified

54  EDU/WKP(2020)7
a type of school in which the principal disagreed with teachers and had overall lower
perceptions of leadership for learning than teachers on average was hypothesised, the study
found no evidence for this congruency-typology type of school that would be similar to the
CB3 type here. Thus, we must assume that the US research and theories do not include this
possible third dimension of school leadership, as until the present more global OECD
datasets have become available, it has not been possible to identify this issue. The third
CB3 type here is a school in which principals and teachers do not agree on leadership for
learning, and one in which the school leader identifies many different issues across
leadership for learning as well as the covariates as challenges and issues for the school. The
point that this model with the TALIS 2018 data captures the current global research issue
that indicates that at the education policy level, mentorship by principals is “contested
practice” across multiple national contexts (Kemmis et al., 2014[25]) provides a means to
extend leadership for learning frameworks to include a wider global lens of schooling
practice that includes these types of differences across national contexts, as mentorship is
a key variable in CB3, and indeed throughout the leadership for learning framework.
A natural critique of course of these conclusions is that if the US school leadership research,
context, and theories have been used to such an extent to lay the foundation of the items in
TALIS 2018, and these theories were unaware of this third type of school leadership
context, then how could this third type of leadership context be detected using these survey
items and the leadership for learning framework? I argue that as the CB3 school type is in
many ways a type of responder in which the TALIS 2018 items may not be able to capture
their experiences, such as around mentoring, that the CB3 type perhaps is identifying
disagreement globally with instructional leadership and leadership for learning, and that it
is this disagreement that the model captures. Rather than indicating much about leadership
for learning practice in CB3 schools, an alternative interpretation of the results is that these
schools have a different set of theories of action from leadership for learning, and are
merely disagreeing that the issues that are surveyed in the TALIS 2018 items are
appropriate items for measuring what they do. This interpretation is supported by the strong
disagreement that CB3 principals have around the mentorship and induction items on
TALIS 2018. Thus, the CB3 type is capturing a type of leadership, but perhaps that type of
leadership is neither instructional leadership nor leadership for learning. It is unclear from
the results presented here to what extent any of the CB school-level types are related to
more or less successful schools, as a link to student achievement outcomes was not
available at the time of the analysis for this study. It may be that the CB3 school leadership
type is unrelated to student achievement, or is focused on management over instruction. I
encourage future research in this area.
Recent research using TALIS 2013 data and a single principal-level latent class analysis
(Soncin, Bowers and Agasisti, 2018[23]) supports the findings of the present study. In Soncin
et al. (2018[23]), the authors drew on the research on the integrated instructional leadership
literature discussed above in the literature review (Marks and Printy, 2003[9]; Printy, Marks
and Bowers, 2009[10]; Urick and Bowers, 2014[11]) comparing the responses of n=9,384
principals on TALIS 2013 for 32 countries/economies across items related to instructional
leadership versus transformational and distributed leadership (Soncin, Bowers and
Agasisti, 2018[23]). Relying on only the principal-level data, they find evidence with this
framework for a four-group typology of integrating leaders (64.9%), supportive leaders
(8.5%), participative leaders (22.2%), and contingent leaders (4.4%). These findings relate
to the findings of the present study in three main ways. First, the finding that a high
responder type is the majority corresponds to the previous research noted above in the
instructional leadership literature as well as in the present study. Second, the majority
integrating leader type, in which principal answers are near the top of the survey scales,
was the most prevalent leader type in the United States. And third, the participative leader

Unclassified

EDU/WKP(2020)7  55

type was the largest proportion of responders in Finland, Norway, and Japan, in which
leaders generally did not agree with items relating to supporting new teacher practices,
increasing responsibility towards teaching effectiveness, and increasing responsibility
towards student outcomes but had high perceptions of participation in the schooling
organisation by staff, parents, and students. Thus, similar to the results of the present study,
the TALIS 2013 principal LCA study (Soncin, Bowers and Agasisti, 2018[23]) also
identifies a large proportion of principals that disagree with the survey items around
mentorship and instructional leadership, and these principals are more often in countries
that for the present analysis are generally on the left side of the Figure 12 multi-dimensional
scaling plot of country/economy differences across the model.
Thus, together, this past research along with the present study suggests that there are
multiple countries/economies that disagree with a selection of items in TALIS, especially
as they relate to instructional leadership and leadership for learning. For countries that are
on the left side of Figure 12 here, and especially Finland, these findings suggest that these
countries provide a unique opportunity to understand different dimensions of school
leadership. Countries/economies with higher proportions of CB3 schools appear to have
school leadership climates in which the principal identifies a lack of practices around
instructional leadership and leadership for learning, as well as lack of autonomy around
school processes and a lack of resources. Combining this issue with the dearth of
instructional leadership and leadership for learning research from outside the United States,
England (United Kingdom) and other proximal countries to this cluster in Figure 12, a
central recommendation of the present study is to encourage researchers who are
constructing these survey items to work much more closely with countries which have a
high proportion of CB3 schools, as well as the countries in the other three quadrants of
Figure 12. The perspectives of the principals and teachers in these schools are a vital set of
information for how school leadership research, policy, and practice can understand the
many and varied ways that school leadership may or may not influence instructional
improvement that has historically been under-researched. Additionally, in constructing
items for these surveys, and future iterations of TALIS, the results of this study suggest that
researchers conduct cognitive interviews across national contexts, perhaps selecting a
sample of countries from each of the four quadrants in Figure 12, to ensure that the items
selected for global school leadership surveys capture these many and varied multiple
perspectives. These results also point to the need for much more in-depth qualitative
research that examines deeply the lived experiences and practices of school leaders and
teachers across the countries/economies in the different quadrants. In-depth comparison
case studies could radically improve and inform the research on the differences across
leadership contexts as suggested through the results presented here.
Moreover, Figure 12 provides an interesting opportunity for school leadership and policy
development for countries/economies within each quadrant. These countries share some
component of agreement from their teachers and principals across TALIS 2018 in the
congruency-typology model of leadership for learning. In the search for collaboration in
school improvement across national contexts, countries/economies near each other in
Figure 12 may find unexpected similarities across their contexts in the perceptions of their
teachers and leaders, and thus could create conversations that could surface important
issues that separate what is idiosyncratic across the contexts versus what could be
generalizable. This type of clustering school contexts for professional development and
dialogue through using these types of pattern analytic methods, such as typology analysis
of survey items or achievement trajectories, is an emerging area of research and
professional development practice (Bowers, 2015[108]; Bowers, Ni and Esswein, 2018[109]),
and I encourage future research in this area.

And finally, as with many of the global studies of education systems cited above, the
present study also identifies Finland as a unique context. This is especially striking given
the differences from the United States findings. Yet, while Finland has received
considerable attention in the media and research, much of that research has been focused
at the classroom level (Sahlberg, 2007[106]; 2011[107]). The results of the present study
suggest that the school leadership context and the congruency with teacher perceptions of
that context may be just as important, if not more so. Given these findings, I encourage
future research to include not only a focus on Finnish teachers and classrooms, but to also
deeply investigate Finnish school leadership and the interplay between teachers and
leaders, and how this differs across national contexts. The mentorship research that
involves researchers from Australia, Finland, and Sweden (Kemmis et al., 2014[25]) is an
excellent example of the types of collaborative research that can help to highlight these
differences and similarities with important implications for research, policy, and practice.

Limitations
While I argue here that the results of this study are robust, this study is limited in multiple
ways. First, there may be additional typology groups at both the Level 1 teacher level and
the Level 2 principal/school level. The research methods in mixture modelling and latent
class analysis is a rapidly evolving field (Nylund-Gibson, Grimm and Masyn, 2019[103]),
and the multilevel extension of these models has received much less attention than the
single-level model research methods to date (Park and Yu, 2018[93]). As the research
continues to advance in the multilevel LCA domain, I encourage future research to build
on the findings here, as there are most likely additional subgroups of both teachers and
principals/schools. Here the interpretation of the LCA results should be interpreted as that
there are at least three groups at Level 1 and at least three groups at Level 2. Second,
measurement invariance (Kim et al., 2016[110]; Kim and Wang, 2019[111]; Masyn, 2017[112];
Vandenberg and Lance, 2000[113]) is an issue widely acknowledged to impact any study
with OECD data that includes cross-country comparisons, especially as the issue of
measurement invariance applies not just to the individual items, but here also to the latent
typology variables. This issue arises when there is a cultural difference in how participants
respond, such as for students reporting mathematics confidence, where in the US they
would provide high confidence on average, in many Asian countries few students will
select the highest response on these types of survey questions (Kim and Wang, 2019[111]).
Thus, average differences across national contexts would be cultural and difficult to
compare. I acknowledge that the measurement invariance issue may be an issue in the
results of the present study. However, given how little is known across national contexts
about instructional leadership and leadership for learning, as discussed throughout this
study, I argue that even if measurement invariance is an issue, the differences across
country/economy contexts are still important to consider for theory, policy, and practice.
Currently the estimation of the level of measurement invariance across a two-level latent
class analysis with three groups at Level 1 across 30 variables, three groups at Level 2
across 41 variables, and across 47 countries is beyond the scope of the present study. The
research literature in this domain however is continuing to advance, and so I encourage
future research to investigate this issue.
Third, the model included neither a country/economy “third level” nor country fixed effects
in the covariate regressions as a well-known issue in multilevel models is that statistical
power is dominated by the size of the sample in the highest level, and so 47 is quite low.
This is compounded in mixture models as the number of parameters expands rapidly with
each class, and statistical power, even with large samples, is limited, and thus researchers
are urged to specify only the minimum most parsimonious model needed (Collins and

Unclassified

EDU/WKP(2020)7  57

Lanza, 2010[83]; Dziak, Lanza and Tan, 2014[114]). Additionally, model convergence of the
model specified here with over 150 000 teachers was computationally intensive given
current computer hardware limitations. Here, as one of the first studies to do so, the focus
of the present study was to examine the congruency-typology leadership for learning
framework across the entire TALIS 2018 dataset. I encourage future researchers to explore
the extent to which either a third level or an additional set of covariates, such as
country/economy fixed effects, could be included in the model, especially as model
estimation software and computer hardware continues to advance.
Fourth, one threat to the internal validity of the present study is the mixture of TALIS 2018
items and scales in the enumeration of the latent classes, as a small number of scales were
included to identify the latent classes. A well-known issue with scaled items that are factors
of combined individual items in OECD surveys is the issue of measurement invariance of
individual scales (Ainley and Carstens, 2018[18]; Van De Vijver et al., 2019[115]). As some
of these scales did not reach scalar invariance, one critique of the present study is that this
makes cross-country comparisons problematic. However, as country/economy was not
included in the model, this issue may be attenuated somewhat. Fifth, at the covariate level,
specific countries across the TALIS survey may have within-country system mandates that
require a specific response, such as mandating Masters degrees for principals. This could
create an additional threat to the internal validity of the study as this type of issue could
induce a system or country fixed effect into the covariate component of the model.
However, as the full specification of the model did not include covariates, as the covariate
model used the smaller sample (see methods), this issue is not applicable to the main
findings beyond a possible effect on the covariates themselves. I encourage future research
to consider this issue.
And finally, the present study does not include student achievement outcomes. It is of
interest to know which teacher types and which school types are related to higher student
achievement, such as on the PISA tests. At the time of this writing, a link between
TALIS 2018 and PISA results is not available. However, this type of link between the
datasets is planned by the OECD for a subset of countries/economies, and so I encourage
future research to investigate this issue.
