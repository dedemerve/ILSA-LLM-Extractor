Does ICT involvement really matter? An investigation
of Turkey’s case in PISA 2018
Erhan Ünal1

· Ahmet Murat Uzun2

· Selcan Kilis3

Received: 5 December 2021 / Accepted: 21 April 2022 / Published online: 11 May 2022
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Science+Business Media, LLC, part of Springer Nature

Abstract
This study aims to investigate how Turkish students’ involvement in information and
communication technologies (ICT) predicts their math and science performance in
the 2018 Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) test. The research
also tests demographic variables including socioeconomic status (SES) and gender as covariates. The data were examined through two-step hierarchical regression
analyses. Regarding demographics, SES revealed a significant positive contribution
to the prediction of math and science performance, whereas gender failed to make a
significant contribution. Additionally, after controlling demographics, ICT availability at home significantly and negatively predicted student math and science performance, whilst ICT availability at school was not found to be significantly contributory. Regarding ICT use variables, all constructs significantly predicted student math
and science performances, but some negatively contributed to the model, whereas
others positively contributed. Amongst ICT attitude variables, all constructs made
a significantly positive or negative contribution to predicting both math and science
performance, with one exception. Only perceived autonomy in ICT use failed to significantly contribute to the prediction of math performance. Based on the findings,
it was concluded that persistent involvement in ICT tends to be related to decreased
math and science performance. We propose that policymakers and practitioners
drop the myth that ICT use results in better achievement under any circumstances
and should therefore refrain from integrating ICT without first undertaking careful
planning.
Keywords Demographics · ICT · ICT availability · ICT attitude · ICT use · PISA

1 Introduction & Background
Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) play a significant role in education, and many countries have invested substantially in this area. For example,
Singapore’s Ministry of National Education initiated Master Plans for Information
Technology in Education (Looi & Hung, 2004), and South Korea’s Ministry of Education Science and Technology (2011) launched a SMART digital education initiative. In Turkey, the Ministry of National Education introduced different types of bigbudget technology integration projects including the Basic Education Project and
the Movement for Enhancing Opportunities and Improving Technology, for which
the latter is more commonly known by its Turkish acronym, the “FATIH” Project
(Gök, 2014; Topuz & Göktaş, 2015).
The use of ICTs has not only increased at the K-12 school level based on country-specific projects, but has also extended globally on an individual user basis, as
technological devices have become considerably less costly and more widely available compared to even the recent past. Today, ICT ownership is considered less of
a problem for many households (Meggiolaro, 2018). A report in Turkey showed
that households using ICTs have tended to increase, with usage rates even higher
in younger generations (Türkiye İstatistik Kurumu [Turkish Institute of Statistics],
2020). Considering that ICT ownership has vastly increased and that many students
now have access to ICT resources both at school and in the home, the question of
how students’ involvement with ICT is associated with their academic achievement
has arisen, and which is discussed in the following section.
Regarding ICT usage and academic performance, their relationship has been
investigated in various contexts, although mainly formed around two arguments:
Following the so-called “digital native” perspective, some have argued that digital
technologies have altered the way in which students learn (Prensky, 2001), while
others have proposed that this belief lacks empirical support and is just an educational “urban legend” (Kirschner & van Merriënboer, 2013). Accordingly, some
research has found higher levels of ICT usage is linked with increased student performance (Drain et al., 2012; Malhi et al., 2016), whilst others have concluded that
ICTs may even pose risks to student academic performance (Uzun & Kilis, 2019;
Bragdon & Dowler, 2016; Chen & Peng, 2008). Some studies, on the other hand,
have reported non-significant differences between involvement in ICT and academic performance (Rashid & Asghar, 2016). Additionally, some studies have even
declared controversial effects of using ICT on student achievement based on the
subject, discipline, or a variety of teaching/learning activities (Falck et al., 2018;
Machin et al., 2007). In the following, the issue is elaborated upon, including ICT
availability, ICT use, and ICT attitude based on PISA assessments.

1.1 Program for International Student Assessment (PISA)
The literature has also focused on the results of international large-scale assessment
tests. For example, the results of PISA have been used to explore how ICT relates to

academic achievement. Organized by the Organization for Economic Co-operation
and Development (OECD) since 2000, the PISA is an event aimed at measuring the
academic performance of 15-year-old students in science, reading, and mathematics, and is held every 3 years. The PISA addresses the question of how students
transfer their knowledge and skills between their schooling and real life (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2018). In this regard,
the assessment program offers an important record that countries can then use to
comparatively assess how their students’ achievements measure up at the international level in the aforementioned majors. The PISA also includes an ICT Familiarity Questionnaire, which assesses the participants’ access to ICTs, their ICT usage,
and their attitudes towards ICTs (OECD, 2012).
Regarding ICT availability and ICT use both at home and at school, the PISA
results have generally produced conflicting findings. For example, Zhang and Liu
(2016) presented evidence from the 2000 to 2012 PISA tests that showed that most
of the ICT constructs were negatively associated with students’ math and science
achievement in the longer term, after controlling for students’ socioeconomic status (SES). Based on the 39-country dataset of PISA 2015, Park and Weng (2020)
concluded that students’ interest in ICTs, their perceived ICT competence, and perceived autonomy in ICT usage had a positive impact on their academic performance,
whereas student ICT usage for both studying and entertainment showed a negative
association.
In a recent study Fernández-Gutiérrez et al. (2020) included three different
rounds of PISA assessment (2009, 2012, and 2015) in the Spanish context, and concluded that there was no significant effect of ICT usage on students’ math achievement scores, although a significant effect was noted in terms of science achievement.
In another study that analyzed the PISA 2015 results across 44 countries, Hu et al.
(2018) found that most of the constructs related to ICT usage were negatively associated with math and science achievement. In a more recent study, Huang et al. (2021)
explored the PISA 2015 datasets of six East Asian countries and regions, namely
Japan, Korea, Mainland China, Macao, Hong Kong, and Taiwan, and revealed that
the participants’ ICT usage significantly and negatively influenced their science
performance.
Güzeller and Akın (2014) tested the influence of ICT variables on math achievement from PISA 2006 with data from 28 OECD and 12 non-OECD countries. Their
analysis revealed that ICT usage negatively predicted math achievement for most
countries, whilst confidence in using ICT was shown to be a positive predictor.
Regarding the Turkish context in the assessment of PISA results, similar findings have been reported in that the use of ICT was found to have a negative correlation with students’ test scores in both PISA 2009 (Biagi & Loi, 2013) and
also PISA 2012 (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018; Petko et al., 2017). In PISA 2015,
however, Turkey did not complete the optional ICT Familiarity Questionnaire;
therefore, how ICT relates to Turkey’s PISA 2015 test scores is unknown. As
for PISA 2018, Özkan and Tekeli (2021) explored the effects of ICT engagement factors on science performance for both Singapore and Turkey. Their study
revealed that most ICT engagement factors were positively associated with science performance, except for social relatedness which had a negative influence.

Kaya and İnci (2021) examined Turkish students’ science achievement scores
based on ICT variables both at the student and school level, and cited conflicting
results, with negative effects for certain ICT dimensions and positive for others.
However, they only tested how ICT-related factors influenced students’ science
achievement.
To the best knowledge of the current study’s researchers, and at the time of
conducting the study, no research has been published that has aimed to reveal
the status of Turkey. Therefore, how ICT is related to students’ math and science achievement scores, controlling for the variables of students’ demographic
variables based on PISA 2018, is therefore deemed worthy of further investigation. As such, the current study inspected the most recent (2018) PISA dataset
in order to understand how ICT availability, ICT use, and ICT attitudes have
changed when compared to data from previous PISA events.

1.2 Confounding variables for PISA
It has been demonstrated that different factors can have an effect on students’ PISA
achievement, including their interest levels, the efficiency of their school, and students’ immigration status (Chiao & Chiu, 2018). When exploring the influence of
ICT in students’ academic achievement according to PISA data, researchers have
considered socioeconomic status (SES) and gender as confounding variables (Gubbels et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2018; Petko et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2019). As for SES,
it has been reported that SES is linked with significant differences in students’
achievement scores for most PISA participating countries (OECD, 2018). Furthermore, Huang et al. (2021) found a significant positive influence of SES on student
science performance across six East Asian countries and regions in terms of PISA
2015 data. To moderate achievement gaps originating from differences in SES,
governments have stressed the importance of ICT, which is considered to have the
potential to equip students with so-called 21st-century skills (Chiao & Chiu, 2018),
and thereby present more equal opportunities to students (Skryabin et al., 2015). On
the contrary, variations in SES might also lead to inequalities in digital proficiency,
resulting in difficulties for certain students in being able to take advantage of using
ICTs (OECD, 2015a; Yang et al., 2019).
According to the PISA 2015 results, gender is another important factor that has
shown variations in student achievement, although the differences vary according to
the country in question (OECD, 2018). Many early studies found significant gender
differences according to students’ test scores in the PISA assessments (Aypay, 2010;
Juhaňák et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2019). An OECD report (2015b) stated that female
students outperformed male students in reading, but that they underperformed in the
other subjects. In addition, regarding students’ ICT usage, research has shown that
gender differences can affect both ICT usage and proficiency (Aesaert & van Braak,
2015; Aypay, 2010). For this reason, it would be plausible to attempt to account for
these variables when exploring the relationship between the ICT usage of PISA students and their academic achievement.

1.3 Problem statement and purpose of the study
Research on how ICT variables influence achievement scores in the PISA exams
has yielded mixed results for different countries (Petko et al., 2017; Zhang & Liu,
2016). Recently, a scoping review on the relation between students’ ICT-related
factors and their performance in math and science test scores in PISA exams
also indicated that results varied based on country, context, and the variables
employed, and that much more research was needed in this context (Odell et al.,
2020a). The current study aims to assess how ICT availability, ICT use, and ICT
attitude related to students’ PISA 2018 test scores for Turkey, controlling for certain variables. The PISA results for Turkey are considered worthy of investigation
for the following reasons.
First, Turkey has invested significantly in technology integration projects such as
the aforementioned Basic Education and FATIH projects. In particular, the FATIH
project equipped the majority of Turkish K-12 schools with interactive whiteboards
and Internet access. Both students and teachers were distributed tablet computers
by the Turkish Ministry of National Education in order to encourage them to utilize technology for academic purposes both in the school and also at home (Gök,
2014; Gök & Yildirim, 2015). However, how they use ICT and how this usage
relates to student success is an unknown factor (Topuz & Göktaş, 2015). In this
sense, the most recent PISA results can be used to inform educational policymakers as to whether or not ICT availability, ICT use, and ICT attitude are contributing
factors to students’ academic success. Second, as the ICT Familiarity Questionnaire
is an optional element of the PISA for participating countries, and that this data is
not available for Turkey from the PISA 2015 event, there is a need to consider the
current status of the relationship between ICT use and student academic achievement. Third, to date, previous studies (e.g., Aypay, 2010; Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018)
have not focused their research on the confounding variables that may influence
ICT usage by Turkish students. Fourth, in the Turkish context, the limited research
undertaken to date has focused solely on science or reading achievement in PISA
2018; but not on math and science, including the aforementioned confounding variables. In light of these issues, the current study aims to address these gaps in the
literature.
Based on the Turkey dataset from PISA 2018, the current study aims to examine
how ICT constructs are associated with math and science achievement after controlling for the variables of students’ SES and gender. These are both treated as control
variables, as potentially influential factors that may either affect students’ access to
and use of ICT, or affect their academic achievement for a variety of reasons (Gubbels et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2018; Petko et al., 2017). Given this rationale, the current
study argues that how ICT variables relate to Turkish students’ achievement in PISA
2018 should be investigated. Accordingly, the following research question forms the
basis for the current study:
• Based on data from the PISA 2018 event, do ICT constructs (ICT availability,

ICT use, and ICT attitude) predict students’ math and science performance in
Turkey after controlling for their socioeconomic status and gender?

2 Methodology
2.1 Data and sample
The analyses were performed based on data from the 2018 PISA event (Turkish
dataset), obtained from the OECD’s PISA 2018 database (OECD, n.d.a). The student questionnaire data were used to address the two research sub-questions. PISA
data are freely available to researchers for the purposes of secondary analysis (Kaya
& İnci, 2021; Özkan & Tekeli, 2021); therefore, no ethical committee approval was
required for the current study to be undertaken.
The PISA focuses on 15-year-old students’ academic performance in science,
reading, and mathematics. The current study concentrated on how Turkish students’
math and science performances were predicted by ICT. Therefore, only students’
math and science test scores and their responses to the ICT Familiarity Questionnaire were analyzed. For the Turkish data, the sample included a total of 6,890 participating students selected from 186 schools based on the stratified sampling procedure (OECD, 2019b). Data from the ICT Familiarity Questionnaire were excluded
for those students (n = 167) who indicated that they had not previously used a digital
device (IC002 scale). Therefore, the data analyzed was based on a total of 6,723
cases.
Missing value analyses were also performed. The ratio of missing values did
not exceed 5% of the whole data at the construct level, which is consistent with the
threshold suggested by Tabachnick and Fidell (2014). Therefore, missing values
were excluded from the analyses through the listwise deletion method, yielding a
final total of 6,101 cases. The age of the participants varied from 15.3 to 16.3 years
old, whilst 49.4% were female and 50.6% were male.
2.2 Measures
International large-scale assessments (LSA) employ complex design procedures
concerning testing and sampling (Caro & Biecek, 2017; OECD, 2012). For testing,
a rotated booklet design is implemented; in which students respond only to a specific portion of the total test items. The estimation of students’ performances can be
computed through the technique of plausible values (PVs), which reflect scores that
a subject could achieve if they had responded to all test items by considering the

13

Education and Information Technologies (2022) 27:11443–11465

11449

measurement error (Caro & Biecek, 2017; Rutkowski et al., 2010; Willms & Smith,
2005). PISA uses a total of 10 PVs to estimate students’ performance on each performance scale (i.e., math, science, and reading). Depending on the research context
of the current study, two separate dependent variables were determined: For estimating math performance, 10 PVs (PV1MATH—PV10MATH) were used. In the same
manner, science performance was estimated using 10 PVs (PV1SCIE—PV10SCIE).
Due to being outside of the scope of the current research, dependent variables for
reading measurement were not included.
In the PISA 2018 dataset, ready-to-use indices exist for statistical comparison
(OECD, n.d.-a). Accordingly, 11 ICT-related indices were used in the current study
to predict students’ math and science achievement scores. Considering the similar
studies in the literature (e.g., Gubbels et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2019),
ICT constructs were categorized under three groups; ICT availability, ICT use, and
ICT attitude, which are explained in detail as follows.
2.2.1 ICT availability
To determine the availability of ICT resources, two indices (ICTHOME and
ICTSCH) were employed. The ICTHOME index originates from an 11-item scale
(IC001) that addresses the availability of different ICT devices (e.g., desktop computer, notebook, tablet computer, Internet connection, etc.) in the home, while
ICTSCH is computed based on a 10-item scale (IC009) considering the availability of ICT resources (e.g., desktop computer, data projector, interactive whiteboard,
etc.) in schools (OECD, n.d.b, 2017).
2.2.2 ICT use
To explore student ICT use either in school or outside of school, five indices
(HOMESCH, USESCH, ENTUSE, ICTCLASS, and ICTOUTSIDE) were used. The
HOMESCH index is derived from a 12-item scale (IC010) and refers to students’
ICT usage outside of school for schoolwork. An example item from HOMESCH
is; “Browsing the Internet for schoolwork.” The USESCH index is obtained from
a 10-item scale (IC011) and pertains to students’ ICT usage at school in general.
“Downloading, uploading or browsing material from the school’s website” can be
given as an example item from the USESCH index. The ENTUSE index refers to
students’ ICT usage outside of the school for leisure purposes and is obtained from
a 13 item-scale (IC008), and “Browsing the Internet for fun” can be given as an
example from this index. Apart from the previous cycles, two new usage indices
(ICTCLASS and ICTOUTSIDE) were also incorporated into the PISA 2018 dataset
(OECD, n.d.a). The ICTCLASS index is derived from a nine-item scale (IC150)
and focuses on how much time students spend on subject-related ICT usage during
lessons, whilst ICTOUTSIDE is retrieved from a similar scale (IC151) and pertains
to students’ time devoted to subject-related ICT usage outside of lessons (OECD,
n.d.b, 2017).

2.2.3 ICT attitude
Four indices (INTICT, COMPICT, AUTICT, and SOIAICT) were used as indicators of ICT attitude. The INTICT index is derived from a six-item scale (IC013)
on students’ interest in ICT. A sample item from the INTICT index is; “I forget
about time when I’m using digital devices.” The COMPICT index is calculated
from a five-item scale (IC014) that focuses on students’ perceived ICT competence. For example, students were presented with an item as; “I feel comfortable
using digital devices that I am less familiar with” from the COMPICT index. The
AUTICT index is derived from a five-item scale (IC015) tapping into the students’ perceived autonomy in using ICT, with an example being; “If I need new
software, I install it by myself.” Finally, the SOIAICT index is derived from a
five-item scale (IC016) dealing with the use of ICTs as a means for social communication. A sample item taken from the SOIAICT index is; “To learn something new about digital devices, I like to talk about them with my friends”
(OECD, n.d.b, 2017).
2.2.4 Demographic variables
As demographic variables, SES and gender were both considered. In the PISA
2018 dataset (OECD, n.d.-a), ESCS refers to the index of economic, social, and
cultural status, which is derived from variables such as parents’ education level,
parents’ occupation, and home possessions, while ST004D01T denotes the participant students’ gender (OECD, n.d.b, 2017). These variables were incorporated
into the analysis of the current study so as to estimate their effect on students’
math and science achievement as well as other ICT constructs. Table 1 presents
the descriptive statistics of predictor variables along with their definitions.

2.3 Data analysis
As previously mentioned, complex testing and sampling procedures are applied
for international large-scale assessments (LSA). For example, as a sampling
procedure, a two-step approach is applied in the case of the PISA: Schools are
selected as the first step, and then participant students are selected from those
schools as the second step (Caro & Biecek, 2017), meaning that the resulting
PISA dataset has a layered structure.
To decrease the standard error of population estimates, sampling weights are
employed as part of the analysis. From this approach, it is aimed that each participating student properly represents the appropriate number of students from
the whole population. As a method of sampling weights, PISA uses the balanced
repeated replication (BRR) procedure (Fay variant) with 80 replications (Caro &
Biecek, 2017; OECD, 2012, 2017; Rutkowski et al., 2010). As ordinary statistical
regression methods cannot handle such complex analysis, International Database

3 Results
Prior to presenting the findings of the main analyses, the following descriptive statistics regarding the Turkish students for PISA 2018 are presented. The majority of
the students indicated that they were 9 years old or younger when they first used
a digital device (cumulative: 57%) or the Internet (cumulative: 53%). Moreover,
30% of the students stated that they did not spend time at school using the Internet,
whilst 28% reported that using the Internet for up to 30 min during a typical weekday. However, when it comes to using the Internet outside of school, 24% reported

usage of between 2 and 4 h during a typical weekday. Furthermore, the percentage
of Internet usage outside of the school on a typical weekend day was comparably
high, showing that 24% of students used the Internet for more than 6 h per day at the
weekend.
The Turkish students achieved lower scores in reading, math, and science than
the OECD average, based on data from the PISA 2018 event. According to the mean
values, the students in Turkey scored as average of 466 in reading, 454 in math,
and 468 in science, whilst the OECD average was 487 in reading, and 489 in both
math and science. Concerning the math and science scores, descriptive statistics
demonstrated that male students (M = 464.91, SD = 87.25) outperformed females
(M = 455.76, SD = 84.95) in math, whilst female students (M = 477.18, SD = 78.40)
outperformed males (M = 473.68, SD = 83.91) in science. In the following, it was
tested whether or not ICT constructs (ICT availability, ICT use, and ICT attitude)
predicted Turkish students’ math and science performance after controlling for
demographic variables.
3.1 Prediction of Math performance by ICT constructs (RQ‑1a)
For the first research sub-question, a two-step hierarchical regression analysis was
performed to analyze whether or not ICT constructs predicted the students’ math
achievement after controlling for their SES and gender. In the first step, the two
demographic controls (SES and gender) were incorporated into the model, whilst
the ICT constructs were incorporated in the subsequent step. Therefore, two separate
analyses for each block of independent variables were performed in IDB Analyzer.
In each model, math achievement scores (plausible values) were entered into the
model as the dependent variables. Table 2 presents the regression results.
As can be seen from Table 2, in the first step of the hierarchical regression analysis, SES and gender accounted for 11% of the total variance. The SES variable
(β = 0.33, t = 11.44, p < 0.001) significantly and positively contributed to the model,
suggesting that higher levels of economic, social, and cultural status are linked with
higher math achievement. The second predictor, gender, failed to significantly contribute to the model.
In the second step of the hierarchical regression analysis, the ICT constructs
were incorporated into the model. The expanded model was found to explain 20%
of the total variance. The results highlighted that after controlling for both SES and
gender, from among the ICT availability variables, the availability of different ICT
devices in the home (ICTHOME) (β = -0.10, t = -4.49, p < 0.001) significantly but
negatively contributed to the model. However, ICT availability at school (ICTSCH)
was not found to significantly contribute to the students’ math performance. It can
be inferred that as students’ access to different ICT devices at home increases, their
math performance decreases; whereas, there was no influence revealed in terms of
access to ICT devices at school.

4 Discussion
The main objective of the current study was to examine how the ICT variables
of availability, use, and attitude related to Turkish students’ math and science
test scores according to data from the PISA 2018 event, after checking for some
demographic variables. With this aim, the study investigated how Turkish students’ ICT involvement predicted their math and sciences test scores in PISA
2018, after controlling for their socioeconomic status and gender. Analysis was
performed on the PISA 2018 Turkish dataset (OECD, n.d.-a) using two separate
forms of two-step hierarchical regression.
Regarding the prediction of students’ math and science performance by ICT
constructs, students’ socioeconomic status and gender were initially tested as
covariates. The initial findings indicated that students’ socioeconomic status significantly and positively predicted both math and science scores, meaning that the
higher the SES level of students, the higher their math and science achievement.
This finding complements the existing PISA-related literature in that SES is considered to be a strong determinant of student achievement (Gubbels et al., 2020;
Hu et al., 2018; Petko et al., 2017). This finding is also consistent with a report
published by the OECD (2019a), in which SES was stated to be a strong predictor
of both math and science scores. SES might therefore be related to students having different opportunities during their respective educational life. In other words,
families with a higher socioeconomic status may offer different opportunities to
their children, which may then lead to improved math and science achievement.
As for gender, as the other demographic variable included in the current study,
the findings revealed that it did not significantly contribute to the prediction of
the students’ math or science scores. In a report published by the OECD regarding PISA 2015 (OECD, 2018), it was highlighted that gender was considered an
important factor influencing student achievement, which is contrary to the result
revealed from the current research. Furthermore, based on the PISA assessment,
a significant gender-based difference was also revealed in some earlier research
(e.g., Aypay, 2010; Gubbels et al., 2020); again, unlike the results of the current
study. Taken together, although some prior studies revealed a significant gender
difference, the current research did not find any significant difference based on
the gender variable. However, it should be noted that the current study’s findings
may be reasoned by the culture of the Turkish dataset; which in turn implies that
the results may differ based on gender if other countries’ data were analyzed.
As the second analytical step, and after having controlled for the demographic
variables of SES and gender, three ICT constructs (ICT availability, ICT use, and
ICT attitude) were then tested for their effect on the prediction of the students’
math and science test scores. The findings from this second stage of analyses
indicated that amongst the ICT availability variables, ICT availability at home
(ICTHOME) significantly but negatively predicted both the students’ math and
science scores, after controlling for their SES and gender. However, this result
is not consistent with the literature, which showed ICTHOME to be significantly
and positively related to Turkish students’ math (Yurttas Kumlu & Doğan, 2020)

13

Education and Information Technologies (2022) 27:11443–11465

11457

and science scores (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018) during PISA 2012. Furthermore,
Özkan (2022) found Turkish students’ math scores to be positively linked with
the ICTHOME variable. Conversely, the negative link between ICTHOME and
student achievement found in the current study can be said to align with other
studies in the literature (e.g., Kaya & İnci, 2021). One possible explanation for
this result may be that while students might have access to ICT tools at home,
they may not necessarily use them for educational purposes, and that this may
negatively influence their math and science scores in PISA. Considering the variable of ICT availability at school (ICTSCH), it was not found to significantly
predict either the students’ math or science PISA scores. This result can be said
to align with some of the existing literature (e.g., Hu et al., 2018). Bulut and
Cutumisu (2018) found the similar result for the Finnish Sample, however, Yurttas Kumlu and Doğan (2020) found there to be a positive relationship between
math scores and ICTSCH in both PISA 2009 and 2012, which is clearly inconsistent with the results revealed in the current study. It may be postulated, therefore, that based on the results of the current study, that there may be several ICT
tools available at schools, but that alone does not guarantee that there will be an
enhancement to the students’ science and math scores. This finding agrees with
Cuban et al. (2001), who concluded that access to various forms of ICT does not
necessarily result in widespread classroom usage or enhanced learning.
Among the variables of ICT use, the current study’s findings demonstrated that
ICT usage outside of the school for leisure purposes (ENTUSE) and subject-related
ICT use during lessons (ICTCLASS) made a significant and positive contribution to
predicting the students’ math and science performances, whilst subject-related ICT
use outside of lessons (ICTOUTSIDE), ICT use at school in general (USESCH),
and ICT use outside of school for schoolwork (HOMESCH) had a significant and
negative contribution. The positive and significant influence of ENTUSE might
be interpreted that undertaking enjoyable activities such as watching videos, surfing social networks or playing online games may decrease students stress levels and
thereby help to increase their motivation (Gumus & Atalmis, 2011). In a similar
vein, Hu et al. (2018) found a positive relationship between ENTUSE and reading
and science literacy that also supports this result. Özberk et al. (2017) also revealed
a positive association between ENTUSE and Turkish students’ math achievement in
data analyzed from PISA 2012. Considering the ICTCLASS variable, the positive
link established with students’ math and science achievement was in line with the
prior literature; for example, Özkan (2022) revealed it to be a significant predictor
of math achievement, whilst Kaya and İnci (2021) stated that it predicted science
achievement. One possible reason why ICTCLASS has been positively associated
with math and science achievement is that the use of specific ICT devices in the
teaching of course-specific subjects within a structured classroom environment may
enhance students’ learning achievement. Moreover, in the physical classroom environment, teachers are able to provide assistance and support to their students so that
ICT devices can be used more effectively to achieve specific course outcomes. However, when it comes to ICTOUTSIDE, USESCH, HOMESCH, they were revealed
to be significant but negative predictors of student achievement. Similarly, Kaya
and İnci (2021) found that both ICTOUTSIDE and HOMESCH significantly and

13

11458

Education and Information Technologies (2022) 27:11443–11465

negatively influenced Turkish students’ science scores. Additionally, Guo et al.
(2022) established that USESCH negatively predicted students’ science literacy,
whereas HOMESCH did not offer a significant prediction in the context of data from
Chinese students during PISA 2015. A negative link between ICTOUTSIDE and
student achievement may be attributable to students needing guidance regarding
how to best use ICT devices for specific subjects. Outside of the classroom is not
considered to be a structured environment; and thereby students may not be able
to efficiently utilize ICT in learning specific subjects, which may lead to a negative influence on achievement. Besides, considering the negative influence of ICT
usage at school in general (USESCH) and ICT use outside of school for schoolwork
(HOMESCH), it may be probable that students lacked proper understanding of the
instructional role of ICT for their particular courses. Alternatively, their teachers
may lack ICT integration competence suitable to supervise their students’ ICT usage
at school in general (USESCH), or had perhaps inadequately taught their students
about the productive use of ICT outside of schools for the purposes of schoolwork
(HOMESCH).
Regarding ICT attitude, interest in ICT (INTICT) and perceived ICT competence
(COMPICT) were revealed to be significantly and positively related to both math
and science achievement, while ICT in social interaction (SOIAICT) was significantly and negatively related to both math and science achievement. Finally, while
autonomy in ICT use (AUTICT) was significantly and positively associated with science achievement, it was not found to be significantly related to math achievement.
Amongst the ICT attitude variables, some research in the literature (Hu et al., 2018;
Park & Weng, 2020) found ICT interest on student achievement (INTICT) to have
a significant and positive influence. Similarly, Meng et al. (2019) reported finding a
significant positive influence of ICT interest on math and science achievement for
students in China, and a significant negative effect for students in Germany based on
data from PISA 2015. In this sense, the current study, which found a significant positive association between interest in ICT and student achievement supports much of
the prior research. The result related to INTICT may be explained as students with
higher levels of interest in ICT tending to have increased motivation to use ICT for
learning, and thereby perform better in both math and science (Park & Weng, 2020).
As for perceived ICT competence (COMPICT), the current study demonstrated
that COMPICT was significantly and positively related to the students’ math and
science performances. This result is congruent to a study by Park and Weng (2020),
which explored data from 39 countries, and also Odell et al. (2020a) who revealed
a mostly positive association between COMPICT and science achievement in their
scoping review. However, Meng et al. (2019) reported finding a significant negative
association between ICT competence and math and science achievement for China,
whilst finding no significant prediction for Germany based on PISA 2015 data.
From the findings of the current study, it may be inferred that as students’ abilities
regarding the use of ICT tools increases, their math and science achievements also
tend to increase. This result might be attributed to the probability that the more students feel competent in using ICT, the less likely they are to encounter difficulties
in using it. Considering that the sense of confidence regarding a specific task is said
to be related to better performance of that task (Bandura, 1997), it is probable that

13

Education and Information Technologies (2022) 27:11443–11465

11459

students with higher levels of competence in using ICT would also adopt ICT more
easily for learning purposes, when compared to students with lower levels of ICT
competence (Güzeller & Akın, 2014; Odell et al., 2020b). This may in turn lead to a
positive relationship between COMPICT and achievement scores.
As for the SOIAICT, the current research found a significant negative contribution with the students’ math and science performance, thus corroborating the findings of Juhaňák et al. (2018) with the Czech dataset, and also Hu et al. (2018) in
their extensive study with data from 44 different countries. The negative relationship
between students’ use of ICTs as a means for social communication and achievement may be due to students spending time on activities such as online gaming,
chatting and using e-mail during the learning process, which may have distracted
them (Ma & Qin, 2021).
Finally, in terms of perceived autonomy in ICT use (AUTICT), the significant
and positive association found in the current study between AUTICT and the students’ science achievement can be said to be congruent with the literature (Gamazo
et al., 2018; Juhaňák et al., 2018). Conversely, the null relationship found between
AUTICT and math achievement in the current study, however, does not align with
most previous research (e.g., Odell et al., 2020a). ICT autonomy relates to taking
control during the learning process when using ICT (Park & Weng, 2020), which
functions similarly to perceived ICT competence (COMPICT). Again, referring
back to Bandura (1997), sense of autonomy, confidence and competence in performing a specific task tends to lead to improved performance (Güzeller & Akın, 2014;
Odell et al., 2020b). Accordingly, sense of autonomy in using various ICTs may be
said to contribute positively to students’ productive use of ICT and that this may
enhance their science achievement.
In essence, the results found in the current study are generally consistent with
the prior international comparative studies (e.g., Hu et al., 2018; Petko et al., 2017)
and scoping reviews (e.g., Odell et al., 2020a). Furthermore, concerning the Turkish context, generally similar findings were evidenced for PISA 2009 (Biagi &
Loi, 2013) and also for PISA 2012 (Bulut & Cutumisu, 2018; Petko et al., 2017).
Finally, not only in the PISA assessments but also in general, ICT usage by learners
requires significant attention due to the potential risk for adverse student achievement, according to some prior studies (Uzun & Kilis, 2019; Bragdon & Dowler,
2016; Chen & Peng, 2008).
4.1 Implications for practice
The findings of the current study imply that substantial investment in ICT integration
within K-12 education requires much more attention, since some governments may
be relying upon pushing the importance of ICT as a means of reducing the achievement gaps caused by the socioeconomic status of their student body through continued heavy investment in school-based ICTs. However, the current study, which
supports much of the existing literature, found significant negative effects from ICT
use by students on their academic achievement. It is therefore highly recommended
that teachers, educational institutions, administrators, and policymakers place much

13

11460

more emphasis on establishing how best to increase the gains and effectiveness of
educational practices and students’ learning outcomes, rather than simply making
increased investment in equipping schools with ICTs and urging ever increasing
usage of ICTs in teaching. On this issue, Srijamdee and Pholphirul (2020) suggested
that governments could incentivize universities to guide K-12 schools on the most
effective usage of ICTs for learning purposes, and to provide guidelines for both
students and their parents. As suggested by Fernández-Gutiérrez et al. (2020), one
of the primary roles and key responsibilities for today’s teachers and educational
administrators is to gain a better understanding of how best to utilize ICTs in educational practices, in which fields, for which types of learning activities, and for what
types of use, method, or strategy, so that ICT usage will enhance student learning in
terms of increased academic achievement. After all, even the subject, discipline, and
nature/type of teaching/learning activities matter when considering using ICT usage
in education (Falck et al., 2018; Machin et al., 2007).
Another issue is that ICT provides an opportunity to equip students with so-called
21st-century skills (Chiao & Chiu, 2018), and to present more equality of opportunity to students across the board (Skryabin et al., 2015). However, according to the
literature, significant attention should be paid to the potential risks associated with
the intensive overuse of ICTs on students’ learning (Uzun & Kilis, 2019; Bragdon
& Dowler, 2016; Chen & Peng, 2008), as also indicated from the findings of the
current study. Therefore, a particular interest could be applied within pedagogy to
realize more effective educational practices as a means to improving student learning outcomes. Teachers should be aware that ICT usage during teaching is not an
aim in itself, but rather as a means towards more effective teaching, and that ICT
resources should only be used when applied as part of an approach to enrich teaching and learning, and to make learning more effective for their students. In essence,
the notion of learning is a complex phenomenon, which can be influenced by many
varied factors (Kilis & Balbay, 2020). For educators, the vision should be grounded
entirely upon how students learn best, how teachers can improve their teaching, and
the mission being to provide students with effective learning through offering highly
efficient teaching. Both governments and educational policymakers should therefore
consider investment in terms of this overriding goal. ICT teachers are also required
to increase their responsibilities in this area, and should actively support the ongoing guidance of classroom teachers from other disciplines regarding the effective use
and successful integration of ICT resources for instruction. This is especially important given the findings of the current study, which signifies that some problems may
exist when heavily reliant upon ICT resource usage within the context of courses
taught to today’s students.
4.2 Limitations and future directions
The findings of the current study should be treated with some caution; as with most
studies, certain potential limitations exist. First, due to the relational nature of the
study, one should not infer causality from the results of the study. Second, the study
used items included within the PISA ICT Familiarity Questionnaire; however, more

13

Education and Information Technologies (2022) 27:11443–11465

11461

actual and predictive ICT indicators might also exist. Moreover, the data used was
based on the self-reported nature of the ICT questionnaire, and students may not
have responded neutrally or entirely. Equally, the data does not represent a completely accurate measurement of ICT usage or attitude. Larger or different datasets
and more direct forms of measurements might provide different insights and representations that are perceived as being more accurate and beneficial. Furthermore, it
is important to recall that teachers play a pivotal role in the successful and effective
implementation and integration of ICT within educational practices; hence, their
involvement and effect on student learning cannot be ignored. However, as argued
by Hu et al. (2018), data collected from PISA events does not include teacher-related
ICT factors; therefore, future research could focus on measuring the influence of
teacher-related ICT factors on student learning. Finally, especially in terms of ICT
attitude, future research could emphasize the detailed and actual measurement of
ICT attitudes with all due consideration with regards to the known difficulties and
nature of measuring attitude.
As a final remark, the current study recommends that the ICT Engagement
Scale, which is a new assessment in PISA, be investigated in the same manner in
future studies. As being out of scope in the current research, ICT engagement and
its related sub-constructs were not included. However, a recent study by Özkan and
Tekeli (2021) explored ICT engagement factors on student science test scores for
both Singapore and Turkey according to data from PISA 2018, and found a significant positive effect on science performance for students in both countries. Student
interest in ICTs, their perceived ICT competence, perceived autonomy in using ICT,
and social relatedness in using ICT, which are four sub-constructs measured in the
ICT Engagement Scale, would expand on the results of the current study. Therefore,
exploring both ICT familiarity factors (ICT availability, ICT use, ICT attitude) in
addition to ICT engagement factors could provide an enhanced understanding and
elaboration of the findings.

5 Conclusion
The current study investigated how Turkish students’ access to, use of, and attitude
towards ICTs relate to students’ math and science achievement, based on data from
PISA 2018. Investing substantial resources in ICT and analysis of Turkish data are
suggested to be useful in helping provide a better understanding of how access to,
usage of, and attitude towards ICT affects students’ academic achievement.
Collectively, based on the current study’s results, SES is considered to be a strong
determinant of achievement. Access to and use of ICT either at home or school
was shown to negatively relate to achievement, whilst ICT use was only positively
associated with achievement when used in classes for specific subjects, or used for
entertainment purposes. Amongst the attitude variables, interest in ICT, perceived
ICT competence, and perceived autonomy in ICT use were found to positively relate
to science achievement, whilst using ICT for social communication had a negative
relation. Finally, except for perceived autonomy in ICT use, similar results were
obtained in the link with math achievement scores.
