This study explores the measurement invariance in the TIMSS test items for Mathematics in the 8th grade from a
longitudinal perspective. The study was led by two research questions: (1) Is there evidence that the international
calibration as carried out by the TIMSS team is not appropriate for individual countries, and if it is not, are there
consequences for the estimation of the trendlines? (2) If the items are calibrated country-wise, is there still
evidence of insufficient measurement invariance? For four countries (Sweden, the United States, Hungary, and
England), 677 items have been calibrated separately per country, covering all 6 TIMSS cycles from 2003. Trend
analyses based on these calibrations revealed marked differences from the published trends in TIMSS. As to the
second research question, measurement invariance has been investigated by Profile Analysis (PA), a generalization of DIF analysis, that allows more than two groups of respondents and can test differential functioning of
categories of items, rather than specific items. In the study, special attention is given to gender differences, as a
tribute to the late Monica Rosén. Interesting differences over time and with respect to gender have been
discovered, but also some differential effects of the mode of administration (paper and pencil versus digital), by
using the so-called bridging data collected by the TIMSS team in 2019, to warrant the stability of the construct.

1. Introduction
International Large Scale Assessments (ILSA), such as TIMSS, PISA,
and PIRLS, are possibly the most ambitious educational projects,
claiming that they measure the same constructs consistently over time
and across countries. Over the past few decades, these assessments have
collected extensive data, including both various cognitive measures,
such as mathematics achievement, and background measures such as
students’ gender and socioeconomic status, from a culturally diverse
range of countries. This wealth of information, which provides opportunities for longitudinal comparisons both within and across countries,
has increasingly attracted the attention of many researchers (e.g.,
Majoros et al., 2021; Strietholt & Rosén, 2016; Strietholt et al., 2021).
However, taking advantage of these opportunities involves a key challenge: ensuring measurement invariance.
1.1. Measurement invariance and ILSAs
Measurement invariance describes how consistently a test assesses

the same construct across different groups or over time (Mellenbergh,
1989; Meredith, 1993; Sandoval-Hernandez et al., 2025). In ILSAs, it is
usually examined from a latent variable perspective using factor analysis
or item response theory (IRT) models.
Measurement invariance is usually described in three hierarchical
levels: configural, metric, and scalar (Meredith, 1993). Configural
invariance refers to whether the same items consistently measure a
similar construct across different groups, even if they do not do so in
exactly the same way. For example, suppose a unidimensional IRT
model fits test data from two separate groups. In that case, configural
invariance is established, regardless of whether the item parameters are
also the same between the groups. The configural level of invariance
ensures that we are talking about the same construct in each group.
Achieving the metric level requires that factorial composition is also
identical across test groups, along with configural invariance. Achieving
metric invariance allows for the comparison of correlations. Finally, a
valid comparison of mean scores across the test groups requires scalar
invariance. The key indicator for this highest level of invariance is the
identical item difficulty parameters across groups (Fischer et al., 2025).
This latter requirement is indicated as scalar invariance.

ensuring it in ILSAs is even more challenging. The statistical, psychometric, linguistic, and technical aspects of ILSAs are quite complex.
These assessments typically use an incomplete test design, meaning that
not all participating subjects receive the same set of test items. Instead,
different test forms are constructed and linked through common items,
allowing student performance to be reported on one or more common
scales. And to make things even more complicated, ILSAs are conducted
periodically, and aim to maintain their scales comparable over time. The
psychometric tool used for this common scaling is Item Response Theory
(IRT), which is neither a simple nor universally accepted tool, but represents a wide variety of approaches and controversies. All these complexities pose significant challenges to accurately interpreting ILSA’s
results and raise concerns about the validity of the reported outcomes.
These concerns led to many research studies on measurement
invariance in ILSAs, but the findings were often disappointing. For
instance, Sandoval-Hernandez et al. (2025) revealed in their systematic
literature review that none of the studies they examined offered enough
evidence for scalar invariance in ILSAs. This finding simply suggests that
making score-based comparisons between countries in ILSAs might be
misleading.
In fact, researchers in the field are aware of this invariance issue in
ILSAs. That is why they often advise educational stakeholders to be
cautious when making score-based comparisons (e.g., Klemenčič &
Mirazchiyski, 2018) across countries or subgroups, such as gender. On
the other hand, league tables and similar outputs based on score comparisons, such as achievement trend tables (see, TIMSS & PIRLS International Study Center, 2024) are arguably the most popular features of
ILSAs that draw widespread attention. This indicates that the educational community generally overlooks the lack of sufficient evidence for
measurement invariance.
We believe one reason for this oversight is the lack of concrete evidence demonstrating how the lack of scalar invariance can affect scale
score comparisons in ILSAs. Most measurement invariance research in
ILSAs is methodological and cross-sectional, typically analyzing a subset
of data to determine if specific countries meet invariance criteria using
techniques like multigroup confirmatory factor analysis. These studies
often evaluate invariance using fit indices such as CFI or RMSEA
(Protzko, 2025). The literature review by Sandoval-Hernandez et al.
(2025) indicates that these indices do not show a good fit for the models
in ILSAs when constrained by the scalar invariance criteria described
earlier. This finding suggests that comparing test scores, which depend
on common item parameters across countries, could be misleading in
ILSAs. The first purpose of this study is to examine whether there is
concrete evidence supporting this inference, using TIMSS eighth-grade
mathematics data.
A straightforward way to find this evidence in ILSAs is to compare a
country's achievement trend based solely on nationally calibrated item
parameters with the trend published in official ILSA publications, which
rely on pooled international calibrations. Performing country-wise calibrations removes the constraint of ‘common item parameter estimates
across countries’ for measurement invariance. Therefore, if scalar
invariance holds, these two trends should be very similar, indicating that
common item parameters accurately represent individual countries'
performance. To explore this issue, the study presents four case studies:
Sweden (SWE), the United States (USA), Hungary (HUN), and England
(ENG). These countries were chosen because of data availability across
six consecutive TIMSS cycles: 2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, 2019, and 2023.
For the analyses, all TIMSS data from these years, published separately
in the IEA database, were merged into a single dataset per country,
considering the incomplete test designs used in TIMSS. These analyses
constitute the first part of the study. More concretely, we formulate
research question one: (a) Can we find evidence in the TIMSS data that
the results of an international calibration are not appropriate to describe
the assessment results in a particular country? (b) If this is the case, can
this have serious consequences for describing the trends over time in the
participating countries?

The next section describes the second purpose of the study.
1.2. Measurement invariance and profile analysis
There is an ongoing debate in psychology literature about how to
handle measurement invariance in cross-cultural research. Opponents
argue that the current approach to measurement invariance relies on
unrealistically strict assumptions for cross-cultural settings that are
naturally heterogeneous because of cultural, linguistic, and contextual
factors (Fischer et al., 2025; Kusano et al., 2025; Protzko, 2025; Rohrer
& Paulewicz, 2025; Walton & Martin, 2023).
These scholars propose a new perspective on measurement invariance in cross-cultural research, where violations of invariance assumptions are not only viewed as threats to the validity of group comparisons
but also as potential signs of meaningful differences among groups. They
also propose various methodological approaches aligned with this new
perspective. For example, Asparouhov and Muthén (2014) recommend
using less restrictive statistical models that allow for partial invariance.
Protzko (2025) suggests conducting causal analyses based on the signs of
violations to understand why they occur.
We agree with the above arguments that expecting the measurement
invariance requirements to be fully met may be unrealistic, especially in
complex testing environments like those in ILSAs. For example, in
TIMSS, the mathematics achievement tests are designed considering
four content domains (Algebra, Number, Geometry, Data) and three
cognitive domains (Knowing, Applying, Reasoning). Items are then
assigned to 14 booklets1 in a non-disjoint manner. This structure has
been maintained throughout each TIMSS cycle, which is conducted
every four years for over two decades. We believe it is quite reasonable
to doubt that all these items from different content areas and with
varying cognitive demands measure students’ math achievement in the
same way across all these years, let alone among culturally different
countries, but even within the same country.
Additionally, we also agree that deviations from measurement
invariance might not pose a major problem in cross-cultural studies,
provided conclusions are not solely based on scale score comparisons,
which are typically an uncommon objective for these studies. Instead,
we see such deviations as potential sources of valuable insights that can
deepen our understanding of the constructs the tests are designed to
measure.
In this context, the second part of the study examines the utility of
Profile Analysis (PA) as an approach that could address some of the
concerns mentioned earlier. PA is an innovative method developed by
Verhelst (2012), that identifies deviations between the observed performance of a specific group of students on a particular set of items and
their expected performance, assuming measurement invariance holds.
PA is a method designed to demonstrate how violations of measurement
invariance can impact scale scores in hypothetical scenarios for particular items and student groups. Therefore, we believe it can properly
serve the new perspective of handling measurement invariance. Further
information about PA is given in the Methods section.
In this second part of the study, we included the TIMSS mathematics
test data from the same four countries (SWE, USA, HUN, and ENG) in the
PA. Separate analyses were conducted for each country based on
country-specific item calibrations. This approach aimed to isolate the
effect of cross-country differences on the PA results, allowing us to
examine whether national data sets in ILSAs are complex enough to
threaten measurement invariance.
In the analyses, we considered the administration years of TIMSS as
the main variable. This allowed us to examine measurement invariance
over time within a longitudinal framework. To our knowledge, no such
study exists in the current literature, probably because of the challenges
of combining and managing all available data that is reported separately

2.1. Item answers and test design

3.1. The IRT model and the calibration

For the four countries included in the study, responses to all mathematics items from all participating students in the TIMSS grade 8 assessments were collected, along with the test design details. This
information is enough to perform an IRT analysis for each country. More
detailed technical information on this aspect is described in Appendix A.
In 2019, participating countries had the choice to administer the test
in the usual (until then) paper and pencil (p&p) mode or in the newly
developed digital mode. All four countries in our study used the digital
mode. To guarantee a smooth transition from p&p to digital administration, a bridging study was carried out by the TIMSS team: all
participating countries using the digital mode in 2019 were asked to
administer the 114 common items from the cycles 2015 and 2019 in p&p
mode to an independent but statistically equivalent sample as the
sample taking the digital test. The answers to these items and the testing
design for this bridging study were also downloaded from the database
for the four countries.
More detailed information on sample sizes and on the design is given
in Appendix B.

Profile Analysis is an innovative approach that provides practical
solutions to the challenges, some of which are mentioned above. It can
manage data from incomplete test designs using IRT measurement
models. It accepts any combination of binary and polytomous items. It is
not limited to comparing two groups of test takers. It is not just an
exploratory process; its ability to define any meaningful item categories
enables testing various research hypotheses. And finally, its capacity to
manage well-defined item categories rather than individual items makes
it a more effective approach compared to standard DIF procedures.
More specifically, in Profile Analysis (PA), the idea of examining
each item individually is abandoned, and the focus shifts to categories of
items, where each category consists of items sharing certain features
(Verhelst, 2012; Yıldırım et al., 2014; Verhelst, 2018).3 The core of PA is
to compare students’ actual performance in these item categories with
their expected performance, which is based on item parameters derived
from the entire test data of the relevant population. The example below
may help clarify this further.

3.2. Profile analysis
An important implication of the claim that the test items all represent
the same construct is that the construct can be measured by any subset of
the items in any group of the tested population. This idea is present in
DIF analysis, where each item in turn is considered as a special subset,
and the tested sample is subdivided into two (mutually exclusive)
groups, like girls and boys. In such a case, the null hypothesis is that the
partial correlation between gender and response quality (correct versus
incorrect), given the latent variable, vanishes. Or, in a more widespread
formulation, boys and girls with the same ability should have the same
probability of having the target item correct.
In TIMSS, the technical staff examines the validity of the claim
mentioned above by checking whether test items function similarly
across participating countries in each TIMSS study. A standard procedure they use for this purpose is to ensure that, for each item, there is no
significant difference between each country’s Rasch item difficulty and
the international average Rasch item difficulty across all countries (e.g.,
Foy et al., 2016, p. 11.7).
However, analyzing test items individually may result in losing the
statistical power needed to detect signals of nonequivalence. For
example, Yıldırım (2021) showed that, in TIMSS 2015, when item parameters are examined collectively, they are much more similar within
culturally similar countries and less similar between culturally different
countries.
There are generalized DIF procedures, such as differential bundle
functioning (DBF) and differential testlet functioning (DTLF), which
analyze a set or bundle of test items collectively rather than individual
items when examining measurement invariance (Beretvas & Walker,
2012). However, these procedures are not flexible enough to handle data
from international large-scale assessment studies, where different

Profile analysis is the study of deviation profiles, and one can do an
analysis at different levels. The basis for the analysis is the individual

expected profile. For a given total score, there can exist several observed
profiles. For example, if there are two categories and the total score is 4,

4.2. National calibration versus international calibration: an example
For the Swedish data of 2011 (n = 5564), two profile analyses were
conducted, one using the item parameter estimates of the international
calibration and the other using the estimates from a separate calibration
of the Swedish data.
Each participating school was treated in the analysis as a separate
group, such that for each school an average deviation (of the partial
score from the expected partial score) became available, and could be
tested for difference with the null hypothesis value. There were 153
participating schools, with 6–85 students (average = 36.4; SD = 12.2),
taking one of the 14 different test forms of TIMSS-2011. In each school,
the average deviation was tested for significance at the 10% level (twosided). Table 4 displays the frequencies of significant results, categorized by whether the school’s average deviation was higher than expected (positive) or lower (negative).

4.3. Estimation of trends (Research question 1)
Besides the league tables, another important outcome of ILSAs is the
estimation of trends, which gives insight into the differences over time of
the average performance in a country. As the outcomes of the measurement on the latent variable (‘the non-observed or latent level of
achievement in the construct as defined by the TIMSS organization’) are
not visible, they have to be estimated. In TIMSS (as in other ILSAs like
PISA), this is done by drawing so-called plausible values (PV). In the
discussion section, more will be said about this technique.
In the TIMSS cycles, 5 PVs are drawn per test taker, and a good estimate for the latent value of the test taker is the average PV.10 Measures
are taken in the trend analyses of TIMSS to make PVs comparable across
time. The trends are made visible by the weighted average PVs per cycle
(in the same country). The weights are quite complicated results of the
sampling scheme and of subsequent adaptations due to attrition of
sampled schools and students within schools. We computed the trends in
this way, and they are identical to the published trends (TIMSS & PIRLS
International Study Center, 2024)
In the country-wise analyses conducted in this study, another estimate of the latent value was used, the weighted maximum likelihood
(WML) estimate, developed by Warm (1989). This estimate has two
important advantages over others, such as the maximum likelihood estimate: it accounts for all possible response patterns and has almost no
conditional bias.11 The exact meaning of ‘almost’ in the preceding sentence is quite technical and not discussed here; in practice it means that
bias occurs for values of the latent variable for which the test is out of
target, i.e., too difficult or too easy, leading to a positive bias in the
former case and to a negative bias in the latter case (Verhelst, 2009). In
practice, this bias (at the individual level) is important mainly for zero
and perfect scores. This can lead to biased estimates of the average of the
latent variable in subgroups with many extreme scores, especially if the
extreme scores are mostly low or mostly high.
For the trend estimates, the weighted averages of the Warm estimates are used, where the weights are the published weights from the
TIMSS database. A strong argument for the stability of the measured
construct in TIMSS would be a similar result of the two trend estimates.
Since the origin and unit of the latent scales are arbitrary, the averages of the WML estimates have been transformed linearly such that
the (weighted) averages and variances of both PV and WML estimates
are equal for the cycle of 2003, the starting year of the trend analysis.
This makes it easier to compare trend curves for the two ways of
computing trends.
In honor of Monica Rosén’s enduring interest in gender differences,
we first give the trends for girls and boys in SWE separately in Fig. 2.
As to the similarities between the two approaches, the gender gap
changes sign four times across the six cycles. For the six time points, twosided tests have been carried out, one based on a method proposed by
Goldberg et al. (2005) and the other by Kish (1965), with the latter being
more conservative. For the PV, five t-tests were carried out, and in all
cases (including SWE and the other countries) the t-tests yielded the
same statistical conclusion: significant results occurred when the estimate for boys was higher than for girls, except for two cases: ENG-2015,
but only for the WML comparisons, and SWE-2011, only for the PV
comparisons.

5. Discussion
The results provide clear answers to both research questions and
support a comprehensive discussion. For research question one, it is
clear that (a) a unique IRT model and its subsequent use of item parameters from an international calibration are not suitable for explaining the variation in response data. (b) Replacing the international
calibration with separate national calibrations in the four countries
studied led to significant differences in trend analyses across the TIMSS
cycles since 2003, highlighting the serious impact of neglecting scalar
invariance.
Regarding research question two, the results of the profile analyses
revealed that the scalar invariance of the scales is not warranted, even
when the IRT calibrations are made within a single country. A key
pattern emerged showing girls performing better than expected in
Algebra, but worse in the Number items, while boys exhibited the
opposite trend. The differences between genders in Algebra and Number
are highly significant in all six cycles studied. For Geometry and Data,
some gender differences were observed, but no consistent pattern
emerged.
An additional analysis using bridging study data indicated that the
assumption of scalar invariance was further jeopardized by the change
in test mode, from paper-and-pencil until 2015 to digital testing from
2019 onward.
In summary, our study supported the idea that expecting measurement invariance at the highest level in ILSAs is unrealistic. Literature
often attributes potential threats to measurement invariance due to

1. Although the methods to compute the trends are quite different – PV
in the TIMSS publications, weighted averages of WML estimates in
the present study – the significant differences in the trend estimates
are not due to the choice of the estimates. Both approaches generally
provide unbiased trend estimates, and if the tests are not grossly off
target in the subpopulations considered (meaning they are neither
overly easy nor overly difficult), they will come to essentially the
same conclusions, provided the same measurement model is used.
The essential point is that PVs, as well as WML estimates, are highly
dependent on the item parameter values, which in both cases are the
estimates resulting from calibration. In the international calibration,
which involves a highly heterogeneous set of item answers, these
estimates will reflect a kind of compromise to account for the
different functioning of the items across the countries participating
in TIMSS.
2. Questions might arise regarding the epistemological status of the
present study: what precisely can we learn from it, and what not?
More practically, do we now know that we should give priority to the
domain classification of the items rather than to their cognitive
classification? The answer is clearly no. Our choice was exclusively
based on a pragmatic reason: we could not identify any regularity in
the deviation patterns – neither concerning time nor gender – when
we used these factors to ‘explain’ the variability in the deviation
profiles.
3. Likewise, the use of gender to form the groups of test takers is
arbitrary in principle, and other grouping variables might also reveal
significant effects in a profile analysis. We found some effects connected to the number of books in the household, but these are not
reported here, because the results were not easy to interpret and
would make the report unduly long.
4. Identifying gender ‘effects’ or the effects of any other grouping does
not allow for a causal interpretation, in the same sense that statistically significant regression coefficients are not proof of a causal effect

As a final remark, this study was primarily aimed, as a methodological study, to clarify potential threats to the validity of the trend
conclusions published in TIMSS reports. A novel method – profile
analysis – was employed, and it proved useful and more flexible than the
traditional DIF-analysis techniques, which are, in general, limited to
comparisons between two groups (a ‘reference’ group and a ‘focal’
group). However, as the study was essentially a reanalysis of the TIMSS
data for four countries, it had some limitations. As we wanted to stay as
close as possible to the collected data, we decided to use all items. In two
cases, we had to eliminate an item due to psychometric reasons. But
more importantly, we had limited control of the validity of the item
classifications: we could only use the classification as published (and 8
items were excluded from the profile analyses because of inconsistent
classification), as many of the items have to be kept secret in ILSAs to
warrant comparability over time.

The decision to consider gender when forming groups of test takers,
along with time, was not arbitrary in the setup of the study. The work by
Rosén (2017) clearly showed that the underlying variable(s) explaining
the variations in a battery of aptitude and achievement tests are not
unidimensional. She elegantly showed that they are best described by a
hierarchy of factors with distinct average scores for boys and girls (at
grade 6). Girls were superior in the factors representing general intelligence (fluid as well as crystallized), while boys were superior in the
intermediate factors like general visualization or spatial orientation, and
in the local factors (i.e., at the lowest level of the hierarchical structure)
such as numerical ability needed in achievement tests (Rosén, 2017, p.
80). This primarily motivated an examination of differential functioning
with respect to gender.
The common finding across the four countries is the different functioning of Algebra and Number, with Algebra being relatively easier for
girls, and Number being relatively easier for boys. This could be
explained by Rosen’s findings, as algebra items might be more abstract
and less influenced by numerical ability, but more by general intelligence. However, as speculative as this explanation may be, it clearly
demonstrates that treating the test performance as governed by a unidimensional trait is not valid, and this has consequences for conclusions
on the so-called gender gap when based on TIMSS results. Except for the
first six booklets of 2003, the 76 booklets used contain, on average, 7.9
items for Algebra and 8.9 items for Number, with very little variation.

Appendix A. Technical details on the downloaded data
Data files (SPSS files), Item Information files (Excel files), and other related documents were downloaded from the data repository web page of IEA.
(https://www.iea.nl/data-tools/repository)
SPSS scoring syntax files provided by IEA were used to score student responses. (For example, responses to multiple-choice items were scored as 1
for correct responses and 0 for incorrect responses).
The item booklets were identified using the booklet design tables in the TIMSS Assessment Framework documents. (Tables for TIMSS 2007 are
given in Figure A1 as an example)

Figure A1. Example of booklet design tables

The IDs of items in each booklet were identified regarding the item blocks they belong to. This information is in the Item Information Excel files

provided by IEA. (A part of the file for TIMSS 2007 is given as an example Figure A.2)
In general, we treated items with different item IDs as different items. However, there are two exceptions: 1) When TIMSS switched to the
electronic version in 2019, they changed the IDs of the trend items previously used in the paper versions by replacing one character. For example, the
item ID M052421 in the 2015 paper version was changed to ME52421 in 2019 for the same item, indicating that it was administered on a computer.
We considered such items to be the same items. 2) In TIMSS 2003, in addition to their permanent IDs, items are also assigned two or three item IDs
unique to the TIMSS 2003 administration. For instance, the item with the permanent ID M022110 was assigned three unique IDs: M022110, MF22110,
and MC22110. We adhered to the permanent item IDs. We used unique item IDs to extract student responses from the data files, but combined them
under the corresponding permanent IDs to keep track of the items across TIMSS administrations.

Figure A.2. An example of an item information file

In identifying the items, only those with an 'In Scaling' status of YES were considered. Scaling Status shows whether the TIMSS team included the
item in the IRT scaling. (The exception is that in TIMSS 2003, IEAs’ scoring file does not include some items indicated by unique IDs in the data files (e.
g., MC022046, MC022110, MC032525, MC032701, and MC032704). Data from such items was ignored.)
SPSS syntax files were created using the list of item IDs (as obtained in the way explained above) in separate booklets. By running these syntax files,
the booklets were extracted from the SPSS scored data files into separate text files. These text files were then merged into a single file to serve as the
data file for OPLM analyses.
For TIMSS 2019 and TIMSS 2023, the digital versions of the items were involved in the main analysis. Paper-and-pencil items in the bridge data
(TIMSS 2019) were used for the supplementary analysis to determine if there is a significant mode of administration effect between the electronic and
paper versions.

Appendix C. Interpretation of the average deviations
The statistics for profile analysis using OPLM as the calibration model are the average deviations per group in weighted score. This is somewhat
unfortunate as the weighted score depends on the (imputed) discrimination parameters, which are usually chosen to optimize the fit of the model. For
example, in the first test form from 2003, the maximum raw score for the items belonging to the category ‘Algebra’ is 9, while for the model applied to
the data from SWE, the maximum weighted score is 45. Although there is no one-to-one relationship between raw and weighted scores, a one-point
change in raw score generally corresponds to a change of 45/9 = 5 points in weighted score. Conversely, a one-point change in weighted score is
approximately equivalent to 9/45 = 0.2 points in raw scores.
As the design in TIMSS has, in general, been very carefully constructed and kept stable over time, there is very little variation across booklets for the
same year (and the same country) in the ratio of raw score to weighted score. This means that the average ratio (across booklets) provides a good
estimate of the ratio of weighted score to raw score. Moreover, the variation of these average ratios across years is also minimal, allowing for a
reasonable translation from weighted scores to raw scores for reporting purposes. However, these doubly averaged ratios do vary across categories and
countries. These averages are displayed in TableC1.
