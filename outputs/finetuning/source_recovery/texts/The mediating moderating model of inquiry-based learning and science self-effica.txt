ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

The relationship between inquiry-based learning and science selfeﬃcacy was analysed using data from 57 countries and
economics participating in the 2015 Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA). This analysis generated a mediating–
moderating model, which involved the mediating role of science
interest and the moderating role of teacher support. The results
indicated that (1) inquiry-based learning had a signiﬁcant positive
correlation with science self-eﬃcacy; (2) science interest partially
mediated the relationship between inquiry-based learning and
science self-eﬃcacy, with an indirect eﬀect accounting for 20.1%
of the total eﬀect; and (3) teacher support moderated the direct
eﬀect positively while moderating the indirect eﬀect negatively.
These details of the link between inquiry-based learning and
science self-eﬃcacy provided theoretical guidance and practical
support to improve the application of inquiry-based learning in
science education.

Introduction
Inquiry-based learning has been deemed to be an eﬀective approach for science education (Jones et al., 2013). Inquiry-based learning encourages students to actively participate (Pedaste et al., 2015), curiously investigate (Minner et al., 2010), and equally
cooperate and communicate (Gillies et al., 2013) by emphasising the process of ‘doing’
science, during which self-eﬃcacy matters. Students with higher self-eﬃcacy are
usually intrinsically motivated to face challenges, while those without suﬃcient selfeﬃcacy often fail to take any action (Margolis & Mccabe, 2006) and do not beneﬁt
from inquiry-based learning. The signiﬁcance of improving students’ science selfeﬃcacy (the domain-speciﬁc self-eﬃcacy (Bandura, 1997) in science learning) during
inquiry-based learning is therefore obvious (Feyzioğlu, 2019). However, the mechanism
of inquiry-based learning’s inﬂuence of students’ science self-eﬃcacy remains vague. This
study uses mediating–moderating analysis to explore the mechanism from the perspective of reciprocal determinism in which personal factors, the social environment and the
inquiry-based learning behaviour are related to each other (Bandura, 1978). Regarding
personal factors, interest and self-eﬃcacy in inquiry-based learning are closely related

Theoretical framework
Inquiry-based learning
The most widely used deﬁnition of inquiry-based learning comes from the National
Science Education Standards, which states that inquiry-based learning facilitates the
development of students’ abilities such as ‘asking questions, planning and conducting
investigations, using appropriate tools and techniques to gather data, thinking critically
and logically about relationships between evidence and explanations, constructing and
analysing alternative explanations and communicating scientiﬁc arguments’ (National
Research Council [NRC], 1996, p. 105). Yang et al. (2019) summarised the goals of
inquiry-based learning based on previous research (Bybee, 2006; Lederman & Lederman,
2012): (1) developing an understanding about scientiﬁc concepts by doing science (learning through inquiry), (2) acquiring the inquiry process skills necessary to conduct a
scientiﬁc inquiry (learning of inquiry) and (3) establishing an understanding of scientiﬁc
inquiry (learning about inquiry).
Regarding these three goals of inquiry-based learning, they are achievable under
certain forms of intervention, including computer games (Srisawasdi & Panjaburee,
2019) and augmented reality technology (Pedaste et al., 2020), thereby contributing
to a better conceptual understanding, explicit instructions for mastering inquiry
skills (Kruit et al., 2018) and out-of-school science camps that focus on comprehending the nature of scientiﬁc inquiry (Leblebicioglu et al., 2017). A meta-analysis of
related studies from 1980 to 2004 showed that the positive eﬀects of an inquirybased learning environment on student achievement are signiﬁcant (eﬀect size =
0.65) compared to the traditional teaching strategies used in control groups (Schroeder et al., 2007). Moreover, inquiry-based learning gives rise to the development of
metacognitive skills (Nunaki et al., 2019). It should be noted, however, that the
eﬀect of inquiry-based learning can be aﬀected by multiple factors ranging from
the guidance method (Koksal & Berberoglu, 2014) to the classroom environment
(Creemers, 2006).
Science self-eﬃcacy
The concept of self-eﬃcacy was proposed by Albert Bandura in 1977, and refers to the
belief that an individual is capable of accomplishing something successfully (Bandura,
1977; Menon & Sadler, 2016). Self-eﬃcacy is believed to arise from several sources,
such as past performance accomplishments, vicarious experiences and somatic and
emotional states (Schunk, 1991), which are all likely to appear in inquiry-based learning.

First, inquiry-based learning urges students to explore science through a method similar
to how scientists conduct scientiﬁc studies, strengthening their initiative of ‘doing’
science and oﬀering them past performance accomplishments (Britner & Pajares,
2006). Second, as students observe the inquiry performances of their peers, they gain
vicarious experience (Bailey et al., 2017). Third, the zest for inquiry-based learning inﬂuences students’ emotions (Taub & Azevedo, 2018), ultimately aﬀecting positively their
science self-eﬃcacy (Borrachero et al., 2013; Ritchie et al., 2011).
Some empirical studies have reported the eﬀect of inquiry-based learning on
science self-eﬃcacy. Inquiry-based learning could improve science self-eﬃcacy
(Çallk, 2013; Pajares & Britner, 2001), the degree of which is aﬀected by the openness
of inquiry-based activities, gender and so on (Berg et al., 2003; Lamoureux et al., 2013;
Yoon, 2009).
Science interest
Interest is a psychological tendency to conduct some activities with positive emotions,
and it depends on the process instead of the instrumental value of the activities (Davis
et al., 1992). Theoretically speaking, the positive emotions brought by interest, as one
of the sources of self-eﬃcacy mentioned above, lead to concentrated and persistent
activity participation, which eﬀectively improves self-eﬃcacy (Hidi, 2006; Tracey,
2001). A 60-sample meta-analysis regarding the relation of self-eﬃcacy and interest in
art, mathematics and science reported that the average weighted mean eﬀect size for
the correlation was 0.59 (Rottinghaus et al., 2003). In the ﬁeld of science education, positive association between interest and self-eﬃcacy was also observed (Blotnicky et al.,
2018; Ogle et al., 2017; C. W. Wang & Neihart, 2015).
The impact of inquiry-based learning on science interest can be explained by the
four-phase model of interest development (J. A. Chen et al., 2016; Hidi & Renninger,
2006). Situational interest is ﬁrst triggered by incongruous, surprising textual features such as a well-designed driving question in inquiry-based learning (phase
one) (Krajcik & Czerniak, 2014). Once triggered, situational interest can be held
through the meaningfulness of the inquiry-based tasks or personal involvement
(phase two). When students actively or passively participate in the inquiry-based
learning activities repeatedly, an emerging individual interest shows up (phase
three), which may ﬁnally lead to a well-developed science interest with positive
emotions, and more stored knowledge and value compared with other activities
(phase four) (Renninger & Hidi, 2011). The above theoretical analysis of how
inquiry-based learning might increase science interest has been empirically proven
(Brown, 2000; Potvin et al., 2017).
Teacher support
An ideal inquiry-based learning requires a benign classroom climate (Klahr & Nigam,
2004) whose essential aspects include teacher support (Pianta, 2004). Teacher support
refers to students’ perceptions of teachers expressing appreciation of them, showing
patience with them and paying attention to their needs (Klem & Connell, 2004; Leﬂot
et al., 2011). As identiﬁed by self-determination theory, competence (to succeed in

something), autonomy (to remain personally independent) and relatedness (to connect
with others) are required for individual development (Deci & Ryan, 1985), which
happen to correspond to science self-eﬃcacy, student self-direction and teacher
support in inquiry-based learning (Wentzel, 2009).
Recent studies have revealed how teacher support might moderate the connection
between inquiry-based learning and learning outcomes (Chi et al., 2018; Kang, 2022).
Students who perceive adequate teacher support feel safe in the classroom and are
more willing to engage in inquiry-based learning (Saunders-Stewart et al., 2015), resulting in stronger self-eﬃcacy. This phenomenon suggests a probable moderating role of
teacher support in the relationship between inquiry-based learning and science selfeﬃcacy, since a moderator is something whose diﬀerent levels accompany diﬀerent
strength or direction in the relationship between the independent and the dependent
variables (Hayes, 2017).
Existing correlational and longitude research has suggested that teacher support is
linked with interest (Lazarides et al., 2019; Yu & Singh, 2018). It is a common phenomenon that students who perceive suﬃcient support from a teacher of a speciﬁc subject are
more interested in that subject more than others (Goodenow, 1993; Wentzel, 1998). High
teacher support with the promotion of verbal participation and acknowledgement of students’ contributions would enhance communication among students, making it easier to
trigger the interest of students (Anderhag et al., 2015; Gillies, 2004). It is thus expected
that teacher support moderates the relationship between inquiry-based learning and
science interest.
With respect to the relationship between science interest and science self-eﬃcacy,
when interested students take part in inquiry-based learning activities, suﬃcient
teacher support and guidance will help them more easily obtain a successful experience
and positive emotions, leading to greater self-eﬃcacy (Martin & Dowson, 2009; Riconscente, 2014). We, therefore, expect a moderating role of teacher support on the relationship between science interest and science self-eﬃcacy.
The current study
The current study concentrated on the mechanism of inquiry-based learning inﬂuencing
science self-eﬃcacy, and was guided by two research questions:
(1) How is science interest associated with the relationship between inquiry-based learning and science self-eﬃcacy?
(2) How is the association above moderated by teacher support?
Regarding the ﬁrst question, the hypothesis was that science interest was a mediator
between inquiry-based learning and science self-eﬃcacy. For the second question, this
study hypothesised that teacher support would positively moderate the direct and indirect eﬀect of inquiry-based learning on science self-eﬃcacy.
To sum up, the hypothetic model is a mediating–moderating model (Figure 1) in
which inquiry-based learning is linked with science self-eﬃcacy through the mediation
of science interest and the moderation of teacher support.

Methodology
Sample
Student data from the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) in 2015
was analysed to test the hypothetic model in Figure 1. PISA assessed 15-year-old students
in school using a two-stage stratiﬁed sample design. The ﬁrst-stage sampling selected
individual schools via systematic probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling
whilst the second-stage sample selected individual students with equal probability
from the selected schools (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development
[OECD], 2017). A total of 346,109 subjects from 57 PISA participants (35 OECD
countries and 22 partner countries and economies) were included in the sample, since
they have administered questions measuring all the variables of our interest in PISA
2015 context questionnaires.
Instruments
In the PISA 2015 student questionnaires, many categorical items measuring latent constructs were scaled based on Item Response Theory (IRT) modelling. For these constructs, the weighted likelihood estimates (WLE) were used as individual student
scores. WLE is a modiﬁcation of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). The idea of
MLE is to maximise the probability of the observed phenomenon by developing a probability model between the estimated parameter and the phenomenon, with all data
having the same weight. But for WLE, it makes the outliers or the data aﬀected by distributions less weighted. WLE scores were ﬁnally transformed into a metric with a
mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 across 35 OECD countries. Higher WLE
scores indicated higher levels of the variables. Several variables published by the
OECD were involved as focus variables, including science self-eﬃcacy (SCIEEFF),
inquiry-based science teaching and learning practices (IBTEACH), interest in broad
science topics (INTBRSCI) and teacher support in science classes of students’ choice
(TEACHSUP), which correspond to the dependent variable, independent variable,
mediator and moderator in this study, respectively. In addition to the regular demographical variable gender (male is coded as 1 and female is coded as 2), disciplinary
climate in science classes (DISCLISCI) was also used as a control variable due to its

INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF SCIENCE EDUCATION

inﬂuence on inquiry-based learning (Chi et al., 2018; Jenkins & Ueno, 2017). This control
variable and the four focus variables above were all measured by 4-point Likert-type
scales and transformed into WLE scores. For a full list of the variable items, see Appendix
1 (OECD, 2017).
Data analysis
The above publicly available data was loaded into the SPSS 23.0 programme for statistical
analysis, the main steps of which were performed using the PROCESS macro for SPSS
developed by Hayes (2017). The PROCESS macro enables the automatic processing of
the bootstrapping needed by mediation analysis, as well as the mean centring and construction of the interaction terms needed by moderation analysis, thereby making it convenient for analysing complex multivariable models (MacKinnon et al., 2004), including
the mediating–moderating model assumed in this research.

The descriptive statistics and correlations of the four focal variables are presented in

self-eﬃcacy; the assumed mediator science interest was positively correlated with both
inquiry-based learning and science self-eﬃcacy. The prerequisite of mediating analysis
was thus satisﬁed, since all of the correlations were signiﬁcant at the 0.01 level.

Discussion
The results above veriﬁed the rationality of the hypothetic model, providing evidence and
in-depth explanations for previous studies and suggestions for the educational application of inquiry-based learning. The eﬀect size of the ﬁnal model can be measured by
Cohen’s f2 which equals to R2 /(1 − R2 ), representing the proportion of explained variance over unexplained variance. According to the criteria of Cohen (1969), f2 of the
ﬁnal model (0.125) should be termed as small, since it is larger than 0.02 while smaller
than 0.15. In many cases, however, small eﬀects measured in terms of the proportion
of variance explained can be important in reality (Lane, 2013). Considering the substantial number of psychological and educational factors relevant with self-eﬃcacy, the eﬀect
size of 0.125 should not be regarded as a meaningless one (Maulana et al., 2016).
The results of this study showed a signiﬁcant positive correlation between inquirybased learning and science self-eﬃcacy, conﬁrming the previous view that inquirybased learning is an eﬀective way to improve and maintain science self-eﬃcacy (Çallk,
2013; Pajares & Britner, 2001). However, Gormally et al. (2009) found that the challenges
students faced in inquiry-based learning made their self-eﬃcacy lower than that of students in traditional classes, which seemed to contradict with our results. The authors
blamed the negative correlation on the challenges facing students in inquiry-based learning, but according to our results, maybe the negative correlation could be explained by
exploring other factors such as science interest and teacher support.
The mediating role of science interest
The ﬁrst research question focused on the relationship between inquiry-based learning
and science self-eﬃcacy with science interest as a mediator. The results were in line
with our hypothesis, revealing the psychological inﬂuence of inquiry-based learning
from the perspective of intrinsic motivation and emotional factors. In our research
using data from 57 participants in PISA 2015, science interest was signiﬁcantly correlated

with inquiry-based learning with a coeﬃcient of 0.286. This was in line with one previous
study conducted in 30 countries reporting that the coeﬃcients in diﬀerent countries
ranged from 0.192 to 0.402 (X. Chen & Hu, 2020), and another study based on PISA
2006 stating that inquiry-based learning arouses science interest (Mcconney et al., 2014).
Science interest served as a mediator between inquiry-based learning and science selfeﬃcacy, and the mediating eﬀect accounts for 20.1% of the total eﬀect. Such an indirect
eﬀect is considered medium (Cohen, 1988), consistent with ﬁndings in elementary school
science classes (Scherer, 2013) and out-of-school science camps (Gibson & Chase, 2002),
where inquiry-based learning as a student-centred way of learning was associated with
stronger science interest and science self-eﬃcacy (J. A. Chen et al., 2016).
The moderating role of teacher support
The descriptive statistics show that the correlation between teacher support and inquirybased learning (0.449) was a moderate one, while the rest of the correlations were rather
weak (0.147 ∼ 0.286). Such a moderate correlation is not surprising if we look at the items
of the two variables (Chi et al., 2018). For example, items for teacher support like ‘the
teacher gives students an opportunity to express opinion’ are similar with the item ‘students are given opportunities to explain their ideas’ in the scale of inquiry-based learning
(OECD, 2017). In the theoretical framework, however, teacher support is a part of classroom climate, and inquiry-based learning is considered as an instructional strategy, so
these two variables can be manipulated by teachers separately. What their moderate correlation indicates is not that a causal link exists, but that the moderation analysis would
not be seriously interfered with by multicollinearity issues (Dormann et al., 2013). Therefore, teacher support was hypothesised to be a moderator rather than a mediator in this
research, and the results show that teacher support moderates not only the direct eﬀect of
inquiry-based learning on science self-eﬃcacy (Path C) but also the indirect eﬀect (Path
A→B) mediated by science interest.
With respect to the direct eﬀects of inquiry-based learning on science self-eﬃcacy
(Path C), the moderation of teacher support was shown in Figure 4(c). When inquirybased learning is low, the science self-eﬃcacy under high and low levels of teacher
support are almost the same (about −0.15). However, when it comes to high level of
inquiry-based learning, the direct eﬀect becomes signiﬁcantly larger for students with
more teacher support than those with less teacher support, in alignment with other
studies showing that teacher support plays an important role in promoting science
self-eﬃcacy in inquiry-based learning (Liu et al., 2021; Zhang, 2017).
The ﬁrst part of the indirect eﬀect (Path A) received a weak negative moderation
(Figure 4(a)), namely the relationship between inquiry-based learning and science interest weakened with high teacher support. This result contradicted the hypothesis that
teacher support positively moderated this path. Such a contradiction might be linked
with the contrast between high teacher support and students’ need of autonomy to cultivate interest. In inquiry-based learning, students are encouraged to explore by themselves instead of listening to teachers, and their interest can be triggered more by ﬁrsthand experience than by praise from teachers, just as the items of inquiry-based learning
scale like ‘students are allowed to design their own experiments’ describes (OECD, 2017).
However, these autonomous characteristics of inquiry-based learning might somehow be

INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF SCIENCE EDUCATION

1107

restricted at a high level of teacher support. Kikas et al. (2016) even found that teacher
support inhibited interest in student-dominated classes. It should not be ignored,
however, that the value of science interest for high teacher support was retained
higher than that for low teacher support, conﬁrming the importance of teacher
support for science interest (Aldridge et al., 2013; Tobin & Fraser, 1990; Xu et al., 2012).
The second part of the indirect eﬀect (Path B) was also negatively moderated by
teacher support (Figure 4(b)), similar to the moderation on Path A. As science interest
increased, science self-eﬃcacy for high teacher support kept increasing and being
above the line of low teacher support, although the former slope was more gradual
than the latter one. When ‘the teacher continues teaching until the students understand’
as described in the teacher support scale (OECD, 2017), such a high level of teacher
support might be accompanied with a context where students are lacking the needs of
autonomy, active engagement, and positive emotion, which were the important products
of interest and sources of self-eﬃcacy (J. A. Chen et al., 2016; Deci & Ryan, 2000), leading
to a reduced association between science interest and science self-eﬃcacy.
To sum up, teacher support positively moderated the direct eﬀect of inquiry-based
learning on science self-eﬃcacy, while negatively moderated the indirect eﬀect, whose
ratio in the total eﬀect dropped compared with the direct eﬀect. Combining the direct
and indirect eﬀects of the mediation model, science self-eﬃcacy still had a stronger connection with inquiry-based learning when students perceived more teacher support, not
less. The moderating role of teacher support in the mediation model indicated individual
diﬀerences in the generation of science self-eﬃcacy with diﬀerent levels of the intrinsic
factor of science interest and the extrinsic factor of teacher support.
The mediating–moderating model
The ﬁnal mediating–moderating model is illuminating for improving the practical eﬀect
of inquiry-based learning. Considerable studies using TIMSS and PISA data have shown
no signiﬁcant correlation or even a signiﬁcant negative correlation between inquiry-based
learning and science achievement (Cairns & Areepattamannil, 2019; Jerrim et al., 2019;
Jiang & McComas, 2015). Researchers have realised that in view of the complexity of conducting inquiry-based learning activities, extra support for teachers was needed (Gao
et al., 2020), or else inquiry-based learning would not be successful (Kaya & Rice, 2010;
Pine et al., 2006). Studies claiming a positive correlation between inquiry-based learning
and students’ performance usually included interventions such as eﬀective professional
development courses or special curriculum materials (Dobber et al., 2017; Taraban
et al., 2007; Wilson et al., 2010). Considering that relevant professional development projects still have some ways to go (Fitzgerald et al., 2019; Wood, 2018), the generated model
in this study would be valuable in designing projects concerning how teachers should
promote students’ science self-eﬃcacy with the involvement of science interest and
teacher support during inquiry-based learning (Anderhag et al., 2015).

Conclusions and limitations
In conclusion, our model revealed ‘how’ (via the mediation of science interest) and
‘when’ (with diﬀerent levels of teacher support) inquiry-based learning was associated

1108

Y. LIU AND J. WANG

with science self-eﬃcacy, thereby contributing to the theoretical studies and practical
applications of inquiry-based learning. Nonetheless, the current study still has the following limitations.
First, ﬁner-grained studies on the internal factors of this model are needed, such as
studies separating students into groups with diﬀerent levels of variables (Forbes et al.,
2020) and depicting their learning process explicitly. For certain kinds of students,
such as those with high science interest but low science self-eﬃcacy, how should teachers treat them individually to facilitate their success in inquiry-based learning? The
answers to these questions might be found with the help of research instruments
other than the self-reported questionnaires used by PISA, such as video analysis,
through which a detailed pattern describing certain behaviours of adequate teacher
support, forms of eﬀective inquiry-based learning activities and equipment of
inquiry classrooms could be extracted. In addition, in order to avoid lopsidedly attributing the total variance in science self-eﬃcacy to only student-level factors involved
in this study, further studies using multi-level modelling or hierarchical linear modelling techniques with the consideration of school-level and country-level factors are
needed.
Second, there might be external factors linked with science self-eﬃcacy in inquirybased learning other than science interest and teacher support (Areepattamannil, 2012;
Kang, 2022). Exploring other possible mediators, moderators as well as control variables
other than gender and disciplinary climate could be promising for reﬁning our model.
Moreover, as science self-eﬃcacy has diﬀerent sources (Bandura, 1997) and inquirybased learning has complex impacts on students (Kaya & Rice, 2010; Pine et al., 2006),
the existing rules of thumb for interpreting the eﬀect sizes in our model should be
treated ﬂexibly (Bosco et al., 2015; Cohen, 1988). Since the coeﬃcients for the two interactions in the moderation model are rather small, additional external factors are needed
to help set benchmarks of the eﬀect sizes, so that the mechanism of self-eﬃcacy development can be better understood by comparing the eﬀect sizes of diﬀerent factors. Please
note that the data analysed in the current study were cross sectional, so the results
should be interpreted as correlations instead of causal links.
Third, the levels of science interest, science self-eﬃcacy and teacher support change
with time and age (De Wit et al., 2010; Tracey, 2002; Vedder-Weiss & Fortus, 2011).
It remains unknown whether this dynamic interaction might change the pathway
from inquiry-based learning to science self-eﬃcacy, since PISA samples consisted of
merely 15-year-old teenagers. More longitudinal studies and experimental studies are
needed to develop a possible dynamic model for the relationship between inquirybased learning and science self-eﬃcacy.
