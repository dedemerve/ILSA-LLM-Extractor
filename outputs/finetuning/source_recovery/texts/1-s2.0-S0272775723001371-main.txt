ABSTRACT
Educational performance gaps are a long-lasting issue of concern in many countries. Many times, these gaps
are measured using low-stakes tests that are especially sensitive to motivation and test engagement. I conduct
a field experiment in 7 Israeli Jewish and Arab middle schools and use data from PISA 2015 to investigate
whether part of the performance gaps between ethnic minority and majority groups could be attributed to
test engagement rather than proficiency. I find that test engagement can account for more than 50% of the
performance gaps measured in low-stakes assessments. Test engagement is also important, as it correlates
with several personality traits important to life outcomes. Therefore, considering the importance of both test
engagement, which influences low-stakes assessments, and proficiency, which is more accurately discerned
in high-stakes assessments, policymakers should make deliberate decisions regarding the stakes assigned to
assessment tests, taking into account the specific evaluation criteria that they aim to emphasize.

1. Introduction
Educational inequality in general, and the educational underperformance of minority groups in particular, is a long-standing issue of
debate and concern. Examples include Hispanic and Black compared to
white students in the United States (see, for example, Currie & Thomas,
1999; de Brey et al., 2019; McDonough, 2015), immigrants to nonimmigrants in OECD countries (Cerna, Brussino, & Mezzanotte, 2021;
Giannelli & Rapallini, 2016), and Arabs compared to Jewish students
in Israel (RAMA, 2017).
One of the main tools used to assess the performance of educational systems and organizations is standardized assessment tests.1
Many of these tests have no consequences for the examinee (low-stakes
tests). Therefore, the student’s performance depends not only on their
proficiency but also, to a higher degree than in high-stakes assessments, on other factors. Intrinsic motivation is an important example.
Heterogeneity in levels of intrinsic motivation (or other factors that
change with stakes) across groups of interest can make cross-group
performance comparisons sensitive to the level of test stakes. Ignoring
this characteristic of low-stakes assessments could lead policymakers to
the wrong conclusions and even distort good policy design.

Heterogeneous engagement levels in low-stakes assessments as a
threat to the validity of proficiency comparisons are a well-known
phenomenon (e.g. Akyol, Krishna, & Wang, 2021; Balart & Oosterveen,
2019; Borgonovi & Biecek, 2016; Robitzsch, 2021). Still, most of the literature has focused on cross-country comparisons and used self-reports
or proxies for motivation. These methods cannot reliably identify the
impact of engagement separately from the effect of proficiency. Experimental evidence that could identify the effect of engagement by
changing the stakes level is scarce and usually uses financial incentives
that are less natural to the education system. To the best of my knowledge, no paper has yet studied the effect of change in test stakes on the
performance comparison of minority and majority school students.
This paper combines engagement measures based on observational
data from PISA 2015 and direct evidence from a field experiment to test
the hypothesis that the proficiency of minority students on low-stakes
assessments is underestimated due to low test-taking engagement. The
experimental results also allow quantifying the effect of this underestimation on the change in performance-gap measurements using low and
naturally high-stakes tests.

test-taking engagement in PISA 2015 and find that in Israel, the United

the test than the average majority student.2 I follow previous studies
that identify and filter out unmotivated students or rapid guesses and
find no significant effect of this lower engagement on performance
gaps measurements. These results are similar to previous studies but
are not conclusive evidence, as engagement and proficiency could
be correlated. To directly address this challenge, I conduct a field
experiment.
I use a field experiment to learn if low test-taking engagement of
minority students manifests into meaningful differences in minoritymajority performance gaps when measured using low or high-stakes
assessments. 599 students in the 8th grade from seven Jewish and
Arab schools in the north of Israel participated in the experiment.
Each student took two similar math tests — one with no feedback or
consequences (low-stakes) and the other, one week later, accounting
for 30% of the final year’s grade (naturally high-stakes).
I find that given a student’s high-stakes grades, an Arab-minority
student in Israel performs, on average, significantly worse on the lowstakes test. Their average performance, measured using a low-stakes
test, was one standard deviation lower than their performance measured using a high-stakes test. For the Jewish students, this difference
was only 0.7 standard deviations. Taken at face value, this suggests that
the Jewish-Arab gap in the 8th-grade is 60% lower than implied by the
official assessment test (0.15 instead of 0.38 standard deviations).
These findings have two important implications. First, it indicates
that one should be careful when using low-stakes assessment tests
to measure proficiency gaps between minority and majority students.
Second, it provides evidence that low-stakes assessment tests capture a
combination of proficiency and other traits, such as intrinsic motivation
and personality traits. As all are important to the future outcomes
of students, it is not only a limitation but also a property (see for
example, Borghans, Meijers, & Ter Weel, 2008; Borgonovi, Ferrara, &
Piacentini, 2021; Kautz, Heckman, Diris, Ter Weel, & Borghans, 2014;
Segal, 2012). Therefore, researchers and policymakers should choose
their assessment tools according to their outcome of interest.
This paper improves the previous experimental literature on lowstakes assessment tests by combining a within-subject design in a
natural-incentives setting on a population of interest. Braun, Kirsch,
and Yamamoto (2011), Gneezy et al. (2019), Schlosser, Neeman, and
Attali (2019) are three papers close to this one, comparing cross-group
heterogeneity in response to incentives on assessment tests. Braun
et al. (2011) show that contingent monetary incentives improve the
average performance of 12th grade students in the United States by
0.13–0.25 standard deviations. They further compare the effect on
ethnicity subgroups and find the effect was most pronounced for white
students. However, the overall effect of incentives is relatively small,
so comparing the findings to this paper’s is hard. Gneezy et al. (2019)
focus on the differential effect of incentives and compare performance
with and without financial incentives offered right before the test to
high-school students in the US and Shanghai. They find that US students
improve their performance substantially with incentives, while Shanghai students do not. In their concluding remarks, the researchers call for
further exploration of their findings in other contexts, such as gender
or ethnicity. To the best of my knowledge, Schlosser et al. (2019) is
the only experimental study that compares the performance of minority
and majority groups on low and naturally high-stakes tests. In contrast
to my findings, they find that holding high-stakes grades constant, the
white majority performs the worst in the low-stakes part. However,
they compare adult GRE examinees at the end of the GRE. Thus, their
results reflect adults at the upper part of the skill distribution, so they
might not generalize well to the assessment of average school students.
This paper extends the above papers by combining the within-subject
design in a natural-incentives setting and minority-majority comparison
used in Schlosser et al. (2019) with the test structure and age of interest
as in Braun et al. (2011), Gneezy et al. (2019).

This paper also contributes to a developing strand of the literature that uses regression analysis or motivation filtering to examine
the effect of heterogeneous engagement on assessments of groups’
performance and ranking. It highlights the strengths and limitations
of these methods by comparing their findings to the experimental
findings. Akyol et al. (2021), Borghans and Schils (2018), Borgonovi
and Biecek (2016), Zamarro, Hitt, and Mendez (2019) find a high correlation between engagement and cross-country performance variation
in PISA assessments. However, after controlling for this variation, only
Borghans and Schils (2018) find a meaningful change in the ranking
of the countries. Several other papers examined the effects of heterogeneous test-taking engagement within-country across genders, ethnic
groups, and schools (Anaya & Zamarro, 2023; Balart & Oosterveen,
2019; Eichmann, Goldhammer, Greiff, Brandhuber, & Naumann, 2020;
Soland, 2018; Wise, Soland, & Bo, 2020). They also find significant
differences in engagement. Some also implement motivation filtering, but the results regarding the change in performance gaps when
implementing motivation filtering are mixed.
I follow this strand and compare the results of motivation filtering
in Section 2 to the experimental results in Section 4. Using motivation filtering, I find similar results to other papers that used this
methodology. Performance gaps do not change when we filter out
unmotivated students or responses. For the Israeli case, this contradicts
the experimental results. I suggest that these papers fail to capture
the full effect of low engagement because they rely on too strong
assumptions and conclude that methods based on engagement measures
are suitable for indicating a possible heterogeneity in engagement but
less reliable in evaluating the magnitude of their effect on performance
measurements.3
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides suggestive evidence that minority groups are less engaged with low-stakes PISA
assessments. Section 3 presents the experimental design and empirical
framework I use to compare performance in high and low-stakes tests.
Section 4 presents and discusses the experimental results and their
implications, and Section 5 concludes.
2. Minority students are less engaged — evidence from PISA assessments
The PISA 2015 test results indicate that minority groups in the
United States and Israel, as well as immigrants in Europe, tend to have
lower levels of engagement, which may lead to an underestimation
of their proficiency level. Motivation filtering methods do not change
the performance gaps between minority and majority groups. This
could mean low minority engagement is not a concern for the validity
of minority-majority performance gap measurements but stands in
contrast to the experimental results presented in the following sections.
2.1. Minority students skip and guess more
Starting in 2015, PISA data include response time per item measurements — the time it took the examinee to answer each question.
This allows us to identify items the student chose not to meaningfully
engage with by – 1. skipping with no response, and 2. answering very
quickly without devoting enough time to solve the problem – ‘‘guessingbehavior’’. Based on a methodology developed by Wise and Kong (2005)
(see also Borgonovi, Ferrara, & Piacentini, 2023; Ulitzsch, von Davier, &
Pohl, 2020), I identify the guessing behavior of students if they answer
a question in a very short time.4 Then, I use the level of skipped and

There are a few reasons to consider that the aforementioned two
assumptions are not fully valid. First, while Segal (2012), Wise and
DeMars (2005) do not find a correlation between engagement and
proficiency as measured in another incentivized test, Rios, Guo, Mao,
and Liu (2017) do find that it is correlated. Deribo, Kroehne, and

use observatory data to show that rapid-guessing and skipping are not
random across test items. Second, the level of effort is not binary. While
not guessing, partly motivated students tend to exert less effort in a
low-stakes test (Gneezy et al., 2019).
As I will show in Section 4.1 the results from the field experiment
suggest these assumptions are indeed too strong, and lower engagement
of minority groups is a concern when comparing performance using
low-stakes assessments.

2.2. Using motivation filtering does not change the measurements
Implementing motivation filtering on the PISA data, I find that
performance gaps remain unchanged. However, this methodology is
based on a few strong assumptions. This motivates the use of the
field experiment that directly measures whether performance gaps
measurements are sensitive to test stakes and test the validity of the
motivation filtering.
Wise and DeMars (2005) are the first to suggest the use of Motivation filtering. They identify the less motivated students and exclude
them from the analysis. By comparing only engaged students, they
argue that they improve the accuracy of their proficiency measurements. More recent papers suggest excluding only responses that are
not answered in an engaged manner — this is mainly identified by
skipped items or responses with very short response times (see, for
example, Akyol et al., 2021; Soland, 2018). By doing so, they claim
to ‘‘correct for non-serious behavior’’ (Akyol et al., 2021). I implement
both methods on the PISA data.
Table 1 shows the effect of filtering unmotivated students or unengaged items on the score and performance of each group. For the
student filtering, I follow Wise and DeMars (2010) and exclude students
that guessed or skipped more than 10% of the items.6 As expected,
almost all groups improved their rate of correct answers using both
methods. However, their percentile ranks do not meaningfully change,
and more importantly, the percentile differences between the minority and majority groups do not change. Filtering only guessed and
unanswered items lead to similar results.
The result that implementing motivation filtering does not meaningfully alter the performance gaps between groups of interest is in
line with the findings of Soland (2018). However, it does not mean
that differences in test-taking motivation or engagement have no effect
on performance-gaps measurements. The ability of motivation filtering
to ‘‘correct for non-serious behavior’’ (Akyol et al., 2021) is based on
two assumptions: (1) there is no correlation between proficiency and
motivation, and (2) students are either fully motivated or unmotivated.
If proficiency and motivation are correlated, filtering out unmotivated students (item answers) means positively selecting the students
(answers) population leading to overestimating the students’ average
proficiency. If there are students that are only partly unmotivated,

3. Design
3.1. Institutional setting
The Israeli population is comprised of 73.9% Jews, 21.1% Arabs,
and 5% other ethnicities. The Jewish population is mostly secular or
religious-orthodox, with a minority of ultra-orthodox. The Arab population is primarily Muslim, with minorities of Christians and Druze.
Schools in Israel are usually segregated by religion or a branch of
religion, so secular Jewish students study with secular Jewish, religious
with religious, and so on. The evaluation of the schools is carried out
by RAMA — National Authority for Measurement and Evaluation in
Education, using anonymous low-stakes tests taken in the 2nd, 5th,
and 8th-grades. Each school participates in the national assessments
once every three years and is allowed to take the assessment tests as
an internal exam in the other years.
On average, in most fields and ages, Jewish students outperform
Arab students (RAMA, 2017). According to the National Israeli Standardized Assessment Tests — GEMS,7 the performance difference between the 8th grade students in math is about one-third of a standard

3.2. Experimental design
I use a field experiment to examine to what extent minority-majority
performance gaps measurements change with the level of stakes. I use a
within-subject design to compare the performance of 8th-grade Jewish
majority and Arab minority students on low and (a week later) highstakes mathematics tests.8 The experiment was conducted in the spring
of 2017 in the northern district of Israel.
The tests were written in the National Israeli Standardized Assessment Test — GEMS format. Hebrew-speaking students took the tests in
Hebrew, and Arabic-speaking students took the tests in Arabic.9
The high-stakes test was written by RAMA (Israeli National Authority for Measurement and Evaluation in Education) for the GEMS
of 2017. Only one-third of the schools participate in the national
assessment tests every year. The other two-thirds are offered to take
the test as an internal test, and many of them use it as a final year test,
determining about 30% of the student’s final year’s grade. Compared to
most assessments, this is quite personally high-stakes for the students.
However, stakes are subjective. For some students, five points in the
final grade are of great importance, while for others, it might not
matter.10 I chose schools that took this offer and thus had the same
naturally high-stakes test across all participating schools.11
As a low-stakes test, I wrote a math test with the help of the
Mathematics Supervision Department that maintains the structure and
difficulty level of the GEMS math test. Schools’ teachers scored the
high-stakes test according to RAMA’s detailed scoring guide. The experiment team scored the low-stakes test using a similar detailed scoring
guide.
Schools in Israel are usually segregated.12 In the analysis, I use the
results of four Jewish schools – two secular, two religious, and three
Arab schools – one Muslim and two Christian.13
While the sample is not statistically representative, I selected the
schools with the advice of the Mathematics Supervision Department
in the Israeli Ministry of Education so that they represent common
ability schools for their ethnicity. Based on the national assessment tests
conducted in the years before the experiment — the Jewish schools
are in deciles 8, 5, 4, 3 and the Arab schools are in deciles 9, 8, 4.
Unfortunately, two Arab schools, ranked in deciles 3 and 4, dropped

All 8th-grade students in the participating schools were assigned
to take both tests. The first test was the low-stakes test. The teachers
notified the students a few days before the test that it would take place.
The students were informed several times that they would not receive a

Still, they were requested to do their best, both for their contribution to
science and as preparation for the high-stakes test. In a questionnaire
handed after the test, I verified that the students understood that the
test was a low-stakes one.14 The second test was a high-stakes test. It
was conducted a week later, and all the students knew it a few weeks
in advance. The students were well notified that this was a high-stakes
test determining about 30% of their final year’s grade.
The first test was not mandatory due to IRB requirements, but
thanks to the commitment of the schools’ staff, most of the students
participated in the experiment. The second test was part of the schools’
syllabus and thus mandatory. Of the seven schools that successfully
participated, 734 students took the high-stakes test, and 599 took both
tests. The only data on students who did not take the low-stakes test is
their high-stakes grades. The students that took both tests had slightly
higher high-stakes grades (see Appendix Table A.2). Table 2 presents
the population included in the experiment analysis by gender and
ethnicity after excluding outliers.15
The selection into the experiment is a limitation of the design. If
the selection is correlated with motivation to do well in the tests and
is heterogeneous across ethnic groups, this could affect the results. To
some extent, this is always a threat to identification in experimental
settings with non-mandatory participation. The within-subject design
allows me to examine how the results change across the performance
distribution, but this is only a partial solution.
The timeline of the experiment poses a few additional challenges to
the identification. First, due to IRB restrictions and school preferences,
I could not randomize the tests to high and low stakes in content
or order.16 This makes the design less clean, as it might violate the
assumption of causal transience. If one group responds differently to
the initial test, it has the potential to influence the overall results.
One heterogeneous response can be in learning gains from the test
itself. Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Schwieren, Barenberg, and Dutke
(2017), Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu, and Shanks (2021) document a significant positive effect of test-taking in improving learning processes.

material. Both low and high-stakes tests are conducted at the end of

possible effect of test-taking. Moreover, Yang et al. (2021) found no

across ethnicity, race, or other minority-majority groups.17 A second
source of heterogeneity can arise in the impact of learning following the
first test. With a one-week interval between the first and second tests,
the difference between the two tests captures variations in motivation

4.5. Implications
The large and significant changes in grades and performance gaps
between high and low-stakes tests across ethnic groups contribute to an
increasing body of evidence suggesting caution when using the results
of low-stakes standardized assessment tests to assess proficiency.
Reassessing national assessment tests results. To give a sense of the scale
of changes in measurement, I calculated how low-stakes assessment
results change if we ‘‘correct’’ them using the experiment’s results —
‘‘as if it was a high-stakes test’’. This is possible under the assumption
that the performance difference between the experimental low and
high-stakes results is similar to the difference between the low-stakes
national assessment test results and hypothetical high-stakes national
assessment test results. The Jewish-Arab math performance gap on
the low-stakes national assessment tests is 10.89 on a 0–100 scale.
𝐺𝑟𝑎𝑑𝑒𝐻𝑆 − 𝐺𝑟𝑎𝑑𝑒𝐿𝑆 in the experiment is 6.4 points larger for Arab
students (see Table 3). Assuming this is also the difference in the
population, if the national assessment tests were high-stakes, the grades
for both groups would increase. Still, the average grade of the Arab
students would increase by 6.4 points more, and so the performance
gap would decrease from 10.89 (0.38 standard deviations) to 4.39
(0.15) points, a 60% decrease.24 There are several other ways to do
this transformation, but they provide similar results.25

4.4. Possible mechanisms
Using a post-test questionnaire and an analysis of the skipped items
in the low-stakes test, I provide suggestive evidence that test-taking
engagement in the low-stakes test explains part of the performance
difference between the high and low-stakes test. Using the post-test
questionnaire from the field experiment, I find that students’ performance difference between the high and low-stakes tests is correlated
with reported effort. For the Arab students only, the performance difference is also correlated with the number of skipped items in the test.
However, the questionnaire results do not support the hypothesis that
performance difference negatively correlates with a sense of belonging.
These results are further expanded in Appendix C
To evaluate the level of engagement with the low-stakes test, I count
the number of items left unanswered (skipped items) per student. If a
high number of skipped items indicates low test-taking engagement in
the low-stakes test, we expect more room for improvement in the highstakes test. So a high number of skipped items should be correlated with
a bigger grade difference between the high and low-stakes tests. For the
pooled sample, I do not find such a correlation. Zooming in on both
ethnicity groups, I find that for Arab students, there is a statistically
significant correlation. Each additional skipped item is correlated with
a 0.04 standard deviation (0.94 points in the test) increase in the grade
difference between the high and low-stakes test (see Table A.5).
After the low-stakes test, students were asked to rank their level of
effort in the test and a hypothetical high-stakes test on a 1 to 10 scale.
Using this ranking, I find that the performance difference between the
high and low-stakes tests is correlated with the difference in reported
effort. The lower the average difference in reported effort between the
tests, the lower the average performance difference between the tests.
The results of the post-test questionnaire in the experiment showed
that a sense of belonging is not correlated with low-stakes test engagement. A sense of belonging fosters intrinsic motivation that increases
engagement in low-stakes assessments. In the post-test questionnaire,
we asked the students how much they care about their school and
hometown to measure their sense of belonging. Their responses are not
correlated with performance differences between the tests, thus, not
supporting this mechanism as an explanation for the results.

Table 4 and Appendix Fig. A.8 present the change in performance
gaps within gender groups and in multi-year comparison. The Arab
gender gap in favor of females almost disappears, and the Jewish
gender gap reverses from a slight advantage for males to a small
advantage for females.
It is essential to acknowledge that this study’s population does not
represent a comprehensive sample of Israeli students. The inclusion
of Arab schools exhibits a slight positive bias, while there is a slight
underrepresentation of Muslim students. Consequently, it is prudent to

findings are supported by evidence from PISA, robustness to Entropy

Challenges to implementing motivation filtering. An additional implication of this paper’s results concerns attempts to adjust low-stakes
assessments using motivation filtering or regression based on test engagement. To identify the full extent to which proficiency measurements in low-stakes are sensitive to low engagement, motivation filtering requires that students or item answers are either unengaged
or fully engaged and that engagement is uncorrelated with students’
proficiency.
The experimental evidence suggests that filtering out very lowmotivation students cannot fully correct the overall effect of low motivation in low-stakes assessments. I implement motivation filtering
in Section 2 and find that minority-majority performance gaps in
PISA do not change if I exclude unmotivated students or items identified as a skip or a rapid guess. While this is supposed to mimic a
change from low to high-stakes assessment, this result contrasts the
significant change in performance gaps when stakes change in the
experiment. Experimental evidence further shows that the assumptions
behind motivation filtering might be too strong. Most students perform
significantly better on the high-stakes test, which suggests that it is
problematic to assume that students are either disengaged or fully
engaged during the low-stakes assessment. Many are partly engaged,
and excluding the students with the lowest engagement cannot control
this. The experimental results further show that most of the difference
in performance gaps comes from differences in the performance of
above-median students, suggesting the assumption that engagement is
not correlated with proficiency might also be problematic.
I conclude that methods based on motivation filtering are suitable
for indicating between-group differences in test-taking engagement and
intrinsic motivation but are less reliable in evaluating the magnitude of
their effect on performance measurements.

Appendix A. Data — PISA analysis
The Programme for International Students Assessment (PISA) assesses the proficiency of 15-year-old students in the core school subjects
of science, reading, and mathematics. PISA surveys take place every
three years. I chose to study the 2015 results as they are the first to be
almost fully computerized. I use the math items to compare the analysis
to the field experiment. Approximately 540,000 students participated
in the 2015 wave representing a sample from 29 million students in 72
countries and economies (OECD, 2016).
The PISA administration places significant importance on ensuring
the representativeness of its results. Approximately 5,000 students are
sampled in each country, with a two-step random selection process
involving schools and individual participants. Minority groups are deliberately ‘‘over-sampled’’ to enhance the precision of the assessment.
Additionally, each student is assigned sample weights to ensure that
the average results accurately reflect the entire population, considering
factors such as age, language, and other covariates.
For the specific scope of this study, the oversampling of minority
students proves advantageous, as it allows for larger samples, enabling more robust evaluations. All the analysis using the PISA data is
weighted by the PISA senate weights. These weights balance the impact
of different student populations and the different countries.
The main part of the PISA survey is a two-hour exam composed
of four 30 min parts (clusters). Students have 60 min for the first two
clusters, followed by a few minutes break in some countries (depending
on the local choice), and then 60 min for clusters 3 and 4. Since science
was the major domain in 2015, each student was randomly assigned
to 2 (out of 12 different) science clusters and two other clusters from
reading (out of 6), mathematics (out of 6), or Collaborative problem
solving (out of 4) which was a special domain added to PISA 2015.
Publicly available data includes student-level data - students’ characteristics and final scores calculated by the OECD and raw data for
each response (answer to an item) - raw answer, response time, number
of actions - and score.27 Ethnicity is not part of the PISA student
questionnaire, so I had to collect this information.28

5. Conclusions
In this paper, I experimentally find that the lower engagement
of minority groups in low-stakes assessments can cause measures of
performance gaps to be twice as high as when measured using highstakes assessments that are less sensitive to lower engagement. This
difference in results of high and low-stakes tests can lead to distorted
or misleading conclusions when evaluating the skills of minority groups
and the effect of policy measures targeted at these groups. Researchers
and policymakers must be aware of these differences and limitations
and make sure they choose the proper method to evaluate students’
abilities. If the main goal is to address cognitive skills, high-stakes
testing, less sensitive to the heterogeneous effort, is better. Low-stakes
assessments could be better if the main goal is to predict future labor
market outcomes, as they capture a combination of cognitive and
non-cognitive skills that are both of interest.26

the guessing behavior thresholds, each response can be classified as a
