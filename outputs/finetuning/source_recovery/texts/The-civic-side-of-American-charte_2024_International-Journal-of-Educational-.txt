A B S T R A C T
There is a notable cross-national effort to incorporate market-based policies in education. This article examines 
one such context, the United States (US). Specifically, we examine the civic side of American charter schools 
through a human capital lens. Comparison of National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) Civics grade 8 
2006, 2010, 2014, and 2018 restricted use data provided awareness of micro-level factors related to youth civic 
knowledge and skill acquisition. Analyses of multiple testing cycles helped further identify patterns and re­
lationships over time that are essential in understanding variations in civic learning across periods and school 
types. Results indicated that American charter schools demonstrated higher average civic performance scores for 
most of the testing cycles than traditional public schools. Black and Hispanic American charter and private school 
students demonstrate higher civic performance levels. Key factors such as teacher time spent on civics instruction 
and teacher credentials are not necessarily correlated with higher civic scores. Though these differences should 
be interpreted with caution, the findings have implications for market-based education policies writ large, 
specifically for charter and charter-like schools in the global north and south.
1. Introduction
There is a notable cross-national effort to incorporate market-based 
policies in education (Hicks, 2015; Gereluk et al., 2016). Though spe­
cific structures and names may differ, concepts similar to those of the 
American charter school exist in both the global north and global south 
(Eyles et al., 2016; Pineda et al., 2022). These charter and charter-like 
schools seek not only to expand equity and opportunity to youth but 
also to develop citizens who will engage in and appreciate core demo­
cratic processes and values (Eyles et al., 2016; Gill et al., 2020); in fact, 
such educative spaces offer youth opportunities to “explore what it 
means to be a member of a political community and can digest core civic 
knowledge and practice the rights and obligations associated with 
membership in that community” (Flanagan et al., 2011, p. 102 as cited 
in Johnson et al., 2020). Limited inquiries have addressed the civic side 
of charter and charter-like schools in democratic societies, even though 
these schools are important influencers of civic knowledge and skills 
(Johnson et al., 2023). The increscent scale and novel structure of 
charter and charter-like schools globally foment a curiosity as to 
whether these market-based educative settings support youth’s civic 
development (Johnson et al., 2020).
To that end, this article examines the civic side of charter and 
charter-like schools in one such democratic context, the United States 
(US). Unlike prior research that analyzes the efficiency of American 
charter schools according to academic success, secondary and post- 
secondary graduation rates and earnings and employability of charter 
school graduates (e.g., Betts and Tang, 2016; Center for Research on 
Education Outcomes CREDO, 2023; Gao and Semykina, 2021; Hoxby 
et al., 2009; Zimmer et al., 2009), this study seeks to clarify the civic side 
of these schools. American charter schools increasingly symbolize a 
metastasizing neo-liberal education reform agenda, whereby markets 
offer a promising panacea for enhancing academic achievement and 
closing opportunity gaps, particularly for those residing on the margins 
of society (Johnson et al., 2020). American charter school proponents 
further suggest that these sites serve “as instruments of civil society as 
well as places of teaching and learning” that not only celebrate cultural 
uniqueness but also expand civic opportunities (Johnson et al., 2023; 
Wilson, 2016, p. 921; see also Finn et al., 2000), and “offer new possi­
bilities for democratic engagement, power, and decision-making in ed­
ucation” (Wilson, 2016, p. 921). Despite proclamations of civic value, 
American charter schools’ civic advantages and efficacy remain clouded 
(Johnson et al., 2020, 2023).
Empirically, our study focuses on the National Assessment of 
Educational Progress Civics grade 8 (NAEP-Civics8) data across the

reflect correlations that may be influenced by factors outside the scope

Conceptually, we apply a human capital lens to examine the civic 
side of American charter schools. The usefulness of human capital theory 
as a lens for examining the civic side of American charter schools resides 
in its ability to a) delineate the civic purpose of schooling in a global age 
of neoliberal education policies characterized by a market-driven 
agenda that values accountability and standardization, b) explain the 
multifaceted connection between American charter school policies and 
the market-oriented functionality of schooling as a return on invest­
ment, and c) provide a roadmap for conceptualizing and structuring 
future work that examines the civic side of charter and charter-like 
schools across the global north and global south. From a functional 
education narrative, human capital theory emphasizes the economic 
value of investing in education to enhance earning potential and eco­
nomic and societal productivity (Marginson, 2019; Schultz, 1961a, 
1961b; Wolf and McShane, 2013). Human capital theory also reinforces 
one of the primary objectives of schooling: to provide students with 
valuable civic knowledge and skills to "prepare effective citizens and, 
therefore, to enhance and stabilize the common good" (Lasley and 
Ridenour, 2005, pp. 35–36). It yields a macro approach for contextu­
alizing the charter school policy goal of enhancing the educational 
quality for and expanding opportunities, including civic development 
opportunities, to those historically marginalized communities, thus 
explicating the multifarious relationship between American charter 
school policy and the functionality of schooling as a societal return on 
investment. The exportation of market-based education policies calls 
into question whether settings like charter and charter-like schools 
prepare youth for participation in democratic societies. With few in­
quiries addressing international trends related to whether or how these 
schools shape youth civic knowledge and skill development, the human 
capital theory may assist researchers in using theory to structure civic 
performance outcomes across different settings.
The following section addresses how civic knowledge and skill are 
measured internationally and within the US. From there, we dive into 
the charter school literature, emphasizing growth, demographics, and 
whether or how American charter schools expand educational quality 
and opportunity to students. We conclude the literature review by 
emphasizing the role and influence of American charter schools and 
teacher credentials on civic engagement and learning outcomes.
2. Measuring civic knowledge and skill across contexts
What do we know about the various tools to measure civic knowl­
edge and skill across contexts? Research tends to highlight two 
prominent international instruments used to monitor and compare stu­
dents’ civic knowledge and skills, identify areas for improvement in 
civics education, and inform policy decisions related to civic education 
and citizenship development (Joris and Agirdag, 2019; Torney-Purta 
et al., 2001, 2007). The first instrument, the International Civic and 
Citizenship Education Study (ICCS), assesses students’ civic knowledge 
and engagement in various countries (Joris and Agirdag, 2019; Schulz 
et al., 2016). The second, the Programme for International Student 
Assessment (PISA), includes a component on students’ knowledge of 
civic and global issues (De Freitas et al., 2019). Both assessments are 
designed to evaluate students’ understanding of democratic principles, 
political systems, the rights and responsibilities of citizens, and their 
capacity to engage in civic activities (Joris and Agirdag, 2019).
In the US, the NAEP Civics assessment is the primary tool for eval­
uating students’ civic knowledge and skills nationally. This assessment is 
crucial for understanding civics knowledge and skills necessary for 
active citizenship in the constitutional democracy of the US. Unlike state 
assessments that focus on individual student achievement based on 
state-specific content standards, the National Center for Education Sta­
tistics (NCES) oversees the administration of the uniform NAEP assess­
ment across all states. This standardized approach offers educators, 
policymakers, and parents a consistent measure of student civic 
achievement (National Center for Education Statistics, 2018).
Administered digitally every four years to students in grades 4, 8, 
and 12, the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) uses a 
diverse sample of students from various school types nationwide to 
ensure that the results accurately reflect American students’ perfor­
mance. Students typically spend between 90 and 120 minutes during 
regular school hours completing the civics assessment, which includes 
time for a survey questionnaire. On assessment day, NAEP representa­
tives provide all necessary materials, such as tablets or laptops, to the 
schools. Accommodations are available for students with disabilities and 
English learners (National Center for Education Statistics, 2018). The 
most recent civics assessment, conducted in 2022, involved approxi­
mately 7800 students in grade 8.1
NAEP presents student civics achievement data using two metrics: 
scale scores and achievement levels. Scale scores indicate student per­
formance on the civics assessment and are aggregated and reported for 
various student groups at a national level. The NAEP assessment levels - 
basic, proficient, and advanced - are determined by performance stan­
dards that outline the knowledge and skills students should possess. 
Students who perform at or above the NAEP Proficient level demon­
strate strong academic performance and mastery of the assessment 
content. It is important to clarify that the NAEP Proficient achievement 
level does not equate to grade-level proficiency as defined by other 
assessment standards, such as state or district assessments (Johnson 
et al., 2020; Authors, under review; National Center for Education Sta­
tistics, 2018).
3. Charters and school choice in context
Charter schools have emerged as a global force within the education 
landscape, rooted in expanding competition, autonomy, and flexibility 
in the education sector. Charters and charter-like schools are found in 
both the global north and global south (Eyles et al., 2016; Gereluk et al., 
2016; Hicks, 2015; Pineda et al., 2022). In Australia, the term "inde­
pendent public schools" describes schools with increased autonomy and 
decision-making power, similar to American charter schools. These 
schools have more control over their budgets, staffing, and curriculum, 
allowing them to customize their programs to address local needs. In 
Britain, academies and free schools share similarities with American

charter schools. Academies are schools that receive public funding but 
operate autonomously from local governments. On the other hand, free

3.1. American charter schools
3.1.1. Growth and demographics
The growth of American charter schools has been swift and wide­
spread (Johnson et al., 2023; Gawlik, 2016; Henig, 2018). With 46 
states/jurisdictions approving charter school legislation since 1991, 
there are currently 3.7 million students enrolled in 7800 charter schools, 
particularly in urban, high-poverty school districts (Johnson et al., 
2023). American charter schools are granted a charter or contract by a 
governing authority, such as a local school district or state education 
agency, which outlines their mission, goals, and accountability mea­
sures. The landscape of charter school authorizers is diverse, with states 
employing various bodies for authorization. Local education agencies 
(LEAs) authorize about half of all charter schools nationally, followed by 
state education agencies (SEAs), independent charter boards (ICBs), 
institutions of higher education (IHEs), and other organizations 
(National Association of Charter School Authorizers, 2022).
Various factors influence the growth and distribution of charter 
schools, including political climate, governance structure, and funding. 
Political factors, such as ideology and interest-group alignments, play a 
significant role in adoption and favorability of charter school legislation 
(Henig, 2018). States like Arizona, known for conservative policies, have 
embraced charters, while others like Michigan and California have seen 
extensive charter proliferation (Henig, 2018). The rise of management 
organizations has also influenced charter school expansion. A substan­
tial and increasing number of charter schools are now associated with 
management organizations that govern multiple schools within net­
works. Whether for-profit or nonprofit, these organizations exert 
considerable influence on the charter school landscape, accounting for a 
significant portion of charter school students. State accountability 
measures for charter schools vary widely, leading to heterogeneity in 
outcomes and practices.
Demographic-wise, American charter schools pepper urban land­
scapes and enroll elevated proportions of minoritized (i.e., racial/ 
ethnic) students compared to US traditional public schools (Authors, 
under review; Henig, 2018). Furthermore, urban charter schools have a 
higher representation of Black and Hispanic students who qualify for 
free or reduced lunch than the traditional public schools they draw 
students from (Johnson et al., 2023). Research conducted by Franken­
berg and Lee (2003) and Bifulco and Ladd (2006) on the racial de­
mographics of charter schools nationwide indicates that many have 
disproportionately high minority students. In states such as Ohio, Mis­
souri, and New Jersey, Black students constitute more than 70 % of the 
charter school enrollment, even though they represent less than 20 % of 
the overall public school population (Johnson et al., 2023). Charter 
schools are required to admit students without discriminatory criteria 
based on protected characteristics, as highlighted by Rotberg (2018). 
Similarly, charter schools are prohibited from denying admission to 
students based solely on disability (Johnson et al., 2023; see also Shum, 
2018). Studies have shown that charter schools tend to have lower 
enrollment rates of English-language learners (ELLs), students with 
disabilities, and low-income students (Johnson et al., 2023). This 
disparity can be attributed to factors such as restrictive enrollment 
practices, inadequate support services, and disciplinary policies that 
disproportionately affect at-risk students.
3.1.2. Educational quality and opportunity
A fundamental premise of the American charter school movement is 
that sites can offer innovative approaches, specialized programs, and 
tailored instruction that expands educational quality and opportunity to 
all students (Wilson, 2016). A small fraction of the literature considers 
American charter schools’ impact on student behavior and educational 
attainment (Betts and Tang, 2016). For the same reason, there is limited 
knowledge of how charters foster a competitive educational environ­
ment (Authors, under review). Research on the competitive nature of 
American charter schools tends to spotlight case studies delineating how 
traditional public school officials react to the specter of charter schools 
in their localities (i.e., Finn et al., 2000; Hess, 2002; Hess et al., 2001). 
However, Gao and Semykina (2021) found that American charter school 
penetration into an education market decreased average teacher cre­
dentials in adjacent traditional public schools.
Key studies have delineated whether or how American charters 
enhance the educational quality for and expand opportunities to his­
torically marginalized populations (Johnson et al., 2020) with ambig­
uous conclusions (Johnson et al., 2023; Betts and Tang, 2016). Hoxby 
et al. (2009) noted increased performance for students enrolled in 
charter schools in Boston and New York. Quasi-experimental studies, 
like those administered by the Center for Research on Education Out­
comes (CREDO) (2023) and Zimmer et al. (2012), indicated no statis­
tically significant charter-school effect. Betts and Tang (2016)’s 
meta-analysis on charter-school achievement distinguished charters as 
producing higher achievement gains in math. No meaningful differ­
ences, however, were found for reading. Regarding Black and Hispanic 
charter school students, Bifulco and Ladd (2006) found that Black and 
Hispanic charter school students underachieve when contrasted with 
their traditional public school peers. Others suggest conflicting or con­
tradictory results (Abdulkadiroglu et al., 2009; Hoxby et al., 2009).
3.1.3. American charter schools and teacher credentials: Elemental 
connections with civic development
In the US, schools and teachers have been recognized as formal ed­
ucation networks that seek to impart civic knowledge, skills, and posi­
tive civic attitudes (Niemi and Junn, 2005) for civic development. 
American charter schools, too, seek to develop students’ understanding 
and knowledge of society and ignite learners’ commitment to civic and 
political engagement (Homana et al., 2006). Gill et al. (2020) indicated 
that American charter school students were predicted to be more likely 
to vote when they attained the voting age. McEachin et al. (2020) re­
ported that students enrolled in American charter schools were highly 
likely to engage in the election process, thus involving themselves in 
registration and voting during an election period. Buckley and 
Schneider (2007)’s study also explored secondary students’ perspectives 
in the Washington D.C. region to determine charters’ effectiveness in 
enhancing civic education. Their study revealed a statistically signifi­
cant relationship between the secondary students’ community involve­
ment and civic-based skills and enrolment in charter schools. However, 
Buckley and Schneider’s (2007) research found no statistically signifi­
cant connection between students’ enrolment in American charter 
schools and their political tolerance. The study’s findings pinpoint that 
though charter schools play major roles in civic education, not all civic 
values might be achieved at a given time. Chudowsky et al. (2013) found 
no statistically significant correlation between school type and students’ 
civic achievement. Contrary to Chudowsky et al. (2013)’s study, Gordon 
(2011)’s research discovered a correlation between school-based civic 
education implemented in an urban American charter school and stu­
dents’ civic inspiration to involve themselves in community service.

4. Conceptual framework
4.1. Human capital theory
Human capital theory informs our inquiry and provides a prominent 
analytical framework for examining the civic side of charter schools 
both in the US and abroad. This framework has been influential in 
assessing the return on investment in education, beginning in the 1960s 
and 1970s with Becker’s (1964) and Schultz’s (1961a), b) seminal works 
correlating education and career choice as a method for understanding 
personal investment. Schultz (1961a) claimed that education must be 
comprehended as an investment mechanism due to its essential factor 
for economic growth (see also Min & Lynn, 2020). Formal education is 
recognized as a positive contributor toward building human capital 
through knowledge, skill, and competency acquisition, though building 
human capital is a complex integration of innate abilities, family so­
cioeconomic status, and education (Anderson and Keys, 2007; Checchi, 
2006; Gillies, 2017). Theorists note the theory’s limitations, particularly 
as it applies to education (e.g., Marginson, 2019; Min and Lynn, 2020; 
Tan, 2014). Primary areas of concern include viewing education in 
narrow economic terms, inattention to historical inequalities and lack of 
access, and the influence of non-economic factors such as motivation, 
social support, and culture on educational attainment (Gillies, 2017). 
Despite limitations, policymakers and other education stakeholders 
across varied contexts accept the broader implications of human capital 
theory, where theorists extend human capital theory to social policy 
discourse, particularly when it comes to enhancing equity and expand­
ing opportunity through education (Gillies, 2017). Human capital the­
ory allows researchers to delineate the civic purpose of schooling in a 
global age of neoliberal education policies characterized by a 
market-driven agenda that values accountability and standardization. 
Rooted in progressivism, American charter schools have come to 
represent a distinctive example of neoliberal education reform that en­
gages the market in developing human capital, particularly as it pertains 
to civic development (Johnson et al., 2020). Though free markets rarely 
consider the costs associated with market capitalism (Authors, under 
review; Dixson et al., 2015), we strive to unpack whether 
market-oriented education policies support a societal civic benefit.
Human capital development theory explains the multifaceted 
connection between American charter school policies and the market- 
oriented functionality of schooling as a return on investment. The 
relationship between human development theory and charter schools is 
multifaceted and encompasses various dimensions, including account­
ability, educational attainment, organizational structure, student 
achievement, and societal stratification (Chapman and Donnor, 2015; 
Gawlik, 2016; Berends, 2015). Fundamentally, human capital develop­
ment theory provides an inquiry roadmap to comprehensively examine 
the civic side of charters and charter-like schools globally. In this case, 
the theory offers a structure for comprehending, interpreting, and 
explaining the civic side of American charter schools and a conceptual 
tool for examining and challenging the efficacy of market-based edu­
cation policies here in the US. Moreover, challenging the efficacy of 
market-based education policies here in the US can allow researchers to 
explore the global implications of market-oriented charter and school 
choice policies on civic development across democratic societies.
5. Current study
The current study puts forward an empirical and conceptual frame­
work for examining the civic side of American charter schools. We 
comparatively analyzed the restricted-use data for the 2006, 2010, 
2014, and 2018 NAEP-Civics8 assessments, as NCES has only made the 
restricted-use data for these four testing cycles available to researchers. 
Restricted-use data offers a higher level of detail compared to public-use 
data, such as web-based NAEP data. Whereas web-based NAEP analysis 
tools permit preliminary exploration of public use data to formulate 
hypotheses, restricted use data allows secondary analyses of micro-level 
data, including student and teacher responses, teacher credentials, 
parental education levels, and regional civic mean scores. Students 
complete questionnaires that provide information on a range of topics 
such as their learning opportunities both in and out of the classroom, 
educational experiences, socioeconomic status, and technology usage. 
Students have the option to skip any question by leaving it unanswered. 
In addition, teachers overseeing the subject of the assessment are also 
required to complete questionnaires that focus on their training and 
instructional methods (National Center for Education Statistics, 2019). 
It is important to note that student responses on the NAEP are kept 
confidential, and safeguarding the privacy of both participating schools 
and students is a top priority (National Center for Education Statistics, 
2019). This paper concentrates on student civic mean scores and teacher 
responses across American charter schools, traditional public schools, 
and private schools. This afforded us a comparative angle to analyze 
micro-level American charter school data. Despite a plethora of data in 
the NAEP-Civics 8 assessments, the absence of data on students’ prior 
academic performance may limit our ability to assess the “value-added” 
of different school types.
To gauge whether American charter schools enhanced the short-term 
civic outcomes of enrolled Black and Hispanic Grade 8 students, we 
compared this population with their traditional public and private 
school peers. We further gauged if key factors such as teacher credentials 
and time spent on civics instruction correlated with higher civics scores 
for Black and Hispanic Grade 8 students enrolled in charters. Our 
research questions were:
• What are the civic performance outcomes of American charters 
compared to other American school types, as measured by the 2006, 
2010, 2014, and 2018 NAEP-Civics8 results?
• To what extent do American charter schools enhance the civic per­
formance outcomes for Black and Hispanic students compared to 
other American school types, as measured by the 2006, 2010, 2014, 
and 2018 NAEP-Civics8 results?
• To what extent do factors such as teacher credentials and teacher 
time spent on civics instruction enhance the short-term civic out­
comes for Black and Hispanic students, as measured by the 2006, 
2010, 2014, and 2018 NAEP-Civics 8 results?
6. Method
6.1. Data set
NCES provided our research team with a license (#17020019) to 
access the restricted-use data for testing cycles 2006–2018. Unlike NAEP 
Reading, Mathematics, and Science, NAEP-Civics8 results are not re­
ported for individual states. However, in 2030, the plan is to collect 
Grade 8 Civics assessment data at the state level. NAEP gathered data 
using a probability sampling design so that participating schools and

are selected from sampled public and private schools nationwide 
through a multistage design. The probability of selecting a school is 
proportional to the estimated number of students in the assessed grade 
(NCES, 2019). Specifically, the 2006 NAEP-Civics8 data were obtained 
from a nationally representative sample of 9500 eighth-grade students

participants.
By design, NAEP data is unavailable at individual student or school 
levels. Typically, reports present results at the state, regional, and na­
tional levels. Since NAEP is a group assessment, each student partici­
pates in only a portion of the overall assessment. In most cases, only a 
small subset of students from sampled schools are selected to take the 
assessment, and these students may only accurately represent part of the 
school population. It is only when student scores are combined at the 
state or national level that the data is considered to be reliable and valid 
estimates of students’ knowledge and skills in Civics (NCES, 2018). 
Finally, there are common items from previous assessments that NCES 
uses for common item equating to create scores and enable reporting of 
trend results. For the current project, we only utilized those common 
items to determine trends across the testing cycles.
6.2. Variables
Besides outcome performance scores, NAEP-Civics8 incorporated 
student, teacher, and school-level data that provided information on 
demographics, teacher time spent on civics, and teacher credentials 
(degree) and served as key factors. Student-level variables of interest in 
this study included gender and race.
6.2.1. Civics performance outcome
Measured the civic knowledge and skills deemed critical to civic 
development in the United States. Student respondents answered 
multiple-choice and constructed-response questions designed to gauge 
student knowledge of government and civil society structures. NAEP 
reported civics achievement scaled scores from 0 to 300. NAEP used 
plausible values3 to address the uncertainty in the true scale score for 
each student. By utilizing plausible values and including numerous 
student group variables in the population-structure models, NAEP en­
ables a wide range of secondary analyses with minimal bias. For 2006 
and 2010, NAEP-Civics 8 data contained a set of five plausible values (i. 
e., CIVRP1 –CIVRP5) to represent student civics scores. The 2014 and 
2018 data sets contained 20 plausible values (i.e., CIVRP1 -CIVRP20). In 
2013, NCES transitioned from 5 to 20 plausible values. Originally, 5 
plausible values were used based on the state of technology, but with 
technological advancements, a move was made to 20 plausible values. 
This has increased the overall precision of the estimated standard errors. 
The plausible values of students’ civics achievement were used as the 
dependent variable in each cycle, forming a set for estimating the pop­
ulation characteristics of the sampled students using marginal maximum 
likelihood analysis (National Center for Education Statistics, 2016).
6.2.2. Student demographics: Gender and race
We also used students’ demographic variables, which included 
gender (DSEX) and race/ethnicity (DRACE). Student gender was coded 
dichotomously, with males coded as 0 and serving as the reference 
group. NAEP data originally classified student race into six classifica­
tions: African-American, Asian, Hispanic, Native American, White, and 
uncategorized. We dummy coded variables to characterize the cate­
gories of race/ethnicity with White students as a reference group, that is, 
Asian students (Asian=1, White=0), Hispanic students (Hispanic=1, 
White=0), Black students (Black=1, White=0), American Indian/Alaska 
Native students (American Indian/ Alaska Native=1, White=0), Native 
Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (Native Hawaiian/ Pacific Islander=1, 
White=0), Two or more races (Two or more races=1, White=0). The 
current study purposefully concentrated on two categories: Black and 
Hispanic (Johnson et al., 2020).
6.2.3. Teacher civics instructional time
To measure teacher civics instructional time in class, we created a 
new variable using data collected from the survey item "In a typical 
week, how much time in total do you spend with this class?" This item 
captures the total weekly time teachers allocate to civics instruction. 
Ordered categorical variable with six categories. Teacher civics 
instructional time (TIMESPENT) was gathered through self-reported

2014a), and participants can choose from None, 1–10 %, 11–40 %, 
41–60 %, 61–90 %, to More than 90 %, to indicate their primary focus in

6.2.4. Teacher credentials
This variable focuses on teacher degree (DEGREE) and initially listed 
six classifications. We accepted that all surveyed teachers possessed a 
bachelor’s degree, at a minimum, to teach. Respondents who selected 
’high school diploma’ as the highest degree were deemed invalid and 
thus eliminated. We combined the classifications, Education Specialist, 
Doctorate, and Professional degree, into one category, "beyond master’s 
degree,” given the ambiguity of each classification order and the limited 
sample populations. Once we combined classifications, the variable was 
used to denote a three-ordered category scale (i.e., bachelor’s degree, 
master’s degree, and beyond master’s degree) to represent teacher 
credential level (Johnson et al., 2020).
6.2.5. School type
Schools were categorized according to charter, non-charter, public 
(traditional public schools), and private schools (SCHTYPE). According 
to National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) (2014b), “a 
charter school is a public school that, in accordance with an enabling 
state statute, has been granted a charter exempting it from selected state 
or local rules and regulation.” In the inferential analysis, two dummy 
variables were created (i.e., private [1=private, 0=otherwise] and 
charter [1=charter, 0=otherwise]). Traditional public schools consti­
tuted a reference group (Johnson et al., 2020). As for school-level ana­
lyses, if there are separate school files, which there are for 2018 Civics, 
school weights are provided.

7.2.1. Overall civic performance trends
First, consistent with the observation in the descriptive results, NAEP 
civic scores are the highest for private schools and the lowest for 
traditional public schools. Private schools consistently achieve higher 
civic performance scores than charter and traditional public schools. In 
2006, private school students had an intercept estimate of 19.56** (SE 
3.39), indicating that they outperformed their traditional public school 
peers by this margin. The private school intercept remained high in 2010 
at 18.79** (SE 3.37), decreased slightly in 2014–17.33** (SE 2.85), and 
further reduced to 14.18** (SE 2.72) in 2018. Despite the decreasing 
trend, private school students consistently show higher civic perfor­
mance scores than traditional public school students across all years, 
suggesting that private schools may offer more effective civics education 
programs or benefit from better resources, smaller class sizes, and more 
supportive learning environments.
Charter schools perform better than traditional public schools but 
show less consistency than private ones. In 2006, charter school students 
had an intercept estimate of 5.41 (SE 3.39), indicating no significant 
difference from traditional public school students. This pattern persists 
in 2010 with an intercept of 4.49 (SE 3.20), increases slightly in 
2014–4.36 (SE 3.04), and is slightly higher in 2018 at 5.44 (SE 3.70). 
These minor differences suggest that while charter schools offer some 
improvements, they do not consistently outperform traditional public 
schools. The variability in charter school performance could be due to 
differences in charter school policies, management quality, and the 
implementation of civics education programs over time.
The intercept values in Table 1 represent traditional public school 
students’ baseline civic performance scores. The intercept for overall 
student performance starts at 153.31 in 2006 with a standard error (SE) 
of 1.40 and gradually increases to 153.43 (SE 0.72) by 2018. This in­
crease suggests an overall improvement in civic knowledge and skills 
among students in traditional public schools over time.

8. Discussion and conclusion
The scale of market-oriented neo-liberal reforms extends beyond the 
US, with charter and charter-like entities prevalent in contexts such as 
Australia, Britain, Canada, and Columbia. However, the systematic dif­
ferences observed in this study should be understood as correlations 
rather than causal effects, as they are likely influenced by a combination 
of factors, including socioeconomic disparities and variations in aca­
demic preparation before entering the 8th grade. The increasing reach 
and novel structure of charter and charter school-like entities globally 
generates curiosity about whether these market-based settings support 
youth civic development (Johnson et al., 2020). Little studies have 
addressed the civic side of charter and charter-like schools in democratic 
societies, even though these schools are important influencers of civic 
knowledge and skills (Johnson et al., 2023). With this study, we sought 
to examine the civic side of charter schools in a particular democratic 
setting, the US. We sought to address the purpose of our study empiri­
cally and conceptually.
The analyses of NAEP-Civics8 mean scores for 2006, 2010, 2014, and 
2018 testing cycles provided empirical data to gauge trends in the 
relationship between American charter schools and youth civic knowl­
edge and skills acquisition. However, these trends should be interpreted 
as descriptive patterns rather than definitive evidence of the effective­
ness of any particular school type, as the data do not allow for causal 
conclusions. For Hispanic students, the effect of attending a Charter 
school fluctuates but shows a generally positive trend, increasing from 
2.01 in 2006–3.70 in 2018. For gender, the effect of Charter schools on 
males (Gender Intercept) is slightly positive but not consistently signif­
icant across the years. These findings are consistent with the Johnson 
et al. (2020) conclusions from their analyses of the 2014 and 2018 
NAEP-Civics 8 data. Overall trend analysis reveals that while overall 
student performance (intercept) shows a slight decline, the effects of 
Private and Charter schools on different demographic groups vary. 
Notably, private schools consistently show positive and significant cor­
relations with the performance of Asian and Black students, with 
increasing trends over time. American charter schools also show positive 
trends, particularly for Hispanic students. This is an interesting point of 
discussion as it suggests that school choice initiatives may extend quality 
education opportunities to typically marginalized populations, particu­
larly students from Black and Hispanic communities (see Hoxby et al., 
2009; Betts and Tang, 2016). The trend analysis across four distinct 
testing cycles provides extensive data points that allow researchers and 
policymakers to draw comprehensive conclusions regarding the civic 
side of American schools. Unlike Chudowsky et al. (2013) findings, 
which employed one testing cycle to determine the civic side of Amer­
ican charter schools, we call for a more robust examination of student 
civic assessment data across multiple testing cycles.
Quite a few positions presented in this study should be considered 
when interpreting the comprehensive implications of the results. Fore­
most, the primary emphasis dealt specifically with civic performance 
outcomes based on NAEP Civics-8 mean scores, which have been 
described as limited and biased (Levinson, 2010). As the previous 
literature suggests, civic achievement should comprise civic knowledge, 
skills, and dispositions. Since the NAEP-Civics scores demonstrate only 
students’ civics knowledge and skills, the NAEP assessment is less 
extensive and objective. Utilizing case study research to address and 
complement the limits associated with secondary analyses of 
NAEP-Civics data would be informative (Wilson, 2016). Second, it is 
also essential to consider the heterogeneity of American charter schools 
where varied models, management organizations, laws, and students 
served by charters shape the civic efficacy and purpose-driven mission of 
these educative sites (Wohlstetter et al., 2013), which has implications 
for charter schools and charter-like schools in other countries. The 
heterogeneity of American charter schools makes it difficult to assess 
which policy aspect is more consequential in advancing civic develop­
ment and opportunity. Is it the relaxed rules and governance? Is it the 
‘innovative” structure? Is it political climate? Understanding how 
various factors that have led to the growth and distribution of American 
charter schools potentially influence the scope and nature of civic 
development in these spaces requires additional unpacking (see Henig, 
2018). Comparative case study research could help ascertain under what 
conditions American charter schools are the most civically efficacious 
(Johnson et al., 2020; Berends, 2015).
A central limitation of this study is the need for more data on stu­
dents’ academic performance before the 8th-grade civics test. This 
limitation is significant because it prevents us from controlling for the 
initial academic preparedness of students—a factor that could substan­
tially influence their performance on the NAEP civics assessment. 
Without this control, any observed differences in civic outcomes across 
school types may be artifacts of earlier educational experiences rather 
than a reflection of the schools’ current practices. We explicitly 
acknowledge this limitation throughout our manuscript. In the findings 
section, we note that the differences observed across school types may 
be influenced by students’ prior academic achievements and other un­
measured factors. In the discussion and conclusions sections, we reit­
erate that our analysis does not account for these variables. Therefore, 
the results should not be used to generalize broadly about the effec­
tiveness of charter, traditional public, or private schools. "In light of 
these limitations, we recommend that future research on this topic 
incorporate longitudinal data that tracks students’ academic perfor­
mance over time. Such data would allow for a more nuanced analysis 
that controls for students’ prior civic-oriented academic experiences and 
provides a clearer picture of different school types’ roles in shaping civic 
outcomes.

side of American charter schools to a) delineate the civic purpose of 
schooling in a global age of neoliberal education policies characterized 
by a market-driven agenda that values accountability and standardiza­
tion, b) explain the multifaceted connection between American charter 
school policies and the market-oriented functionality of schooling as a 
return on investment, and c) provide a roadmap for conceptualizing and

structuring future work that examines the civic side of charter and 
charter-like schools across the global north and the global south. For 
discussion, let us contextualize our findings in the human capital theory 
conceptual framework.
We can use our findings to delineate the civic purpose of charter 
schooling in a neoliberal education policy age. Across the testing cycles, 
it would appear that students enrolled in American charter schools 
faired on par or slightly better than their peers enrolled in traditional 
public schools. The school-type correlation with students’ civic perfor­
mance lends some credence to the positions and policy perspectives that 
American charter schools have a civic purpose in a neoliberal education 
policy age (i.e., Gill et al., 2020; McEachin et al., 2020). As previously 
mentioned, unlike the Science, Mathematics, and Reading assessments 
reported for individual states, NAEP Civics achievement results are not 
recorded for each state. As such, we cannot confirm whether charter 
school students in “charter-friendly” states with specific civic education 
mandates and standards performed better than their peers enrolled in 
states with low charter representation and no civic education mandates 
and standards.
Next, we wanted to explain the multifaceted connection between 
American charter school policies and the market-oriented functionality 
of schooling as a return on investment. A fundamental premise of charter 
school policy is that competition, autonomy, and flexibility expand 
educational quality and opportunity to all students, thus suggesting a 
morally centered market response. Although the charter school move­
ment may have started as a market-oriented moral response to persistent 
achievement gaps, it appears that markets, in this case, do provide a 
benevolent means for providing equitable civic learning opportunities to 
Black and Hispanic students (Johnson et al., 2020; Authors, under re­
view). When we hone in on the covariation of charter schools and the 
civic performance of Black and Hispanic students, we see a positive 
correlation with Hispanic students. The placement of American charter 
schools in urban communities produces higher percentages of Black and 
Hispanic students enrolled in these settings than in traditional public 
schools in general (Henig, 2018). For many Black and Hispanic youth, 
the absence of civic learning opportunities compounded with their his­
torically subordinated status may aggravate existing civic opportunity 
gaps (Authors, under review). Based on the percentage of Hispanic and 
Black youth enrolled in American charter schools, we anticipated that 
those who identify as Hispanic and Black youth enrolled in charter 
schools surpass those enrolled in traditional public schools. The findings 
of the last conditional model revealed that Black eighth students 
enrolled in American charters perform better than their racial/ethnic 
cohorts who are enrolled in traditional public schools but not statisti­
cally significant, which aligns with those researchers who suggest mixed 
results (see Berends, 2015; Betts and Tang, 2016; Zimmer, 2009; Zim­
mer et al., 2012).
Teacher credentialing and teacher time spent on civics instruction 
are also crucial to expanding educational quality and opportunity. Our 
findings did not reveal significant differences in students’ civics scores 
based on teacher credentials. More significant findings indicate that the 
influence of teacher credentials differs when American charter schools 
and traditional public schools are compared. Curiously, charter school 
students with teachers holding advanced credentials were apt to 
perform lower on the NAEP-Civics 8 tests. These findings support 
Hanushek et al. (2005) and Ladd and Sorensen (2015)’s findings, which 
suggested that advanced credentials had little to no association with 
charter school students’ academic achievement. This might be because 
charter school educators are inexperienced and deficient in advanced 
degrees and certifications. However, the data set did not reveal whether 
the teachers’ advanced degree qualifications were mainly focused on 
social studies or civics. Our findings aligned with those of Torney-Purta 
et al. (2001) and Gillies (2017), who found that teacher time spent on 
civics instruction positively relates to student civic performance 
outcomes.
How does this study help us understand the civic side of charters here 
and abroad? First, this study provides a conceptual framework for 
addressing the social returns associated with attending charter or 
charter-like schools in democratic societies. Fundamental premises 
associated with charter and school choice policies globally are related to 
foundational principles of human capital theory. A human development 
conceptual lens provides a comparative framework for understanding 
the civic side of charter schools in a global context. Given the few in­
ternational civic assessments, it would seem that a theoretical frame­
work could provide language to help build an ecosystem that will help 
researchers bring comprehensive aspects of the civic side of charter and 
charter-like schools in democratic societies. Human development theory 
contextualizes the civic side of global charter and charter-like schools.
Across global contexts, schools have been recognized as a means for 
developing civic knowledge, skills, and behaviors (Torney-Purta et al., 
2001). The findings from our study suggest that school type is a pre­
dictor of youth civic development, particularly for students enrolled in 
private schools in the US. Whereas comparison of US private school 
student performance with American charter and traditional public 
school student performance was beyond the scope of this article, it is 
essential to note that inherent variation is seen across school types 
continuously. Why is this important? We hope that advancing research 
that delineates the correlation between school type and student civic 
performance outcome, particularly at an international level, will pro­
duce inquiries that address the type and extent of civic instruction 
occurring globally in private schools, charters, and traditional public 
schools. Such inquiries will help generate what works for whom in 
different contexts.
