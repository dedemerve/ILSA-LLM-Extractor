ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

Most educational systems use either an integrated or a separated
science curriculum. However, it is unclear which of these science
curricula benefits students more author and existing research
provides insufficient information about the implementation
details of the curriculum employed. Therefore, this study
compares the effects of two science curricula on students’ science
literacy, drawing on socio-ecological theory and employing
educational data mining techniques. Results from Grade 8
Science students in 44 countries sampled in the Trends in
International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2019
showed that (1) the integrated curricula benefitted students
marginally more than the separated curricula; (2) curriculum type
was not essential in directly predicting students’ academic
performance; and (3) random forest outperformed linear
regression, lasso regression, decision trees, and neural networks
in predicting student science achievement. This study advances
our understanding of the predictors of student science
performance, demonstrates that machine learning techniques can
be applied successfully to examine curriculum effects, and
provides directions for implementing integrated science curricula.

Introduction
The advancement of technology in the twenty-first century has been accompanied by an
avalanche of data, information, and knowledge, which has led to the rapid development
of all aspects of society and increased global communication and cooperation. However,
this has also brought about many contemporary unique issues, such as climate change
and rapidly spreading diseases. These modern distinct challenges call for citizens to
‘learn integrated and usable knowledge, rather than the sets of compartmentalized and
decontextualized facts’ (Sawyer, 2022, p. 2). Moreover, schools should be ready to
prepare students for this integrated thinking to help them develop competence when
entering the workforce. Correspondingly, many educational systems have already
implemented integrated science curricula rather than traditionally separated science curricula (Haggis & Adey, 1979). The former is believed to have the capacity to foster

students’ holistic thinking to address contemporary environmental and societal issues
(Lee & Wan, 2022; Nagle, 2013; Venville et al., 2012), whereas the latter places less
emphasis on cultivating this kind of ability in students (Wei, 2018).
Moreover, students learn more deeply when provided with authentic contexts
(Sawyer, 2022). An integrated curriculum is more capable of providing authentic practices than a separated science curriculum (Lee & Wan, 2022). For example, an integrated
curriculum for science regarding climate change (Nagle, 2013, p. 1) blends ‘concepts in
the earth sciences (atmosphere, weather, and climate), physics (heat and temperature),
and life sciences (effects of climate change on ecosystems).’ In contrast, a separated
science curriculum often distributes the relevant concepts across several subjects. For
instance, the educational system in Finland deploys climate change-related concepts in
Chemistry (e.g. sustainable consumption and products lifecycle), Physics Biology (e.g.
the habitats of animals and observing changes in the environment), and Geography
(e.g. lifecycle assessment and limited natural resources) (Dawson et al., 2022).
In this study, integrated science curricula enable students to learn interdisciplinary
topics within a single class. For instance, students can learn concepts of earth sciences,
physics, and life sciences in the same unit of an integrated curriculum that targets
climate change. Conversely, the separated science curricula enable students to learn
topics as part of individual courses. For example, students can learn Physics topics
from a dedicated Physics course.
However, there is no consensus regarding which science curriculum benefits students
more. Several researchers advocated for the integrated science curriculum (e.g. Beichner
et al., 1999; Czerniak & Johnson, 2015; Thang & Koh, 2017), whereas others criticised it
(e.g. Ogunkola & Samuel, 2011; Sun et al., 2014). This leads to the following research
question: Which type of science curriculum helps students achieve more in science?
Answering this question may help deploy a science curriculum in K-12 education that
can prepare students for a competitive environment in a society that relies increasingly
on scientific knowledge. This study used propensity score matching and machine learning methods to investigate the effect of two science curricula in addressing the research
question.
Separated curriculum
Separated curriculum instruction has been the mainstream from the start of science education (Brown, 1977; Wei, 2009). Universities have had well-established programmes for
training individual science discipline teachers. Secondary education has developed separated science curriculum frameworks to prepare students for high-stakes examinations
(e.g. the university entrance exam). In most nations, students must follow four major
science curricula: Biology, Physics, Chemistry, and Earth Science.
However, such a segmented learning experience is not well positioned to help students
form a holistic understanding of contemporary ‘scientific perplexities,’ such as environmental sustainability (Venville et al., 2012). The outbreak of Coronavirus constituted an
example that stressed the importance of a holistic understanding of science. In particular,
Linn et al. (2016) asserted that students cannot coherently understand and internalise
more than 60 distinct science concepts or topics in a year. Due to the inability to link
segmented curricula with job expectations, many students regard science as useless

(Wei, 2009). Meanwhile, schools or regions often choose a few science disciplines as the
essential teaching and learning target due to limited teaching time but neglect others in
many regions. For example, Wan and Lee (2021) concluded that Korea and Taiwan
(China) both emphasise Physics topics more in science textbooks. Au (2007) also
argued that teachers often prioritise concepts that are covered in high-stakes tests
when delivering instructional materials.
Several instructional frameworks were proposed, including knowledge integration
(Linn et al., 2016), aiming to make science more practically meaningful for students. It
is believed that an integrated curriculum allows students to build a holistic thinking
pattern by considering real-world contexts (Lee & Wan, 2022; Venville et al., 2012).
Integrated curriculum
TIMSS 2019 reported the science curriculum type adopted by each participant country or
region. Combining these statistics, more than 60% of countries or regions implemented
integrated science curricula, while others applied separated science curricula.
Linn et al. (2016) classified a century of science education history in America into four
stages: separation (1916–1960), interaction (1960–1980), partnership (1980–1995), and
integration (1995–2016). Such a development pattern reflects the common trends in
the history of science education. Many official standards have been inspired by the promised benefits of integrated science. For example, the United States published the 2011
Framework for K–12 Science Education, which includes the ‘cross-cutting concepts’ in
science curriculum standards (National Research Council, 2012). Therefore, combining
different subjects into one curriculum is a common solution to address several drawbacks
of separated curricula. Such integration could remove content repetition between subjects and save teaching time (Brown, 1977), while increasing the number of covered
topics in disciplines that have long suffered from neglect.
However, an integrated curriculum requires much more development than a separated
curriculum to benefit students. The need for such additional effort arises from the challenging fulfilment of the prerequisites of a successfully integrated curriculum in practice.
First, compiling a coherent textbook for the integrated curriculum can be laborious
(Scotter et al., 2000; Wan & Lee, 2021; Wang, 2018). Wan and Lee (2021) argued that
the design of the textbook, curriculum, and syllabus should be consistent with the inner
logic of that discipline. Nevertheless, an examination of integrated science textbooks in
Korea and Taiwan (China) suggested that coherence is only partially guaranteed (Lee &
Wan, 2022). Linn et al. (2016) agreed with Bruner’s (1960) standpoint that science
topics need to be sequenced by their abstraction level. Therefore, Linn et al. (2016) also
argued that selecting and sequencing topics for science textbooks are the most prominent
challenges in science instruction. Moreover, Pearson et al. (2010) argued that the poor
quality of science textbooks hinders the proliferation of integrated science instruction.
Second, a qualified teacher is essential for the success of integrated science (e.g. Lee &
Wan, 2022; Wei, 2018). Teachers with a background in only one discipline find it challenging to teach four subjects (Wei, 2009). One of the solutions is to assign different
instructors to teach different parts of an integrated curriculum but leave the scientific
design of the structure and sequence of the integrated curriculum content ignored in
practice (Wei, 2009). In turn, this approach may hinder students’ ability to link topics

from different subjects. Training instructors to be competent in teaching integrated curricula with multiple science discipline backgrounds is also complex and time-consuming
(Harrell, 2010). Moreover, teachers tend to prioritise knowledge topics that carry more

Third, the lagging development of measurement instruments that do not comply with

In summary, successfully implementing an integrated science curriculum requires the
‘transformation of the curriculum, instruction, assessments, and teacher professional
development’ (Nagle, 2013). Although integrated curricula are gradually being adopted
around the globe (Haatainen et al., 2021), there has yet to be an agreement on which
science curriculum benefits students more. Some studies indicated that integrated
instruction cultivates students’ confidence and satisfaction with science (Beichner
et al., 1999; Hurley, 2001). In contrast, another research branch asserts that integrated
science is not as helpful as it promised (Meichtry, 1993; Sun et al., 2014; Venville
et al., 2012).
Research gap
There is considerable discrepancy in research on the effects of the two curricula on
student achievement. Several studies advocate for the benefits of an integrated science
curriculum for students’ science achievement, attitudes toward science, and scientific
skills (e.g. Alake & Ogunseemi, 2013; Beichner et al., 1999; Czerniak & Johnson, 2015;
Thang & Koh, 2017). Other studies found that integrated science did not contribute to
student learning and attitudes and that integrated science teachers are not always
qualified to teach it (e.g. Harrell, 2010; Ogunkola & Samuel, 2011; Sun et al., 2014).
However, these studies report insufficient information about the implementation
details of the selected integrated science curriculum, such as the teacher’s academic background and the instructional time employed. Consequently, comparing the implementation details between different studies that reach the opposite conclusion is
complicated due to inadequate information provided. Furthermore, the comparison of
the two curricula is biased without controlling the instructional detail-related variable.
In addition, existing studies are often restricted to small sample sizes, making the conclusion less general. Although these results might be suited for their sampled areas,
the effect of curriculum needs to be explored more generally across multiple geographical
regions. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate curriculum effects on student learning
outcomes by using large-scale assessment data and controlling instructional detailrelated variables. In contrast to locally collected data, TIMSS provides curriculum data
and, thus, a chance to examine the curriculum effects beyond the district restrictions.

Literature review
Integrated and separated science curricula
Modern science education traditionally classifies nature science into Biology, Chemistry,
Earth Science, and Physics. Many countries and regions have implemented this curriculum design, known as the separated science curriculum, for a long time.
The twentieth century saw the emergence of many integrated science curricula. Many
conferences and symposiums surrounding the evolution of integrated science curricula
were held, such as ‘Coordination of the Teaching of Mathematics and Physics’ in 1967
and conferences in 1968, 1973, 1975, and 1978, which were held by the United
Nations Educational Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) and other institutions (Frey, 1989). Following these conferences, UNESCO published a series of
reports about integrated science curricula named ‘New Trends in Integrated Science
Teaching’ (Chisman, 1990).
This study analyzes the TIMSS data set. Given that the participants of TIMSS 2019
include many countries and regions around the globe, selecting the most general definition
of integrated science would be appropriate to all places adopting an integrated science curriculum: ‘An integrated science curriculum contains three or more science discipline
topics from Biology, Earth science, Physics, and Chemistry’ (Lee & Wan, 2022).
Essential factors for successful integrated science implementation
As Rutherford (2000) indicated, an integrated science curriculum could only be helpful
after substantial development. Therefore, it is necessary and critical to evaluate whether
its goal can be achieved through intentional implementation (Lee & Wan, 2022). When

the necessary prerequisites for an effective curriculum are not met, even the most promising design will not suffice. Several studies pointed out that the essential factors for the
success of integrated science include a qualified teacher (e.g. Lee & Wan, 2022; Wei,
2018) and a coherent textbook (e.g. Scotter et al., 2000; Wan & Lee, 2021). Venville
et al. (2012) argued that administrative policy, testing constraints, and school traditions
could also impact the success or failure of integrated science. For example, the assessment
of an integrated curriculum tends to overlook its unique characteristics, such as the effect
of coherent teaching strategies (Perkins & Simmons, 1988).
An integrated curriculum’s teaching needs to cover a broad knowledge according to its
original design. However, most teachers possess only one or two academic backgrounds
in four science subjects, making them uncomfortable or unable to teach integrated curricula (e.g. Harrell, 2010; Wang, 2018). Wan and Lee (2021) argued that the design of the
textbook, curriculum, and syllabus should be consistent with the inner logic of that discipline. For example, as the only province in China that insists on implementing integrated science, 30 years of practice in the Zhejiang province have revealed a significant
lesson: the deficiency of the coherence textbook that stems from the challenges in compiling a coherently integrated textbook (Wang, 2018).
Discrepancy between the effect of the two curricula
There is a considerable discrepancy in the research that has compared the effects of the
two curricula (Venville et al., 2012). Some studies advocate for the integrated science curriculum due to its benefits to student science achievement, attitudes toward science, and
scientific skills (such as Beichner et al., 1999; Czerniak & Johnson, 2015). Richardson and
Showalter (1967) suggested that students who were delivered an integrated curriculum
showed more interest in scientific problems and were more willing to apply their
science knowledge in life by combining multiple subjects’ knowledge facts than students
in a separated science system. According to a meta-analysis conducted by Hurley (2001),
an integrated curriculum has been shown to positively impact students in terms of discipline, attitudes, and engagement.
Conversely, several studies found that integrated science did not contribute to students’ learning and attitudes, and integrated science teachers are often not qualified to
teach such curricula (e.g. Harrell, 2010; Sun et al., 2014). Ross and Hogaboam-Gray
(1998) found that students form a more solid knowledge structure in separated
science education than students in integrated ones. A branch of researchers believes
that separated disciplines are essential to teaching and studying because the segmented
curriculum provides a sense of order that could help students understand themselves
and their surroundings (Gardner, 2004; Schoenfeld, 2004). Young (2008) argued that segmented discipline is the price we have to pay to understand novel knowledge better.
Lee and Wan (2022) pointed out that, under ideal conditions, integrated science helps
students to understand, master, and apply twenty-first-century competencies. However,
we must not overlook the very prerequisite for this argument, that is, under ideal conditions. It is challenging to determine the reasons behind the effectiveness of the
different curricula above. This challenge arises from the insufficient implementation
details reported in existing studies. For example, Turpin and Cage (2004) conducted
an experiment to compare the effects of integrated and traditional separated science

curricula on students’ achievement, skills, and science learning attitudes and found that
integrated science helps students more. These studies report limited information about

Commonly used methods in existing studies
The existing studies that investigated the impact of integrated science on student learning
often employ a qualitative or mixed-research design (e.g. Sun et al., 2014; Wei, 2018).
These studies typically use questionnaires to assess teachers’ and students’ reactions to
integrated science, or they undertake experiments to evaluate the effect of integrated
science. One of the disadvantages of these traditional methods is the small sample size,
which hinders the generalizability of the results, especially when using convenient
sampling (e.g. Zhang & He, 2012).
Recently, many studies analyzed this topic using the educational data mining paradigm. The decision tree is one of the most frequently adopted algorithms in educational
data mining studies that are based on large-scale assessment data (Abu Saa et al., 2019;
Gamazo & Martínez-Abad, 2020). Other commonly used methods include clustering,
neural networks, logistic regression, support vector machines, and Naive Bayes. Thus,
it is necessary to explore which algorithm performs the best among these widely used
algorithms in the context of this study.

Theoretical framework
The present study used the socio-ecological theory as the foundational framework to
guide the research design, which stresses social interaction in explaining learning and
captures the complexity of factors that mould human development (Boyd & Bee, 2015).
Socio-ecological theory
The socio-ecological theory was first introduced as an ecological theory (Bronfenbrenner,
1979, 1986) that explains how factors and people in the environment impact an individual’s development, emphasising the role of socialisation. Subsequently, Bronfenbrenner
and Evans (2000) recognised the role of the individual in this theory and renamed it as
the bioecological model, although some educational studies still refer to this theory as
socio-ecological theory (e.g. Allen et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023). Specifically, the
socio-ecological theory is visualised as three concentric circles of systems or layers:
microsystem, exosystem, and macrosystem.
The process-person-context-time (PPCT) framework
Bronfenbrenner and Morris (1998) introduced the Process-Person-Context-Time
(PPCT) PPCT model based on the socio-ecological theory. The process dimension is
similar to the original microsystem in the socio-ecological theory, including the

immediate interaction between the individual and the environment (Bronfenbrenner &
Morris, 1998). The definition of the person dimension is also the same as the socio-ecological theory, indicating individual-related factors. Moreover, the context contains the
higher-level environmental factors (Dai et al., 2023). Finally, the time dimension suggests
when the interactions happen (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 1998).
The socio-ecological theory is widely used as the theoretical framework in educational
studies due to its capability to describe complex factors in the learning process. For
example, Wang et al. (2023) also matched the socio-ecological model with variables
from TIMSS 2019 and explored the high performances of East Asian students in mathematics based on machine learning algorithms. Dai et al. (2023) examined student
reading achievement based on the PPCT model for the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 data.
Consistent with some examples illustrated above, this study also plans to analyze the
large-scale assessment data. Considering the complexity of the variables in science learning, this study adopted the socio-ecological theory as the theoretical framework, particularly using the PPCT model to understand the relationships between science curriculum
setting and student science learning experiences and outcomes.
Aligning the PPCT model to this study
This section aims to provide an alignment of the variables used in the present study to the
PPCT model. However, the present study does not consider the time dimension of the
PPCT model, given that the implementation of TIMSS 2019 does not contain several
time periods.
First, the person dimension corresponds to the student-related variables. Dai et al.
(2023) align the PPCT model with the PISA 2018 reading assessment and analytical
framework, suggesting that the person dimension should contain student background
and metacognitive variables, such as family background and attitudes toward learning.
Given the similarities between PISA and TIMSS assessments, this study decided to
follow the suggestions from Dai et al. (2023). Therefore, this study included four attitude
variables (the first four variables at the bottom of Figure 1), two background variables
(the fifth and sixth variables at the bottom of Figure 1), and the dependent variable of
this study (student science achievement).
According to Bronfenbrenner and Morris (1998) and Dai et al. (2023), the process
dimension ought to represent the interactions between individual and immediate surrounding environments, particularly in the context of education, encompassing variables
related to teaching and learning. Because the science curriculum choice influences practical instruction, this study selected eight variables for this dimension, as shown on the
right side of Figure 1: variables 4–8 were reported by teachers, whereas the remaining
variables were reported by students.
The context dimension corresponds to the higher-level settings, such as variables
related to school policy and governance (Dai et al., 2023). Although Dai et al. (2023)
mentioned the curriculum in the process dimension, they also argued that the curriculum
setting impacts student reading achievement in the context dimension. The decision
between separated and integrated science curricula has several consequences, such as
the recruitment of teachers, the choice of textbooks, and the design of educational

Measures
The target (i.e. dependent) variable of this project was the student science achievement
score. Following the procedures of previous studies, all plausible values (PVs) were used,
and separate analyses were conducted for each PV.
The related literature suggests that the science curriculum type influences students’
attitudes toward science. Therefore, this study used two student attitude features as independent variables for students who were taught integrated curricula: liking to learn
science and feeling confident about science. The corresponding variables were selected
for students taught using separate curricula, including liking and feeling confident

about Biology, Chemistry, Physics, and Earth Science. TIMSS standardised the original
Likert-response items onto a common scale (Martin et al., 2020). Consequently, variables
of separated curricula were averaged to enable a comparison between the two curriculum
types. As an illustration, the liking variables for Biology, Chemistry, Physics, and Earth
Science were averaged into a single variable. Other variables related to the instruction
details include instructional clarity and time, experiment frequency, homework frequency, homework time, instruction preparation time needed by teachers, instructional
strategies, emphasis on scientific investigation, and curriculum type.
TIMSS provides the sampling weights that allow an accurate estimate of the population characteristics by a sample of students (Martin et al., 2020). TIMSS provides
three teacher weight variables to help analyze teacher context data at the student level,
which are calculated by dividing the student weight by the number of that student’s teachers (Fishbein et al., 2021). Moreover, it is also suggested to use subject-wise (e.g.
science curriculum type) teacher weight when investigating teacher-level variables as
student attributes (Liou & Hung, 2015). Specifically, some researchers believe that
if teachers of a particular subject (either math or science) in TIMSS are of analytic interest, it
is then suitable to select either math teacher weight for analyses that include math teacher
variables or science teacher weight for analyses that include science teacher variables. (Rutkowski et al., 2010, p. 144)

As variables four through eight in the Process dimension of Figure 1 were reported by
science teachers, the science teacher’s weight was used in the current study to decrease
the sampling bias, given that the focus of this study was to understand the effect of the
science curriculum type on student science achievement from an instructional perspective. In this respect, the science teacher’s weight can provide a more accurate measure of
instructional details than a student weight. Moreover, TIMSS data was released by
country and region, and the IEA IDB Analyzer (IEA, 2022) was suggested as a tool to
combine data across countries and regions (Fishbein et al., 2021). When using the IEA
IDB Analyzer, if one teacher-level variable was selected, the teacher-lever weight was
automatically exported along with the selected variables. The independent variables
employed in this study are shown in Table 1. The dependent variable is the plausible
value of science achievement (BSSSCI01, BSSSCI02, BSSSCI03, BSSSCI04, BSSSCI05),
the same for the separated and integrated groups.
How do the effects of the two curricula on students’ achievement compare using
the TIMSS 2019?
Randomly assigning students to one of two curriculum groups could guarantee a fair
comparison between the curricula. When the random assignment is challenging to
fulfill, propensity score matching is a promising alternative to balance the covariates’ participant differences. The propensity score matching method could provide two matched
groups on covariates, making it suitable to compare the effects of two curricula. Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) proposed the propensity score method, which treats the confounding variables as covariates and estimates the conditional probability of each
sample being classified into the treatment group on a group of covariates. The estimated
probability is denoted as the propensity score, which could balance the covariate differences brought by selection bias.

Furthermore, Autenrieth et al. (2021) indicated that machine learning-based propensity score calculation methods could improve the estimation performance compared to
commonly used logistic models. They proposed a new method based on stacked ensemble learning and achieved high accuracy. The stacked ensemble learning-based method
demonstrated superior performance over logistic regression in three simulation
studies, exhibiting less bias (average bias = 1.06%) in estimating treatment effects compared to logistic regression (average bias = 4.82%). Bias refers to the difference
between the estimated and true treatment effects. Henceforth, the present study used
stacked ensemble learning to calculate the propensity score.
Stacked ensemble learning employs a two-level analysis. First, it uses several base learners to predict the original data, and then it uses a meta-learner to predict a new data set
comprising base learner outcomes (Autenrieth et al., 2021). This project selected KNN,
random forest, and Naïve Bayes as stacking classifiers or base learners for stacked ensemble learning-based propensity score calculation and logistic regression as a meta classifier.
After deleting replication data entries, imputing the missing data, dummy coding, and
standardisation of the features, we split the whole data set into a training set (70% of
the original data set) and a test set (30% of the original data set). The hyperparameter
tuning procedure is detailed in Table A4.
The core idea behind the propensity score matching method is to match students who
received an integrated science curriculum with similar students who received a separated
science curriculum, based on the calculated propensity scores. First, stacked ensemble learning was used to estimate the propensity score for each student, considering all observed covariates. This study treated all independent variables, except for the curriculum type variable,
as covariates. Second, students in the integrated curriculum group were matched with those
in the separated curriculum group using the calculated propensity scores, employing the knearest neighbour (KNN) algorithm for this purpose. Third, the distributions of covariates
for the two curriculum types were compared to ascertain if they were similar for both groups.
Fourth, the effect of the science curriculum type was evaluated after obtaining balanced
samples to understand the effect of curriculum type on each PV. The propensity score
matching was also used to investigate the effect of inquiry teaching on student science
achievement and attitudes based on the PSIA 2006 data, where five levels of inquiry teaching
in estimating the propensity score were used (Jiang & McComas, 2015).

Interaction analysis
This section investigates whether the presence of the curriculum type influences the
relationship between independent variables and the dependent variable. Figure 8
draws the relationships between curriculum type and student attitude variables, and
Figure 9 depicts the relationship between curriculum type and instructional clarity.
Trends in Figures 8 and 9 were observed in fitting models for each PV. Only the
results from the first PV were presented in the current section, and the results for
other PVs were presented in Figures A19–A26 in Appendix A. The overall trends of
the variables ‘students feel confident about science’ and ‘instructional clarity’ were
similar for the two curriculum types.
However, according to the right panel of Figure 8, at a horizontal axis value of 8, the
estimated SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) value for ‘Students like to learn science’
was larger than 0 for the separated group (red), indicating that students’ achievement
increased the more they liked science when students displayed low or medium enjoyment
in learning science. Conversely, for the integrated group (blue), the SHAP value was
smaller than 0 at this point (X-axis = 8), suggesting that an increase in liking science
was associated with a decrease in science performance for these students. Moreover,
the relationship was reversed when the students highly enjoyed science learning (e.g.
X-axis = 14), suggesting that the curriculum type influenced the relationship between
liking science and student achievement.

Discussion
The curriculum setting is critical in cultivating student science literacy by influencing the
formation of student thinking habits, problem-solving strategies, knowledge concept
structure, and studying attitudes (Beichner et al., 1999; Czerniak & Johnson, 2015; Lee

& Wan, 2022). Also, curriculum relates to science teacher demands, curriculum standards, educational measurements, and many other critical educational aspects.
Primary and secondary education constitute the foundation for the pursuit of higher
education. Thus, the decision of which curriculum type to use in a particular country
or region is significant, as it can profoundly impact students’ future educational
trajectories.
Despite the importance of the curriculum setting, previous research results did not
reach an agreement on which curriculum aids students more. One reason is that these
studies often collect data from nearby schools, thus their results may only suit the
selected school units. This questions the generalizability of these conclusions to nearby
cities and, especially, to whole countries or regions. The integrated science curriculum
is relatively new compared to the separated one. Therefore, its relatively short developmental and implemental history indicates that several fundamental challenges have yet to
be solved. Several aspects must be considered, including a universal curriculum standard,
a coherent textbook, a competent teacher, and a valid and reliable measurement instrument. When the implementation of the integrated curriculum does not match the design,
the integrated curriculum is sometimes less helpful than the separated curriculum in
some student attitudes and instructional details. However, insufficient information
regarding curriculum implementation in existing studies hinders the determination of
the exact reason for the poor effects of an integrated curriculum.
Studies examining one region’s curriculum effect are likely to influence educational
change in that region. However, the outcome is affected if these studies yield potentially
biased results. Therefore, this study analyzed large-scale assessment data to avoid the
drawbacks of the existing studies. The curriculum implementation details from almost
all participants in TIMSS 2019 help to elucidate this issue. However, one premise of
the analysis is that the TIMSS 2019 science assessment was not designed to favour
either type of science curriculum. Although the background questionnaires provide a

different version for separated and integrated science curricula, the achievement items
were designed for different science knowledge concepts based on the released item information from TIMSS 2019 (Fishbein et al., 2021). For example, the SP52021 item
measures ecosystems in the biology content area in TIMSS 2019 (Fishbein et al., 2021).
This study calculated propensity scores based on stacked ensemble learning to
compare the impact of two curricula on student science achievement. First, we used
KNN to generate the matched integrated and separated groups. The Mann–Whitney
U test results suggest that the integrated group slightly outperformed the separated
one. However, the effect size was small, suggesting that the learning difference
between the two curricula was not large among TIMSS participants.
Furthermore, this study investigated whether the science curriculum type is critical in
predicting student academic performance by comparing multiple algorithms. Random
forest achieved the best prediction accuracy among the five algorithms, with the possibility of overfitting. The poor performance of linear and lasso regression suggested
that the relationship between independent and dependent variables was likely nonlinear. Therefore, results suggested that the random forest algorithm could be suitable
for similar tasks where the relationship between a large set of independent and dependent
variables is likely to be non-linear.
Moreover, the first research question found that the effect of integrated curricula on
students’ achievement was better than that of separated ones. The machine learning
results found that curriculum setting was a weak predictor in directly predicting students’
achievement. Combining two results leads to the conclusion that curriculum setting may
indirectly influence students’ science literacy by influencing the covariates included in
this study. The interaction analysis revealed that the science curriculum influenced the
relationship between students’ enjoyment of science learning and their achievement.
Among the students in the separated group with low or medium enjoyment in learning
science, a positive association existed between liking science and achievement. In contrast, within the integrated group, an increase in liking science corresponded to decreased
science performance; furthermore, this relationship was reversed for students with high
enjoyment. This interaction relationship indicated that the marginal advantage of an
integrated science curriculum in enhancing student achievement was indirectly realised.
Moreover, the average value for most of the independent variables in Table 2 was
higher for the integrated group (e.g. ‘instructional clarity,’ ‘students like to learn
science,’ ‘students feel confident about science,’ and ‘teachers emphasize science investigation’). Notably, the average instructional time reported by teachers who teach integrated science was almost twice that of teachers who teach separated science. These
descriptive statistics constitute another piece of evidence that supports the indirect marginal advantage of integrated science over separated science curricula. Therefore, a
follow-up analysis (e.g. structural equation modelling) is needed to examine the indirect
influence mechanism between curriculum type, other variables in the PPCT model, and
student achievement.
Educational implications
Many students who received integrated science curricula reported unclear instruction,
which echoes the previous results that the implementation effect of the integrated

INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF SCIENCE EDUCATION

1127

science curriculum requires several prerequisites. For example, a coherent textbook, a
competent instructor, and an appropriate measurement instrument indicate that educational practitioners and researchers could devote themselves to achieving the abovementioned prerequisites. Moreover, some instruction or curriculum-related background
variables in the process dimension of the PPCT model (e.g. science experiment frequency) were critical in predicting student achievement. Concomitantly, many students
disliked science experiments, although they may like science in general. Indeed, the correlations between ‘students like science experiments’ and ‘students like to learn science,’
‘instructional clarity,’ and ‘students feel confident about science’ were all significantly
negative: −0.60, −0.43, and −0.36, respectively. This attitude is important in the
person dimension of the PPCT model. Henceforth, improving the curriculum of
science experiments instruction is also necessary.
Although the answers to the two research questions appear to be contradictory, propensity score matching revealed that integrated science benefited students more than
separated curricula. However, machine learning algorithms indicated that the science
curricula did not predict student achievement directly. It is worth noting that the
findings are not in opposition to the discussion that follows. Machine learning algorithms
revealed feature interaction relationships, combining the descriptive statistics, enabling
the inference that the instructional details of integrated science curricula exhibited
greater variance among all participating countries or regions than separated curricula.
This disparity arises from the difficulties in satisfying the essential prerequisites of an
integrated curriculum, as discussed previously.
It is possible that some implementations satisfy the prerequisites of an integrated
science curriculum while others do not. This discrepancy leads to large variability in
instructional detail variables. However, when only considering central tendency statistics,
such as the mean of these instructional details, those differences were mitigated by the
average. On average, students who received integrated science instruction showed
more positive attitudes toward science learning enjoyment and confidence than those
who received separated science instruction, as shown in Table 2. Therefore, it is critical
for some educational systems to elucidate the reasons behind the poor direct effect of
science curricula on student achievement.
Theoretical implications
Regarding the long-standing debate between separated and integrated science curricula,
this study first suggested that an integrated curriculum benefited students marginally
more than a separated one based on a large-scale data set. Additionally, several features
predicting students’ performance were identified, including ‘students feel confident
about science, ‘instructional time reported by teachers,’ and ‘science experiment frequency.’ The relationships between curriculum background features that predict
student performance verified the several prerequisites for effective curriculum
implementation. Specifically, when students reported explicit instruction, they often
liked learning science and felt confident about science. However, when students performed positively on these three variables, they tended to dislike science experiments,
indicating that the overall science experiments were not satisfactory for students, at
least among TIMSS 2019 participants. Moreover, this study found that the relationship

1128

Y. SONG AND M. CUTUMISU

between the learning process-related variables and student science performance was not
linear. Future research is needed to verify whether curriculum type indirectly influences
students’ achievement through curriculum aspects, such as instructional time.
Methodological implications
Previous studies on this issue have primarily been empirical with small sample sizes. If
the sample cannot represent the population or if the implementation does not meet
the intended design, then the student benefits from the curriculum will be affected. To
overcome the limitations of existing studies, this study employed a large-scale assessment
data set and machine learning approaches to compare the effects of the curricula on
student learning gains. The significant comparison results and high prediction suggested
that machine learning methods are well suited to this research problem. Big educational
data could answer questions that traditional methods could not quickly solve. Especially
when the problem relates to educational policy or is of immediate worldwide interest,
educational data mining techniques can be used to extract implicit relationships and
meaning from big educational data. The current study employed the student science
achievement as the dependent variable and all variables in the Process dimension of
the PPCT model as the independent variables. However, using some variables in the
Process dimension as the dependent variable would be worthwhile to explore in the
future to better understand whether the curriculum type directly predicts such attitude
variables (e.g. ‘students feel confident about science’). Moreover, some variables in the
Process dimension of the PPCT model were reported by the teacher. Therefore, in
future analysis, applying the hierarchical model for teacher- and student-level variables
is necessary to investigate whether multilevel relationships exist among variables.
Practical implications
The most salient predictive features include student socio-economic status, attitudes
toward science, instructional time, and experiment conducting frequency. Although it is
challenging to change some of these student features (e.g. socio-economic status), others
have the potential to be impacted by practitioner-led interventions, situated in the
process dimension of the PPCT model adapted for this study (e.g. changing the instructional time and experiment frequency based on the student’s needs and capabilities).
The high positive correlation between ‘students like to learn science,’ ‘students feel
confident about science,’ and ‘instructional clarity’ was in line with the previous conclusions. However, despite these positive associations, this study also found that students
often dislike scientific experiments. Such a finding warrants further investigation because
conducting experiments may teach students several knowledge and skills. Moreover, it is
important to pay attention to student attitudes, as they may influence their learning
experiences and outcomes.
Limitations
This study found that integrated science was marginally more beneficial to students than
a separated curriculum. However, the present study did not explain why integrated

INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF SCIENCE EDUCATION

1129

science benefits students more. To answer this question, exploring the internal influence
mechanisms between curriculum type and student achievement is necessary, which is
crucial for understanding and enhancing science curriculum implementations.
Moreover, this study did not analyze the specific participant country because the
primary purpose was to determine which curriculum benefitted students more.
However, it is worthwhile to deeply analyze participant countries and their curriculum-related policies, design, practice, and feedback from teachers and students to understand why integrated science benefits students more in one country than in another.

Conclusions
This study applied several popular machine learning algorithms to compare the effects of
two curricula on student science learning. Two comparable groups were obtained using
propensity score matching, and the integrated group showed slightly better science
achievement than the separated group among TIMSS 2019 participants. Findings
revealed that random forests achieved the best prediction accuracy. In addition to
student socio-economic backgrounds, other essential features could be considered in
future research, including student attitudes toward science and instruction-related background details. These relationships need to be examined to further understand the
influence mechanism of curriculum setting on student science literacy.

Appendix A
Data pre-processing
The raw data contains duplicates of data for one student in different classes, which means the same
student taught by the same teacher but in different classes has two entries in the data set. Therefore, unique data records were kept for each student.
The missing data reports for integrated and separated curricula are shown in Figures A1 and
A2, respectively. After checking the region sources of the five parallel blanks in Figure A2, we
found that the data on variables linked to Earth Science for two countries, Lebanon and
Sweden, in the separated curriculum was missing systematically. The Lebanon and Sweden chapters in the TIMSS 2019 Encyclopedia (Kelly et al., 2020) suggested that Earth Science and Biology
are integrated in Lebanon (Center for Educational Research and Development, 2020, p. 6),
whereas Earth Science and Physics are integrated in Sweden (Axelsson, 2020, p. 12). Hence, as
it is not easy to determine the curriculum type partition for these two nations, the data from
these two countries were excluded from this analysis.
Entries for variables that have less than 5% missing records were removed. Deleting data
for variables with systematically missing patterns may lead to bias. In this case, the preferable
option is to impute the missing data. Therefore, the present study employed the KNN
imputation technique. As part of the pre-processing step, dummy coding was adopted to
represent categorical variables, and the features were standardised for their use in similarity
matching.
