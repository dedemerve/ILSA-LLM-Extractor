Self-efficacy is a critical psychological construct that exerts a positive impact on students’ learning experiences
and global well-being. Previous studies explored the factors related to the development and variation of students’
self-efficacy, but they only focused on a limited set of predictors. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of
the factors affecting self-efficacy, it is necessary to build a predictive model based on a large number of predictors
using a data-driven approach. Therefore, guided by socio-ecological theory, we categorized 256 candidate
predictors from the PISA 2018 student and school questionnaires in five levels of socio-ecological systems. We
then used two machine learning algorithms, Lasso and XGBoost, to predict self-efficacy of 612,004 students aged
15 to 16 years from 79 countries and regions. The results showed that XGBoost outperformed Lasso. We then
extracted feature importance from the best-performing XGBoost model to rank the features both overall and
within each level of the socio-ecological systems. The analysis revealed that individual-level attributes such as
mastery goal orientation, meaning of life, and positive emotions were the most important predictors of selfefficacy. Other significant contextual factors included parents’ emotional support, home possessions, and
school climate factors (e.g., cooperation climate). Furthermore, self-efficacy varied significantly across countries.
This study advances our understanding of self-efficacy by identifying the important predictors from different
levels of socio-ecological perspectives. The results suggest that self-efficacy is a composite outcome shaped by a
myriad of influences spanning from individual factors to broader socio-ecological perspectives.

Self-efficacy comprises a set of beliefs regarding an individual’s
ability to carry out specific tasks or meet specific objectives (Bandura,
1977, 1997). Moreover, self-efficacy is a central component of Albert
Bandura’s (1977, 1985) social cognitive theory, which offers a lens to
understand one’s behaviors, motives, affective states, thought patterns,
and cognitive processes. Although Bandura initially conceptualized selfefficacy as domain- or task-specific, subsequent researchers (e.g., Sherer
et al., 1982; Schwarzer, 1992; Schwarzer, 1997; Schwarzer & Jerusalem,
1995) introduced the idea of general self-efficacy as a broad, stable sense
of personal competence across diverse or novel situations. In this
framework, domain-specific self-efficacy emphasizes perceived competence within specific situations, whereas general self-efficacy captures
an overarching belief in one’s competence. Aligning with this broader
interpretation, the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA;
OECD, 2019) defines self-efficacy as a belief that one’s actions can lead
to desired outcomes, serving as a driving force behind one’s motivation

to take action and persist in the face of challenges. The present study
adopts PISA’s definition to examine how students’ self-efficacy relates to
an array of individual- and contextual-level predictors. Compared to
domain-specific self-efficacy, which reflects beliefs about one’s ability to
succeed in a particular setting or task, general self-efficacy tends to be
more strongly associated with a broader array of personal and contextual influences (Grether, Sowislo and Wiese, 2018; Lisá, 2020).
Students’ general self-efficacy significantly influences their learning
experiences, well-being, and even life outcomes (Govorova, Benítez, &
Muñiz, 2020). There is consistent evidence that students’ self-efficacy is
one of the most reliable and important predictors of their learning
achievement (e.g., Areepattamannil, Freeman and Klinger, 2011;
Govorova et al., 2020) and learning engagement (Linnenbrink & Pintrich, 2003). High self-efficacious students tend to use more effective
learning strategies at all levels of competencies (Schunk & Ertmer,
2000). Bassi, Steca, Fave and Caprara, 2007 also suggested that these

Although extensive studies have been conducted regarding the
relationship between self-efficacy and various aspects of students’
learning and personal development (e.g., Bandura, 1977; Joët, Usher, &
Bressoux, 2011; Maddux & Stanley, 1986; Shehzad, Alghorbany,
Lashari, Lashari, & Razzaq, 2019), most of them have only examined the
relationship among a few variables. To gain a more comprehensive
understanding of the development and variation of students’ selfefficacy, it is essential to consider a larger number of variables that
capture both individual level (e.g., personal backgrounds and characteristics) and broader contextual levels (e.g., home background, teacher
background, and school environment). This study addresses this gap by
applying machine learning (ML) algorithms to predict students’ selfefficacy using data from PISA 2018. Leveraging a large, diverse student sample and a wide array of candidate predictors categorized across
socio-ecological levels, we aim to uncover the most important factors
associated with students’ self-efficacy and contribute to a more integrated understanding of this critical construct.

Lastly, physiological and emotional states provide authentic information that one can draw to infer one’s competencies. When individuals
feel negative emotions such as fear or experience physical symptoms like
shaking, they often interpret these reactions as signs of incapability
which further undermines their self-efficacy. Conversely, positive
emotions such as feeling proud or excited can boost people’s confidence
in their competencies. Empirical evidence has supported the close
relationship between emotions and self-efficacy (Andretta and McKay,
2020; Caroli and Sagone, 2014; Villavicencio & Bernardo, 2016). This is
because students with positive emotions tend to generate thoughts and
actions that increase their self-efficacy, such as setting higher learning
aspirations, perceiving higher task values, and promoting selfregulatory behaviors (Fredrickson, 2013; Lyubomirsky, King, &
Diener, 2005).
While these four primary sources of self-efficacy all reside at the
individual level, they can also interact with other individual and
contextual factors, leading to further variations of self-efficacy. For
example, a cross-cultural study reported that vicarious experience and
social persuasion predicted self-efficacy equally well for Korean and US
students, whereas social persuasion was the best predictor for Filipino
students (Ahn, Usher, Butz and Bong, 2016). Students’ self-efficacy can
also vary by gender, although the empirical evidence to support this is
largely inconsistent in the related literature. For instance, Arslan (2013)
found that female Turkish secondary students were more often influenced by mastery experience, social persuasion, and physiological state
than male students. On the other hand, a US study revealed that mastery
experience, vicarious experience, and social persuasion could predict 18
% of the variance in male students’ self-efficacy, but only 15 % of the
variance in female students’ self-efficacy (Stevens, Wang, Olivárez, &
Hamman, 2007). In addition, the sources of self-efficacy depend on
students’ achievement levels. Specifically, Arslan (2013) found that
both high and low achievers could benefit the most from mastery
experience and vicarious experience in terms of boosting their selfefficacy beliefs, whereas social persuasion and mastery experience
work best for middle achievers. Similarly, Usher and Pajares (2006)
found that high achievers’ self-efficacy was best predicted by mastery
experience and physiological state, while medium achievers’ selfefficacy was more influenced by mastery experience and verbal
persuasion. In similar ways, the effects of sources of self-efficacy also
differ across grade level, social-economic status, and learning style
(Arslan, 2013; Pajares, Johnson, & Usher, 2007; Wigfield, Eccles and
Rodriguez, 1998), suggesting the importance of understanding the
relationship between self-efficacy beliefs and their contextual factors.
These studies imply that self-efficacy is not only formed based on
individuals’ perspectives and their direct interactions with other people
and their surroundings (i.e., mastery experience, vicarious experience,
verbal persuasion, and physiological and emotional states) but also
shaped and reinforced by factors in higher-level contexts, such as cultural differences. Therefore, for education stakeholders to better understand the development and variation of students’ self-efficacy, a
more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between selfefficacy and predictors at the individual level and at higher levels is
needed.

Primary sources for development and variation of self-efficacy
Bandura’s (1977, 1985) social cognitive theory is the most wellestablished theory for self-efficacy. The theory posits that self-efficacy
beliefs originate from four primary sources: mastery experience, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological and emotional
states. Although Bandura originally focused on domain-specific self-efficacy, the same four primary sources he identified are also relevant for
general self-efficacy (Schwarzer, 1992; Luszczynska, Gutiérrez-Doña, &
Schwarzer, 2005).
Mastery experience refers to an individual’s past experience of successfully performing specific tasks. Researchers often consider mastery
experiences the most powerful source of developing high self-efficacy, as
they provide direct evidence that students truly had the capability to
succeed on similar tasks (Palmer, 2006; Schunk & Meece, 2005).
Empirical research supports this view. For instance, Kleppang, Steigen,
and Finbråten (2023) examined predictors of adolescents’ self-efficacy,
including mastery experience, social support, and sociodemographic
factors (e.g., gender, school level, and parental education). Their findings showed that mastery experience accounted for the largest proportion of variance in adolescents’ self-efficacy.
Vicarious experience involves learning through observing others.
This type of experience provides opportunities for students to alter their
self-efficacy beliefs by modeling and comparing themselves with other
people (Bandura, 1997; Usher & Pajares, 2008). Students are likely to
develop a higher self-efficacy if they see their peers succeed, especially
when they share similar attributes (e.g., age, gender, and life background; Schunk, 1987).
Verbal persuasion is experienced as another’s direct influence on an
individual through the use of language. Especially in uncertain and

whereas performance-avoidance and mastery-avoidance goal orientations showed weaker associations. Additionally, longitudinal studies
highlight a reciprocal relationship between self-efficacy and academic
achievement, with higher self-efficacy leading to better performance,
which in turn reinforces self-efficacy (Hwang et al., 2016). Researchers
have identified several mediators in this relationship, including learning
engagement (Linnenbrink & Pintrich, 2003), intrinsic motivation,
learning strategies, and learning aspirations (Honicke & Broadbent,
2016). Beyond academic outcomes, self-efficacy is also closely tied to
students’ mental health and emotional well-being. Research consistently
shows that students experiencing depression tend to report lower
self-efficacy, whereas higher levels of life satisfaction and a sense of
meaning in life are positively associated with stronger self-efficacy beliefs (Çakar, 2012; Yuen and Datu, 2021). Personal experiences and
behaviors further shape self-efficacy development. For example, students repeating a grade often report lower self-efficacy, likely due to
setbacks in academic progression and self-perception (Marsh et al.,
2017; Rosário, Núñez, Valle, González-Pienda, & Lourenço, 2013).
Similarly, school attendance behaviors (e.g., skipping class or arriving
late) have been negatively associated with self-efficacy (Becker and
Gable, 2009; Bianchi et al., 2022).

While social cognitive theory identifies the sources of self-efficacy at
the individual level and within the immediate environment, exploring
the relationship between self-efficacy and additional predictors across
both individual and broader levels requires another theoretical framework. Urie Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) socio-ecological theory is one of the
most widely applied frameworks in understanding child development.
This framework specifies that children’s development is a joint function
of the influences of multiple nested ecological systems from narrow to
extremely broad, including five levels of systems (i.e., individual,
microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem). Researchers
often use this framework to understand how factors at various socioecological levels interact and collectively impact child development.
At the center of the socio-ecological model is the individual, representing a constellation of individual characteristics, attributes, behaviors, and experiences. A higher level is the microsystem, which contains
the relationships and activities in which the child directly connects or
participates (e.g., family, teachers, and peers). Following that, the
mesosystem is constructed as the set of interconnections among the elements in the microsystem, such as the connections between students’
parents and teachers. School resources, policies, and climate stand at the
exosystem, where the elements in the system do not directly affect students but affect the elements in the microsystem such as teachers. Those
effects eventually pass on to the individuals, influencing their development. Finally, the macrosystem is the highest level of socio-ecological
contexts, including social or cultural beliefs and ideologies that affect
the inner systems (e.g., political and cultural predictors).
Researchers in developmental psychology and education have
widely employed the socio-ecological model to investigate a variety of
constructs and phenomena related to child development and students’
lives, including school bullying (Lim & Hoot, 2015; Shams, Garmaroudi,
Nedjat, & Yekaninejad, 2018), school belonging (Allen, Vella-Brodrick,
& Waters, 2018), mental health (Scharpf, Kaltenbach, Nickerson, &
Hecker, 2021), physical activity participation (Devís-Devís, Beltrán-Carrillo, & Peiró-Velert, 2015; Essiet, Baharom, Shahar and Uzochukwu,
2017), problem behaviors (Hsieh et al., 2015, 2016), executive functioning (Scharpf, Mueller, & Hecker, 2022), and academic achievement
(Strohmeier & Wagner, 2023; Wang, King, & Leung, 2023). For instance,
in the realm of self-efficacy, Chen, Ho, Goh, Ooi, & Siau (2024)
employed the socio-ecological model to examine the relationship between parental control and self-efficacy among 453 secondary school
students in Malaysia. They found a significant negative correlation between both paternal and maternal control and students’ self-efficacy.
Despite such work, we did not identify any prior studies that systematically use the socio-ecological model to categorize a large set of predictors of self-efficacy.
The present study utilizes Bronfenbrenner’s framework as a theoretical guide to categorize a wide range of candidate predictors from the
PISA 2018 dataset, aiming to enhance the interpretability of the predictive results regarding self-efficacy. By organizing these predictors
into the five socio-ecological levels (i.e., individual, microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem levels), the study seeks to identify
the most important predictors of students’ self-efficacy, both overall and
within each specific level.

Microsystem level
At the microsystem level, students’ self-efficacy is directly shaped by
their immediate interactions with parents, teachers, peers, and the
school environment. Parenting strategies significantly influence students’ self-efficacy, with authoritarian and permissive parenting styles
often linked to lower self-efficacy levels (Scott & Mallinckrodt, 2012;
Yousaf, 2015). In contrast, parents who provide challenges, set high but
attainable expectations, model problem-solving behaviors, and
encourage perseverance foster greater self-efficacy in their children
(Schunk & Meece, 2005). Family support also plays a crucial role, as
research indicates a positive relationship between family encouragement and self-efficacy that, in turn, enhances students’ psychosocial
well-being (Vieno, Santinello, Pastore, & Perkins, 2007). Additionally,
socioeconomic status (Han, Chu, Song, & Li, 2015; Karaarslan & Sungur,
2011) and family structure (e.g., intact vs. single-parent households)
have been identified as key factors in self-efficacy development (Kalter,
Alpern, Spence, & Plunkett, 1984; Kurtz & Derevensky, 1994). Beyond
the family, students develop self-efficacy beliefs through their interactions with teachers and the school environment. Teachers serve as
pivotal agents in fostering self-efficacy by employing effective instructional strategies, demonstrating enthusiasm, offering encouragement
and support, and creating supportive learning environments (Chan &
Lam, 2010; Sawyer, Graham, & Harris, 1992; Siegle & McCoach, 2007;
Mitchell & DellaMattera, 2010). Moreover, teachers’ own self-efficacy,
qualifications, and teaching experience have been positively associated with students’ self-efficacy levels (Mojavezi & Tamiz, 2012; Öqvist
& Malmström, 2018). Peer interactions also contribute to self-efficacy
development by providing opportunities for collaborative learning, social comparison, modeling, and positive reinforcement (Nyman, Parisod, Axelin, & Salanterä, 2019; Wheeler and Ladd, 1982). Furthermore,
the school environment itself contains multiple potential influences on
self-efficacy at the microsystem level, such as curriculum design
(Krsmanovic, 2021), grading practices (Alkharusi, Aldhafri, Alnabhani,
& Alkalbani, 2014; Bub, 2021), and availability of academic support
services (Smith, Walter, & Hoey, 1992).

Such large-scale assessment datasets offer researchers valuable opportunities to explore students’ academic achievement alongside personal and contextual factors, including self-efficacy. For example,
Borgonovi and Pokropek (2019) analyzed data from PISA 2012 to
investigate the development of self-efficacy among middle-school students. Their findings suggest that task-specific experiences significantly
shape self-efficacy beliefs, underscoring the importance of tailored
educational interventions. Moreover, Jin, Wu, Xia, and Zhao (2023)
analyzed data from PISA 2018 to investigate the cultural values influencing student self-efficacy. Their research implies that cultural contexts
significantly shape students’ self-efficacy beliefs, suggesting that
educational strategies should be tailored to align with cultural values to
effectively enhance self-efficacy.
Given the high dimensionality of large-scale datasets, researchers are
increasingly using advanced data-driven methods such as ML to extract
meaningful insights. For example, Acıslı-Celik and Yesilkanat, 2023
used ML approaches to predict students’ science achievement in PISA
2015 and PISA 2018. Additionally, researchers have applied ML algorithms to predict students’ metacognitive variables. For example,
Lezhnina and Kismihók (2022) developed a flexible approach that
combined statistical and ML methods to explore German students’ attitudes toward information and communication technology in relation
to their mathematical and scientific literacy, as measured by PISA 2015
and 2018. Yi and Na, 2020 employed the random forest algorithm, logistic regression, and the hierarchical general linear model to build
prediction models for math anxiety based on 194 variables from PISA
student questionnaires. The most significant predictors identified were
enjoyment of math, self-concept, and attributions to failure.
Despite the growing use of ML in analyzing large-scale assessment
data, most existing applications have focused on predicting students’
academic achievement and metacognitive variables. To our knowledge,
no prior studies have specifically applied ML approaches to predict
students’ self-efficacy using large-scale international assessment data.

Exosystem level
The exosystem level comprises external factors that, while not
directly influencing students, shape their environment by affecting key
microsystem elements (e.g., teachers and families) and mesosystem interactions (e.g., school-family partnerships). For example, advantaged
schools—those that are well-funded and located in desirable areas—tend to attract highly qualified teachers and provide access to
advanced curricula, technology, and academic support services, all of
which contribute to a learning environment that fosters self-efficacy
(Baker, 2016; Maranto, 2013; Palmer, 2006; Wang, Tigelaar and
Admiraal, 2019). Additionally, teacher evaluation systems and governance policies play a crucial role in shaping teachers’ well-being, job
satisfaction, and effectiveness that, in turn, influence students’ learning
experiences and self-efficacy (Shinkfield & Stufflebeam, 2012).
Macrosystem level
At the macrosystem level, broad sociocultural, political, and religious factors may influence both general and domain-specific self-efficacy in students. For instance, Ahn, Usher, Butz and Bong, 2016 found
that collectivist cultures tend to report lower academic self-efficacy
despite high levels of academic achievement. The PISA 2018 results
(OECD, 2019d) support this pattern, which show that five economies—Vietnam, Malaysia, Russia, Macao (China), and Kazakhstan—ranked the lowest in the percentage of students reporting high
levels of general self-efficacy. Additionally, Constantine, Okazaki and
Utsey, 2010 found that, among international students in the U.S., Latin
American students reported the highest levels of social self-efficacy
compared to their African American and Asian American peers. Religious beliefs may also play a role in shaping self-efficacy. For example,
Junaidi, Anwar, & Sahrir (2023) found that religion fosters social capital
(e.g., social interaction, knowledge exchange, and trust) that, in turn,
enhances entrepreneurial self-efficacy among Indonesian students.
While direct research on the relationship between macrosystem factors
and student self-efficacy remains limited, existing studies suggest that
self-efficacy varies significantly across different macrosystem
influences.

The current study
The present study aimed to predict 15- and 16-year-olds’ self-efficacy
using a large number of candidate predictors from PISA 2018. Guided by
Bronfenbrenner’s socio-ecological theory, we categorized these predictors into five levels (i.e., individual, microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem) to enhance the interpretability of our
findings. By structuring the analysis through this theoretical lens, the
study sought to identify the most important factors influencing selfefficacy both overall and within each socio-ecological level. To this
end, the study posed the following research questions:

Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS), and the
Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) have gained
increasing popularity in recent decades. Conducted across numerous
countries, these studies generate high-dimensional datasets encompassing a wide range of student-, school-, and system-level variables,
and involve large sample sizes—often hundreds of thousands of students
globally. For example, PISA periodically assesses the reading, mathematics, and science knowledge of 15- and 16-year-old students

Dataset
This study drew on data from the publicly available PISA 2018
dataset (OECD, 2019a), specifically utilizing variables from the Student
Questionnaire and the Principal Questionnaire (OECD, 2019b). We
merged data from these two sources by linking students to their corresponding school principals, noting that multiple students were often
associated with the same principal. The merged dataset includes responses from 612,004 students across 79 countries and regions and offers a wide range of variables. These include, but are not limited to,
information on students’ backgrounds, attitudes, and learning environments, as well as school-level characteristics reported by principals.
Together, these variables serve as candidate predictors of students’ selfefficacy across different socio-ecological levels.
Response Variable.
In this study, we treated self-efficacy as the response variable, representing the variable to be predicted. PISA 2018 Student Questionnaire
measured self-efficacy using five items: “I usually manage one way or
another”, “I feel proud that I have accomplished things”, “I feel that I can
handle many things at a time”, “My belief in myself gets me through
hard times”, and “When I’m in a difficult situation, I can usually find my
way out of it”. Students responded to these items on a five-point Likert
scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). To align
with the ML approach and optimize the performance of predictive
models, we transformed students’ responses to these five items into a
single continuous self-efficacy score using an Item Response Theory
(IRT) model. Further details of this transformation are provided in the
Data Preprocessing section.
Features.
Students’ test results along with other contextual factors collected
from the school questionnaire and the mandatory parts of the student
questionnaire constituted the features (i.e., predictors) in this study.
These predictors included both individual item responses and, where
applicable, derived construct scores based on multiple survey items. We
first grouped the predictors into modules based on the PISA 2018
Questionnaire Framework (OECD, 2019c). Then, we mapped each
module onto one of the five levels of ecological systems depicted by the
socio-ecological model. Table 1 presents the final classification of the
predictors by module and the level of the ecological system.

Methods
To predict students’ self-efficacy using PISA 2018 data, we developed a multi-stage methodological workflow, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We
drew on prior research (Farhood, Joudah, Beheshti, & Muller, 2024;
Swathy & Saruladha, 2022) to inform this visual representation and
adapted and expanded to better accommodate the large-scale analysis of
self-efficacy and its predictors in our study. Regarding data preparation,
we categorized predictors according to socio-ecological levels and performed data cleaning and variable transformation. In the model training
and evaluation stage, we selected two ML algorithms—XGBoost and
Lasso regression—and trained models using nested cross-validation. We
then compared model performance and identified the best-performing
model, from which we extracted feature importance scores for each
predictor. For interpretation, we ranked predictors by their feature
importance both overall and within each socio-ecological level. We also
conducted reduced and exclusion model analyses to support further
interpretation. The following subsections provide detailed description
and explanations of our methodology.

Data preprocessing
We handled missing data in the dataset in two stages. In the first
stage, we eliminated rows with missing data on any of the five items
used to measure self-efficacy. We chose listwise deletion over imputation to avoid introducing additional errors to the response variable. This
step eliminated 84,179 records from the original dataset, reducing it to
its final 527,825 records. In the second stage, we imputed missing predictor values by substituting median values (Maniruzzaman et al.,
2018).
Next, we transformed the responses on the self-efficacy items into
item response scores using a polytomous IRT model, the generalized
partial credit model (Muraki, 1992). We adopted this method following
the approach used in the PISA 2018 Technical Report (OECD, 2019a).
An advantage of using the IRT scores is that they differentiate values of
different items even though respondents score the same on those items.
For example, because each item has a different ability to discriminate
respondents’ attributes, scoring “3” on item A is not equivalent to
scoring “3” on item B. Thus, respondents are more likely to score high on
certain items over others. With this consideration, IRT scores reveal
more information about students’ self-efficacy according to their
divergent response composites on the five items measuring self-efficacy.
Theoretically, IRT scores range from negative infinity to positive infinity
on a continuous scale. To make the IRT scores more interpretable, we
linearly transformed them by multiplying them by 15 to increase the
standard deviation and adding 100 to them to ensure that all values were
positive. The choice of 15 and 100 was arbitrary, being meant to facilitate interpretation (i.e., yield positive self-efficacy scores). However,
this linear transformation does not change the underlying relative values
of the measured constructs.
We reverse-coded responses on negatively worded items for the
predictor variables, computed total scores for relevant scales, and
created dummy variables for the categorical predictors. The final preprocessed dataset contained a total of 256 predictors (including dummy
variables) and one outcome variable (self-efficacy).

strict assumptions (e.g., linearity, normality). While these methods are
the most popular and effective for studies involving a limited number of
variables, they might not be the optimal choice for high-dimensional
datasets due to challenges, such as managing a large number of

To understand how predictors (individual level and beyond) are
associated with students’ self-efficacy, we trained two explanatory
predictive models using the XGBoost and Lasso Regression algorithms.
We selected these algorithms for three principal reasons. First, both deal
well with many predictors or features (Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Tibshirani, 1996). Second, XGBoost is a nonlinear, nonparametric, treebased ensemble that captures nonlinearities and interactions (Denisko
and Hoffman, 2018), whereas Lasso is a parametric linear regression
method with an L1 penalty for feature selection (Tibshirani, 1996). This
contrast enables us to cross-check results from two fundamentally
distinct approaches (Bzdok & Meyer-Lindenberg, 2018). Third, both
algorithms are noted for their high predictive accuracy in educational
and psychological research (Grover and Mehra, 2008; Lee et al., 2018).
Importantly, they each provide a way to evaluate feature importance,
enabling us to pinpoint key determinants of self-efficacy while
concomitantly benefitting from their high predictive performance.
Below, we describe each algorithm and discuss their strengths and
limitations.
XGBoost is a combination of decision trees and boosting algorithms
(Chen & Guestrin, 2016). The decision tree algorithm generates predictions using a sequence of if-then rules. In each stage of the sequence,
the if-then rules partition the data points into subsets based on the node
with the lowest error rate. By repeating the procedure until the stopping
criterion is met, the split will eventually end. Boosting is a method for
correcting the mistakes of current models by adding new models
sequentially to predict the residuals of the existing model, and then
making another prediction along with the existing model. Throughout
iterations, each execution is performed on the same dataset, and subsequent models are refinements of earlier models. Eventually, the prediction errors will be gradually reduced, and the iteration process will
terminate when the model’s performance converges to a steady state.
Although XGBoost is powerful for capturing complex relationships due
to its optimization of if-then rules among variables (Denisko and Hoffman, 2018), it requires careful hyperparameter tuning to avoid overfitting—a situation where the model performs well on training data but
fails to generalize to new or broader datasets.
Lasso regression is a type of regularized linear regression that works
the same as ordinary least-squares linear regression, with additional
operations that impose larger penalties on unimportant predictors and
shrink their regression coefficients toward zero (Tibshirani, 1996). The
penalties are determined by seeking to minimize the sum of the absolute
value of the regression coefficients. Such operations are advantageous in
reducing model complexity and improving prediction performance by
decreasing the influence of unimportant predictors. As the shrinkage
function of the Lasso regression forces some of the coefficients of the
unimportant predictors to be exactly equal to zero when its shrinkage

Model training and evaluation
We implemented the XGBoost regressor and Lasso regressor in the
Python 3.7 environment using the xgboost library (Chen & Guestrin,
2016) and the scikit-learn library (Pedregosa et al., 2011), respectively.
The scikit-learn library was also used for all phases of the model development process including training, validation, and testing. To ensure
robust and reliable (i.e., less biased) model tuning and performance, we
applied nested cross-validation combined with a grid search algorithm
(Krstajic, Buturovic, Leahy, & Thomas, 2014). Fig. 2 depicts the two
layers that make up the nested cross-validation algorithm: both outerand inner cross-validation employed four folds. The code, processed
data, figures, and model results are available in a GitHub repository
(GitHub Resources, 2025).
Given the robustness of prediction results achieved with a large
sample size and nested cross-validation algorithms, we considered a
four-fold split acceptable. With inner cross-validation, the algorithm
seeks to find the hyperparameters that result in the best model performance on the validation dataset, whereas in the outer cross-validation,
the algorithm aims to test the generalizability of the tuned model performance to a new dataset (i.e., the test set). In the inner folds, we tuned
hyperparameters for the XGBoost included the maximum depth of the
trees (max_depth) and the number of trees used in the model (n_estimators). We selected the maximum tree depth from a range of 5 to 25,
with a 5-step increment, and set the number of trees to 100, 150, or 200.
For the Lasso algorithm, we tuned only the learning rate, choosing from
the values 0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1. To evaluate
the model performance during both validation and testing, we used
Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and R2.
We also used R2 to identify the optimal hyperparameters and compare
model performance.
Feature importance extraction and ranking
From the best-performing model with all predictors, we extracted the
feature importance value for each predictor. In XGBoost, the default
feature importance is based on how frequently a given feature is used to
split the data within all the decision trees (Chen & Guestrin, 2016).
Because of its algorithmic design, XGBoost can capture non-linear relationships effectively (Jin, 2023; Farhood et al., 2024). The algorithm
then normalizes the importance values, expressing each feature’s
importance as a percentage, with the total summing to 100 %. This
approach highlights the features that contribute the most to the model’s
predictive power. In this context, higher feature importance values
indicate greater contributions to the model’s ability to explain variations
in self-efficacy.
For Lasso regression, feature importance is reflected in how large or
small each predictor’s regression coefficient is, after accounting for the
shrinkage penalty (Tibshirani, 1996). Predictors with coefficients that
remain larger in absolute value (i.e., further from zero) indicated greater
importance, whereas those with coefficients shrinking closer to zero (or
set exactly to zero) indicated lesser importance.
After extracting these feature importance values, we ranked each
predictor in descending order based on its importance. We conducted
this ranking both overall (across all predictors) and separately within
each socio-ecological level. This approach allowed us to address our
research questions concerning the most influential predictors of self7

Reduced and exclusion model analysis

The IRT model effectively transformed responses to the five selfefficacy items into a single continuous score with substantial variability. The mean IRT score of self-efficacy was 99.99, with a standard
deviation of 13.28 and a range from 57.60 to 128.32. Fig. 3 shows the
histogram of self-efficacy scores, indicating a leptokurtic distribution
(kurtosis = 3.48), characterized by a sharper peak and heavier tails
compared to a normal distribution. This distribution suggests that a
greater proportion of students scored at the extremes (very high or very
low self-efficacy) than a normal distribution would predict. Additionally, the slight positive skew (skewness = 0.20) suggests a nonsymmetrical distribution, with a tendency for students to cluster toward higher self-efficacy scores.

After determining which algorithm (XGBoost or Lasso) produced the
best overall performance, we constructed an additional model using
only the top predictors from that best-performing method (referred to as
the reduced model). We defined “top predictors” as those with a relative
importance exceeding 1 % in XGBoost or a regression coefficient above 1
in Lasso. While this threshold is somewhat arbitrary, this practice—selecting and testing subsets of high-importance predictors—is
commonly used in ML studies to confirm how much predictive power a
small subset can retain (e.g., Chandrashekar & Sahin, 2014; Guyon &
Elisseeff, 2003; Li et al., 2017). Next, we created a complementary
model that excluded these top predictors entirely (i.e., exclusion model),
thereby revealing how much variance the remaining features alone
could explain. Both subset models used the same algorithm and hyperparameter settings as the full model.
Comparing the R2 values across the full model, reduced model, and
exclusion model addresses three key points. First, if the reduced model
was to exceed the full model’s R2, the result might indicate overfitting in
the original, more complex model (Bishop, 2006). This result would also
suggest that the full model includes unnecessary predictors that introduce noise rather than improving predictive accuracy. Second, if the
reduced model’s R2 is lower than that of the full model, the result indicates that these top predictors are particularly important, while also
acknowledging that less important predictors contribute to the overall
explained variance. The difference in R2 between these two models
highlights the added value of these less important predictors. Third,
examining the exclusion model clarifies whether the less important
predictors still capture a meaningful proportion of the self-efficacy
variance. If the model retains a substantial R2, the result suggests that
these predictors capture important indirect relationships (even though
they may not explain as much variation as the top predictors).
Conversely, if the exclusion model’s R2 is close to zero, the result suggests that the majority of the predictive power stems exclusively from
the top predictors.

important predictors of self-efficacy, both overall and within each specific socio-ecological level. To achieve this goal, we employed XGBoost
and Lasso Regression ML algorithms to model contextual factors from
the PISA 2018 dataset as predictors for self-efficacy. Guided by socioecological theory, we categorized the candidate predictors into five

The prediction results of both the XGBoost and Lasso regression algorithms indicate that the prediction error was small and that the
candidate predictors collectively could explain a substantial proportion
of the variation in students’ self-efficacy. The satisfactory prediction
results lay the foundation for exploring how these predictors relate to
students’ self-efficacy. Particularly, XGBoost outperformed Lasso

nonlinear ensemble predictor XGBoost compared to the linear LASSO

2018), while Lasso regression models all predictors simultaneously

a combination of multiple factors through mechanisms such as mediation (i.e., chains of influence) and moderation (i.e., interactions). For
example, students’ academic aspirations and expectancy-value beliefs
(e.g., subject value, achievement, and cost expectancy) can mediate the
relationship between learning achievement and self-efficacy (Carroll
et al., 2009; Doménech-Betoret, Abellán-Roselló, & Gómez-Artiga,
2017). Owing to its sequential consideration of features in a tree-based
structure, XGBoost is well-suited to identify complex interactions between predictors, thereby enhancing its performance (Denisko and
Hoffman, 2018). While the model does not confirm causal pathways, it
supports the theoretical proposition that self-efficacy is shaped by the

Among factors at the level of the microsystem, parents’ emotional
support emerged as the highest ranking predictor. This finding echoes
several previous studies that have reported associations between parents’ emotional support and self-efficacy (Scott & Mallinckrodt, 2012;
Vieno et al., 2007). From the perspective of social cognitive theory,
parents may shape their children’s self-efficacy through verbal persuasions, such as expressing concerns and providing positive feedback to
their children (Cassé et al., 2015a, 2015b). A similar explanatory
mechanism might account for the high importance of teacher instructional strategies and teacher understanding, both of which being among
the top five predictors in the microsystem. Additionally, home possessions and study possessions were important predictors of students’ selfefficacy at home, which is in line with other studies that suggested a
strong association between self-efficacy and socioeconomic status (Han
et al., 2015; Karaarslan & Sungur, 2011). In contrast, school-related
factors within the microsystem did not rank as highly in this study.
We interpret the relatively weak predictive power of these school-level
factors as possibly reflecting their more indirect influence on students’
self-efficacy. For example, school disadvantages may affect students’
self-efficacy through reduced access to qualified teaching staff and
educational materials (Palmer, 2006). Also, the school curriculum might
shape teachers’ instructional strategies and the time spent in interactions with students (Larson & Keiper, 2012). Nonetheless, although
students spend much of their time in school settings, they may not
interact directly with many of these broader school-level factors, which
could operate at the mesosystem level instead.
Shifting to the mesosystem level, school climate factors, including
competition and cooperation, ranked among the most important predictors of students’ self-efficacy, consistent with previous studies (Lee
et al., 2019; Shin, 2018). For example, both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies showed that students’ learning motivations, sense of
challenge, and learning goals are higher in a more competitive learning
environment (Ding et al., 2022). One possible explanation could be that
competitiveness may affect students’ self-efficacy through the mechanism of vicarious experience (Schunk, 1984). Another explanation
might be that competitive climates provide extensive opportunities for
social comparisons, while social comparison provides standards for
students to judge their own competencies by observing their peers’
performances (Steyn & Mynhardt, 2008). This social comparison
mechanism may also help explain how differentiated classrooms impact
students’ self-efficacy through the well-documented big-fish-little-pond
effects. Beyond competition, cooperative environments may also foster
students’ self-efficacy by enabling vicarious learning through peer
observation. For example, In’am and Sutrisno (2021) found that students’ self-efficacy in learning mathematics was improved after cooperative learning activities. Similarly, Schindler and Bakker (2020) noted
that collaborative problem solving appeared to promote mastery experiences, which could further reinforce students’ self-efficacy beliefs
(Schindler & Bakker, 2020).
The most salient predictors within the exosystem were admission
criteria (the recommendation of feeder schools and the student’s residence in a particular area) and funding source (tuition and government
funding). However, they still had a relatively weaker relationship with
students’ self-efficacy. These predictors were associated with students’
socioeconomic backgrounds and the advantages/disadvantages of
schools and may have indirect relationships with students’ self-efficacy.
As stated previously, several economies and countries of origin
ranked highly among all predictors. This finding suggests that broad
societal and cultural influences may be highly associated with students’
self-efficacy, even though individual-level factors remain the most
directly predictive. The importance of cultural and national background
is consistent with previous research indicating that the relative importance of different sources of self-efficacy can vary across cultural

motivations (Schunk, 1991; Usher & Pajares, 2008). In his social
cognitive theory, Bandura postulated that motivated actions depend
heavily on expected positive consequences which are strongly shaped by
self-efficacy. Thus, the importance of positive emotions and motivational factors suggests potential implications for improving students’
self-efficacy.
Given the cross-sectional nature of the PISA 2018 data, the direction
of influence cannot be conclusively established. However, regardless of
directionality, the strong predictive value of these variables in XGBoost
reinforces their theoretical and empirical importance in understanding
self-efficacy development and variation. Notably, these predictors outperformed many other individual-level and contextual variables
included in the survey, suggesting that students’ affective and motivational characteristics are more strongly associated with self-efficacy
than other surveyed factors. From a socio-ecological perspective, these
individual-level variables may reflect mechanisms through which
broader contextual conditions are linked to students’ beliefs about their
competence.

Practical. As high self-efficacy benefits students’ motivation and
learning experience (Chowdhury and Shahabuddin, 2007), whereas low
self-efficacy is associated with various mental and behavioral problems
(e.g., Tahmassian & Moghadam, 2011), early identification of students
with low self-efficacy is crucial for their educational careers and overall
well-being. Our results suggest that a relatively small set of key predictors can explain students’ self-efficacy nearly as effectively as the full
set of variables. This finding implies that education systems can feasibly
employ ML approaches to monitor and support vulnerable student
populations by focusing on a manageable subset of variables. Such early
warning systems could help educators and policymakers identify students at risk of low self-efficacy and provide targeted interventions.
The results also offer actionable insights for education stakeholders
aiming to promote self-efficacy through well-informed policies and
programs. Notably, we found that affective states emerged as some of
the strongest predictors, indicating that efforts to support students’
emotional well-being—such as promoting positive emotions, reducing
stress, and fostering a sense of meaning in life—may significantly
enhance self-efficacy. From a broader applied perspective, our findings
also imply a layered nature of influences on self-efficacy across socioecological levels. Factors from higher socio-ecological levels may
relate to self-efficacy indirectly by interacting with more proximal
mechanisms at the individual or microsystem level. Therefore, interventions at the policy or institutional level should consider how they
can directly or indirectly enhance the primary sources of self-efficacy
and facilitate individual-level processes.

Contributions and Implications
Theoretical. To our knowledge, this study is the first to predict students’ self-efficacy using a large number of variables at different socioecological levels. Our findings highlight the close relationship between
students’ self-efficacy, their affective states, and motivational attributes,
which is consistent with Bandura’s social cognitive theory. Moreover,
we assessed the importance of predictors simultaneously, revealing that
self-efficacy may closely relate to variables at socio-ecological levels
beyond the individual level, such as parents’ emotional support and the
students’ countries of origin. This finding implies that students’ selfefficacy could be shaped not only at the individual level but also
through interactions with higher-level contextual factors. This interpretation is also consistent with previous research examining the differences in self-efficacy across countries and other demographic
background variables (e.g., Ahn, Usher, Butz and Bong, 2016; Arslan,
2013).
Moreover, our findings suggest a possible hierarchical pattern of
association: while affective and motivational attributes appear to have
more immediate, proximal associations with self-efficacy, higher-level
socio-ecological factors—such as those at the mesosystem or exosystem
level—may relate to self-efficacy indirectly through these individuallevel mediators. Notably, the emergence of certain countries of origin
as top predictors challenges traditional assumptions about the distance
of cultural and societal factors from individual psychological constructs.
This finding raises theoretical possibilities about whether such macrolevel variables are directly associated with students’ self-efficacy or
operate sequentially through intermediary factors at more proximal
levels. Therefore, future theoretical models of self-efficacy may benefit
from more explicitly considering how variables at higher socioecological levels interact with and potentially channel their influence
through individual-level processes.
Methodological. While ML methods have been used to study selfefficacy in prior research, they have typically been applied to smaller
datasets, where classical statistical models may still be viable alternatives. To our knowledge, this study is the first to use ML methods to
predict students’ self-efficacy using a large number of predictors on a
large dataset. As introduced in the Methods section, traditional statistical methods were originally designed to analyze relatively small
samples, whereas ML approaches were particularly developed to efficiently analyze large-scale datasets with many predictors and are thus
more suitable for understanding the response variable more comprehensively (Grover and Mehra, 2008). Additionally, ML approaches
provide tools for assessing and ranking the importance of predictors,
allowing for more nuanced analyses and interpretations of the factors

Limitations
First, while this study focused on general self-efficacy, we recognize
the importance of domain-specific self-efficacy and its potential differences in development and variation. Given that general and domainspecific self-efficacy may relate to different sets of individual and
contextual factors, future research could explore whether domainspecific self-efficacy follows distinct patterns of association and development compared to general self-efficacy. Specifically, studies could
investigate whether the most influential predictors of domain-specific
self-efficacy differ from those identified for general self-efficacy or
whether contextual factors show varying degrees of association
depending on the specificity of self-efficacy being assessed.
Second, the current study relied on the variables available in the
PISA 2018 questionnaires, which limited our ability to examine a
broader range of other potentially important predictors. As such, the
PISA questionnaires did not capture all relevant factors across the socioecological levels that may relate to students’ self-efficacy. Moreover, we
included only those variables that were available for analysis in our ML
models. In particular, the exosystem-level predictors included in the
study were relatively weak in their predictive power, potentially due to
the absence of other critical exosystem-level variables in the dataset. For
instance, teachers’ participation in professional development programs,
which could potentially influence teaching practices and indirectly
affect students’ self-efficacy, was not available for analysis. Therefore, it
is crucial to acknowledge that the relative importance of predictors in
this study reflects comparisons among currently available variables,

rather than encompassing all potential variables within the socioecological levels. As such, the findings of this study should not be
interpreted as representing the relative importance between socioecological levels or among all possible variables within each level in
shaping students’ self-efficacy.
Third, this study cannot make causal claims about the relationships
between the contextual factors and students’ self-efficacy, given the
cross-sectional design of PISA 2018. While XGBoost captures complex
relationships among variables, it does not directly identify specific
causal pathways or moderation effects. Much early research on selfefficacy and development relied on cross-sectional designs, comparing
different age groups at a single time. While informative, cross-sectional
studies provide only a snapshot and have important limitations in
capturing the development of self-efficacy over time.
Finally, a common concern that comes with employing ensemble
learning algorithms like the XGBoost algorithm is that they do not offer
result interpretation to the extent of single decision trees (Zopluoglu,
2019). Unlike single decision trees, XGBoost makes it challenging to
visualize the interactions among predictors and fully understand how
predictions are made. However, the XGBoost model was still able to
provide information about the relative importance of the predictors,
offering insight into the most important predictors of self-efficacy,
which aligns with the research question.

Conclusion
This study simultaneously identified important predictors of students’ self-efficacy and assessed their relative importance, both in the
overall context and within each socio-ecological system. The findings of
this study attempt to advance the theoretical understanding of how selfefficacy may develop and vary by interpreting associations between
contextual factors and self-efficacy from both socio-ecological and social
cognitive perspectives. As the results of relative importance suggest,
self-efficacy is a joint function of a multitude of different levels of
contextual factors. Thus, researchers and education practitioners may
benefit from considering how factors across different layers of students’
socio-ecological environments might jointly relate to the development
and variation of self-efficacy. By identifying the most important and
proximal predictors of self-efficacy, this study paves the way for future
research to further investigate the complex relationships between students’ self-efficacy and its key influencing factors.
