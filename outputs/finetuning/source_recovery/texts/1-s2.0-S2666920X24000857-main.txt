A B S T R A C T
The majority of AI literacy studies have designed and developed self-reported questionnaires to assess AI learning 
and understanding. These studies assessed students’ perceived AI capability rather than AI literacy because self- 
perceptions are seldom an accurate account of true measures. International assessment programs that use 
objective measures to assess science, mathematical, digital, and computational literacy back up this argument. 
Furthermore, because AI education research is still in its infancy, the current definition of AI literacy in the 
literature may not meet the needs of young students. Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate an AI 
literacy test for school students within the interdisciplinary project known as AI4future. Engineering and edu­
cation researchers created and selected 25 multiple-choice questions to accomplish this goal, and school teachers 
validated them while developing an AI curriculum for middle schools. 2390 students in grades 7 to 9 took the 
test. We used a Rasch model to investigate the discrimination, reliability, and validity of the items. The results 
showed that the model met the unidimensionality assumption and demonstrated a set of reliable and valid items. 
They indicate the quality of the test items. The test enables AI education researchers and practitioners to 
appropriately evaluate their AI-related education interventions.
1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) education in K–12 is gaining increasing 
attention (Casal-Otero et al., 2023). Other than creating curriculum 
content, it is necessary to design and develop an AI literacy test to assess 
student learning and understanding. AI literacy refers to individuals’ 
abilities to critically use and evaluate AI and collaborate with it effec­
tively in multiple contexts (Long & Magerko, 2020). Recently, more 
studies have appeared to investigate how to measure AI literacy (Chiu, 
Ahmand, Ismail, & Sanusi, 2024; Laupichler, Aster, & Raupach, 2023; 
Ng, Luo, Chan, & Chu, 2022; Wang & Chuang, 2023). Most of them 
suggested using self-reported questionnaires to assess student AI 
learning and understanding. In this study, we argued that they tend to 
assess perceived abilities to use AI or AI self-efficacy, but not AI literacy. 
In other words, they assess students’ beliefs and perceived abilities and 
skills about the use and evaluation of AI. They are unable to measure the 
students’ actual performance in AI because self-perceptions are seldom 
an accurate account of true measures (Tempelaar, Rienties, & Nguyen, 
2020; Weber, Pinski, & Baum, 2023). For example, science and mathe­
matical literacy are assessed by tests; see Program for International 
Student Assessment (PISA), Trends in International Mathematics and 
Science Study (TIMSS), and International Computer and Literacy Study 
(ICILS). Research on objective assessments for AI literacy is needed. A 
few studies have designed and validated AI literacy tests for higher ed­
ucation (Knoth et al., 2024; Weber et al., 2023). Their tests are not 
unable for young students; i.e. K-12.

Although there is a growing focus on AI education in K-12, there is a 
significant lack of objective evaluation approach for measuring student 
AI literacy. The majority of current research depends on self-reported 
surveys, which may not provide an appropriate assessment of stu­
dents’ comprehension of AI. Hence, the main goal of this study is to 
develop and validate an AI literacy test for middle and high schools. 
Establishing this appropriate assessment methods will encourage 
teachers to accurately evaluate their AI curriculum and lessons, and 
enable researchers to scrutinize their intervention studies pertaining to 
AI learning.
This study is part of an interdisciplinary project that created and 
evaluated an AI curriculum for schools. To achieve this goal, we define 
what AI literacy is for K–12 and clarify what the appropriate approach to 
measures should be. The findings allow AI education researchers to 
evaluate their educational interventions and practitioners to understand 
their students’ learning needs. They also contribute to the international 
assessment programs for K–12 by providing a new set of objective 
measures in AI.
2. Literature review
2.1. Projects on AI education for K–12 and their AI content
Various organisations have put forth their efforts to introduce AI to 
non-technical populations in order to increase the future workforce di­
versity for AI. Governments in different regions around the world (e.g., 
Austria, Belgium, China, India, Korea, Qatar, Serbia) have pursued ini­
tiatives to broaden AI education to K–12 by endorsing their official 
curriculum (United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Orga­
nization, 2023). Universities and industries are co-designing AI learning 
resources for K–12 (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Chiu et al., 2022; 
Gardner-McCune et al., 2022; Sperling et al., 2023). In addition, re­
searchers have published guidelines on how to incorporate AI into K–12 
curricula (Chiu, 2021; Chiu et al., 2022; Touretzky, Gardner-McCune, & 
Seehorn, 2023). For example, Chiu and colleagues (2022) created and 
evaluated a 12-topic AI curriculum for 7th to 9th graders that includes 
fundamental AI knowledge, AI learning processes in different applica­
tions, AI for social good, AI for the future of work, and AI ethics. 
Touretzky et al. (2023) suggest five big ideas for AI to guide the stan­
dards development for K–12 education. The ideas are: 1) “Computers 
perceive the world using sensors”; 2) “Agents maintain models or rep­
resentations of the world and use them for reasoning”; 3) “Computers 
can learn from data”; 4) “Making agents interact with humans is a 
substantial challenge for AI developers”; and 5) “AI applications can 
impact society in both positive and negative ways.” Mertala et al. (2022)
used a qualitative approach to understand what AI concepts 5th and 6th 
graders should have. “AI as a technology?” “Where is AI used?” and 
“Why is AI used?”. Breazeal et al. (2023) used the Day of AI to bring AI 
literacy to K–12. In their curriculum, all graders learned about AI and its 
careers; 3rd to 5th graders learned how to use teachable machines; 6th 
to 8th graders learned about creative AI; and 9th to 12th graders learned 
about AI in social media. These studies have mixed findings on what AI 
content should include in certain grades, but they can be summarized 
into three areas: knowledge of AI, process in AI, and impact of AI (Chiu, 
2021). They inform researchers and practitioners what to include in AI 
curriculum content for K–12 that is directly associated with the abilities 
of AI literacy (Casal-Otero et al., 2023). The suggestions from these 
studies on what to include in AI K–12 curricula provide a strong foun­
dation for future research on fostering and assessing AI literacy. They 
also guide the development of AI literacy tests.
2.2. The current studies on assessing AI literacy and their pitfalls
Literacy refers to the ability to express and communicate using 
printed signs, symbols, or language (Long & Magerko, 2020). Knowl­
edge closely links with literacy as it provides individuals with the 
necessary tools and skills to effectively access, comprehend, and convey 
new information (Oo et al., 2021). Knowledgeable individuals are more 
likely to have a strong literacy foundation and become more successful 
learners and communicators. Several disciplines, such as science, 
mathematics, information, data, digital, and computational literacy, 
extend the term literacy to describe skill sets that have the same po­
tential to facilitate access to information, communication, and expres­
sion (Long & Magerko, 2020). AI literacy is defined as “the abilities to 
critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate 
effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the 
workplace.” (Long & Magerko, 2020). This literacy has very close re­
lationships with other literacies. Digital literacy refers to an individual’s 
ability to find, evaluate, create, and communicate information using 
digital tools and technologies (Martin & Grudziecki, 2006). This literacy 
is a prerequisite for AI literacy because people must understand AI 
through computers (Long & Magerko, 2020). Data literacy refers to the 
ability to employ an inquiry-based approach to read, understand, 
analyze, create, and communicate data as information (Ow-Yeong et al., 
2023; Wolff et al., 2016). This literacy overlaps with AI literacy because 
AI’s main idea is "learning from data," which is also a subfield of ma­
chine learning. Mathematical literacy refers to an individual’s capacity 
to formulate, employ, and interpret mathematics in a variety of contexts 
(Jackson et al., 2021), as defined by PISA 2012. It is the foundation of AI 
and fosters advanced AI knowledge, such as algorithms for machine 
learning. Computational literacy is defined as the ability to use and 
apply coding and other computational tools to develop and explain so­
lutions to problems (Jacob & Warschauer, 2018). It would foster stu­
dents’ skills in developing AI applications. However, not everyone in 
K–12 is an AI developer or researcher; thus, it is not required for AI 
literacy. AI literacy does not include learning how to code, as students 
can learn AI without coding. Overall, this definition of AI literacy is 
directly tied to its assessment.
Literature has been suggesting what abilities and skills should be 
included in the assessment of AI literacy in non-expert settings, 
including K–12 contexts (Chiu et al., 2022; Long & Magerko, 2020). In 
the study of Chiu and colleagues (2022), the AI engineering professors 
suggested three main areas: knowledge of AI, AI in process, and the 
impact of AI in 12 areas of topics for the assessment. In their ground­
breaking multidisciplinary literature analysis, Long and Magerko 
(2020), with a strong engineering academic background, laid forth a 
framework for assessing AI literacy. Their framework included five main 
themes with 17 skills: (1) What is AI? (2) What can AI do? (3) How does 
AI work? (4) How should AI be used? (5) How do people perceive AI? 
Overall, these two studies used an engineering approach to identify the 
AI topics and skills needed for assessment.
To evaluate AI learning activities and curriculum designs, more 
research appears to suggest ways to assess student learning outcomes 
(Breazeal et al., 2023; Laupichler et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2022; Sanusi 
et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2023). Most of them used self-reported 
questionnaires to examine their learning activities. In the studies of 
Breazeal, Du, Abelson, Klopfer, & Park (2023), Xia et al. (2022), and 
Williams et al. (2023), questionnaires on student engagement and 
learning experience were used to examine their AI learning activities, i. 
e., Day of AI and self-determination theory approach, and three 
projects-based AI + ethics curricula. They did not directly assess AI 
literacy, but rather student perceptions of learning AI. They clearly and 
explicitly stated that their measures captured student perceptions of AI’s 
capabilities and skills. Furthermore, Laupichler et al. (2023) and Ng and 
colleagues (2022) used the definitions from engineering perspectives to 
suggest self-reported questionnaire items to assess AI literacy. We argue 
that their studies did not assess AI literacy, but perceived ability to use 
AI, or self-efficacy (Wang & Chuang, 2023). In other words, their in­
struments are unable to capture student actual performance or learning 
in AI because students’ self-perceptions of their own abilities and 
progress are seldom reflective of actual performance (Tempelaar et al., 
2020). Therefore, we need objective measures to evaluate students’ AI

2.3. Research gaps
As we discussed, most studies designed and developed question­
naires to assess AI learning and understanding (Breazeal et al., 2023; 
Laupichler et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2022; Sanusi et al., 2023; Williams 
et al., 2023; Xia et al., 2022). Some of them claimed they were assessing 
AI literacy (Laupichler, Aster, & Raupach, 2023; Ng, Luo, Chan, & Chu, 
2022). Self-reported items are more convenient to deliver compared to 
objective measurements, as objective tests necessitate actual learning. 
Objective metrics may accurately assess students’ AI literacy, similar to 
how assessments evaluate their literacy in science, mathematics, digital 
skills, and computational literacy (as demonstrated by PISA, TIMISS, 
and ICILS). A few studies proposed objective measures for AI literacy: 
Hornberger, Bewersdorff, and Nerdel (2023) and Knoth et al. (2024)
were designed for higher education; Weber et al. (2023) methods were 
not rigorous (88 participants). We need a reliable and valid objective 
measurement for student AI literacy in K-12.
3. This study and method
3.1. Research goal and context
To the best of our knowledge, no studies have sought to validate 
objective assessments of AI literacy in younger students. The main goal 
of this paper is to design, create, and validate instruments for an AI 
literacy test in a K–12 setting. This study is part of a project called AI for 
the Future (AI4Future) started in 2019. AI4Future is a collaborative 
project involving the authors’ The Chinese University of Hong Kong 
Faculty of Engineering, Faculty of Education, local schools, the gov­
ernment, and the local AI industry. In its first year, the project involved a 
team of 14 professors, 15 postdoctoral fellows, research assistants, un­
dergraduate student helpers, and 17 secondary school principals and 
teachers. The team members collaborated to create and evaluate an AI 
curriculum for 7th to 9th graders from 2019 to 2022. In 2023, the 
project had 217 schools (60% of the total schools in Hong Kong), and its 
work was endorsed by the local government to become the official 
curriculum, i.e., all the schools began to teach AI using the project 
curriculum resources.
The literature review emphasized the connection between knowl­
edge and skills and literacy (Oo et al., 2021). Knowledgeable people 
learn and communicate better, and have stronger literacy foundations. 
What a person should learn defines their subject-domain knowledge and 
skills. Moreover, objective measures like tests should assess AI literacy, 
while subjective measures like self-reported questionnaires can measure 
perceived ability to use AI. In this study, we began by developing 
curriculum-based items through a co-design process, and then used item 
response theory analyses to validate all the items.
3.2. Participants
We invited 2380 student participants (grades 7–9) from six second­
ary schools to join this test. Four hundred forty-one identified as male 
and 1149 as female 790 chose not to discover gender. The disparity in 
gender representation is due to the selection of schools, which included 
five co-educational schools and one girls’ school. The girls’ school 
contributed 596 students. Seven hundred and ninety-five of them were 
Grade 7, 790 were Grade 8, 793 were Grade 9, and 2 of them were 
unknown. The participants represented a diverse range of academic 
performance, ensuring the validity of the items for a variety of students’ 
abilities. They learned about AI from the curriculum we designed.
3.3. Items development
There were three main stages in the item development, see Table 1. 
In the first stage, the first version of the items was created using the 
project’s curriculum, AI4future, which consists of 12 topics, see Table 2. 
The topics are divided into three learning areas: AI knowledge, AI pro­
cesses, and AI impact. In the first area, students should learn about AI 
technologies, how they learn from data, and how they work in the real 
world. The second area is concerned with processes in AI. Students 
should learn how machines perceive and interpret sensory information, 
as well as how to use representation and reasoning to make sound de­
cisions in real-world situations. In this area, we used human sense and 
thinking to make each topic more accessible to students. In the third 
learning area, students should understand how and what impact AI 
technologies have on our society, with a focus on social good, ethics, and 
the future of work. There are five modules for each topic: awareness 
(basic knowledge), knowledge (technical knowledge), ethics (specific 
ethics issues), interaction (coding), and empowerment (advanced 
knowledge). The first three modules are required for all students; the last 
two modules are optional for schools.
The first version had 160 items, with 8–15 questions per topic. It was 
designed based on the first three modules in the curriculum content, i.e., 
awareness, knowledge, and ethics, and aimed to assess students basic AI 
learning and understanding. The items were in the form of four option 
multiple-choice questions, and were drafted by the curriculum and 
assessment teams.
In the second stage, a team of experts, which includes two experi­
enced AI education professors and three experienced school AI educa­
tion teachers, reviewed all the items. We aim to ensure that the items 
encompass the learning areas through the reviewing process. After the 
initial round of expert validation, we retained a total of 136 items out of 
the original 160 questions. The criteria for removing inappropriate 
questions were as follows: (i) they were considered too challenging for 
the K-12 context, (ii) they had inappropriate options, (iii) they were 
duplicates, and (iv) they had ambiguous meanings.
The third stage was item testing. One hundred and thirty-six items of 
a test are too long for K–12 students, so the researchers and teachers 
selected the 25 items based on our curriculum; see Table 3. The students 
completed a 30-min test online in their classrooms. The test was proc­
tored by two to four project staff members (i.e., research assistants or 
postdoctoral fellows) and one school teacher.

categorizing responses as either correct or incorrect. WINSTEPS soft­
ware version 3.74.0 facilitated the analytical processing of the test data 
using the Rasch model. The Rasch model is particularly advantageous 
for evaluating item quality and determining the reliability of the test by 
constructing a measurement scale that operates independently of both 
the sample and the test items used.

4.1. Descriptive statistics of schools’ performance
Fig. 1 presents the boxplots of students’ grades for each school and

with School 1 exhibiting comparatively lower performance. The 
remaining schools demonstrated average performance.
4.2. Person and item reliability
The reliability of the person and item responses was evaluated using 
the separation and reliability coefficients, see Fig. 2. The person sepa­
ration coefficients were consistently below 2, indicating limited spread 
in respondent abilities, and the Cronbach’s Alpha (KR-20) was recorded 
at 0.7, suggesting moderate consistency in respondents’ answers. The 
item separation coefficients hovered around 19, suggesting that the test 
items could differentiate across 19 levels of difficulty. The reliability 
coefficient for items was approximately 1, indicating high item reli­
ability, and affirming that the items elicited consistent, reliable re­
sponses that significantly contributed to the overall test reliability.
4.3. Item Fit Statistics
As outlined in Table 5, the item difficulty ranged from −0.5 to 0.35. 
The standard error (S.E.) associated with estimating the difficulty co­
efficients was consistently between 0.01 and 0.02, confirming the sta­
bility of the test in assessing student abilities. The Infit Mean Square 
(IMNSQ) and Outfit Mean Square (OMNSQ) values, crucial for deter­
mining the relevance of the content to the study’s objectives, should 
ideally range between 0.5 and 1.5. In this study, values closely 
approximated 1.0, ranging from 0.89 to 1.16 for IMNSQ and from 0.78 
to 1.27 for OMNSQ, affirming the suitability of the data and model fit.

4.5. Item difficulty and student ability
The Wright map provides a valuable graphical representation that 
juxtaposes the abilities of respondents with the difficulty of items on an 
isometric scale, facilitating an intuitive comparison between these two 
variables. In the person-item map illustrated in Fig. 5, it can observe the 
distribution of respondent abilities and item difficulties.
The ability range of students spans from −0.93 to 0.87 logits, while 
the difficulty of the items ranges from −0.53 to 0.37 logits. This broader 
range of student abilities suggests the presence of outliers with extreme 
scores, although the majority of scores are concentrated around the 
mean. Items Q12 and Q13 are challenging for most students and serve 
effectively as discriminators for identifying students with higher abili­
ties. In contrast, item Q1 is generally answered correctly by the majority 
of students, indicating its relative ease.
Moreover, the average ability level of students in this test closely 
aligns with the average difficulty level of the test items. This interpre­
tation is substantiated by the data presented in Table 5, where the Mean 
of Person Measure for the test is recorded as 0.04, proximate to the 
neutral baseline value of 0.00. This consistent trend indicates that the 
average respondent ability closely matches the level demanded by the 
test items, suggesting that this set of test items poses a moderate level of 
difficulty for the students. Additionally, it is noteworthy that more than 
100 students—approximately 4 to 5 percent of the test takers—possess 
ability levels that surpass the difficulty levels of items Q12 and Q13. 
However, these questions fail to effectively distinguish the abilities of 
these students.

5. Discussions
AI education for K–12 is a global education initiative that aims to 
promote student AI learning and understanding, also known as AI lit­
eracy (Chiu et al., 2022; Touretzky, Gardner-McCune, & Seehorn, 2023; 
United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, 
2023). However, there are very few relevant and appropriate objective 
assessment tools or research addressing students’ AI literacy in a K–12 
setting. Two plausible reasons are: (i) the two measures of perceived 
ability to use AI and AI literacy may have been mixed up in previous 
studies (Laupichler et al., 2023; Ng et al., 2022), and (ii) the definition of 
AI literacy may not fit the needs of K–12 students in previous studies 
because AI education research is still new and emerging. The findings of 
this study have an empirical finding and two theoretical implications.
5.1. Empirical finding
The results showed that the Rasch model met the unidimensionality 
assumption, demonstrated reliable item and person reliability indices, 
and had acceptable infit values. The reliability of the test items, as 
evidenced by the high separation and reliability coefficients indicates a 
good quality of the test items. These metrics suggest that the items are 
capable of eliciting consistent and reliable responses from the partici­
pants, thereby enhancing the overall validity of the test. Moreover, the 
assessment of point-measure correlation coefficients and their mostly 
satisfactory range supports the discriminative power of the test items. 
Furthermore, the range of item difficulty and the spread of respondent 
abilities reflect a substantial variance, indicating the test’s capacity to 
differentiate across a wide spectrum of abilities. The alignment of item 
difficulty and respondent ability, as represented in the Wright map, 
provides clear evidence of the tests’ effectiveness in measuring the 
intended constructs across diverse respondent groups. This is particu­
larly notable in the test where the item challenges match the abilities of 
the respondents, as demonstrated by the infit and outfit mean square 
values approximating the ideal value of 1.0.
5.2. Theoretical implications
First, our critical review of the literature suggested that while sub­
jective measures like self-reported questionnaires can measure 
perceived ability to use AI, scientific and objective tools like tests should 
measure student AI literacy. International assessment programs such as 
PISA, TIMSS, and ICILS, which use tests to measure mathematical, 
digital, and computational literacy, support this argument. This implies 
that researchers would gain new insights into how to design and 
implement their AI and education research.
Second, these results created and validated a scientific and objective 
tool that incorporated three dimensions: knowledge of AI, process in AI, 
and impact of AI. We created the questionnaire after critically reviewing 
major literature (Chiu et al., 2022; Gardner-McCune et al., 2022; Long & 
Magerko, 2020; Sperling et al., 2023; Touretzky et al., 2023) and 
consulting with AI and education researchers and school practitioners 
(Chiu et al., 2022). That said, the results suggest that AI literacy should 
include three learning areas and 12 topics.
In terms of the last theoretical implication, this study demonstrated a 
novel methodology for developing objective instruments. We believe 
that research on literacy instrument development should use a co-design 
partnership between teachers and researchers (Chiu et al., 2022; Irgens, 
Hirsch, Herro, & Madison, 2023), which makes the instruments more 
relevant to the school context. We suggest starting with the co-design 
and implementation of curriculum content, which determines what 
should be part of student learning. The next step involves developing 
and validating the instrument for assessing student learning. Literacy, 
unlike generic skills, is frequently context-based. In other words, stu­
dents must gain comprehension in order to improve their literacy.

5.3. Practical suggestions
This study provides some practical recommendations based on its 
findings. In schools, students can utilize the instrument for self- 
evaluation in the field of artificial intelligence. Teachers can employ 
objective instruments to evaluate their AI curriculum as well as their 
after-school STEM education program that pertains to AI. They can view 
the instrument as a learning outcome to design their AI program (Yau 
et al., 2023). They also can use the instrument as a template to construct 
more questions.
6. Conclusions and limitation
In this study, we created and tested an AI literacy instrument that 
performed well in terms of discrimination, reliability, and validity. This 
study also has four major limitations. First, since literacy closely relates 
to knowledge and skills, its assessment should incorporate practical 
skills and hands-on activities. Future studies should suggest items for the 
practical tests. Second, only six Hong Kong schools participated in this 
test. We need more extensive research to validate the instrument’s 
feasibility and stability. Third, all of the participants and experts were 
from Hong Kong, so the questionnaire may not be appropriate for 
investigating AI education among students in western countries. We 
encourage the conduct of cross-sectional and regional research on AI 
literacy assessment using this test in the future, to establish a scientific 
foundation for its use in AI education intervention studies. Final: AI 
education for K–12 is still in its infancy. More investigation is needed to 
determine what the student should learn and what knowledge and skills 
they should acquire. Future studies may be expanded to include more 
skills and knowledge for assessment.
