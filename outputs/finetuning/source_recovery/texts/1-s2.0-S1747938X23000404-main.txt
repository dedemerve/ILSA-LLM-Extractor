A B S T R A C T  
The Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) is the world’s largest international 
assessment of primary-aged students’ reading comprehension. However, PIRLS has previously 
been found to be scarcely used for reading-related research. This systematic review aimed to 
investigate how PIRLS has been used as a tool for educational research and critique, utilising an 
existing catalogue and a further article search exploiting five digital databases. A total of 221 
articles published in academic journals between January 2004 and May 2022 were analysed. 
Results show the changing engagement in PIRLS throughout the lifetime of the study and across 
different countries and research disciplines. We also discuss the findings and implications of 
PIRLS-related research in three of the most frequent topics of these articles; teacher character­
istics and instruction practices as predictors of reading performance, socioeconomic predictors of 
reading performance, and critiques of the psychometric scaling approaches used in PIRLS. Ave­
nues we believe to have untapped potential, particularly in the context of reading-related 
research, are also discussed.   
1. Introduction 
Large-scale international assessments such as the Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS), the Trends in Inter­
national Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS), and the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) are playing an 
increasingly influential role in evaluating and shaping educational systems around the world. In many countries, the results and 
comparative international rankings draw national media attention, and in some cases have been directly tied to governmental changes 
in educational policy (Michel, 2017; Pons, 2017). The organisations behind these studies - the International Association for the 
Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) in the case of PIRLS and TIMSS, and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and 
Development (OECD) in the case of PISA - also publish their datasets to allow researchers in the social sciences to address a wide range 
of research questions. 
PIRLS is of particular interest to those researching reading literacy and/or primary education. Over 300,000 children took part in 
PIRLS 2016, which assessed and compared the reading comprehension of children in their fourth year of formal schooling (Mullis, 
Martin, Foy, & Hooper, 2017). The scale of PIRLS has continued to grow, with more than 50 countries, across every continent,

Schools in each participating country are randomly selected via a complex stratified sampling approach that ensures that the 
students in each country’s sample are nationally representative (LaRoche, Joncas, & Foy, 2017). Each student that participates in

self-efficacy beliefs and experiences at school. Questionnaires are also completed by teachers and headteachers, as well as by

Despite its potential, PIRLS has been relatively underrepresented in academic literature, especially in comparison to the larger 
academic interest in PISA (Domínguez, Vieira, & Vidal, 2012; Hern´andez-Torrano & Courtney, 2021; Hopfenbeck, 2016; Hopfenbeck 
et al., 2018). Further, as noted in a previous review of PIRLS-related research (Lenkeit, Chan, Hopfenbeck, & Baird, 2015), surprisingly 
little of the published research on PIRLS has focused on student’s reading comprehension beyond the estimates of students’ overall 
reading ability. Few studies were interested in performance on the different reading purposes or comprehension processes, and even 
fewer had explored PIRLS at the test-item level. The previous review also noted that many other parts of the PIRLS datasets had been 
almost entirely untouched in scientific journal literature, including student motivation, ability grouping, and learning strategies. 
Observing that more PIRLS-related research had been published by economics journals than journals concerned with reading, Lenkeit 
et al. argued that PIRLS has been somewhat ignored by researchers in the fields of reading and literacy education. Topics including 
socioeconomic predictors of overall PIRLS performance, and teacher characteristics and instruction practices were more frequently 
published, though Lenkeit et al.’s review provides no synthesis of the findings of these. Such a synthesis would not only help to collate 
relevant findings for researchers interested in those specific research fields but would also highlight the impact that PIRLS data has, 
and can have, on research within education and other social sciences. 
The purpose of this review is to provide a synthesis of findings and arguments covered within some of the main thematic areas of 
PIRLS-related research, adopting an approach mirroring that used by Hopfenbeck et al. (2018). Additionally, this review serves as an 
update to the review provided by Lenkeit et al. (2015), identifying how the themes of PIRLS-related research in scientific journals have 
changed over time, with a particular emphasis on research engagement with PIRLS data since the inception of the study and changes 
since the previous review, the major disciplines and foci of the research, and the countries producing such research. Our two main 
research questions were: (1) how have the themes of PIRLS-related research changed over time, particularly since 2015, and (2), is 
PIRLS now used more specifically as a tool for reading related research? This review intends to showcase the research that has been 
conducted using PIRLS for researchers who may be less familiar with the study. It is also hoped that the results and discussion provided 
here will also encourage researchers, especially those in the fields of reading research and educational assessment, to capitalise on the 
potential of using PIRLS data in their research. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Database 
The article database of Lenkeit et al.’s review of PIRLS-related research was used as a starting point, consisting of 113 articles 
published between 2004 and 2015. Our process for identifying more recent articles to add to the database began with a search of the 
same five digital databases used in the original review – ERIC, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, and Zetoc. Searches were made in all 
fields (title, abstract, keywords, etc.) using the terms ‘PIRLS’ and ‘Progress in International Reading Literacy Study’. However, to 
minimise the overlap with articles identified in the previous review, the date range spanned from January 2015 to May 2022. All of the 
publications located through the search were stored and maintained in EndNote X8. This search yielded a total of 343 unique articles, 
after eliminating duplicates. 
A peer-reviewed journal known to publish PIRLS-related research, and known to have a limited coverage in the five digital da­
tabases, ‘Large-Scale Assessment in Education’ was also directly targeted. Using the same search criteria yielded a further 16 unique 
articles, for a total of 359 newly located articles. However, as found in the original review, many of the newly located articles were not 
directly concerned with PIRLS. That is, PIRLS was mentioned somewhere in the article, often as a reference work cited by the authors, 
but was not the focus of the article. We continued to follow the protocol established by Lenkeit et al. to exclude articles not relevant to 
this review. 
2.2. Exclusion criteria 
The process of excluding articles from the review is shown in Fig. 1. We began by locating copies of each article, identifying 
whether a version could be found written in English, and identifying whether the article had been published in a peer-reviewed 
journal. If a peer-reviewed, English-language copy of the article could not be located, the article was excluded from our review. We 
also excluded articles published as conference proceedings, such as those in Procedia Social and Behavioural Sciences. These phases 
reduced the number of articles down from 359 in the initial searches, to 205. 
We read the abstract of each article to determine whether the articles used PIRLS as a main data source or topic of discussion. If the 
abstract alone did not suggest that PIRLS was the focus of the article, we searched for the terms ‘PIRLS’ and ‘Progress in International 
Reading Literacy Study’ in the main body of the article, looking at how, where, and when the term(s) were used. A further 84 articles 
were deemed not to have focused on PIRLS, and were excluded through this phase. We also excluded articles using PIRLS assessment 
materials on smaller independent samples, (e.g., McGeown et al., 2015) and articles providing commentaries on other PIRLS-related 
research (e.g., Mullis & Martin, 2016). This stage excluded a further 12 articles. 
Of the 359 articles identified in the search, 109 were initially deemed to meet all of the inclusion criteria. One was later identified as 
a duplicate of an article included in the original database (Skryabin, Zhang, Liu, & Zhang, 2015), and thus excluded. This resulted in a 
total of 108 newly identified articles, which were added to the existing database of 113 articles. Our final database therefore consisted

2.3. Categorisation scheme, data, and measures 
The 108 newly identified articles were added to original review’s database of 113 articles, and coded based on a series of review

2.4. Reliability checks 
An initial sample of 25 of the newly located articles published between 2015 and 2018 were randomly selected for reliability 
checks. The initial inter-rater reliability was 88.8%. The discrepancies mainly arose from how the two raters applied a small number of 
the codebook themes, such as whether cognitive outcomes needed to arise from PIRLS, or other sources (e.g., other ILSAs). Each 
discrepancy was discussed, with a final inter-rater agreement of 99.5%. Disagreement remained on just two codes – in both cases, the 
first rater’s codes were retained. The other 31 articles that had been coded up to that point were recoded by the first rater, yielding an 
intra-rater reliability of 97.3%. In all cases, the original codes were corrected to the new codes. 
A similar process was used for the articles published between 2019 and 2022, with 25 articles randomly selected for coding checks 
by another author. The inter-rater reliability of this was substantially higher, at 96.6%, with a final inter-rater reliability of 100% after 
corrections. Finally, all 221 articles were checked for plausibility and completeness. For example, secondary analysis articles could not 
be assigned codes for topics of critiques or policy studies, but required a valid code for a topic of secondary analysis. 
3. Results 
Our results are separated into two sections. In the first, we briefly present descriptive statistics building upon those presented by 
Lenkeit et al. (2015). In particular, we draw comparisons between findings in the old database, and how this has changed (or remained 
similar) in the years following their review. The second section is devoted to qualitative discussions of the published literature in three 
thematic areas identified as being among the most common topics of PIRLS-related research; a) teacher characteristics and instruction 
practices, b) socioeconomic predictors of reading performance and c) critiques of the psychometric scaling and specifically the Item 
Response Theory (IRT) approaches used in PIRLS.

3.1.1. PIRLS articles across time 
Fig. 2 presents the number of articles published in each year. Although the numbers have fluctuated across years, the overall trend 
represents an increased research engagement with PIRLS, particularly after the 2011 cycle.

3.1.2. PIRLS articles discussing multiple assessments 
As shown in Table 2, the majority of the newly located articles used data from PIRLS 2011. This cycle was administered simul­
taneously with the TIMSS 2011 study, also conducted by the IEA. Thirty-four countries took the opportunity to assess the same students 
in both PIRLS and TIMSS (Martin & Mullis, 2013) and a number of the newly identified articles also capitalised on the research op­
portunities afforded by this (e.g., Caponera, Sestito, & Russo, 2016). However, not all of the comparisons were to TIMSS 2011; for 
example, Jakubowski and Pokropek (2015) also used PISA data to explore the development of reading from primary to secondary 
education, while Solheim and Lundetræ (2018) explored differences in the reading performance gender-gap between PIRLS and PISA 
with respect to their different test formats. The number of articles using PIRLS data alongside other ILSAs is shown in Table 3. 
3.1.3. PIRLS articles across countries 
The first authors of PIRLS-related research have been based in 32 different countries. Of these, Germany and the United States have 
produced the most research, with 41 and 32 articles respectively. The next four greatest contributors are Sweden (18), Spain (14), 
England (14) and South Africa (11). While England demonstrated an early initial interest in PIRLS, they produced no publications 
between 2007 and 2012, in contrast to Germany, which published 13 articles over the same period (Fig. 3). Spain meanwhile produced 
no articles until 2015, but has been a consistently strong contributor since. 
3.1.4. Topics of PIRLS research 
Over two-thirds of the articles were classified as secondary data analyses. As shown in Table 4, the proportion of secondary data 
analysis articles has increased since 2015, with a particularly large reduction in the number of critique articles. The natures of these 
articles also vary by country (Fig. 4). In particular, England has produced a disproportionately large number of critique articles and 
impact/policy studies compared to the other major publishing countries, yet has only produced two secondary analysis articles. In 
contrast, all but one of Spain’s articles has focused on secondary data analysis. 
Table 5 presents the number of articles in Lenkeit et al.’s database and those newly located in this review that were concerned with 
different topics in their secondary analyses. Prior to 2015, 13 articles had been published that investigated socioeconomic characters, 
representing more than 20% of the secondary analysis articles. More recent secondary data analysis articles, however, have focused 
more on analyses of attainment gaps, and those using socioeconomic variables from the PIRLS datasets have more commonly used 
them as part of a mixture of broader predictors of reading attainment. 
As shown in Table 6, 44 articles were identified as being critiques of PIRLS, with just 12 of these being newly identified in this 
review. Of these, four were concerned with critiques of the scaling and IRT methods used in PIRLS, adding to the ten articles published 
in this area before 2015. Table 7 meanwhile shows that only three policy study articles have been published since 2015, with no 
recently published articles being concerned with country-level performance. 
Table 8 shows the number of articles which focused on specific aspects of the reading literacy constructs used in PIRLS. That is, 
articles that discussed performance on the two purposes for reading (literary and informational) or the four reading comprehension 
processes that PIRLS assesses. Consistent with Lenkeit et al., only seven of the newly located articles were concerned with either of 
these subscales, with the overwhelming majority of secondary analyses and critiques only focusing on overall reading performance. 
3.2. Qualitative discussions 
3.2.1. Teacher characteristics and practices 
Twenty-five articles were broadly concerned with the teacher and their practice in the classroom. In particular, 14 focused on 
teacher characteristics, and 11 on instruction practices. Although our coding framework treated these topics separately, we believe it 
to be sensible to collate these topics for this discussion to reflect the body of work utilising PIRLS data to explore the role of teachers in 
students’ reading achievement.

Drawing on the PIRLS 2011 4th grade sample, the most recent of the Swedish papers in this category (Myrberg, Johansson, & 
Ros´en, 2018) finds that teachers’ specialised education towards teaching certain grades and subjects has a positive relationship with 
student achievement while simultaneously accounting for parental education and early literacy skills. 
Two papers in this category address the claim that boys show lower educational achievement than girls because of the prevalence of 
female teachers, particularly in primary schools, and the “girl-friendly” climate that this prevalence creates. First, using extension data 
from Germany’s PIRLS 2001 sample and accounting for a comprehensive set of student (e.g., SES), classroom (e.g., share of male 
students), teacher (e.g., teaching experience) and school (e.g., shortage of teaching aids) level characteristics, Neugebauer, Helbig, and 
Landmann (2011) found no differences in the achievement of boys or girls in reading, mathematics or science with respect to teacher 
gender. Helbig (2012) extends this investigation to 21 European and OECD countries with data from PIRLS 2006 and TIMSS 2007. 
Controlling for a similarly comprehensive set of potentially confounding factors, he finds again that teacher sex is not positively or 
negatively related to the achievement of boys and girls in all but two countries. 
Jürges and Schneider (2007), in line with the teacher effectiveness tradition, compare the ranks of teachers by their students’ raw 
scores with effectiveness measures adjusted for non-malleable student and classroom characteristics (e.g., individual and composi­
tional SES characteristics). Using Germany’s PIRLS 2001 data, they found, unsurprisingly, that teacher rankings depend on the 
measure used to evaluate their quality. 
Using Hong Kong SAR’s PIRLS 2006 data, Cheung, Tse, Lam, and Loh (2009) found that instruction practices including time and 
frequency spent on developing student reading strategies and using reading materials from other subjects and assessments are posi­
tively related to student achievement. Teachers’ classroom assessment practices are the focus of an article by Hao and Johnson (2013), 
where they distinguish between performance assessment (e.g., short-answer and paragraph-length writing, or oral communication) 
and more traditional test-type assessment in their analysis of PIRLS 2001 data for Canada, United States, New Zealand and England. 
They find that these forms of assessments are used with different frequency across the four nations but concluded that there are no 
consistent significant relationships with reading achievement. 
Drawing on PIRLS 2006 data from Israel, Zuzovsky (2010) finds that three instructional variables (teacher reads aloud in class, 
students read as part of their homework and students talk to other students about what they have read) are positively associated with 
reading achievement specifically among Arab-speaking students who face the challenge of Arabic diglossia. 
Zimmerman and Smit (2014) drew on South Africa’s PIRLS 2006 data to select a sample of schools performing at lower, middle and 
high achievement benchmarks for an in-depth qualitative study of instructional practices in six schools. They find that “developing 
learners’ comprehension”, “reading skills” and “vocabulary” were elaborated by teachers, while “improving spoken English” and 
“encouraging positive emotional responses to reading” were neglected. 
Motivated by an increasing literature on the importance of reading and writing informational text in college and career contexts, Li, 
Beecher, and Cho (2018) explored whether the frequency of reading informational text is related to achievement with PIRLS 2011 US 
data. While most teachers reported that their students read at least two types of informational text every week, reading literary texts 
was still dominant. There was also no significant relationship between the frequency of reading information text and achievement in 
PIRLS, after accounting for students’ SES and gender. 
In their analysis of Spain’s TIMSS & PIRLS 2011 data, Lopez-Agudo and Marcenaro-Gutierrez (2017) found that discussion with 
other teachers on how to teach a concept in reading and mathematics, monitoring the completion of homework and discussing its 
content in order to provide clarifications, frequently making students take a written quiz in mathematics, and putting some emphasis 
on national achievement tests are all positively related to student engagement. 
More recently, Yan and Cai (2022) used PIRLS 2016 data from Singapore, and a structural equation modelling approach to analyse 
whether there were differential benefits for teaching approaches focusing on macro-level, or micro-level reading strategies.

3.2.2. Socioeconomic predictors of reading outcomes 
The student and home questionnaires used in PIRLS contain a wide range of items associated with students’ socioeconomic 
background, such as parental education, resources at home (both literacy and non-literacy based) and cultural capital (e.g., parental 
attitudes to reading) which are often used to represent a construct of SES. PIRLS therefore offers researchers many opportunities to 
explore the contributions of different facets of SES to reading performance both nationally and cross-nationally. This is reflected by the 
high number of articles (20) identified in this review that specialised in secondary analysis of socioeconomic predictors of reading 
outcomes, seven of which have been published since 2015. 
Many of the articles concerned with socioeconomic predictors of reading performance have focused on how parental educational 
levels predict child reading performance. Myrberg and Ros´en (2008, 2009) have published two articles in this area. Their 2008 article 
investigated the direct and indirect contributions of parental education on PIRLS 2001 reading performance in seven countries, finding 
that parental education effects were strongly mediated by the size of the household library. Differences were noted between the 
countries, with parental education effects being lower in Hong Kong and notably high in Bulgaria and Hungary. Myrberg & Ros´en, 
2009 article meanwhile focused exclusively on Sweden’s results in PIRLS 2011, using structural equation modelling to demonstrate the 
direct and indirect effects of parental education on reading achievement. Parental education was found to have a direct, albeit modest, 
effect on reading performance, as well as indirect effects mediated by other variables, most strongly by the number of books at home. 
Araújo and Costa (2015) investigated how parental education moderates the effects of early home reading activities on later 
reading performance. Though it was hypothesised that early shared reading activities at home have the most positive effects on later 
reading achievement for children in lower SES households, the results were inconsistent across countries; in many of the countries, the 
performance gap between children who read often before school and those that read less often was greater for children from high SES 
families. 
Other articles have attempted to compare the relative contributions of parental education with other aspects of SES. Rindermann 
and Ceci (2018) used PIRLS data alongside data from TIMSS, and from a range of psychological test batteries, to explore the con­
tributions of different aspects of SES on various measures of child intelligence. They found that parental education was a stronger 
predictor of child performance than family income, though it should be noted that their focus was on fluid and crystallised intelligence, 
rather than specifically on reading performance. 
PIRLS data has also been used in conjunction with other international assessments to explore changes in SES effects over time, and 
across subjects. Yang-Hansen, Rosen, and Gustafsson (2011) also explored SES effects on the performance of Sweden’s students in 
PIRLS 2001. Their article draws comparisons to Swedish data in the 1991 Reading Literacy Study (RLS; Elley, 1992) to assess how the 
impact of SES on reading performance has changed over time, and how this relates to the decentralisation of the Swedish school system 
during the 1990s. Using a variety of home possessions as a measure of SES, it was found that SES-related inequalities explained an 
increased proportion of the variance in between-school differences in reading performance in PIRLS. Yang-Hansen (2008) used a 
similar approach to explore trends in SES effects across nine different countries between RLS 1991 and PIRLS 2001. Evidence for the 
hypothesis that highly decentralised educational systems contribute to between-school differences was mixed; while countries such as 
Greece (highly centralised) had no change in SES effects from 1991 to 2001, and the USA (highly decentralised) had the largest in­
crease, the results from other countries in their analyses did not clearly fit these patterns. 
More recently, D¨ammirch and Triventi (2018) used data from PIRLS, TIMSS, PISA and the Programme for the International 
Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIACC) to look more widely at SES effects on reading and mathematics performance from 
childhood to early adulthood. Their study adopted a pseudo-cohort design by comparing PIRLS 2011 data with that from PISA 2006 
and PIAAC 2011/2012 to track the progress of a similar-age cohort across ten years of reading assessments. It was found that SES 
effects in primary reading performance were greater than those for mathematics, though the mathematics SES gap increased with 
student age (i.e., were greater in PISA and PIACC than in TIMSS). It was therefore suggested that lower SES particularly affects op­
portunities for early reading development, but has negative impacts across the curriculum. The authors did however note that their SES 
measure, the number of books at home, was directly tied to reading, and that using other questionnaire items such as parental edu­
cation could lead to different conclusions. 
Two articles published by Caro and colleagues used a composite SES measure consisting of parental education and occupation, 
household possessions, and the parental perception of the family’s financial situation (Caro & Lenkeit, 2012; Caro & Mirazchiyski, 
2012). Caro and Lenkeit (2012) tested ten hypotheses about the contributions of SES to PIRLS 2006 reading performance in five 
countries with differing income-per-capita. Their findings indicate that family-level SES independently contributes to reading per­
formance after accounting for school-level and country-level SES, that SES coefficients are greater in higher-income countries, and that 
the lower performance of students in rural areas is partially explained by school-level SES. This latter finding was explored further by 
Caro and Mirazchiyski (2012) in 12 Eastern European countries that took part in PIRLS 2006, again finding that the SES composition of 
schools accounts for much of the variation in performance between students at schools in urban and rural areas, though this varied by 
country. In particular, they noted that countries which experienced post-Soviet educational reforms most recently (Bulgaria, Romania 
and Macedonia) had the largest gaps between urban and rural student performance, which were not fully accounted for by school SES 
composition. They also found that SES coefficients were particularly low in Latvia and Lithuania, both of which were heavily influ­
enced by Scandinavian educational policy upon gaining independence in 1991.

The third of Caro’s articles (2011) used data from PIRLS 2006 and PISA 2000 to explore how parent-child communication predicts 
reading performance. In particular, the study tested the hypotheses that parent-child communication is more effective for higher SES 
families within countries, and that this relationship is stronger in lower SES countries. For both hypotheses, the evidence of these 
relationships was stronger for PISA performance than performance in PIRLS. Caro argued that this may be because of the different 
assessment methods used in PIRLS and PISA, or the different ages of the testing populations. Supporting the latter suggestion, Schubert 
and Becker (2010) also found that SES-related gaps in Germany widened with age between PIRLS and PISA, arguing that the SES gap 
was reinforced and widened by the German schooling system, rather than ameliorated. 
The four most recent articles concerned with socioeconomic characteristics have all focused on conceptualisations of academic 
‘resilience’ against the disadvantages associated with a lower socioeconomic background. Three of these articles were published by 
García Crespo (2019a, 2019b, 2021), using data from PIRLS 2016 to identify students in European countries who performed well in 
PIRLS in spite of economic disadvantage relative to other students in their country. García Crespo, Fern´andez-Alonso and Mu˜niz 
(2019a) found that confidence in reading was the most consistent predictor of ‘resilient’ students in all countries. García Crespo, 
Gali´an, Fern´andez-Alonso and Mu˜niz (2019b) reported similar findings, also emphasising that ‘resilient’ students also tended to come 
from schools where the headteacher reported higher levels of focus on discipline. García Crespo, Fern´andez-Alonso and Mu˜niz (2021) 
later expanded on this work to identify other factors associated with higher levels of ‘resilience’, including students’ sense of school 
belonging, and childrens’ early involvement in literacy activities before school – however, these effects were not as strong as those 
found for confidence in reading and school emphasis on discipline. 
In synthesising the findings of the articles that investigated socioeconomic predictors of reading performance, three overarching 
conclusions emerged. Firstly, through comparisons of PIRLS data with those in other assessments, SES effects in reading performance 
appear to increase with age, though they are already present at a primary level, and may be particularly strong in reading when 
compared to mathematics. Secondly, there is a complex interplay between parental education, the number of books at home, early 
reading activities at home on later reading performance, all of which also interact with wider school and country-level SES factors. 
Thirdly, the sizes of SES coefficients are inconsistent across countries, and so should be considered by researchers investigating the 
relationships between socioeconomic backgrounds and educational outcomes. Although links have been drawn to wider socioeco­
nomic and educational policy differences between these countries, these do not always map on well to SES coefficients, and more 
research is needed in this area. 
3.2.3. Critiques of scaling and IRT practices 
Given that PIRLS and other international large-scale studies rely on establishing a common measurement scale across diverse 
cultural, language and country groups, it is unsurprising that the majority of psychometrically focused articles identified in this review 
focused on issues of measurement invariance in the scaling of the reading comprehension test, and particularly on Differential Item 
Functioning (DIF), which is a statistical technique used to establish whether items are biased (i.e., easier or more difficult) for certain 
groups. Three articles identified small percentages of substantial DIF between countries, including between students from Turkey and 
USA students in PIRLS 2001 (Ogretmen, 2009), for students from Colombia versus United States and England in PIRLS 2001 (San­
dilands, Oliveri, Zumbo, & Ercikan, 2013), and for students from Taiwan and Hong Kong versus Qatar and Kuwait in PIRLS 2006 
(Oliveri, Ercikan, Zumbo, & Lawless, 2014). Jeon, Rijmen, and Rabe-Hesketh (2013) also identified a small number of items with 
gender DIF in the PIRLS 2006 data from the United States. 
However, several articles that applied multiple statistical approaches to identifying DIF showed that the results differed, sometimes 
markedly, across the approaches, and so DIF should not be interpreted in simple statistical terms, particularly as it tended to balance 
out across the reference and focal groups in these analyses (Ogretmen, 2009; Oliveri et al., 2014; Sandilands et al., 2013). To this point, 
Sandilands et al. (2013) propose a more theory-driven, confirmatory approach to DIF detection so that the test creators and researchers 
know what to do when items are identified as showing DIF, including balancing the number of items of different cognitive loads in the 
PIRLS reading comprehension test, as items with higher cognitive load may favour students of some countries over others. 
Several other articles examined alternative scaling approaches to overcome issues of measurement invariance for the PIRLS data. 
Oliveri and von Davier (2014) used their Discrete Mixture Distribution Model on PIRLS 2006 reading comprehension data to establish 
a quasi-international scale of reading performance, which allows for the estimation of country-specific parameters for items that show 
violations of the invariance assumption, which they found for around a quarter of the countries in the study. This model, unsur­
prisingly, better fits the data, and the country-means obtained using this method were very highly correlated with the means from the 
current scaling approach. Wendt, Kasper, and Trendtel (2017) also applied different models that allowed for country-level differences 
in parameters to the PIRLS 2011 ‘home resources for learning scale’ items, and argued that a partial-invariance approach was also 
superior for this variable, as certain items showed marked differences in discrimination across countries. 
In terms of broader scaling issues, Rijmen (2011) applied different bifactor models to the PIRLS 2006 reading comprehension data 
and showed that the bifactor model that included specific factors for the clustering of items in booklets showed the best fit, suggesting 
that the current scaling approach for PIRLS, which assumes unidimensionality, may not adequately account for this test-design issue. 
Kasper and Ünlü (2013) used a subset of the German PIRLS 2006 reading comprehension data to demonstrate differences in factor 
analytic solutions when model assumptions are not met. Moreover, Chiu, Seo, and Douglas (2009) used the PIRLS 2001 reading test 
responses to demonstrate how a Cluster Analysis approach may be used for the classification of cognitive profiles in the data. 
The other major psychometric issue addressed in the reviewed articles was scale linking for the interpretation of trends over time. 
Strietholt and Ros´en (2016) demonstrated that the PIRLS scale could be linked to earlier IEA studies, including the Reading 
Comprehension Study (RCS) and RLS, using common item links across the studies. This potentially enables much longer-term inter­
pretation of trends for participating countries across the studies, although the linking was highly dependent upon Sweden’s inclusion

variable. They argue that there is a need for a more scientific approach to the design, use and interpretation of the PIRLS background 
questionnaires. 
4. Discussion 
This review had two key aims. First, we aimed to provide a synthesis of the findings and arguments in research areas that have 
already embraced the opportunities offered by PIRLS. This was in response to one of the key limitations of the previous review of 
PIRLS-related research. Following our descriptive analysis, we settled on three thematic areas for discussion; classroom and teacher 
characteristics, socioeconomic predictors of reading performance, and critiques of the study’s scaling and IRT processes. Second, we 
wanted to provide an updated account of the state of PIRLS-related research in scientific journals and identify how this has changed, 
both over the course of the study’s lifetime and since the previous review. However, little appears to have changed since 2015 in terms 
of the major topics of research being produced, though we did note that an even greater proportion of the articles are using PIRLS data 
for secondary analyses, as opposed to policy/impact studies or critiques of PIRLS. It should however be noted that many of the articles 
did not focus on a specific area, and instead covered a mixture of topics within the same article. 
4.1. Limitations 
It should be noted that there are a number of limitations with our approach that perhaps have underestimated the impact of PIRLS 
on the research community. Many of these limitations are common to both this review, and in Lenkeit et al.,‘s previous review. For 
example, our database only consists of articles published in English. In our initial search, we located 16 articles that were published in 
languages other than English, all of which were excluded from our review without their content being examined. Further, the search 
terms ‘PIRLS’ and ‘Progress in International Reading Literacy Study’ are not used in all countries. Germany, for example, refers to the 
study as IGLU, an abbreviation of the study’s German name. It is therefore possible that our approach has excluded a number of articles 
that may change the overall perspective of the use of PIRLS for academic research cross-nationally. Additionally, we have focused on 
the publication of articles in academic journals, rather than forms of grey literature. 
It should also be noted that many of the articles were only coded once; although reliability checks were conducted on two different 
samples of the newly identified articles, we accept that there is an element of subjectivity made in these codes. Further, many of the 
articles looked at a mixture of predictors of reading performance, which were coded as such, often utilising data from across the test- 
items and the student/parent/teacher questionnaires. While some secondary analysis topics were reported to have been rarely 
explored in these articles, it should also be noted that the large number of ‘mixture’ articles invariably masks some of the work that has 
been done in these spaces. 
4.2. Future research 
PIRLS remains underutilised for research specifically concerning primary-age reading skills, with few studies looking beyond the 
aggregated plausible values of reading performance. It should be remembered that PIRLS is, by name, a study of reading literacy, and 
there is a wealth of data beyond the overall performance scores. Researchers interested in specific aspects of reading comprehension 
can utilise plausible values for different purposes of reading (literary or informational), or for different comprehension processes (e.g., 
retrieval and simple inferences, integration and evaluation), or even look at performance at the individual item level. For example, in 
PIRLS 2016, students in Anglophone countries typically performed less well when answering questions on informational texts than 
literary texts. The breadth of the PIRLS datasets would allow researchers to explore whether this might relate to home reading be­
haviours, early exposure to different kinds of reading resources, or the instruction practices of teachers. Additionally, comparisons 
could be made to the performances of East Asian countries, which have historically performed better on the informational reading 
texts. 
Researchers interested in reading motivation could explore gender differences in reading attitudes with respect to the texts used in 
PIRLS. Although girls have consistently demonstrated more positive attitudes towards reading, and girls continue to outperform boys 
in PIRLS reading assessments, these gender gaps are not consistent across the different texts used in PIRLS. Solheim and Lundetræ 
(2018) recently investigated how the Norwegian gender-gap in reading performance relates to item-format in PIRLS, though more 
could be done in this area to explore how the reading content affects performance at the item and booklet level.

4.3. Final remarks 
This review has provided an overview of the body of PIRLS-related research in academic journals, with the intention of highlighting 
how PIRLS has been used, or not been used, for a variety of different purposes as a source of secondary data analysis, methodological 
and theoretical critique, and focus of impact or policy studies. We aimed to provide a synthesised discussion of three of the main topics 
of PIRLS-related research, including of teacher characteristics and instructional practices, socioeconomic predictors of PIRLS per­
formance, and critiques of the scaling and IRT procedures used in PIRLS, updating and building upon a previous summary review of 
PIRLS-related research (Lenkeit et al., 2015). Our main findings are that while PIRLS has been increasingly used as a source of sec­
ondary data analysis as opposed to critiques or impact and policy study, relatively little has actually changed in terms of the main 
topics of those analyses. Additionally, Germany and the United States are the largest producers of PIRLS-related research, mirroring the 
top-producers of research related to PISA (Hopfenbeck et al., 2018), though there does appear to be an increasing interest in PIRLS in 
countries including Spain and South Africa. PIRLS has fantastic potential as a source of research into reading literacy education, but it 
is our belief that it’s use in this context has been underutilised. We hope that this review acts as an introduction to the study for 
researchers interested in educational assessment and reading assessment specifically, as well as an overview of the current body of 
research for researchers already making use of PIRLS results and datasets.
