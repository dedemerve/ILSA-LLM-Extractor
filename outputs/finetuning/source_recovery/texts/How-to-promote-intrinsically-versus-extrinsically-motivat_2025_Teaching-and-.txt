A B S T R A C T
Job satisfaction is essential for teachers’ well-being and education quality. Using Korean Educational Longitu­
dinal Study data from 3677 elementary school teachers, we conducted sequential regression to systematically 
examine distinct levels of job satisfaction predictors and multigroup analysis to explore how demographic, in­
dividual, and school-level factors differentially predicted satisfaction among teachers who chose their career 
primarily for intrinsic versus extrinsic reasons. Self-efficacy in teaching was more salient for intrinsically 
motivated teachers, while passion among colleagues was more salient for extrinsically motivated teachers. The 
findings highlight the need for nuanced, individualized efforts targeting both teachers and school climate to 
enhance job satisfaction.
1. Introduction
Teaching is considered a profession with elevated emotional and 
mental health risks due to professional complexities and challenges 
(Acton & Glasgow, 2015; H¨olscher et al., 2024; Slemp et al., 2020). 
Moreover, the unprecedented COVID-19 pandemic posed immediate 
and lasting impacts on teacher well-being and job satisfaction world­
wide, such as increased stress, anxiety, and burnout (Katz & Mo`e, 2024; 
Schulze-Hagenest et al., 2023). In Australia, for instance, 65.6 % of 
teacher respondents reported being satisfied with their work in 2019, 
but this figure dropped to 45.8 % in 2022 (Allen et al., 2023). Not sur­
prisingly, nearly 20 % of teachers indicated they intended to leave the 
profession within five years. According to a recent Pew Research Center 
report (Lin et al., 2024), public K-12 teachers in the United States are 
notably less satisfied than US workers overall. More than half of re­
spondents stated they would not recommend a teaching career to a 
young person entering the profession today. In South Korea, where this 
study was conducted, a recent survey of over 11,000 teachers revealed 
that job satisfaction among teachers has reached an all-time low (Jung, 
2024; —in 2024, only 21.4 % of respondents reported satisfaction with 
their profession, a steep decline from 67.8 % in 2006.
Like any other profession, teaching attracts individuals with diverse 
motivations and career trajectories (Watt et al., 2014). Nevertheless, 
teachers are often regarded as professionals living a “calling,” and ex­
pected to take care of their own welfare, hence the issue of teacher 
well-being, including job satisfaction, has been neglected in research 
and practice (Viac & Fraser, 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). When previous 
research has addressed teacher well-being, it has often focused solely on 
predictors of well-being at a single level, whether personal or 
school-level, and treated teachers as a homogeneous group (Zhang et al., 
2024). Given the centrality of job satisfaction to teachers’ well-being, we 
addressed this gap by examining demographic characteristics, individ­
ual efficacy beliefs, and perceived school climate as predictors of job 
satisfaction, while simultaneously considering teachers’ diverse motives 
for choosing a teaching career.
The first aim of the study is to systematically examine three de­
mographic factors (i.e., biological sex,1 non-tenure status, years of 
teaching experience), two individual factors (i.e., self-efficacy in 
teaching, self-efficacy in classroom management), and four perceived 
school climate factors (i.e., passion among colleague teachers, collabo­
ration with colleagues, conflicts with colleagues, principal support) to 
explore their relationships with teachers’ job satisfaction (RQ1). Taking 
a step further, we aim to account for teachers’ job selection motivation, 
which is an important source of job satisfaction that has rarely been 
examined in combination with other job satisfaction predictors by 
employing multigroup structural equation modeling (RQ2) (Hoque

1.1. Job satisfaction
Job satisfaction is a complex emotional reaction to one’s job, shaped 
by the relationship between the worker and their work environment 
(Locke, 1969). More specifically, it refers to the extent to which in­
dividuals perceive their job-related needs are being fulfilled (Evans, 
1997). Teachers’ job satisfaction has significant, far-reaching implica­
tions. Satisfied teachers are generally less inclined to leave the profes­
sion (Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2011), demonstrate stronger job commitment 
(Collie et al., 2012), and are more likely to provide higher quality in­
struction and support for their students (Klusmann et al., 2008). Addi­
tionally, job satisfaction is closely linked to personal welfare and is often 
regarded as a key component of well-being (Song et al., 2020; Van­
steenkiste et al., 2007). Considering that one’s job-related needs are 
fulfilled within the interactions of teachers and school environment, it is 
important to examine both individual and contextual factors that predict 
teachers’ job satisfaction.
1.2. Demographic, individual, and school climate predictors of job 
satisfaction
Prior research on teacher job satisfaction has broadly categorized its 
predictors into categories pertaining to demographic characteristics, 
psychological beliefs or attitudes, and environment (Crossman & Harris, 
2006). These demographic, individual, and school-level factors collec­
tively influence how teachers perceive and experience their work and 
career, impacting both their professional commitment and overall 
well-being (Toropova et al., 2021). Here we present an overview of 
research on teacher job satisfaction, focusing on these three broad cat­
egories of predictors.
1.2.1. Demographic traits
Biological sex, years of teaching experience (or age), and tenure 
status are demographic traits often examined as predictors of job satis­
faction; however, research findings are generally marked by in­
consistencies. The relationship between sex and job satisfaction varies 
even within the same national contexts. In the United States and Canada, 
for instance, some studies report higher job satisfaction among female 
teachers (Liu & Ramsey, 2008; Topchyan & Woehler, 2020), while 
others suggest greater satisfaction among male teachers (Klassen & 
Chiu, 2010; Mertler, 2002). Among Korean teachers, Hwang (2012)
found that biological sex was not related to job satisfaction whereas Choi 
and Kim (2024) reported that male teachers had higher levels of job 
satisfaction. Similarly, findings on the impact of teaching experience on

more complex pattern: teachers with more than 20 years of experience 
reported the highest job satisfaction, those with 10–20 years of experi­
ence reported the lowest, and those with less than 10 years reported a 
moderate level of job satisfaction. Studies on tenure status also present 
conflicting evidence, suggesting negative, positive, and null relation­
ships between job satisfaction and tenure (Dobrow et al., 2018).
1.2.2. Individual efficacy beliefs
Self-efficacy is the belief individuals hold regarding their ability to 
successfully execute a specific course of action (Bandura, 1997). In their 
seminal study, Tschannen-Moran and Hoy (2001) conceptualized 
teacher self-efficacy as teachers’ judgment about their abilities to 
implement instructional strategies, engage students in the learning 
process, and manage student behaviors. Teacher self-efficacy has been 
examined as an important determinant of job satisfaction (Caprara et al., 
2006). Teachers with higher self-efficacy in teaching, and teachers with 
more exposure to professional development, generally report higher 
levels of job satisfaction (Toropova et al., 2021). Moreover, Herman and 
colleagues (2021) found that teachers’ self-perceptions of classroom 
management skills are consistent and robust predictors of job satisfac­
tion. Teacher self-efficacy is often found to be a positive predictor of job 
satisfaction among teachers in Korea. While some researchers focus on 
general teacher self-efficacy (e.g., Choi & Kim, 2024), others have 
examined specific subcomponents, such as teacher self-efficacy in 
teaching and classroom management (e.g., Moon, 2018). Teacher 
self-efficacy, through its interplay with positive affect and job satisfac­
tion, can also shape teacher enthusiasm (i.e., the extent to which 
teachers generally feel excitement and pleasure in their professional 
duties; Kunter et al., 2008) and mediate the relationship between posi­
tive affect and teacher enthusiasm (Buri´c & Mo`e, 2020).
1.2.3. Perceived school climate
School climate can be defined as “the quality and character of school 
life that reflects norms, goals, values, interpersonal relationships, 
teaching, learning and leadership practices, and organizational struc­
tures” (National School Climate Council, 2007, p.5; see also Bradshaw 
et al., 2021). School climate is comprised of collective experience that 
includes larger organizational patterns or vision (e.g., shared passion 
among teachers), interpersonal relationships (e.g., collaboration/con­
flicts with colleagues), and administrative and leadership support (e.g., 
principal support) (Cohen et al., 2009). Teachers’ perceptions of their 
school climate—morale of the school, connectedness among the school 
members, and support from the leadership team—can significantly 
impact teachers’ job satisfaction (Aldridge & Fraser, 2016). Toropova 
and colleagues (2021) found that, among various working conditions 
including teacher workload and school resources, teacher cooperation 
was the most strongly associated with job satisfaction. Conversely, 
teachers experiencing conflicts reported negative emotions, such as 
disappointment and uneasiness, which may lead to dislike towards their 
profession (G¨oksoy & Argon, 2016). Supportive behaviors exhibited by 
school administrators were also closely related to teacher job satisfac­
tion (Ertürk, 2021). The importance of school climate for teacher’s job 
satisfaction has been reported in South Korea. Various school climate 
predictors—such as transformational leadership, teacher passion, and 
collaboration—have been found to be positively associated with job 
satisfaction (H. Kim, Lim, et al., 2013; Lee and Kim, 2018).
1.3. Teachers’ job selection motive as a potential moderator
Motivation is defined as “the process of initiating and maintaining 
goal-directed behaviors” (Schunk et al., 2014, p. 5). Drawing on 
well-established 
motivational 
frameworks, 
such 
as 
situated 
expectancy-value theory and self-determination theory, researchers 
have explored the reasons and mechanisms behind individuals entering 
and persisting in teaching careers (Sinclair, 2008; Watt & Richardson, 
2008, 2023). Based on expectancy-value theory (Wigfield & Eccles, 
2000), for example, Richardson and Watt (2014) developed the Factors 
Influencing Teaching Choice (FIT-Choice) Framework identifying values 
and perceived ability factors that influence the decision to pursue 
teaching. However, relatively few studies have examined teacher 
motivation in relation to job satisfaction (Kunter & Holzberger, 2014).
In the present study, we addressed this gap specifically focusing on 
teachers’ job selection motives because initial motivations for choosing

a teaching career significantly influence later professional engagement 
and teaching behaviors (Watt et al., 2014). Broadly, motivations for 
entering teaching career fall into three categories: (a) intrinsic reasons, 
such as love for teaching children and a desire to use one’s subject 
matter knowledge, (b) extrinsic reasons, such as salaries, job security, 
and extended holidays, and (c) altruistic reasons, such as a desire to help 
children experiencing difficulty or to make contributions to society 
(Jungert et al., 2014). Closely related to these job selection motives is 
self-determination theory (SDT), a comprehensive theoretical frame­
work for understanding factors that facilitate or undermine intrinsic 
motivation, different types of extrinsic motivation, and psychological 
well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2020). SDT posits several types of motivation 
along a continuum, from amotivation (i.e., absence of motivation) to 
intrinsic motivation (i.e., participating in an activity for its intrinsic 
value or because it is inherently engaging and enjoyable) (Ryan & Deci, 
2020). Situated between these are various forms of extrinsic motivation, 
which pertains to activities performed as a means to achieve an outcome 
that is distinct from the task itself.
Extensive research highlighting the adaptiveness of intrinsic moti­
vation and more autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation for academic 
achievement and well-being suggests benefits in choosing a career based 
on intrinsic motivation (Kunter & Holzberger, 2014; Taylor et al., 2014). 
Using the SDT framework, Yildiz and Kiliç (2021) found that intrinsic 
motivation (i.e., putting effort into the profession because the job is 
interesting) is positively correlated with job satisfaction among Turkish 
primary school teachers. Similarly, a study of Croatian and Slovenian 
primary school teachers by Rijavec and colleagues (2016) showed that 
intrinsically oriented teachers reported higher job satisfaction than 
extrinsically oriented teachers. However, these studies did not examine 
teachers’ job selection motives and relied on simple correlation analyses 
without accounting for other relevant demographic, individual, and 
school climate factors. Considering that one’s extrinsic versus intrinsic 
job selection motives may relate to professional identity, satisfaction 
and commitment, and perceptions of the work environment (Kunter & 
Holzberger, 2014; O’Reilly & Caldwell, 1980; Su & Bozeman, 2009), it is 
important to investigate the mechanism of how job satisfaction pre­
dictors operate differently between extrinsically versus intrinsically 
motivated teachers.
1.4. Job selection motives of Korean teachers and their job satisfaction
Teachers in South Korea choose their career for diverse intrinsic, 
extrinsic, and altruistic reasons (E. Kim, Park, Cho, Jeon, & An, 2024; M. 
K. Kim & Hur, 2024). The country has one of the most selective processes 
for teacher certification, ensuring that only individuals with high 
motivation and strong academic performance enter the profession 
(Akiba et al., 2023). Teachers’ salaries—higher than the average among 
countries in the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Develop­
ment (OECD, 2023)—along with several benefits such as paid vacation 
and a well-established pension system, serve as extrinsic attractors. 
Teachers are respected and regarded as important contributors to soci­
ety, while also being subject to high expectations, as education has been 
a main driving force of the country’s economic growth (Akiba et al., 
2023; D. J. D. Shin et al., 2018).
Since teaching has been traditionally considered a prestigious and 
secure occupation, the recent tragedies in which multiple teachers died 
by suicide (Oaten & Lee, 2024; Yeung et al., 2023) have shocked Korean 
society and brought greater attention to teacher well-being and job 
satisfaction. These events also highlighted the unique challenges Korean 
teachers face, such as high parental expectations and intense academic 
competition among students (Shim et al., 2024). Meaningful studies 
have been conducted on job satisfaction, primarily focusing on 
teacher-level predictors (e.g., self-efficacy, grit; Cho & Ahn, 2023) and 
school-level predictors (e.g., school size; K. Kim, 2024). However, fewer 
studies have systematically explored how teachers’ demographic traits, 
psychological beliefs, and perceptions of school climate contribute to job 
satisfaction (e.g., Choi & Kim, 2024).
Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no study in Korea or 
internationally has examined teachers’ job selection motivation as a 
moderator in the relationships between job satisfaction and its various 
predictors. Considering that teachers enter the profession for diverse 
reasons, and that motivation is closely linked to job satisfaction (J.-S. 
Kim, Park, & Shim, 2024; M. Lee & Kim, 2024; Watt & Richardson, 
2023), it is important to investigate job selection motives as a potential 
moderating factor in these associations.
1.5. The current study
Accordingly, the present study has two purposes. First, we examined 
how job satisfaction is associated with distinct levels of factors. Specif­
ically, using Crossman and Harris’s (2006) broad categorization of job 
satisfaction factors, we analyzed how teachers’ demographic traits (i.e., 
biological sex, years of teaching experience, non-tenure status), indi­
vidual efficacy beliefs (i.e., self-efficacy in teaching, self-efficacy in 
classroom management), and perceptions of school climate (i.e., per­
ceptions of colleagues’ passion, collaboration with colleagues, conflicts 
with colleagues, principal support) are related to their job satisfaction 
(RQ1). Due to inconsistent findings in the existing literature, no direc­
tional hypotheses were proposed for the relationship between teachers’ 
demographic traits and job satisfaction. For individual efficacy beliefs, 
we hypothesized that self-efficacy in teaching and classroom manage­
ment would be positively linked to their job satisfaction as it is closely 
related to one’s task accomplishment and positive emotions (Buri´c & 
Mo`e, 2020; Klassen & Chiu, 2010) (Hypothesis 1a). Regarding perceived 
school climate, we hypothesized that perceptions of colleagues’ passion, 
collaboration with colleagues, and principal support would be positively 
linked to job satisfaction as these factors may serve as important re­
sources for managing professional demands (Kingsford-Smith et al., 
2023; Toropova et al., 2021), while conflicts with colleagues would be 
negative predictors of job satisfaction as they can hinder teachers’ 
effective functioning (G¨oksoy & Argon, 2016) (Hypothesis 1b).
Second, we investigated whether demographic traits, efficacy beliefs, 
and perceived school climate factors relate to job satisfaction differently 
for teachers who entered the profession primarily for extrinsic reasons 
versus those primarily motivated by intrinsic reasons (RQ2). Limited 
research has explored how teachers’ job selection motives might mod­
erate the relationship between demographic traits and job satisfaction. 
Therefore, consistent with RQ1, no directional hypotheses were set for 
demographic traits in relation to the moderation effect of job selection 
motives. Teachers’ efficacy beliefs, which are closely linked to teachers’ 
intrinsic needs for competence, should be a stronger predictor of job 
satisfaction for intrinsically motivated teachers (Richardson & Watt, 
2014; Ryan & Deci, 2000b). In contrast, perceived school climate fac­
tors, which are closely related to external support and resources, might 
be related more strongly to the job satisfaction of extrinsically motivated 
teachers. However, due to a dearth of prior studies, these factors were 
explored across the two groups without any a priori hypotheses.
2. Method
2.1. Data source and participants
Data were drawn from the Korean Educational Longitudinal Study 
(KELS) 2013. The Korean Educational Development Institute (KEDI), a 
non-profit organization supported by the South Korean government, 
selected 242 elementary schools for participation through stratified 
cluster random sampling. We utilized the first two waves of the data 
because the survey was administered to teachers only during these two 
years. The total sample comprised 3677 teachers from 242 elementary 
schools (nmale = 1,058, nfemale = 2614). Among them, 3157 teachers held 
tenure, while 482 were non-tenured, working either as temporary 
teachers (ntemporary = 286) or part-time instructors (npart-time = 196). The

2.2. Measures
The teacher questionnaire used in KELS 2013 was adapted from KELS 
2005, the previous longitudinal study, and it contained survey items that 
assessed teachers’ perceptions of school life and work. The survey items 
were developed and validated by the experts from KEDI, one of the most 
renowned educational policy and research institutes in South Korea. 
Most items have been consistently used both for KELS 2005; KELS 2013. 
Korean researchers have validated the scales and used the data from 
these longitudinal studies (M. Kim & Park, 2008; Y. Lee & Seo, 2019; 
Moon, 2018; Park & Yun, 2007). Before the survey administration of 
KELS 2013, the items were piloted with 50 teachers from 8 different 
elementary schools and the results indicated that the items had satis­
factory reliability ( Y. Kim, Lim, et al., 2013). Teachers responded to 
items assessing job satisfaction, self-efficacy in teaching and classroom 
management, and perceived school climate on a 5-point Likert scale 
ranging from (1) not at all to (5) very much so. Table 2 displays sample 
items and internal reliability scores for each measure used in the current 
study.
Teachers’ job satisfaction was measured using three items that 
assessed the extent of teachers’ satisfaction with the profession and 
whether they would choose to become a teacher again if given the 
choice (α = .83). Self-efficacy in teaching and self-efficacy in classroom 
management were each assessed with four items. Self-efficacy in 
teaching items assessed perceived competence in identifying issues 
during lessons, evaluating students’ achievement of learning objectives, 
adjusting instructional strategies based on subject content, and gauging 
students’ interest in class (α = .83). Self-efficacy in classroom manage­
ment items represented perceived competence in analyzing the causes of 
students’ problematic behaviors, utilizing student information in 
behavioral management, improving problematic behaviors, and effec­
tively supporting students from low socioeconomic backgrounds (α =
.82).
In terms of perceived school climate, colleague teachers’ passion was 
measured with three items assessing perceptions of pride and enthu­
siasm among teachers in the school (α = .85). Collaboration with col­
leagues was assessed by three items that asked teachers to indicate the 
extent of collaboration, the support they receive from the colleagues, 
and shared beliefs and values about educational activities (α = .86). 
Conflict with colleagues was assessed with three items that ask how 
frequent conflicts arise and how indifferent teachers are to other col­
leagues’ responsibilities (α = .77). Principal support was assessed with 
eight items that focus on teachers’ perceptions of principal support in 
teaching and learning, professional development, and the reduction of 
administrative tasks (α = .93). We focused on teachers’ perceptions of 
school climate rather than school characteristics such as size and 
physical location, because individuals’ appraisal of the school environ­
ment may have a greater influence on their professional satisfaction 
(Guk et al., 2023).
Teachers also selected their primary and secondary job selection 
motives from six close-ended options (i.e., job stability, salary, respect 
from others, spare time, love for teaching, love for children) or con­
structed their own responses via an open-ended option. Based on their 
primary job selection motive, we classified teachers who chose the 
former four options into an extrinsically motivated group (nEXT = 1655) 
and those selecting love for teaching or love for children into an 
intrinsically motivated group (nINT = 1882).2 We excluded teachers 
from the analysis who did not indicate their job selection motives (nMISS 
= 19) or constructed their own responses (nETC = 121), as their written 
motives (e.g., religious faith, following parental advice) did not align 
clearly with either intrinsic or extrinsic reasons. Since the KELS 2013 did 
not contain items measuring teachers’ altruistic reasons, our focus 
remained on intrinsic and extrinsic job selection motives.

2.3. Data analysis
To systematically examine demographics, efficacy beliefs, and 
perceived school climate as predictors of job satisfaction (RQ1), 
sequential linear regression was employed in SPSS Version 28. We first 
examined the statistical assumptions of sequential linear regression 
(Osborne & Waters, 2002). The scatterplot of standardized residuals 
versus predicted values showed a fairly even distribution, supporting the 
assumption of homoscedasticity. From the histogram and the normal P-P 
plot of standardized residuals, we observed that the errors were 
approximately normally distributed. Both the residual plot and the 
partial regression plots indicated that the linearity assumption was met. 
Because teachers were nested within schools, there is a possibility that 
the assumption of independence of errors may have been violated. 
Multicollinearity was not a concern, as all Tolerance values exceeded .54 
and all VIF values were below 1.86 (Pituch & Stevens, 2016). After 
checking assumptions and correlations among the variables, de­
mographic traits were entered in the first step to predict teachers’ job 
satisfaction. In the second step, efficacy beliefs were added, followed by 
perceived school climate factors in the third step. We used the sequential 
approach to examine the proportion of variance in teachers’ job satis­
faction explained by each category of the predictors.
Next, we conducted multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) 
and multigroup structural equation modeling (SEM) to investigate how 
these factors differently predicted job satisfaction for teachers who 
chose the profession primarily with extrinsic motivation and those who 
chose it with intrinsic motivation (RQ2). Mplus Version 8.4 (Muth´en & 
Muth´en, 2017) was employed. To account for the nested structure of 
KELS 2013 data (i.e., teachers nested within schools), we used the “type 
is complex” command and maximum likelihood estimators with robust 
standard errors (Muth´en & Satorra, 1995). We assessed the model fit of 
the multigroup CFA and SEM models with comparative fit index (CFI) 
and root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA). CFI values 
above .90 and .95 are considered acceptable and excellent fit to the data, 
respectively (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Marsh et al., 2004). RMSEA values 
below .06 are considered an acceptable fit, although no absolute stan­
dard exists (Chen et al., 2008; Hu & Bentler, 1999; Marsh et al., 2004). In 
addition, we reported Chi-square (χ2) values.
Multigroup CFA precedes multigroup analysis because it is essential 
to confirm that any observed differences when using teachers’ job se­
lection motive as a moderator are due to genuine variations in the 
constructs, rather than inconsistencies in how they were measured 
across groups (Brown, 2015; Niehaus & Adelson, 2013). We first 
established a CFA model (Fig. 1) with the full sample. The CFA model 
included seven correlated latent constructs along with 28 indicators 
with error terms. We then examined the fit separately for extrinsically 
and intrinsically motivated teachers.
Second, we tested for configural variance to check if the number of 
factors and the pattern of factor loadings were consistent across groups 
(i.e., equal form). We constrained the latent means to zero to ensure 
stability in estimation in the measurement invariance test. We then 
proceeded to subsequent models that entail increasingly restrictive 
constraints. In the third step, metric invariance, also known as “weak 
invariance” (Meredith, 1993), was tested by constraining factor loadings 
to be equal between the two groups. In the fourth step, we evaluated 
scalar invariance (“strong invariance”; Meredith, 1993), by making the 
item thresholds uniform between groups. In the fifth step, we set the 
residual variances equal across groups to examine “strict invariance” 
(Meredith, 1993).
Chi-square-based likelihood ratio tests are typically used to evaluate 
whether a more restrictive model, which includes additional equality 
constraints, provides a comparable fit to the data as the more complex 
model in which it is nested (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). However, 
many researchers have noted that the chi-square test is highly sensitive 
to increasing sample sizes, often flagging trivial differences as significant 
(Cheung & Rensvold, 2002; Meade et al., 2008). Given our large sample 
of teachers, we sought to avoid misinterpreting minor changes in 
chi-square as evidence of measurement non-invariance. Therefore, we 
primarily relied on alternative fit indices, specifically the CFI and the 
RMSEA. According to Chen (2007), in the case of our study where 
sample sizes are adequate (total N > 300) and relatively equal across the 
groups, measurement invariance should be upheld if the change in CFI 
is ≤.01 and the change in RMSEA is ≤.015.
Prior to performing multigroup SEM, we conducted independent 
sample t-tests using SPSS Version 28 to see if two groups of teachers 
showed difference in mean composite scores of each construct. Then, we 
conducted multigroup SEM where demographics, efficacy beliefs, and 
perceived school climate were set to predict the job satisfaction of 
extrinsically versus intrinsically motivated teachers. Consistent with the 
MCFA, latent constructs were correlated in the model, and we evaluated 
model fit using multiple indices. For the significant predictors, we

2.4. Missing data
For the variables considered in the multigroup CFA and SEM, the 
percentage of missing data was low. Most items had missing data for less 
than 1 %, with the maximum being 1.5 %. Low percentages—5 % or 
less—of missing data from a large dataset are typically not problematic 
(Tabachnick & Fidell, 2013). Nevertheless, we used the full information 
maximum likelihood approach to handle missing values. This approach 
incorporates all available information to estimate the model parameters 
(Schafer & Graham, 2002).
3. Results
3.1. Sequential linear regression
The descriptive statistics and correlations between continuous vari­
ables are reported in Table 3. In the initial sequential regression model 
(Table 4), less than 1 % of the variance was explained (p < .001). 
Entering individual efficacy beliefs (i.e., self-efficacy in teaching, self- 
efficacy in classroom management) accounted for an additional 16.9 
% of the variance in job satisfaction (p < .001). Perceived school climate 
predictors accounted for an additional 6.8 % of the variance (p < .001). 
In the final step, non-tenure status positively predicted job satisfaction 
(β = .09), whereas years of teaching experience was a negative predictor 
(β = −.09). Supporting Hypothesis 1a, self-efficacy in teaching (β = .14) 
and self-efficacy in classroom management (β = .20) positively pre­
dicted teachers’ job satisfaction. Consistent with Hypothesis 1b, 
perceived school climate factors—colleague teachers’ passion (β = .19), 
collaboration with colleagues (β = .06), principal support (β = .06)— 
positively predicted teachers’ job satisfaction, with the exception of 
conflicts with colleagues, which negatively predicted job satisfaction (β 
= −.05).
3.2. Multigroup confirmatory factor analysis
Before conducting multigroup CFA, we tested the CFA with the full 
sample to ensure an acceptable model fit for each latent variable 
(Table 5) as well as the overall scale (Table 6). For principal support, 
modification indices suggested adding a covariance path between two 
error terms. Both items shared a similar stem and measured principal 
support in reducing administrative tasks. Accordingly, we allowed their 
residuals to correlate. The CFA model for the overall scale demonstrated 
an acceptable model-to-data fit for the full sample (CFI = .941, RMSEA 
= .046), extrinsically motivated teachers (CFI = .936, RMSEA = .048) 
and intrinsically motivated teachers (CFI = .942, RMSEA = .046). As 
shown in Fig. 1, most items had factor loadings greater than .7 (Kline, 
2016). Only two items, SECM4 (λ = .66) and CFT1 (λ = .58) had lower 
but acceptable factor loadings greater than .4 (Wang & Wang, 2019). 
The correlations among the latent variables were in the expected di­
rections, having positive correlations with each other except for the 
teacher conflict which was negatively associated with job satisfaction, 
teacher efficacy beliefs, and other perceived school climate factors. 
None of the correlations were greater than |r| = .85 (Henseler et al.,

3.3. Preliminary analysis – mean differences in constructs across groups
The established strict invariance indicates that observed differences 
across groups stem from genuine variations in the construct, rather than 
measurement inconsistencies. As part of the preliminary analysis, we 
compared how extrinsically versus intrinsically motivated teachers 
differ in their reporting of job satisfaction, individual efficacy beliefs, 
and perceived school climate factors. As shown in Table 7, independent 
sample t-tests indicated that intrinsically motivated teachers reported 
significantly higher job satisfaction (Mdiff = .41) and self-efficacy in 
teaching (Mdiff = .08) and classroom management (Mdiff = .11). They 
also reported more positive perceptions of colleagues’ passion (Mdiff =
.15), collaboration (Mdiff = .10), and principal support (Mdiff = .11). 
Conversely, extrinsically motivated teachers reported higher levels of 
conflicts among colleagues (Mdiff = −.15). All differences were statisti­
cally significant, ps < .001.

3.4.1. Demographic factors and job satisfaction
Non-tenure status positively predicted job satisfaction for extrinsi­
cally motivated teachers (βEXT = .07, p < .001), whereas it was not 
statistically significant for intrinsically motivated teachers. Satorra- 
Bentler (S-B) scaled chi-square difference test indicated a significant 
difference in the effect of non-tenure status on job satisfaction across two 
groups, Δχ2 (1) = 4.548, p = .033. Years of teaching experience nega­
tively predicted job satisfaction for both extrinsically motivated (βEXT =
−.05, p < .05) and intrinsically motivated teachers (βINT = −.14, p <
.001). However, the effect was stronger for intrinsically motivated 
teachers, as supported by the results of the chi-square difference test. 
Constraining the path across groups significantly deteriorated the model 
fit, Δχ2 (1) = 6.375, p = .012, indicating that the path coefficients 
differed significantly between the two motivational groups.
3.4.2. Individual factors and job satisfaction
Self-efficacy in teaching positively predicted the job satisfaction of 
intrinsically motivated teachers (βINT = .13, p < .05), while it was not 
statistically significant for extrinsically motivated teachers. Self-efficacy 
in classroom management positively predicted job satisfaction for both 
extrinsically (βEXT = .23, p < .001) and intrinsically motivated teachers 
(βINT = .25, p < .001). When the paths to job satisfaction from teaching 
and classroom management self-efficacy were each constrained across 
groups, the model fit did not significantly deteriorate, ps > .05.
3.4.3. Perceived school climate factors and job satisfaction
Colleague teachers’ passion was the sole significant predictor 
positively associated with job satisfaction across both groups (βEXT =
.30, p < .001; βINT = .17, p < .001). Constraining the path significantly 
deteriorated the model fit, Δχ2 (1) = 5.783, p = .016.
4. Discussion
We examined how demographic traits, individual efficacy beliefs, 
and perceptions of school climate were associated with job satisfaction 
among teachers who chose their careers primarily for extrinsic reasons 
versus those who chose their careers primarily for intrinsic reasons using 
the KELS 2013 data from 2013 to 2014. While the results may not reflect 
the current educational landscape, particularly post-pandemic, they still 
have meaningful implications as the study examined distinct levels of 
job satisfaction predictors and the moderating effect of job selection 
motive among teachers from 242 elementary schools recruited through 
stratified cluster random sampling. In the discussion, we drew more 
recent studies to enhance the timeliness and applicability of our find­
ings. The regression coefficients of efficacy beliefs and perceived school 
climate factors from the sequential linear models were all in the ex­
pected directions (Hypotheses 1a, 1b) in line with the prior studies (e.g., 
Aldridge & Fraser, 2016; Klassen & Chiu, 2010). Teachers with intrinsic 
job selection motives held more positive efficacy beliefs and school 
climate perceptions. Demographic, individual, and school-level factors 
show somewhat different associations with job satisfaction for extrin­
sically versus intrinsically motivated teachers.

4.1. Lower job satisfaction reported by experienced/tenured teachers
Across both the extrinsically and intrinsically motivated groups, 
teachers tended to report lower job satisfaction as their years of expe­
rience increased. Notably, this negative association was stronger among 
intrinsically motivated teachers. In Korean elementary schools, experi­
enced teachers often assume greater administrative responsibilities, 
such as serving as department heads or planning for the school board. 
These tasks, which typically increase with experience but are unrelated 
to teaching, may have a greater impact on intrinsically motivated 
teachers, who chose their careers out of a passion for teaching and 
working with children (K. N. Kim, 2019). Furthermore, Korea’s 
seniority-based incentive system, where salaries and benefits increase 
with years of teaching experience, may serve as a stronger buffer against 
declining job satisfaction for extrinsically motivated teachers.
Within the extrinsically motivated group, temporary teachers and 
part-time instructors reported higher job satisfaction than tenured 
teachers. Non-tenure track teachers generally have fewer re­
sponsibilities related to student guidance and classroom management. 
Part-time instructors, who often teach specialized subjects such as math 
and English, typically face a lighter teaching load compared to tenured 
teachers, who are responsible for instructing multiple subjects. Addi­
tionally, the higher hourly pay for part-time instructors relative to 
tenured teachers may further contribute to the greater job satisfaction 
reported by non-tenured teachers in the extrinsically motivated group.
The lower job satisfaction reported by experienced and tenured 
teachers—particularly among intrinsically and extrinsically motivated 
teachers, respectively—is concerning and warrants further investiga­
tion. One possible explanation is that intrinsically motivated novice 
teachers are often enthusiastic and find joy in their work (Clark et al., 
1996). In contrast, teachers with more experience may begin to 
encounter the limitations of their careers, leading to a gradual decline in 
satisfaction (Dobrow et al., 2018). Structural factors, such as insufficient 
support for experienced teachers and the increasing administrative re­
sponsibilities that come with years of teaching, may also contribute to 
this decline, particularly among intrinsically motivated teachers (K. N. 
Kim, 2019). The lower job satisfaction reported by tenured teachers with 
extrinsic motivation is not consistent with the studies that showed the 
importance of job stability/security in choosing a teaching career (Lee, 
Kang, & Park, 2019). Tenure may be an important attractor for selecting 
a job, but tenure status itself may not be a positive predictor of job 
satisfaction. Finding the sources of lower job satisfaction reported by 
experienced and tenured teachers with different job selection motives is 
beyond the scope of this study. Considering that most Korean elemen­
tary school teachers are tenured and continue their profession near 
retirement, however, future studies should examine the reasons behind 
these negative associations.
4.2. The importance of self-efficacy in classroom management
Teacher self-efficacy in classroom management was a stronger pre­
dictor than self-efficacy in teaching for both groups. Indeed, self-efficacy 
in teaching was not significantly associated with the job satisfaction of 
extrinsically motivated teachers. Caution is warranted in interpreting 
this finding as suggesting that teaching self-efficacy is not important for 
extrinsically motivated teachers, because constraining the path across 
groups did not significantly deteriorate the model fit. Instead, the results 
underscore the critical role of classroom management skills in fostering 
job satisfaction, irrespective of teachers’ job selection motives (Klassen 
& Chiu, 2010).
Whether teachers have chosen their careers primarily for extrinsic or 
intrinsic reasons, it is vital to prioritize professional development pro­
grams that equip teachers with effective classroom management stra­
tegies. According to the Teaching and Learning International Survey 
(TALIS) 2018 report (OECD, 2021), elementary school teachers in Korea 
reported experiencing greater difficulties in maintaining order in the 
classroom compared to middle school teachers or elementary school 
teachers in other countries. For example, they spent a higher proportion 
of class time on keeping order, and the percentage of teachers who re­
ported experiencing job-related stress due to classroom management 
was also higher than the TALIS average. Furthermore, the need to 
maintain classroom discipline has become increasingly pressing as 
recent studies highlight the challenges teachers face in managing stu­
dent behavior, which have been exacerbated in the post-COVID-19 era 
(Gülmez & Ordu, 2022; Welsh, 2022). Providing enhanced support and 
tailored training within teacher education and professional develop­
ment programs is essential to better prepare teachers for the complex­
ities of the contemporary classrooms, ultimately contributing to their 
professional satisfaction (Herman et al., 2021).
4.3. Collegial passion, the sole significant school-level Predictor, was more 
salient for extrinsically motivated teachers
Among the school climate factors examined in the multigroup SEM, 
collegial passion emerged as the only significant predictor of job satis­
faction. This finding contrasts with prior studies that emphasize the 
importance of relationships with colleagues and principal support in 
shaping teachers’ job satisfaction (Ertürk, 2021; Toropova et al., 2021). 
However, this discrepancy may be attributable to the highly “individu­
alistic” culture prevalent in Korean elementary schools, where teachers 
work independently in their own space (Oh, 2010). Once entering the 
school, most teachers reside in their own classrooms, teaching their 
homeroom students and handling administrative tasks. Indeed, teachers 
in South Korea reported engaging in team teaching and professional 
collaborative learning less frequently than the OECD average (OECD, 
2020). While efforts have been made to foster a collaborative learning 
culture in Korean schools, and recent studies demonstrate the impor­
tance of such efforts for teachers’ job satisfaction (Guk et al., 2023; Kim 
et al., 2024), factors such as collaboration, conflict, or principal support 
may not directly impact job satisfaction due to limited interaction with 
colleagues and school leadership.
Instead, collegial passion—reflecting the overarching school atmo­
sphere characterized by shared pride and enthusiasm for educational 
activities—appears to be more salient. Even in an “individualistic” 
school culture where teachers rarely interact, fostering a community 
where ideas and practices are shared to achieve the school’s educational 
goals can enhance job satisfaction (Aldridge & Fraser, 2016). Notably, 
shared collegial passion emerged as a stronger predictor of job satis­
faction for teachers who selected their careers based on extrinsic moti­
vations. Extrinsically motivated teachers may be more sensitive to the 
broader school climate because their motivation stems outside of the self 
and a shared sense of purpose and enthusiasm can play a stronger role in 
sustaining their professional engagement and satisfaction (Ryan & Deci, 
2000a). Collegial passion can serve as a vicarious source of motivation, 
helping extrinsically motivated teachers find meaning and value in their 
work through shared enthusiasm. While the organizational climate re­
mains important for teachers who chose the profession for more intrinsic 
reasons, they are more likely to experience satisfaction through personal 
fulfillment, autonomy, and passion for teaching itself, thereby reducing 
the salience of collegial passion (Fernet et al., 2008). To effectively 
promote job satisfaction among teachers with diverse job selection 
motives, it is important to cultivate an environment that fosters high 
morale and passion, rather than placing the responsibility for profes­
sional contentment solely on individual teachers (Carbonneau et al., 
2008; Toropova et al., 2021).
5. Limitations and future directions
We acknowledge limitations and offer suggestions for future 
research. First, while this study focused on participants’ primary career 
motives for methodological conciseness, extrinsic and intrinsic motiva­
tion should be understood as a spectrum rather than a dichotomy (Ryan

& Deci, 2020). Teachers’ motivational profiles are likely more diverse 
(Richardson & Watt, 2014). Future studies should adopt more nuanced 
measures to assess motivation for entering the teaching profession and 
examine its relationship with job satisfaction and other important psy­
chological variables, such as burnout and organizational commitment. 
Second, this study relied on cross-sectional data, which limits causal 
inference. Although predictors were identified based on prior research, 
longitudinal studies are needed to explore causal relationships and dy­
namics over time. Lastly, using secondary data prevented us from 
considering factors that may have a more direct impact on teacher job 
satisfaction and well-being, such as violence against educators and in­
fringements on teachers’ rights by abusive parents (McMahon et al., 
2024; Rashid, 2023). Future studies should incorporate these experi­
ences to provide a more comprehensive understanding of teachers’ job 
satisfaction and well-being.
6. Conclusion
Teachers enter the professions for various reasons, and their job se­
lection motives may impact the operation of factors underlying profes­
sional 
satisfaction. 
By 
systematically 
examining 
demographic, 
individual, and perceived school climate factors as the predictors of job 
satisfaction and investigating job selection motive as a potential 
moderator, we found that teacher self-efficacy in classroom manage­
ment was a key predictor across both extrinsically and intrinsically 
motivated teachers, even more important than teacher self-efficacy in 
teaching. Among perceived school climate factors, collegial passion was 
the sole significant predictor although its salience was more pronounced 
for extrinsically motivated teachers.
We conclude our paper with practical applications and recommen­
dations. First, given the importance of teacher self-efficacy in classroom 
management among teachers with diverse motives, more teacher edu­
cation and professional development programs addressing classroom 
discipline and effective behavior management should be offered to both 
pre-service and in-service teachers. Workshops on financial literacy and 
effective asset management may also be beneficial for teachers, espe­
cially those with extrinsic motivation (E. H. Shin & Lee, 2025). 
Acknowledging that teachers have diverse motives for pursuing a 
teaching career, school administrators can support them by helping 
identify workshops and conferences that align with their interests and 
needs (Rimmer & Floyd, 2020).
In addition, cultivating a school climate where teachers share pas­
sion and pride in educational activities is crucial. School leaders should 
create opportunities for peer collaboration and mentoring, enabling 
teachers to collectively reflect on and improve their classroom practices, 
thereby fostering a culture of growth and collaboration within the 
school (Garner & Kaplan, 2018; Zavelevsky et al., 2022). A school 
climate characterized by shared passion and communal growth may 
further enhance teacher self-efficacy and promote the job satisfaction of 
teachers, especially among those who may not initially find meaning or 
joy in teaching but can still be positively influenced by enthusiastic 
colleagues. As teachers face increasing challenges in contemporary 
classrooms, more research is needed to examine the distinct predictors 
of job satisfaction and the nuanced mechanisms shaping professional 
fulfillment in order to effectively support diverse teachers with different 
job selection motives.
