A B S T R A C T
This study explores the role of school students' acceptance of artificial intelligence (AI) in mathematics learning 
and its impact on mathematics achievement. It also examines the effect of time spent using digital resources in 
mathematics learning on both AI acceptance and mathematics achievement. A cross-sectional survey was con­
ducted with 804 students from 13 schools in the Kathmandu district of Nepal. The research employed several 
statistical techniques, including confirmatory factor analysis, structural equation modeling, cluster analysis, and 
machine learning. The findings indicate that students’ level of AI acceptance in mathematics learning signifi­
cantly affects their mathematics achievement. Furthermore, the study shows that while time spent using digital 
resources positively influences AI acceptance, it has a negative effect on mathematics achievement.
1. Introduction
Mathematics is essential in daily life and professional fields, serving 
as a foundation for science and social subjects (Vos, 2018). Despite its 
importance, mathematics remains one of the least liked subjects among 
students worldwide, often perceived as difficult, abstract, and discon­
nected from real life (Cahyono & Ludwig, 2016, pp. 24–31; Dunning & 
Stenhouse, 2023; Mazana et al., 2019). This widespread disengagement 
continues to affect students’ learning motivation and performance 
across education systems. Globally, mathematics proficiency varies 
substantially. Across OECD countries, 69 % of students attained at least 
baseline proficiency (Level 2) in mathematics, indicating a foundational 
ability to apply basic mathematical skills in familiar contexts while only 
9% reached the highest proficiency levels (Level 5 or 6) demonstrating 
advanced reasoning, problem-solving, and the capacity to apply math­
ematics in complex or unfamiliar situations (OECD, 2023a). These fig­
ures reveal persistent gaps in mathematical literacy even in developed 
contexts. Technological innovation, especially artificial intelligence (AI) 
is now reshaping educational practices by offering adaptive tutoring, 
personalised feedback, and data-driven insights to support learning 
(Adiguzel et al., 2023; Wardat et al., 2023). Yet, while the potential of AI 
in mathematics education is widely recognized (Govender, 2023), its 
impact remains uneven across global regions due to disparities in access, 
infrastructure, and teacher preparedness (Mhlongo et al., 2023; 
UNESCO, 2024).
Regionally, in South Asia, many countries continue to face structural 
barriers to technology integration in schools. Studies show limited ac­
cess to digital tools, insufficient teacher training, and cultural hesita­
tions surrounding new technologies (Karuppiah et al., 2023; Nazir & 
Khan, 2024). While AI use in education is expanding globally, empirical 
research in

Nationally, in Nepal, mathematics achievement remains consider­
ably lower than in other subjects such as Nepali, Science, and English 
(Education Review Office [ERO], 2020, 2022). Only 28 % of students 
meet baseline proficiency in mathematics (ERO, 2019), reflecting 
deep-rooted challenges in engagement and learning outcomes. Although 
the government has introduced several initiatives such as the National 
Education Policy (Ministry of Education Science and Technology 
(MoEST, 2019a)), the Digital Nepal Framework (MoEST, 2019b), School

This contextual backdrop reveals a clear research gap. While global 
and regional studies acknowledge AI's transformative potential, little is 
known about how students in developing countries particularly at the 
school level perceive and accept AI for mathematics learning. Under­
standing students' acceptance of AI through the TAM is therefore 
essential to inform contextually relevant and equitable digital strategies. 
To address this gap, the present study explores the factors influencing AI 
adoption in mathematics learning among Nepalese secondary school 
students. It examines how perceived usefulness, ease of use, and 
contextual variables (such as access, engagement, and ethics) shape 
students' acceptance and learning outcomes. The findings aim to provide 
insights for educators and policymakers seeking to promote sustainable 
and inclusive AI integration in school mathematics. To address these 
gaps and aim of the research, this study seeks to answer the following 
questions:
1. What are the levels of acceptance of AI tools among school students 
in mathematics learning?
2. How much time do school students dedicate to using digital devices 
for mathematics learning at home?
3. To what extent do school students adopt and accept AI tools for their 
mathematics learning?
4. What is the combined impact of AI acceptance and time spent using 
digital resources on students' mathematics learning outcomes?
5. How does the level of AI acceptance among students correlate with 
variations in their mathematics achievement scores?
2. Literature review
2.1. Impact of AI usage on academic performance
Research exploring the effects of emerging technologies on student 
learning reveals mixed outcomes. Studies on AI and educational tech­
nologies consistently highlight AI's potential to enhance learning moti­
vation and academic performance (B¨orekci & Çelik, 2024; Hwang, 2022; 
Hwang & Tu, 2021). However, these effects depend strongly on how AI 
is integrated into instruction (Kachakova, 2020). When used effectively, 
AI can support differentiated instruction and learner engagement, but 
poor implementation or limited teacher preparedness often reduces its 
impact (Fern´andez-Guti´errez, 2020; Kachakova, 2020).
Meta-analyses by Hillmayr et al. (2020) and study of Joshi, Khanal, 
et al. (2025) indicate that technology can significantly improve perfor­
mance in mathematics, science, and language learning when pedagog­
ical and contextual factors are well aligned. From the perspective of the 
TAM, factors such as perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, con­
fidence, and anxiety influence students’ willingness to adopt AI in their 
studies (Li et al., 2024). Additionally, some recent studies show that 
AI-driven intelligent tutoring systems and generative AI models can 
yield learning gains (L´etourneau et al., 2025) comparable to human 
tutoring, especially when designed to promote active reasoning (Deng 
et al., 2025; Pardos & Bhandari, 2024). These systems can enhance ac­
ademic performance, motivation, and higher-order thinking while 
reducing mental effort. Nonetheless, the positive effects of AI depend on 
thoughtful pedagogical design and structured integration within 
learning environments (Kestin et al., 2025). Given the variability of 
these findings, it remains necessary to investigate how AI adoption af­
fects mathematics learning in diverse contexts, including developing 
countries such as Nepal, where disparities in access, digital literacy, and 
instructional support can moderate learning outcomes.
2.2. Time on digital learning resources and mathematics performance
Parallel to research on AI adoption, studies on students’ use of digital 
learning resources suggest a non-linear relationship between digital 
engagement and mathematics achievement. Analyses of PISA 2022 data 
show that students who spend a moderate amount of time as approxi­
mately one to 5 h per day using digital devices for learning purposes 
tend to achieve higher mathematics scores than those who use such tools 
either very little or excessively (OECD, 2023b). Findings by Joshi, 
Chapai, et al. (2025), based on PISA 2022 data, further demonstrate that 
both in-school and out-of-school use of digital tools for mathematics 
learning are strong predictors of achievement. Moderate use supports 
access to interactive exercises, practice opportunities, and feedback 
mechanisms that reinforce understanding. However, excessive or mis­
aligned use such as multitasking or recreational screen time has been 
associated with distraction, reduced concentration, and lower perfor­
mance (OECD, 2023b). This pattern aligns with the “inverted-U″ hy­
pothesis, suggesting that digital engagement supports mathematics 
learning only up to an optimal threshold. These findings underscore the 
importance of balanced, purposeful use of digital technologies, partic­
ularly when integrating AI tools into mathematics education.
2.3. Theoretical framework: Technology acceptance model (TAM) and 
acceptance of AI
The TAM, developed by Davis (1989), provides a robust framework 
for understanding technology adoption in education. It identifies 
perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) as key de­
terminants of users’ attitudes and behavioural intentions. In the 
educational context, PU refers to the extent to which AI tools enhance 
teaching and learning, while PEOU reflects how easily students and 
teachers can use such tools. Research highlights that AI-driven feedback 
and personalised learning pathways can enhance engagement and 
achievement when systems are intuitive and user-friendly (Dwivedi 
et al., 2021; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). Later extensions of TAM 
include additional constructs such as attitudes, behavioural intentions, 
and facilitating conditions (Unal & Uzun, 2021). While positive attitudes 
foster adoption, barriers including inadequate infrastructure, limited 
teacher training, and low digital literacy continue to constrain AI inte­
gration, particularly in developing regions (Adhikari et al., 2022).
In developed contexts, the focus increasingly lies on advanced tools 
such as AI and virtual reality (Hwang & Tu, 2021), whereas developing 
nations often prioritize access to basic digital resources. In mathematics 
education, teacher resistance and limited familiarity with digital peda­
gogy hinder effective technology adoption (Egara & Mosimege, 2024). 
Effective integration therefore requires teacher training, pedagogical 
alignment, and attention to ethical and curricular issues (Hwang, 2022). 
Whereas in Nepal, technology acceptance in education is shaped by 
socio-economic and infrastructural disparities. Urban schools exhibit 
moderate levels of technology adoption, while rural schools face sig­
nificant challenges including inadequate internet access, insufficient 
teacher training, and limited digital devices (Baral, 2025). Although 
government initiatives such as e-learning platforms and tools like Geo­
Gebra and Khan Academy have enhanced student engagement 
(Mollakuqe & Mollakuqe, 2025), rural schools continue to experience 
inconsistent utilisation. Cultural perceptions and resistance to peda­
gogical change further hinder effective integration (Adhikari, 2024). 
TAM thus provides an appropriate framework to examine how perceived

2.4. Hypotheses development with conceptual model
The TAM developed by Davis (1989) has been widely applied to 
predict technology adoption across educational contexts (Venkatesh 
et al., 2003). In the context of AI-assisted mathematics learning, the 
model offers a systematic approach to analysing students’ attitudes and 
behaviours in relation to AI tools. Generative AI (GenAI) systems such as 
adaptive platforms, AI-powered tutors, and problem-solving assistants 
can personalise learning, provide real-time feedback, and support con­
ceptual understanding in mathematics (Alshahrani & Qureshi, 2024; 
Mollakuqe & Mollakuqe, 2025). TAM constructs can be linked directly 
to AI features in mathematics learning as follows:
Perceived Usefulness (PU): AI tools enhance mathematics perfor­
mance by providing immediate feedback, personalised exercises, and 
adaptive learning paths, supporting students’ understanding of algebra, 
geometry, and other topics (Teo & Noyes, 2011). PU is strengthened 
when students perceive AI as improving learning efficiency, conceptual 
mastery, and problem-solving skills.
Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU): The intuitive design and interactive 
interfaces of GenAI platforms reduce cognitive load and facilitate 
engagement, especially for students with limited digital literacy (Baral, 
2025; Mollakuqe & Mollakuqe, 2025). Tools like AI-driven tutoring 
systems simplify complex mathematical concepts, making learning more 
approachable.
Attitude Toward Use: Positive experiences with AI features such as 
gamified problem-solving, instant feedback, and adaptive challenges 
foster favorable attitudes, which are crucial for sustained adoption (Mok 
& Anderson, 2021; Sergeeva et al., 2025).
Behavioral Intention: When students perceive AI tools as both useful 
and easy to use, they are more likely to integrate these tools into regular 
learning practices, influencing their actual engagement and perfor­
mance in mathematics (Wang et al., 2024).
In the Nepalese context, contextual variables such as AI literacy, 
perceived enjoyment, trust in AI, social influence, and time spent on 
digital devices further shape adoption patterns (Bernardo & Seva, 2023; 
Falebita & Kok, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Rural–urban disparities in 
infrastructure and access (Adhikari, 2024; Baral, 2025) also mediate 
these relationships. By linking TAM constructs with contextual factors, 
this framework offers a comprehensive lens to study AI adoption in 
school mathematics in Nepal. It helps identify barriers, inform peda­
gogical design, and guide policies for equitable integration of AI tech­
nologies. Based on the reviewed literature and the theoretical 
framework, the study proposes the following hypotheses with concep­
tual model presented in Fig. 1_a.

3. Methodology
3.1. Study setting
This study adopted a cross-sectional survey design within a quanti­
tative research framework to examine the acceptance of AI-based tools 
among school students in Kathmandu district, Nepal. This design was 
appropriate because it allowed the collection of data from a large 
number of students at a single point in time, enabling the identification 
of patterns and relationships among variables such as AI acceptance and 
mathematics achievement without manipulating any conditions. Ac­
cording to the Ministry of Education (MoE, 2016), Nepal has 34,837 
schools, including 883 schools (179 community and 704 institutional) 
situated in Kathmandu district. The Flash I Report 2076 (2019–2020) by 
the Ministry of Education, Science, and Technology (MoEST, 2020) in­
dicates that 75,021 students (54,216 in institutional schools and 20,805 
in community schools) were enrolled in classes eight and ten across 
these schools. Consequently, the target population for this study 
comprised all students enrolled in classes eight and ten within the 
district.
3.2. Sample and sampling
The sample size of 384 was calculated for a population of 75,021 
students using a 95 % confidence level, 5 % margin of error, and 50 % 
population proportion via an online calculator (https://www.calculator. 
net/sample-size-calculator.html). A 5 % adjustment was made for non- 
responses, resulting in a final sample size of 404 for each category. 
Stratified sampling ensured representation across sex (boys and girls), 
school type (community and institutional), and academic levels (basic: 
grades 1–8, secondary: grades 9–12) consisting 50 % in each category. 
Based on the Flash I Report 2076 (2019–2020), the average number of 
students per community school was 58, while institutional schools had 
39 students (MoEST, 2020). Survey response rates can vary across 
contexts (Holtom et al., 2022; Ngulube, 2005), with Holtom et al. (2022)
reporting an average rate of 68 %. Flash Report 2022 showed an average 
student attendance rate of 181 out of 220 school days (MoEST, 2023), 
with Bagmati Province's attendance rates at 68.9 % and 62.2 % for upper 
basic and secondary levels, respectively (GoN & UNICEF, 2019). A 67 % 
participation rate was assumed for Kathmandu. Thirteen schools were 
selected by random number table method, resulting in a final sample of 
836 students, with 32 excluded due to missing data, leaving 804 for 
analysis. Details of the sampling frame is presented in Fig. 1_b.

3.3. Data collection
Data were collected through face-to-face mode by sharing the 
research instrument with the presence of research team by visiting the 
selected schools. The participant consent was obtained in written form 
in research instrument. For making item understandable to the students, 
it was translated into Nepali language. The participants were informed 
about the purpose of research before data collection. Additionally, the 
mathematics achievement (MathA) was taken from official record of the 
related schools. Data were collected from October 2024 to January 
2025.
3.4. Research instrument
An adapted instrument named “AI Acceptance Scale in Mathematics 
Learning,” based on the TAM, was employed in this study which consists 
13 items. The items were adapted from different previous validated 
scales and contextualized to fit the study focus on students’ acceptance 
of AI in mathematics learning in Nepali context. All items were related to 
acceptance of artificial intelligence tools in mathematics learning. The 
items were measured in five-point scale as strongly disagree to strongly 
agree. The items were categorized in three constructs as perceived 
enjoyment using AI (PEUI), attitude towards using AI (ATUA), and 
perceived usefulness of AI (PUA) by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) 
method. Similarly, the tool consists three items relating to the time for 
using digital devices/resources (TUDR) for mathematics learning as 
mobile, laptop/computer, and tablet which were measured in five-point 
scale as no time given, less than 1 h, 1–2 h, 2–3 h, and more than 3 h. The 
scale was self-developed by the researcher based on the literature as 
Rayce et al. (2024) and Twenge and Campbell (2018).
3.4.1. Reliability and validity of instrument
The face, construct, and content validity of the tool was ensured by 
sharing the tool with five mathematics education related experts and 
their feedbacks as language correction and modification of item were 
ensured before data collection. Reliability was calculated using Cron­
bach's Alpha (α) and the value found to be ranged from 0.73 to 0.81 
which exceed the threshold value of 0.70 (Kenny, 2006). The overall 
Alpha value found to be 0.85 which also exceed the threshold value of 
0.70.
3.5. Data analysis
This study employed descriptive statistics, machine learning tech­
niques, and multi-group structural equation modeling (MSEM) to 
analyze AI acceptance in mathematics learning. Missing values and 
outliers were excluded, resulting in the removal of 32 cases. Machine 
learning techniques, specifically model-based clustering, identified 
three levels of AI acceptance: high, moderate, and low, based on 13 
items measuring AI acceptance. This model was applied to identity the 
AI acceptance level of students (for RQ1). Visualizations, including 
sample distribution and cluster mean plots, were used to represent the 
data. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) validated three factors of AI 
acceptance: perceived enjoyment (PEUA), perceived usefulness (PUA), 
and attitude towards using AI (ATUA), ensuring model fit (Cheung & 
Rensvold, 2002). Bar diagram (in percentage) was used to find status of 
students in spending time for the use of digital devices for mathematics 
learning at home (For RQ2). A one-way ANOVA tested differences in 
PEUA, PUA, ATUA, and mathematics achievement based on time spent 
using mobile devices, laptops, and tablets for mathematics learning (for 
RQ3). Furthermore, SEM and machine learning were applied to find the 
effect of AI acceptance and time spent in using digital devices on 
mathematics achievement (RQ4 and RQ5). Multi-group structural 
equation modeling (MSEM) tested the hypothesized model for the total 
sample and AI acceptance levels, with model fit indices assessed 
(O'Connell et al., 2013). A random forest regression model evaluated 
data accuracy and the item-wise effects of time spent on digital resources 
and AI acceptance on achievement (Hweju et al., 2022). Results were 
visualized through accuracy plots and error plots. Mathematics 
achievement was categorized by grade levels, with frequency distribu­
tions computed. A linear discriminant classification model was used to 
assess response accuracy within achievement categories, with results 
presented using ROC curves and other visual tools. AMOS 23 was uti­
lized to run SEM models whereas JASP was utilized for machine 
learning.
4. Results
4.1. Data clustering
The data were clustered based on AI acceptance of respondents by 
using model-based clustering analysis under machine learning ap­
proaches for comparing achievement scores among different level of AI 
acceptance of the students. Different levels of clustering (i.e., 2-cluster to 
10-cluster) were checked however 3-cluster model was chosen based on 
the better BIC, AIC, Silhouette, and Entropy values as compared to the 
other model in JASP. The model divided the data into three clusters as 
cluster 1 containing 289 respondents (35.9 %) with positive average 
values across all variables (i.e., indicating it represents a "high-value" 
group), cluster 2 contains 118 (14.7 %) respondents with strongly 
negative average values (i.e., making it a "low-value" group), and cluster 
3 contains 397 (49.4 %) respondents and shows average values near zero 
(i.e., representing a "neutral-value" group). In based on these evidences, 
researcher utilized high (High value group as cluster 1), moderate 
(neutral-value group i.e., cluster 3), and low (low-value group i.e., 
cluster 2) AI acceptance categories. The model explains 58 % of the 
variance indicates that the model fit of clustering is good fit (Milligan & 
Cooper, 1985). Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Infor­
mation Criterion (BIC) found to be 1346.8 and 1403.07 respectively 
shows that the selection criterion of the model has good balance model 
complexity and goodness of fit (Burnham & Anderson, 2004). Silhouette 
Score in the model found to be 0.32 indicates that the structure is 
weak-defined clusters (Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 2005, p. 88). That value 
is comparatively better in cluster 1 (0.38) and cluster 2 (0.39) as 
compared to cluster 3 (0.25). Similarly, the Calinski-Harabasz index

4.5. Results of machine learning
The random forset regression model under mancine learning was 
employed to find the item wise role of AI acceptance and time of using 
digital devices in mathematics learning in shaping achievement score. 
Table 6 shows that the model split the data into three groups as n(Train) 
= 515, n(Validation) = 119, n(Test) = 160 including 85 trees with 4 
features considered per split. The training dataset consists of 515 sam­
ples, with 129 and 160 samples allocated to validation and test datasets, 
respectively. The validation Mean Squared Error (MSE) is 418.99, and 
the test MSE is 397.15, both relatively high, reflecting significant pre­
diction errors (James et al., 2013). The Out-of-Bag (OOB) error (Fig. 5_a) 
is 485.61, further suggesting a high generalization error (Genuer et al., 
2010). The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is 19.93, indicating the 
magnitude of residual errors is substantial. The Mean Absolute Per­
centage Error (MAPE) is 49.63 %, implying the model's predictions 
deviate by nearly 50 % on average from actual values (Khanal et al., 
2024). Lastly, the R2 value of 0.07 shows the model explains only 7 % of 
the variance in the data however that variance is only 3 % in the SEM 
model (See Fig. 4).
Table 7 shows the feature importance metrics reveal which variables 
significantly impact model accuracy and node purity (Fig. 5_b and 5_c). 
The most influential feature based on mean decrease in accuracy is 
PEUA3, with a value of 14.36, indicating that excluding this feature 
significantly reduces the model's predictive accuracy. It is closely fol­
lowed by ATUA4 (11.23), PEUA5 (10.86), and PUA01 (10.25), which 
also strongly contribute to the model's performance. For total increase in 
node purity, PEUA2 (7339.23) and ATUA4 (7334.90) rank highest, 
demonstrating their critical role in improving decision splits within the 
trees. Other notable contributors to node purity include PEUA3 
(7148.68) and ATUA2 (6872.13). Interestingly, some features, like 
PEUA1 and PEUA4, have relatively low mean decreases in accuracy but 
still contribute significantly to node purity, suggesting they influence 
tree splits without drastically affecting overall accuracy.

The study reveals that approximately half of the students exhibit 
moderate AI acceptance, while smaller proportions show high or low 
acceptance. This indicates a combination of interest and hesitation to­
ward AI adoption, reflecting patterns observed in the literature where 
students engage cautiously with AI technologies in mathematics 
learning (Adiguzel et al., 2023; Egara & Mosimege, 2024; Wardat et al., 
2023). Barriers contributing to moderate acceptance include uncritical 
use of AI, weak curriculum alignment, and limited digital literacy. These 
results align with studies reporting low enthusiasm for mathematics in 
Nepal (ERO, 2019) and internationally (Mullis et al., 2011). Prior 
research emphasizes that AI tools can enhance engagement, 
problem-solving, and conceptual understanding, but only when 
accompanied by effective pedagogical support (Vos, 2018). Therefore, 
the observed moderate acceptance underscores the need for targeted 
interventions, including teacher guidance and digital literacy programs, 
to foster higher levels of AI acceptance.
5.2. Time spent in using digital devices for mathematics learning
The findings indicate that a substantial portion of students report no 
daily use of digital devices for mathematics learning: approximately 
one-third for Android devices, more than half for laptops/computers, 
and around three-fourths for tablets. Moderate daily usage (1–2 h) 
supports learning, while excessive use (>2 h) is associated with reduced 
focus and lower mathematics performance, consistent with PISA 2022 
data (OECD, 2023b). These patterns support prior research suggesting a 
non-linear relationship between digital engagement and mathematics 
achievement (Joshi, Khanal, et al., 2025; Salanova et al., 2013). Mod­
erate engagement provides access to interactive materials, practice op­
portunities, and timely feedback, whereas overuse can cause distraction, 
multitasking, and diminished attention. The results highlight the 
importance of equitable access, improved digital infrastructure, and 
guidance for effective ICT use, particularly in rural and resource-limited 
contexts (Baral, 2025; Adhikari, 2024).
5.3. Extent of adoption and acceptance of AI tools for mathematics 
learning
The study demonstrates that students with higher AI competency 
tend to show greater acceptance of AI tools. However, the relationship 
between AI acceptance and mathematics achievement is complex. While 
high AI acceptance correlates with positive attitudes and engagement, it 
does not consistently translate into higher achievement, particularly for 
low-acceptance students or those lacking digital literacy. Perceived 
enjoyment and perceived usefulness strongly influence AI adoption. 
Enjoyable AI features, such as gamified platforms, enhance engagement 
(Sergeeva et al., 2025; Vladova et al., 2021), but without structured 
instructional support, enjoyment alone does not yield academic gains 
(Salanova et al., 2013). Perceived usefulness drives engagement and

6. Implications
6.1. Pedagogical Implication
The study highlights significant barriers to digital resource use, with 
large proportions of students reporting no engagement with use of 
digital resources for mathematics learning. Coupled with moderate AI 
acceptance, these findings underscore the need to integrate digital lit­

training to guide students in critically using AI tools such for problem- 
solving and conceptual understanding, preventing uncritical use that 
may hinder deep learning. Structured workshops can support students in 
leveraging AI-generated practice questions while promoting reflection 
and critical thinking, consistent with constructivist and connectivism 
pedagogical approaches.
The results also indicate that perceived enjoyment of AI tools posi­
tively influences attitudes and perceived usefulness but negatively affect 
mathematics achievement if overused. Educators should design AI-

broader adoption of AI in mathematics learning.

7. Contribution of the research
The theoretical and methodological contribution of this research 
includes the use of AI acceptance (under TAM), model-based clustering, 
multi-group structural equation modeling (MSEM), and machine 
learning to examine how AI acceptance in mathematics learning in­
fluences students' achievement. TAM was contextualized in AI accep­
tance focusing in mathematics learning in Nepali context. Clustering 
identified three levels of AI acceptance as high, moderate, and low 
allowing meaningful comparison among student groups. MSEM pro­
vided deeper insights into how perceived enjoyment, usefulness, and 
attitudes toward AI affect learning outcomes within Nepal's educational 
context. It also helped capture group-specific differences among stu­
dents with varying levels of digital access and AI familiarity. Unlike 
traditional methods, MSEM revealed diverse learning patterns, 
improving understanding of how AI acceptance operates across student 
subgroups. Machine learning techniques, such as random forest regres­
sion and discriminant analysis, complemented these findings by testing 
predictive accuracy and identifying key influencing factors. By inte­
grating these approaches, the study developed a robust and innovative 
framework for analyzing AI acceptance and its impact on learning. This 
framework offers valuable guidance for future research on educational 
technology, particularly in resource-limited contexts.
8. Conclusion
This study offers valuable insights into the role of AI acceptance and 
digital device usage in mathematics education. It highlights the complex 
and multifaceted relationship between AI integration and student 
achievement, revealing both positive and negative effects. While AI 
tools show promise in improving engagement and attitudes towards 
learning, their direct impact on mathematics achievement remains 
insignificant. The findings suggest that to maximize AI's potential, it is 
essential to address barriers to access, improve digital literacy, and 
ensure that AI tools are thoughtfully integrated into the curriculum. 
Furthermore, balancing technology use with traditional learning 
methods is critical. Excessive use of digital devices may have adverse 
effects on achievement, pointing to the need for purposeful, balanced 
integration of AI tools in mathematics education. This study examines AI 
acceptance through the lens of perceived enjoyment, perceived useful­
ness, and attitudes toward technology, based on the TAM. Future 
research could explore additional dimensions of this framework to gain 
a deeper understanding of AI's educational impact. Time spent using 
digital resources was included as an external variable, suggesting that 
subsequent studies should consider other contextual factors influencing 
student achievement.
Several methodological limitations should be noted. Some model- 
based clustering indices (BIC, AIC, Silhouette, Entropy) indicated rela­
tively weak cluster separation. Similarly, the Random Forest regression 
showed modest fit, and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) exhibited 
limited accuracy and performance. These outcomes likely reflect the 
study's focus on the effects of AI acceptance and digital usage on 
mathematics achievement, rather than on optimizing predictive models. 
Nonetheless, integrating machine learning with TAM represents a 
methodological extension, highlighting the potential of combining 
theoretical models with predictive analytics to guide future research.
The study relied on official institutional records of students' first 
terminal examination (academic year 2080/81). Future research could 
use self-developed assessments to provide a broader perspective. Addi­
tionally, this study was limited to a survey of students in the Kathmandu 
district, Nepal, focusing on AI acceptance in mathematics learning. 
Subsequent studies could implement targeted interventions across 
diverse student populations, explore a wider range of AI tools, include 
multiple subjects, and consider different national contexts to provide a 
more comprehensive understanding of AI's educational implications.
