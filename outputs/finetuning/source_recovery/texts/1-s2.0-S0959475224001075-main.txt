Background: This special issue sheds light on how students’ well-being can be enhanced through teaching
practices, emphasizing the significance of well-being in the educational context. My discussion was rooted in
extensive well-being literature, which allowed me to outline key findings about the importance, effects, and
conceptualizations of well-being, and factors that may influence well-being.
Method: The issue comprises seven empirical studies, each exploring the relationship between teacher-related
variables (e.g., emotions, growth mindset) or teaching-related variables (e.g., intercultural education, needsupportive teaching) and student well-being. It utilizes studies with methodologies ranging from large-scale
assessments like the Program for International Student Assessment (PISA) to detailed longitudinal designs
across various educational levels and contexts.
Results: Findings underscore the pivotal role of teaching in influencing student well-being. That is, specific
teacher behaviors and pedagogical approaches were identified that correlate with student well-being. Key aspects included intercultural education, need-supportive teaching, and positive teacher–student relationships. The
methodological strengths of the included studies were their use of large datasets, longitudinal designs, and multiinformant data. Based on these findings, I presented some suggestions for possible methodological
improvements.
Conclusion: This SI contained impressive articles that can be used as excellent models for research in this area.
The findings can also expand our current knowledge by providing a multifaceted, multinational, and multiinformant understanding of the topic. Practical applications are suggested how teachers can support their students’ well-being and highlighted the importance of integrating well-being into educational discourse to provide
a foundation for future investigations of teaching and learning processes.

2.5. Processes of well-being
Set-point theory is an influential theory of well-being that states that
each individual has a specific level of well-being that they are likely to
return to after fluctuations in either direction. A similar idea is expressed
through terms like hedonic adaptation or the hedonic treadmill. Brickmann, Coates, and Janoff-Bulman (1978) famously compared individuals with spinal cord injuries and lottery winners, showing that
both groups eventually returned to the happiness levels reported at
baseline before the extreme event. Set-point theory has, however, been
criticized (Luhmann & Intelisano, 2018). An alternative approach that is
particularly useful for the design of interventions is the hedonic adaptation prevention model (Lyubomirsky, 2011), which outlines several
moderators that affect the rate of hedonic adaptation and show how it
can be avoided. Additionally, well-being regulation is influenced by
comparison mechanisms. For example, downward comparisons with
people experiencing bad situations can lead to better evaluations of
one’s own well-being (Schwarz & Strack, 1988).

2.2. The effects of well-being
Subjective well-being (SWB) is related to better health and longevity
(Diener, Oishi, & Tay, 2018). Specifically, happier people have better
marriages and more friends. At work, they perform better, have a lower
job turnover, are absent less frequently, and are better organizational
citizens than those who are unhappier. Well-being thus has enormous
effects on important aspects of daily life.
2.3. Conceptualizations of well-being

2.6. Well-being interventions

Well-being is commonly studied as SWB (Diener, Oishi, & Tay,
2018). It can also be divided into eudaimonic and hedonic forms.
Eudaimonia has been defined as realizing one’s potential, while hedonia
relates to the experience of pleasure and the absence of pain. More
modern approaches to well-being incorporate broader concepts like
flourishing, which Seligman (2011) defined as positive emotion,
engagement, social relationships, a sense of meaning, and accomplishment (i.e., the PERMA model).

A series of meta-analyses have reported positive effects of interventions aimed at enhancing well-being (e.g., Bolier et al., 2013; Sin
& Lyubomirsky, 2009). Many interventions found to be effective involve
simple exercises that individuals can apply easily during their daily
lives. Notably, these interventions are often applied on the individual
level and less frequently on the level of agents in the educational
domain.

3.4. Limitations
The limitations of the included studies are summarized in Table 4.
One notable problem was that although some studies used multiple informants, teaching behavior was mostly reported by students (except for
Holzer et al.‘s study). One can argue that the student’s perspective is the
most important and relevant. The problem with students rating teaching, however, is that they also report on their well-being, leading to
possible common method bias. If, for example, a student is in a good
mood, they may perceive and assess teaching as better than if they were
in a bad mood, and their mood would also influence their well-being
report. Interestingly, none of the studies that assessed teachers’
perceived teaching and student well-being found any association between the two, while in Holzer et al.‘s study, the highest correlation
between the teacher or teaching and student well-being was 0.11.
Table 4 lists some further limitations specific to each study. Notably,
most of the data were self-reported, but the PISA studies also provided
PISA test results, and a few studies added observational data. Another
key limitation was that some of the studies were cross-sectional, even
though the question of whether student well-being also impacts teachers
and teaching can only be answered in longitudinal studies (for example,
Hascher & Mori). A final limitation of the studies was that none provided
an intervention to test the causal mechanisms behind the effects found.

3.3. Methods used
The methods of the included studies were compared in terms of their
design, participants, and analytical approaches (Table 3). Regarding
design, three studies used a longitudinal design, with Hascher and Mori
assessing bidirectional lagged relationships. Two of the cross-sectional
studies used large, multi-country PISA datasets.

although some used a multi-informant approach. For instance, Basarkod
et al. combined data from principals, teachers, and students. Notably,
using a multi-informant approach does not necessarily mean that multiple informants give information about the same topic, as can be seen in

With some exceptions, the papers included largely justified the need for
more investigations into well-being and its links with variables like academic achievement and social behavior, but they did not all emphasize
the importance of well-being as an independent value.
Most authors noted the hedonic and eudaimonic aspects of wellbeing, but only Hascher and Mori used a multidimensional framework
that included positive and negative aspects to conceptualize well-being,
even though other broad models of well-being exist, including the
PERMA model (Butler & Kern, 2016) and its adaptation for younger
people and the engagement, perseverance, optimism, connectedness,
and happiness model (EPOCH) (Kern et al., 2015). Other researchers
have applied still more differentiated conceptualizations of well-being:
Marsh et al. (2020) proposed a 15-component model of well-being
(including competence, clear thinking, emotional stability, engagement, meaning, optimism, positive emotions, positive relations, resilience, self-esteem, vitality, acceptance, autonomy, empathy, and
pro-sociality), while another approach is the art-of-living model
(Schmitz, 2016; Schmitz et al., 2022), which combines hedonic and
eudaimonic components, as well as both positive aspects and negative
events. This model also integrates bodily aspects that are important for
well-being but are not included in the PERMA model. Most importantly,
the art-of-living model clearly differentiates between well-being and
ways to achieve well-being. Ways to achieve well-being can be learned,
and there have been evaluation studies with interventions based on the
art-of-living model showing that well-being can be enhanced, especially
in the school context (Lang & Schmitz, 2016; Schmitz, 2016; Tavakoli,
Müller, Jeske, Schäfbuch, & Schmitz, 2022).
It would be interesting to study the effects of different teacher- and
teaching-related variables on the various aspects of well-being outlined
in such models, such as social and achievement aspects. Future studies
can also assess multiple outcome variables so that well-being and performance can be investigated together. Theoretically, different types of
effects are possible. For instance, good performance might lead to
increased well-being (Hascher & Mori, 2022), or good well-being may
lead to improved performance. However, it can be worthwhile to study
the assumption that high achievement can lead to poorer well-being due
to the intensive time and resources required. Available empirical
research has also suggested that greater well-being improves academic
performance (Bücker et al., 2018), but this is not yet conclusive.

3.5. Key findings and strengths
Table 5 shows some of the studies’ key results and methodological
strengths. The studies showed a diverse range of associations between
teachers or teaching and student well-being, and the aspects of teaching
found to predict well-being included intercultural education, the
teacher’s mindset, autonomy, need-supportive teaching, justice and
failure management, and the teacher–student relationship. The studies
also showed that well-being is not affected by cultural background
(Basarkod et al., 2024) or the teacher’s emotions (Holzer et al., 2024).
Wang et al. reported that teaching practices mediate an association
between the teacher’s mindset and student well-being, while Collie et al.
found that all variables related to the social-emotional competence
framework are associated with well-being. The effects of
need-supportive teaching on well-being were also independent of
various cultural, economic, and political contexts (King et al., 2024).
Further, Hascher and Mori found student well-being to be related to
teaching. The methodological strengths of these papers included their
use of large PISA datasets from many countries, longitudinal data
collection, multilevel analyses, and multi-informant data.
3.6. Practical implications
The authors suggested the following ways to apply their results in
practice: implementing effective intercultural activities at school and
intercultural sensitivity training for teachers; offering teacher preparatory programs and in-service teacher training to enable teachers to
communicate a growth mindset; implementing social-emotional
learning programs for enhancing autonomy-supportive teaching that
may help develop students’ perceived social-emotional competence
(perceived SEC); teachers supporting their students in achieving goals
and learning, including in making errors. There is also a need to
encourage teachers to engage in need-supportive teaching, while
workshops or curricula can be integrated into preservice teacher education and professional development opportunities for in-service
teachers. The research also called for intervention efforts to preserve
and develop students’ school belonging and positive teacher–student
relationships.
3.7. Future directions

3.7.3. General future directions
Research is needed on other factors that can influence well-being,
such as parents and schools. Growing evidence for positive education
has shown that schools can add significantly to student well-being
(White & Kern, 2018). Students can also foster their own well-being,
with a broad research base showing how students can enhance their
well-being using validated exercises (Lang & Schmitz, 2017; Tavakoli
et al., 2022).
With respect to processes of well-being regulation, the hedonic
treadmill was mentioned, in addition to ways to avoid adapting to the
well-being equilibrium. In future, researchers might also examine how
any changes to well-being can be solidified so that effects lead to stable
increases over time. This is relevant because adaptation can occur due to
comparison processes like students comparing their well-being to the
well-being of their classmates. Research is also needed on how teachers
can better support students through these inevitable comparisons.

Below, I included suggestions for further research with respect to
future possibilities mentioned in the studies included in this SI, suggestions for future studies not mentioned in this SI, and suggestions for
research in the field of well-being in general. I added suggestions
addressing the methodological limitations and improving the strengths
of the studies, as well.
3.7.1. Future directions from each study included
Authors of this SI’s included studies suggested many interesting avenues for further investigations, such as applying more rigorous longitudinal designs (Basarkod et al., 2024; Collie, Martin, Renshaw, &
Caldecott-Davis, 2024; Holzer et al., 2024) and including teacher measures of teaching behavior (Archambault et al., 2024; Collie, Martin,
Renshaw, & Caldecott-Davis, 2024; Holzer et al., 2024). Holzer et al.
proposed collecting student ratings of teaching behavior, King et al.
believed that their study could be extended by using different measures
for need support, and Wang et al. mentioned that it might be interesting
to study whether a subject other than math would yield similar results.
Further investigations following Basarkod et al.‘s work would benefit
from measuring the quality and frequency of intercultural practices
according to the authors.

4. Conclusion
To conclude, the papers included in this SI gave a broad overview of
current research on the effects of teaching on student well-being in
schools. These studies included large samples and considered important
contexts like culture. The samples also included special subgroups like
immigrants. In this SI, I focused on the options that teachers have, laying
the foundation for establishing a broader perspective of what teachers
and schools can do to improve student well-being. Interesting bidirectional relations between teachers and student well-being were found,
and well-being was studied from various perspectives. Hence, these
studies offered interesting and promising perspectives and are excellent
models for how research in this area should be conducted. Looking at the
goals formulated by Collie and Hascher (2024) in their editorial, they
stated: “This special issue on “Student Well-Being and the Role of
Teaching and Learning Factors” considers this significant topic to shed
light on both the construct of student well-being, along with important
school-related factors linked with greater level of well-being among
students.”. My findings approve that the reached their goals. The studies
covered many conceptualizations of well-being and teaching-related
components. Practical applications were also suggested. Together, the
studies offered rich opportunities for further research into student wellbeing in specific and more general contexts. A key development in this
field would be the application of a differentiated model of well-being
and ways to achieve well-being, with a greater emphasis on longitudinal studies and interventions that explore how well-being can be
enhanced and maintained.

3.7.5. Addressing methodological limitations and improving strengths
In terms of how the methodological limitations of these studies can
be addressed, a main criticism was that too many studies used crosssectional data and self-reporting. Although some studies collected data
from multiple informants, the source of these data was still problematic
since only students reported on teachers’ behavior. Data from other
informants should thus be collected. An important result of this SI was
that none of the studies found direct relationships between teacherreported teaching behavior and student well-being, so it would be useful to collect data from students and teachers about the same teaching
behavior. While three of the seven included studies used longitudinal
data and found interesting results, a logical next step would be to design
intervention studies in which teaching behavior is manipulated. A good
example of this is the work of Adler (2016), who studied the well-being
of approximately 100,000 students in Mexico and Peru using PISA data.
As noted above, there were also intervention studies on self-directed
well-being improvement in students (Lang and Schmitz, 2016; Tavakoli et al., 2022).
Despite their limitations, however, the strengths of these studies
should be acknowledged and built upon. One such strength was the
samples used, which were massive (for the PISA studies) or included
members of populations like immigrants, the disadvantaged, and young
students, all of whom can be difficult to recruit for research. It is
impressive that teaching effects were shown in these samples. Attempts
to replicate and generalize these results are thus warranted, and there is
a need to test whether similar effects can be found in “normal” samples.
A second strength that can be built upon is the use of different measures
to assess teaching. Combining diverse measures like intercultural and
need-supportive teaching would be a valuable next step.
A topic not dealt with in this SI was whether there are negative effects of certain types of teaching. For example, if a teacher takes much
class time to support student–student interactions, there would be less
time to explain difficult concepts, and this can lead to reduced
achievements. Furthermore, the many teaching methods studied in this
SI can be expanded by looking at other teaching practices that might
enhance well-being. Possible variables that can also be studied include
cognitive activation, time on task, and aspiration levels. It would also be
interesting to examine teachers’ feelings about using strategies to
enhance well-being. While a few authors included here, such as Hascher
and Mori, analyzed bidirectional associations between teaching and
student well-being, they did not directly address teachers’ emotions.
Hascher and Mori also raised the possibility of studying how to reduce
the negative aspects of their well-being framework. Combining these
recommendations, a worthwhile goal would be to develop a matrix
comprising those teaching aspects known to influence specific components of well-being. While in this SI, I addressed how characteristics of
teachers and aspects of teaching relate to well-being, broadening the
topic can reveal the importance of variables related to schools (White &
Kern, 2018), parents (Hoferichter et al., 2021), and national education
systems in promoting well-being (Diener, Oishi, & Tay, 2018).
