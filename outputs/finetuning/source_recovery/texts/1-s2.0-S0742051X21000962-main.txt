This study examines teacher collaboration in nine German schools to identify factors that explain the
structure of teacher networks in relation to speciﬁc types of collaboration and schools' structural environment. Using inferential network analysis, we ﬁnd that teachers’ individual attributes are important
for collaboration in schools with basic collaborative environments, and for the exchange of material. By
contrast, in schools with well-established collaborative structures, ties are formed predominantly based
on prior relations. Our ﬁndings emphasize the importance of collaborative structures to lower the barrier
for individual teachers to engage in collaboration and to bring together colleagues from different
backgrounds.

1. Introduction
The degree to which teachers are embedded in school-based
collaboration networks has proven to be a crucial factor for the
work of teachers and has been theoretically linked to schools'
innovative capacity (Harris, 2011; Peurach, Glazer, & Winchell
Lenhoff, 2016). Studies show that collaboration (i.e., intentional
and goal-oriented collaborative activities) can positively inﬂuence
teacher motivation (Kolleck, 2019), support innovative educational
practices (Vangrieken, Dochy, Raes, & Kyndt, 2015; Vangrieken,
Grosemans, Dochy, & Kyndt, 2017) and improve student learning
(Moolenaar, 2012). Educational researchers have applied techniques of social network analysis (SNA) to uncover the importance
of teacher networks as a potential source of school improvement

A limited number of studies has addressed this gap by examining the structures of advice networks (i.e., advice-seeking
behavior) among teachers in British and American schools as one
potential type of interaction (Ortega, Boda, Thompson, & Daniels,
2020; Siciliano, 2015). However, we still know little about the
emergence of collaboration among teachers, especially in relation
to different collaborative activities (e.g., the exchange of material,
mutual classroom observations, or instructional support). In
particular, types involving different levels of effort and cost have
not yet been considered. For example, while exchanging materials
represents a collaborative activity that involves comparably little
cost, mutual classroom observations present a higher hurdle in that
they require more organization and time. As such, a systematic
examination of explanatory factors (i.e., antecedents) of teacher
collaboration and how they vary across different collaborative activities is missing.
Furthermore, teacher collaboration highly depends on a school's
structural environment (i.e., structural factors that hinder or foster
collaboration among teachers, such as time and space for exchange,
formal contact persons, administrative leadership, or the school's
location) (Coburn & Russell, 2008; Glover et al., 2016; Vangrieken
et al., 2015; Woodland & Mazur, 2019a). Also, prior collaborative
structures can lead to the initiation of short-term collaboration
(Jong, Meirink, & Admiraal, 2019; Woodland & Mazur, 2019b).
However, while the question of whether the structural environment
can be linked to teacher collaboration has been examined previously, empirical evidence on how structural characteristics of the
school relate to the emergence of teacher networks is limited. In
particular, effects of differences in the structural environment of
schools on the emergence of teacher networks have not been
adequately considered in previous research.
The present study addresses these gaps by examining teacher
collaboration in order to answer the following research questions:
What are antecedents of teacher networks in schools? How do these
antecedents differ according to different types of collaboration (i.e.,
high- and low-cost collaboration)? How do these antecedents relate to
differences in schools’ collaborative structures (i.e., their structural
environment)?
To answer these questions, we draw on ideas of expectancyvalue theory. This theory assumes that options of engaging in a
task include both positive and negative characteristics and are
associated with costs that individuals weigh against the potential
achievements (or beneﬁts) related to a task before choosing an
option (Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2002). According to Butler (2007),
schools can be conceived as an achievement- and performanceoriented context for teachers. Hence, we assume that teachers
base their decision whether to participate in a collaborative activity
on an evaluation of costs and beneﬁts (Drossel, Eickelmann, van
Ophuysen, & Bos, 2009; Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2002; Nebus, 2006). In
our study, we investigate collaboration networks in nine German
schools each containing between N ¼ 11 and N ¼ 93 teachers (in
total N ¼ 366). By using techniques of inferential network analysis
(i.e., exponential random graph models; ERGMs), we calculate and
compare models for the general collaboration networks and for
type-speciﬁc collaborations, and further compare the ﬁndings
regarding different structural environments.

2.1. The importance and potential of teacher networks
Scholars have increasingly turned their attention to the investigation of teacher networks during recent decades. Reviews of
existing literature have identiﬁed several beneﬁts of teacher
collaboration at different levels, such as teacher level, student level,
or school level (Kelchtermans, 2006; Lomos, Hofman, & Bosker,
2011a; Vangrieken et al., 2015). For instance, studies show that
teacher collaboration has a positive impact on teachers' well-being
and job satisfaction (Reeves, Pun, & Chung, 2017; Yıldırım, 2014)
and that collaboration can help teachers to develop improvement
strategies and induce individual learning processes (Jong et al.,
2019). Furthermore, teacher collaboration can improve collective
efﬁcacy, which then in turn increases teachers' willingness to take
on additional tasks (Moolenaar, Sleegers, & Daly, 2012; Somech &
Drach-Zahavy, 2000). At the student level, scholars found that
teacher collaboration positively inﬂuences student understanding
and performance (Lomos, Hofman, & Bosker, 2011b; Moolenaar,
2012; Reeves et al., 2017). At the school level, beneﬁts include
general school improvement and an increase in schools’ innovative
capacity (Harris, 2011; Peurach et al., 2016; Vangrieken et al., 2015).
Teacher networks can represent different types of connections,
such as collegiality or collaboration. For instance, while collegiality
rather refers to the relationships among teachers, collaboration
covers their cooperative actions (Kelchtermans, 2006; Vangrieken
et al., 2015). A ﬁrst conceptualization of collaboration as a heterogenous construct was developed by Little (1990), who identiﬁed
four different types of collaboration on a continuum from dependence to interdependence between the parties involved. Sawyer
(2009) further suggests a differentiation between collaboration
and cooperation: while collaboration entails types of teacher relations with all parties simultaneously involved in the progress of
reaching a common goal, cooperation represents the distribution of
different tasks to the teachers towards the achievement of the
outcome. For the present study, we use the term teacher collaboration as an intentional collaborative activity between two or more
teachers, which is characterized by shared goals, communication

have focused on different types and qualities of relations, such as

Kaufman, & Stein, 2012; Ortega et al., 2020), or collaborative
discourse among teachers (Lefstein, Louie, Segal, & Becher, 2020).
However, systematic investigations of teacher collaboration as a
construct with differing prerequisites are still scarce. In order to
understand the emergence of collaboration ties among teachers, it

The reasons for teachers to engage in collaboration with their
colleagues can be manifold (Kolleck, 2019). Several empirical
studies have identiﬁed different characteristics that can explain the
formation of collaboration ties, such as teachers' experience,
particular role, or attitude towards collaboration (e.g., Drossel et al.,
2019; Ortega et al., 2020; Siciliano, 2017). Due to the complexity of
the topic, there is yet no unifying theory that can explain the
emergence of collaboration networks. Instead, the existing literature proposes different approaches that contribute to the understanding of the issue. For this study, we draw on ideas of
expectancy-value theory, which has proven to be a suitable
model for the identiﬁcation of factors explaining teachers’ collaboration activities (Drossel et al., 2019; Siciliano, 2017).
The expectancy-value model of Eccles and Wigﬁeld (2002), on
which we mainly base our assumptions, is characterized by a focus
on the negative and positive characteristics of a task that must be
carried out to achieve a goal. According to their model, decisions to
engage in a task depend, on the one hand, on the subjective value
and probability of success associated with the goal and, on the
other hand, on the costs expected to be incurred in initiating and
performing a task (Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2002). As Drossel et al. (2019)
note, considering the subjectivity of the value related to a goal is
particularly important for the study of teacher collaboration,
because the utility of engaging in collaborative activities can differ
highly between teachers. While gaining new teaching methods
through an exchange of ideas appears valuable to one teacher,
another teacher, who is convinced of the success of his or her own
methods, may be prone to neglect possible beneﬁts. Similarly,
choosing a particular colleague to collaborate with can be related to
different beneﬁts: collaborating with an experienced colleague
might be more beneﬁcial than collaborating with less experienced
colleagues. The same applies for the individual costs associated
with collaboration. These can vary widely across teachers and situations, depending on both the individual attributes of the parties
involved and the general environmental context. For example,
approaching a colleague who is similar in certain characteristics
such as gender, experience, or formal role (e.g., assistant principal,
school subject coordinator, or head of student councils) can imply
lower costs (Siciliano, 2015; Ortega et al., 2020). According to Nebus
(2006), who developed a theory of network generation for collegial
networks in public organizations, individuals weigh the beneﬁts of
achieving a goal against the costs of initiating and performing the
task necessary to achieve the goal. Following this, we assume that
teachers perform similar cost-beneﬁt considerations when
deciding whether or not to engage in particular collaborative activities, which can partly explain the emergence of teacher networks (Drossel et al., 2019).
In general, Robins, Pattison, Kalish, and Lusher (2007) differentiate two main groups of factors inﬂuencing the formation of any

or dyad level) and endogenous factors (i.e., self-organizing structural dependencies). More speciﬁcally, this means that the formation of a tie in a network can be seen as a result of individual
attributes of both the sender and the receiver; of mutually shared
attributes; and of the general network structure. Consequently, this

2.3.4. Network closure
Social network theory shows that individuals tend to use
existing connections when initiating new relations, such as reciprocating ties or closing triads (i.e., network closure) (Burt, 2000;
Granovetter, 1985). This means that if a person A connects with
another person B, B is likely to reconnect to A (i.e., reciprocity).
Furthermore, if A has a relation to B and B is connected to C, it can
be assumed that A also has a relation to C (i.e., transitivity). In the
context of teacher collaboration, these processes can also be seen in
the light of cost and beneﬁt calculations. The costs of using an
already existing connection are lower than creating a new one.
Hence, approaching a teacher who has already asked for support
implies higher chances for a positive response. This can then lead to
the desired outcome. The same applies for transitive ties: reaching
out to a colleague with shared contacts is easier and also increases
the chances of getting valuable support (Siciliano, 2015). In line
with previous research on advice networks among teachers (Ortega
et al., 2020; Siciliano, 2017), we therefore test whether network
closure can be found in our data and hypothesize that teacher
collaboration ties tend to be reciprocal (H5) and transitive (H6).

2.3.1. Formal role/experience
Collaborating with colleagues with a certain expertise can be
assumed to be particularly beneﬁcial and is thus conceived as one
of the main drivers for collaboration among teachers (Spillane,
Hopkins, & Sweet, 2015; Wilhelm, Chen, Smith, & Frank, 2016).
Two characteristics of teachers can be regarded as signals for a
certain level of expertise: their professional experience and their
formal role in a school (e.g., assistant principal, school subject
coordinator, or head of student councils) (Siciliano, 2015). Research
on advice networks e both from schools and from other public
organizations e suggests that the attributed value of information
increases with the perceived level of expertise of the addressee
(Nebus, 2006; Siciliano, 2017). Although Siciliano (2017) in his
study could not conﬁrm the inﬂuence of experience on advice
seeking behavior, we follow ﬁndings from other studies (e.g.,
Ortega et al., 2020; Spillane et al., 2015; Wilhelm et al., 2016) and
hypothesize that teachers are more likely to collaborate with colleagues who have a formal role in their school (H1), and who have a
high level of experience (H2).

2.4. Schools’ structural environment
Various scholars have emphasized the importance of schools’
structural environments for teacher collaboration (Vangrieken
et al., 2015). Empirical studies have shown, for instance, that it is
important to schedule enough time for collaboration (Berry,
Daughtrey, & Wieder, 2009), give room for frequent formal interactions (Stephenson, Warnick, & Tarpley, 2008) and establish
physical structures (e.g., open doors or double classrooms) (Main,
2007). In addition, school leaders or administrators can positively
inﬂuence the willingness of teachers to engage in collaborative
activities (Datnow, 2011; Drossel et al., 2019; Kelchtermans, 2006;
Stephenson et al., 2008; Woodland & Mazur, 2019b).
At the same time, little is known about how different levels of
schools’ structural environments relate to the antecedents of
teacher collaboration. Several scholars suggest that collaborative
structures are highly related to school improvement and that a
well-established structural environment that fosters teacher
collaboration can be regarded as a characteristic of school quality
(Darling-Hammond, Wei, Andree, Richardson, & Orphanos, 2009;

Similarly, schools with formal networks established by their principals show higher levels of informal interactions among teachers

Hence, for this study we expect the above-mentioned antecedents

the same time, these factors may be less important in schools with
structural environments that facilitate collaboration (e.g., through
predeﬁned teams, supportive leadership, or scheduled times for
collaboration).

2.3.2. Attitude
Another crucial factor that needs to be considered when trying
to explain the emergence of a collaboration tie between two
teachers is their attitude towards collaboration (Vangrieken et al.,
2015). The willingness of teachers to engage in collaborative activities depends on the expected beneﬁt of an interaction and this
is, in turn, a result of their general perception of collaboration as
beneﬁcial. At the same time, when teachers have a positive attitude
towards collaboration, they might themselves be perceived as easy
to reach as a collaboration partner, which keeps the engagement
costs low (Drossel et al., 2019; Moolenaar, Daly, Cornelissen, et al.,
2014). Similarly, Vangrieken, Grosemans, Dochy, and Kyndt (2017)
argue that a positive attitude from a potential collaboration partner can reduce one's own fear of losing freedom to make decisions.
This, in turn, can lower the costs of engaging in collaboration. In
line with previous research (e.g., Keller-Schneider & Albisser, 2013;
Richter & Pant, 2016), we hypothesize that teachers with a positive
attitude towards collaboration are more likely to collaborate with
colleagues (H3a), and that teachers are more likely to collaborate with
colleagues who have a positive attitude towards collaboration (H3b).
2.3.3. Homophily
According to social network theory, individuals tend to form
connections with others having similar attributes (i.e., homophily)
(McPherson, Smith-Lovin, & Cook, 2001), a behavior that reﬂects a
basic psychological process of being attracted to similar others
(Byrne, 1971). This is not only due to the general perception of
similar characteristics as positive (Ortega et al., 2020; Tajfel, 1974),
but it also minimizes psychological costs, such as embarrassment or
rejection, and increases the beneﬁt of receiving relevant support
(McPherson et al., 2001; Siciliano, 2015). Teachers might expect a
colleague with similar characteristics to show more willingness to
help and to provide support that is more directly related to one's
own needs. Given previous studies of teacher networks that identiﬁed similarities in gender, experience, or school subject as

3. Methodology
3.1. Sampling and instruments
The study presented in this article was conducted in 2019 in 16
German secondary schools in mostly urban districts of Germany. All
schools decided to participate in a project on teacher collaboration
and schools’ structural environments. The survey used for this
article included questions to assess teacher networks. It was
implemented at the outset of the research project. Written consent
was obtained from each individual teacher and federal guidelines
for data protection were met in the collection and analysis of the
survey. Data protection concerns led us to operate with a code list
for each school that listed all teachers and staff members with a
randomized code. This list allowed for a data collection procedure
that assured conﬁdentiality to each individual participant. Teachers
ﬁlled out the network questions with the codes as pseudonyms.
The list that matched name to pseudonym was distributed only in
the data collection setting. It was then collected from all the participants and shredded immediately. As the data collection was part
of regular teacher meetings where participation was obligatory, the
project reached an average participation rate of 70% of the teachers
in a school. However, seven schools had to be excluded from the
sample of this study due to response rates below 60% in the
network questions, which is a response level needed for the type of
analysis conducted.3 Hence, the networks analyzed for this study
covered nine schools, each containing between N ¼ 11 and N ¼ 93
teachers. This led to a total of 366 teachers who were included in
the analyses.
In order to collect information on teachers’ social relations, we
used techniques of SNA. First, teachers were asked to select up to
ﬁve colleagues with whom they collaborated in general (“Now
please enter the codes for the ﬁve people with whom you have
spoken particularly frequently over the past year about situations in
class and developed solutions”). A limit of ﬁve possible nominees
has proven to be sufﬁcient for the generation of network data in
recent SNA research (Merluzzi & Burt, 2013). In their study,
Merluzzi and Burt (2013) not only show that results do not differ
greatly when the number of possible nominees is limited to ﬁve,
but also that asking for more nominees increases the burden on
respondents, which can lead to lower response rates. In addition,
teachers provided information on different types of collaboration
with the nominated colleagues by answering on how often they
exchanged material (“I exchange teaching materials (e.g., tasks and
worksheets) with this person”) or performed mutual classroom
observations (“I carry out mutual classroom observations with this
person”). Given that the three types of collaboration can be
considered on a continuum from low to high cost and that international studies also tend to distinguish between two types (e.g.,
OECD, 2020), we decided to include only low-cost collaboration
(i.e., exchange of material) and high-cost collaboration (i.e., mutual
classroom observations). The teachers could assess these statements on a Likert-type scale ranging from never (1) to almost daily
(5). For the analyses we performed for the study, we had to
dichotomize the data by coding only values of 3 (monthly) to 5
(almost daily) as relations. In this way, three different networks
were generated for each school: a general collaboration network
and two type-speciﬁc networks.

models (ERGMs) (Robins et al., 2007). This method allows to make
predictions about the probability of tie formation in a social
network based on certain characteristics and to statistically
examine its topology. Hence, the network represents the independent variable and the characteristics represent the dependent
variables. The main idea is to model the characteristics of a theoretical network and to estimate their parameters by ﬁtting it to the
observed network. In this way, individual and structural network
characteristics that occur signiﬁcantly more often than would be
expected by chance can be identiﬁed. ERGMs can consist of
endogenous (i.e., network-inherent structural features) and

4.2.1. Exogenous factors
Exogenous factors include both the effects of teachers’ individual attributes and homophily effects. The effect for incoming ties
according to formal role is negative and signiﬁcant, suggesting that
teachers are less likely to address colleagues without a formal role
and, in turn, prefer approaching teachers who hold formal positions. This effect conﬁrms our H1 and can also be observed in Fig. 1.
In this exemplary school network, the size of the nodes represents
the in-degree and the color indicates whether the teachers hold
formal roles. Those teachers with formal roles tend to be addressed
more frequently. At the same time, the effect for incoming ties in
relation to the level of experience is positive but does not meet the
strict signiﬁcance level (p ¼ 0.09). Hence, our results do not show
sufﬁcient evidence for H2.
The expected effects of attitude in the general collaboration
networks cannot be observed. Not only are the results mixed with
regard to the direction of the effect (the covariate for outgoing ties
is negative, while the one for incoming ties is positive), both
covariates do not have a signiﬁcant effect. Hence, neither the attitude of the sender nor that of the receiver of general collaboration
activities have an inﬂuence on the emergence of the network,
thereby disconﬁrming our H3a and H3b.
For homophily effects, the covariates are expected to be positive
in the case of gender, formal role, and subject, and negative in the

case of attitude and experience.7 The homophily terms for gender,
formal role, and subject are positive and signiﬁcant. The term for
experience homophily is negative and almost meets the signiﬁcance level (p ¼ 0.098). Only the homophily term for attitude is not
signiﬁcant, albeit negative. In total, these results largely conﬁrm our
H4. This means that teachers are more likely to collaborate with
colleagues of the same gender, if they both have (or do not have) a
formal role at school, if they teach similar subjects, and if they have
a similar level of experience. Only similarities regarding the general
attitude towards collaboration do not seem to have an impact on
the decision to collaborate. Fig. 2 illustrates two of these homophily
effects. The network on the left depicts the teacher collaboration
network of an exemplary school. Nodes (i.e., teachers) tend to be
grouped by their color (i.e., the interviewees’ gender). As for the
teacher network on the right, nodes are colored according to their
level of experience. Similarly, nodes with the same color tend to be
closer than nodes with different colors.

4.2.2. Endogenous factors
In our model we included two main structural effects along with
several control variables. The estimate for reciprocity is positive
and signiﬁcant, indicating that teachers tend to reciprocate
collaboration activities: it is 23.6 (¼exp[3.16]) times more likely for
a tie to exist between A and B if a tie from B to A already exists.
Therefore, we ﬁnd evidence for our H6. However, the question
asked for the nominations implied mutual interaction behavior,
which is why this particularly strong effect needs to be interpreted
with caution.
For transitivity we included two terms, one for edge-wise
shared partners and one for dyad-wise shared partners. The estimate for GWESP is positive and signiﬁcant. Combined with the
negative and signiﬁcant estimate for GWDSP, these results indicate
a strong tendency of teachers to collaborate with colleagues with
whom they already have joint collaboration partners and conﬁrm
our H7.

5. Discussion
The purpose of this paper was to study the emergence of teacher
collaboration as a function of type of collaboration and schools'
structural environment. Based on ideas of expectancy-value theory
(Drossel et al., 2019; Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2002), we derived hypotheses and tested them drawing on survey data from nine
German schools. By using ERGMs, a technique of inferential
network analysis, we systematically identiﬁed antecedents of
teacher collaboration and compared them over different types of
interaction and schools’ levels of collaborative structures.

collaboration based on the others' expertise, in this case represented by formal role. This ﬁnding further supports assumptions
that teachers' collaboration behavior depends on the beneﬁts of a
collaboration, which are expected to increase with the level of
expertise of the collaboration partner (Drossel et al., 2019). At the

In addition, other factors at the individual level, such as attitude
or gender, were found to inﬂuence only the exchange of teaching
materials. Especially the individual attitude towards collaboration
seems to be an important precondition for exchange relationships.
Not only the positive attitude of a teacher increases the probability
of exchanging teaching material (H3a). Rather, the perceived or
known positive attitude of colleagues also increases the probability
that teachers will ask these colleagues for teaching materials (H3b).
This suggests that teachers attribute higher values to the beneﬁts of
interaction in the case of exchanging material if they generally
perceive collaboration as beneﬁcial. Furthermore, expecting a
positive response to an exchange request due to a perceived positive attitude of the approached colleague decreases the costs of
initiating a collaboration. These ﬁndings are in line with the theoretical assumptions based on expectancy-value theory (Drossel
et al., 2019; Eccles & Wigﬁeld, 2002) and further conﬁrm previous ﬁndings that attitude has an important effect particularly for
the exchange of materials (Richter & Pant, 2016). However, the
attitude scale used in this study was based on self-developed items
that showed an acceptable level of reliability and psychometric
quality, but could not cover all dimensions of attitude. Hence, the
results regarding attitude must be interpreted with caution and
future studies could include more complex concepts of attitude.
At the dyadic level, our study suggests that the emergence of
teacher collaboration highly depends on similarities, which mainly
conﬁrms H4. Teachers tend to collaborate with similar colleagues
regarding gender, formal role, or subjects. These ﬁndings conﬁrm
those of other studies on teacher networks (Moolenaar, Daly,
Sleegers, & Karsten, 2014; Ortega et al., 2020; Siciliano, 2015;
Spillane et al., 2012). In addition, they accord with our assumptions
based on expectancy-value theory: on the one hand, initiating
collaboration with similar others reduces the psychological costs of
the embarrassment of being rejected and, on the other hand, increases the beneﬁts of receiving valuable results due to the similar
background. Hence, both main features of the expectancy-value
model by Eccles and Wigﬁeld (2002), the subjective value and
the expected costs, are addressed with the homophily effect.
Finally, our ﬁndings indicate that factors related to the more
general network structure also explain the emergence of teacher
collaboration. Not only do teachers tend to a high degree to
reciprocate relations (H5), but they also rely on shared contacts
when approaching colleagues concerning collaboration (H6). These
results are consistent with those of Siciliano (2015) and Ortega et al.
(2020) who found that processes of network closure are at work in
the formation of advice networks. Overall, these ﬁndings lend
support for the assumptions that teachers try to minimize their
costs of approaching colleagues for different types of collaboration
by using existing connections (Siciliano, 2015, 2017). The probability of getting rejected is much smaller if a contact has existed
before or if, at least, having mutual contacts can facilitate the
initiation of a collaboration. Furthermore, these ﬁndings add to

5.2. The role of type of interaction and schools’ structural
environment
Our study shows that both the type of interaction and the
schools’ structural environment are consequential for the emergence of teacher collaboration. Concerning the type of interaction,
individual characteristics seem to play a subordinate role in
explaining the emergence of classroom observations (i.e., coconstruction). Following Little (1990) and Gr€
asel et al. (2006), we
assume that this can be attributed to the dependence of such highcost collaboration types on more established structures that require
a high level of trust. In this regard, these forms can be seen as highly
dependent on the provision of adequate structural support, which
may also reduce concerns among participating teachers about a
loss of autonomy. Hence, attributes that inﬂuence other types of
collaboration do not necessarily facilitate co-construction activities.
However, these ﬁndings partly contradict results from Richter and
Pant (2016) who found that less experienced teachers tend to
practice other types of co-construction, such as team-teaching,
more frequently than their more experienced colleagues. Furthermore, we focused on one particular type of co-construction activity,
so generalizations are limited. Future studies could test our ﬁndings
by examining different co-construction activities, such as team
teaching or collaborative professional learning (OECD, 2020).
Our ﬁndings also indicate that the emergence of networks differs regarding schools' structural environment. The complete
absence of signiﬁcant factors related to individual attributes in our
models for the schools with well-established collaborative structures (according to the school prize and the self-reported conditional framework for collaboration) suggests that these schools
already have structures in place to facilitate collaboration and keep
the costs associated with collaboration low. This, in turn could
reduce the need for additional individual factors of inﬂuence. In this
way, our results extend ﬁndings from scholars who have already
emphasized the inﬂuence of a collaborative environment on the
emergence of teacher collaboration (Drossel et al., 2019; Jong et al.,
2019; Kelchtermans, 2006; Woodland & Mazur, 2019b). Furthermore, these ﬁndings conﬁrm the assumption by Moolenaar, Daly,
Sleegers, and Karsten (2014) that variables at the school level,
such as the collaborative environment inﬂuence patterns of
teachers' social relations at a higher level than the dyad. Improved
structural environments not only increase the number of collaborative activities, but also seem to reduce the inﬂuence of individual
characteristics on the formation of relationships. In this way, such
environments can be conceived as lowering the barrier to collaboration for teachers who do not have collaboration-enhancing
characteristics. Moreover, providing adequate time and space for
collaborative activities can lead to an increase of collaboration between teachers of different subjects or levels of experience, or it can
reduce the importance of a teacher having a formal position.
However, some individual characteristics, in particular teachers’
attitudes towards collaboration, might vary systematically between
the two groups, which could additionally inﬂuence the differences
regarding the facilitating factors. Future studies could further
examine the extent to which teachers with positive attitudes might
intentionally choose schools with well-established collaborative
structures.

Furthermore, teachers’ tendency to collaborate with similar
others carries the risk of group formations, which might limit the
fruitfulness of exchange and interaction. We suspect that this tendency is due to teachers' conscious or subconscious desire to keep
psychological costs low e a ﬁnding that is not limited to teachers,
but to people in general. However, in order to strengthen the
innovative capacities of schools, it is necessary to expand collaboration and strengthen it beyond the known circles (Moolenaar,
Daly, Cornelissen, et al., 2014). Our results further indicate that in
schools with well-established collaborative structures, where the
costs for collaboration might already be lowered, the networks
show less homophily. This suggests that creating ﬁxed times and
spaces for collaboration can already reduce the costs to an extent
where teachers might be willing to approach others who are dissimilar to them. Another solution could be the formal composition
of groups of teachers with different characteristics and backgrounds regarding, for instance, gender, experience, or subjects. In
this way, teachers could combine their different knowledge to
achieve common goals.
Finally, as pointed out by other scholars before (Drossel et al.,
2019; Moolenaar, Daly, Cornelissen, et al., 2014; Vangrieken et al.,
2015), teachers’ attitudes towards collaboration can positively inﬂuence their willingness to engage in collaborative activities.
Consequently, by enhancing collaborative structures in schools,
administrations may always address the beneﬁts of such developments in order to improve or maintain the generally positive
attitude found in this study as well as in previous ones (e.g., Richter
& Pant, 2016).

5.3. Limitations and prospects for future research
One limitation of our study is the rather small sample size. Due
to low response rates, seven schools had to be excluded from the
analyses. Although the characteristics of these schools did not
systematically differ from those of the schools included, a larger
sample size would probably provide more robust results. Furthermore, some of the included schools still had response rates at the
border of the acceptable range, which may have affected the results. Thus, future studies would need to increase response rates.
However, it is very difﬁcult to obtain social network data between
teachers. Many of the previous SNA studies have focused on individual schools. Since we were able to include different schools and
their networks in this analysis, the data base obtained nevertheless
represents a valuable contribution to complement the current state
of research on teacher collaboration.
A second recommendation concerns the statements used to
survey both the collaboration partners and the different types of
interaction. Although the general form of the data allowed for an
analysis of directed networks (that is, networks with a sender and a
receiver of a connection), the statements implied a form of mutuality e in particular for the mutual classroom observations.
Consequently, in contrast to ﬁndings from advice networks, our
results for reciprocity have to be interpreted with caution. In order
to strengthen our ﬁndings, future research could further emphasize
the direction of the connection in the survey items. Furthermore,
future studies could survey the tie strengths in a way that can be
used for analyses of valued networks in order to enhance the understanding of the networks.
Another limitation relates to the formal structure in schools,
which might have an inﬂuence on the emergence of collaboration
ties and which could not be considered in this study. For instance,
Penuel et al. (2010) found that teachers who participated in the
same meetings were more likely to interact, and Meredith, van den
Noortgate, Struyve, Gielen, and Kyndt (2017) showed that predeﬁned department structures within schools also inﬂuenced tie
formation processes. Hence, in future studies scholars could ask for
further information on pre-deﬁned structures existing in schools
and include these in more complex techniques of inferential
network analysis (e.g., multi-level approaches).

5.4. Practical implications
For some time now, there has been an increasing demand for
collaboration between teachers worldwide in order to support,
among other things, processes of teacher professionalization and
quality development in schools. Our study emphasizes the need for
facilitative environments that are provided by the school administration. Such environments can lower the barrier for individual
teachers to engage in collaboration e thereby reducing the
importance of teachers’ individual characteristics e and foster the
emergence of tie formation as a result of ongoing structural processes. In this way, these collaborative environments are particularly important for bringing together teachers from different
backgrounds. This applies especially for high-cost co-construction
activities, such as mutual classroom observations, which seem to be
less inﬂuenced by individual characteristics, but rather result from
transitive structures. Given the importance of such activities for
developing teaching in schools, the promotion of such structures
can be considered mandatory for school improvement.
