A B S T R A C T
In large-scale, low-stakes assessments, understanding and evaluating students’ test-taking motivation (TTM) is 
essential for educators to gain insight into individual psychological characteristics and the effort students invest 
in specific tests. Traditional motivational measures, such as self-reported effort and response time effort (RTE), 
can provide information about a student’s usual characteristics related to TTM. However, these measures often 
overlook situational factors that can influence performance during the testing process. Therefore, it is important 
to propose a new approach to evaluate TTM that addresses the limitations of traditional measures. In this study, 
we introduce a new concept—behavioral effort—based on process data to assess TTM. We used data from the 
Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018, which comprised 2853 students, to compose such 
a concept. We validated the behavioral effort by comparing it to traditional measures of TTM, and it was used to 
predict academic performance through multilevel analysis. The results indicated that (1) behavioral effort 
effectively assessed TTM and showed a positive correlation with self-reported measures and RTE, and (2) 
behavioral effort was a strong predictor of academic performance, demonstrating a larger effect size than 
traditional measures. This study successfully extracted behavioral effort from process data, providing a valuable 
tool for evaluating TTM.
1. Introduction
In educational settings, test-taking motivation (TTM) is one of the 
psychological factors that can influence an individual’s learning process, 
the effort they put into testing, and ultimately their academic outcomes 
(Bai, 2020; Cole, Bergin, & Whittaker, 2008; Knekta & Ekl¨of, 2015; 
Nauzeer & Jaunky, 2021; Penk, P¨ohlmann, & Roppelt, 2014; Penk & 
Schipolowski, 2015). Traditionally, TTM has been measured using a self- 
reported approach (subjective measure) or a response time approach (i. 
e., response time effort, RTE, which is an objective measure) derived 
from a dispositional perspective. However, since motivation, particu­
larly TTM, can be state-like and may fluctuate during the testing process, 
recent literature has increasingly favored a situational perspective for 
measuring TTM (Horst, 2010; Knekta & Ekl¨of, 2015; Penk & Schipo­
lowski, 2015; Renninger & Hidi, 2019). The present study introduces a 
new approach to evaluating TTM from a situational perspective by uti­
lizing multiple sources of process data. We propose a new concept called 
“behavioral effort.” Empirical evidence is drawn from the Programme 
for International Student Assessment (PISA) 2018 database, which 
supports the validation and application of the behavioral effort concept.
1.1. Test-taking motivation definition and its influence
Test-taking motivation, as a specific form of achievement motiva­
tion, refers to an individual’s willingness to engage with test items and 
to invest effort and persistence in this undertaking (Baumert & Demm­
rich, 2001, p. 441; Ekl¨of, 2010). Motivation can be understood through 
various psychological theories. For example, in self-determination the­
ory, motivation is associated with intrinsic autonomy; in expectancy- 
value theory, motivation is divided into “expectancy” and “value” di­
mensions; and in goal theory, it is defined in terms of “mastery”. TTM 
could be traced back to expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 
2002; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) and is closely related to test-taking 
effort, which involves individual engagement and persistence

(Ivanova, Michaelides, & Ekl¨of, 2020). This psychological characteristic 
has garnered widespread attention in low-stakes tests that carry no 
immediate consequences, such as PISA, the National Assessment of 
Education Progress (NAEP), and the Trends in International Mathe­
matics and Science Study (TIMSS).
Research has demonstrated that test-taking motivation (TTM) can 
significantly influence academic performance, a finding supported 
across various genders, family backgrounds, and cultures (Ekl¨of, 2010; 
Ivanova et al., 2020; Nagy, Nagengast, Frey, Becker, & Rose, 2019; Pools 
& Monseur, 2021; Silm, Pedaste, & T¨aht, 2020). Additionally, TTM af­
fects the utilization of learning strategies, levels of test anxiety, self- 
efficacy, long-term learning performance, and career development, 
among other outcomes (Lent, Brown, & Hackett, 2000; Pintrich & De 
Groot, 1990; Schunk, 1991). Given that these elements can lead to 
biased assessments of an individual’s capabilities (Rios, Guo, Mao, & 
Liu, 2017; Wise & Gao, 2017), it is crucial to identify and evaluate an 
individual’s test-taking motivation within instructional activities.
1.2. Assessment of test-taking effort based on the dispositional perspective
Previous studies have described test-taking motivation (TTM) as a 
relatively stable disposition, representing a general response to specific 
tasks or situations (Arvey, Strickland, Drauden, & Martin, 1990; Wig­
field & Cambria, 2010). There are two primary approaches to assessing 
TTM: the subjective approach and the objective approach. The subjec­
tive approach utilizes psychological scales to quantify students’ self- 
reported motivation through Likert-type questionnaires. Examples of 
well-known tests in this category include the Attitude Scale (Arvey et al., 
1990), the Online Motivation Scale (Boekaerts & Boscolo, 2002), and the 
Student Opinion Scale (Sundre & Moore, 2002; Thelk, Sundre, Horst, & 
Finney, 2009). These measures typically consist of multiple items that 
evaluate test-taking effort. Additionally, some tests directly ask in­
dividuals to provide their subjective judgments about their seriousness, 
such as the Effort Measurement Scale from the PISA (effort thermom­
eter; OECD, 2019a). In contrast, the objective approach relies on 
behavior-based process indicators, particularly from computer-based 
assessments that utilize log files. A widely recognized measure in this 
approach is the response time method, which aims to detect rapid 
guessing behavior based on response time information. The Response 
Time Effort (RTE) is a popular metric that quantifies the proportion of 
test items answered with adequate effort. If an item is answered faster 
than a predetermined threshold, it is classified as low-effort or rapid 
guessing behavior (e.g., Lee & Jia, 2014; Wise & Kong, 2005). 
Conversely, motivated individuals are less likely to engage in rapid 
guessing and tend to spend sufficient time on each item (Sahin & Colvin, 
2020; Silm et al., 2020). In the response time approach, determining the 
threshold for identifying rapid guessing behavior is crucial (Molenaar, 
Bolsinova, & Vermunt, 2018; Qian, Staniewska, Reckase, & Woo, 2016; 
Ranger, Wolgast, & Kuhn, 2019).
The above measures, based on the dispositional perspective, have 
limited effectiveness in complex test situations. On the one hand, psy­
chological tests are completed before or at the end of the test, so in­
formation during the test is missing; disadvantages of self-report 
methods, e.g., social desirability, drifting, Rosenthal effect (a re­
searcher’s or teacher’s expectations unconsciously influence partici­
pants’ behavior), etc., could also bias the measure of TTM (Liu & Hau, 
2020; Sugden, Thomas, & Kiernan, 2022; Vangsness, Voss, Maddox, 
Devereaux, & Martin, 2022). On the other hand, RTE overcomes the 
limitations of social desirability or effortless responding in self-report 
methods, but assumptions about the distribution of data for effortful 
answering behaviors and rapid guessing behaviors are sometimes 
imprecisely defined; the distribution of effortful and effortless behaviors 
usually overlap, leading to difficulties in determining thresholds (Nagy, 
Ulitzsch, & Lindner, 2022; Wise, 2020; Wise & Kuhfeld, 2021). These 
limitations necessitate a new approach to capture authentic, in-the- 
moment testing behaviors.
1.3. Process data and test-taking motivation from situational perspective
Some researchers have proposed assessing motivation as a state-like 
construct through a situational perspective, with empirical evidence 
showing that motivational changes can be detected during the testing 
process (Knogler, Harackiewicz, Gegenfurtner, & Lewalter, 2015; 
Welling, Gnambs, & Carstensen, 2024). For example, the later devel­
oped Expectancy-Value theory—the Situated Expectancy Value Theory 
(Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), stresses the importance of situational factors 
(e.g., students’ effort decreased towards the end of the testing sessions) 
on an individual’s motivation (Michael & Kyriakides, 2023; Pools & 
Monseur, 2021). Other theoretical assumptions, e.g., situational interest 
(Horst, 2010; Knekta & Ekl¨of, 2015; Penk & Schipolowski, 2015; Ren­
ninger & Hidi, 2019) and embedded interest (OECD, 2019a), suggest 
that motivation is not static and may fluctuate even within the same 
testing situation or throughout a single test (Horst, 2010; Pastor, Ong, & 
Strickman, 2019; Perkins, Pastor, & Finney, 2021; Schiel, 1996).
The measurement of state-like characteristics is an important 
concern. While the experience sampling method, which involves mul­
tiple and repeated questionnaires, can capture individual states during 
specific activities (e.g., Csikszentmihalyi & Hunter, 2003), it is complex 
and often lacks economic validity. Recent research has highlighted the 
value of process data—a structured, time-stamped record of an in­
dividual’s interactions with a task or system during an assessment, 
experiment, or digital activity (Bergner & von Davier, 2019; Han & 
Wilson, 2022; Ivanova & Michaelides, 2023; Welling et al., 2024). This 
type of data captures detailed behavioral traces that reflect the dynamic 
cognitive and behavioral processes underlying task performance, 
providing various modalities and a large number of data points, such as 
response time, number of actions, instances of trial-and-error, and action 
processes (Akyol, Krishna, & Wang, 2021; Bergner & von Davier, 2019). 
Analyzing process data is beneficial for evaluating individuals’ higher- 
order cognitive skills, including problem-solving, critical thinking, and 
creative thinking (Bergner & von Davier, 2019; Han & Wilson, 2022; 
Liu, Liu, & Li, 2018).
It is reasonable to infer that process data could also be associated 
with test-taking efforts (Akyol et al., 2021; Zamarro, Hitt, & Mendez, 
2019). For example, as response time could build individual disposi­
tional TTM, it could also be differentiated into time to last visit and time to 
first action. The time to last visit is the duration from when a student first 
interacts with an item to their final action on it, which is positively 
related to the level of effort (Scherer, Greiff, & Hautam¨aki, 2015) or has 
a nonlinear relationship (Greiff, Niepel, Scherer, & Martin, 2016). The 
time to first action is the time from the start of presenting an item to an 
individual’s first action, which may capture deep thinking and is often 
positively correlated with effort (Lundgren & Ekl¨of, 2020). In addition, 
the number of actions represents the individual’s interaction with a 
specific item, reflecting their engagement behavior in the task. A high 
frequency of actions reflects goal-directed behavior, whereas a low 
frequency indicates negative behavioral engagement (Greiff et al., 2016; 
Ivanova et al., 2020). Furthermore, missingness is another important 
aspect of the test-taking effort, as the random omission or out-of-range 
responses signal a lack of effort (Liu, Li, Liu, & Luo, 2019). Liu and 
Hau (2020) proposed the “participant-own-defined” approach, which 
adopts user missingness, such as a student intentionally skipping an item 
or randomly selecting an answer, as a direct measure of (the lack of) test- 
taking motivation.
The combination of the aforementioned process data can effectively 
identify low-effort students. Akyol et al. (2021) utilized various types of 
missingness and response time to distinguish non-serious students 
within the PISA database. They noted that when students have ample 
time but still skip items, this behavior indicates a lack of effort. Such 
non-serious patterns tend to emerge at the end of the test clusters (the 
PISA test consists of four clusters, each lasting 30 min, with a 5-min 
break after the second cluster), where both the response time and test 
performance of those non-serious students are significantly lower

(Sahin & Colvin, 2020).
1.4. The influence of culture on test-taking effort
Cross-cultural studies have extensively demonstrated that cultural 
dimensions, such as individualism and power distance (Hofstede, 2011), 
influence the relationships between motivational constructs and aca­
demic outcomes. For example, research indicates that the connection 
between self-efficacy and achievement is stronger in individualistic 
cultures (e.g., the U.S.), compared to collectivistic societies (e.g., China; 
Klassen, 2004; Oettingen, Marquardt, & Gollwitzer, 2012). Similarly, 
the effect of achievement goals on performance varies across cultures; 
mastery goals are more predictive of performance in egalitarian cul­
tures, while performance goals are more relevant in hierarchical con­
texts (Zusho & Clayton, 2011). In collectivist cultures, individuals may 
explain their behaviors with statements like, "I did that because I should, 
because it is what people do, or because it was the right or proper thing 
to do" (Markus, 2016, p. 162). In these contexts, students are more likely 
to accept and endorse extrinsic or instrumental values, often feeling 
motivated when choices are made for them by trusted authority figures 
(Hagger et al., 2014; Markus & Kitayama, 2010). This cultural gap re­
mains evident despite PISA data highlighting significant cross-national 
differences in effort metrics, such as survey disengagement rates 
(Zamarro et al., 2019). Confucian students often demonstrate high 
persistence, showing less disengagement and exhibiting more effortful 
behaviors (Liu & Hau, 2020).
1.5. The present study
In the discussion above, a key consideration is that the traditional 
methods for assessing motivation, whether through self-reporting or 
response time techniques, have their limitations. While previous studies 
have identified associations between process data indicators and test- 
taking effort, they rarely develop a psychological construct based on 
these indicators but rather relate them directly to existing measures of 
motivation or achievement. Moreover, individual behavior and under­
lying psychological constructs do not always align perfectly; the latter 
can manifest as various behavioral outcomes in different situations. This 
means that a single or limited indicator cannot capture test-taking 
motivation (Ivanova et al., 2020; Noroozi et al., 2020; Penk et al., 
2014; Wolgast, Schmidt, & Ranger, 2020). Consequently, we propose 
that a new method for evaluating TTM is necessary. How can we assess 
TTM using multi-modal process data? Is this new assessment valid? 
What is the relationship between TTM and academic performance?
To address these research questions, we propose a concept called 
“behavioral effort,” which refers to an individual’s involvement in 
problem-solving activities based on their response processes. This 
concept includes four indicators: time to last visit (response time), time 
to first action, number of actions taken, and user missingness. Together, 
these indicators can comprehensively explain test-taking motivation. By 
extracting latent classes from the multi-modal process data, we can 
identify effortful versus non-effortful individuals. We hypothesize that 
this new concept of behavioral effort will more effectively reflect TTM. 
Since it is derived from multi-modal process data, it is expected to 
provide a stronger interpretation of TTM and better predict academic 
performance compared to traditional measures. Additionally, we are 
interested in exploring cultural differences, so we will also examine the 
cultural effects on this relationship.
2. Methods
2.1. Data
The data were obtained from the PISA 2018 student cognitive items 
in the science field (https://www.oecd.org/pisa/data/2018database/). 
PISA assesses the ability of 15-year-olds to apply their knowledge and 
skills in reading, mathematics, and science to tackle real-life challenges. 
In the present study, we excluded adaptive testing items (i.e., reading 
literacy) to maintain consistency among items responded to by different 
students. Additionally, the number of test items in the mathematics 
domain was relatively smaller compared to the science domain. 
Consequently, we finally selected two clusters from the science domain, 
Form 13, which contain a larger number of items and provide more 
comprehensive process data. The selected clusters (S01 and S02) 
comprise a total of 38 items, consisting of 25 multiple-choice (MC) items 
and 13 constructed-response (CR) items. For detailed information, 
please refer to the PISA 2018 Technical Report (OECD, 2019b).
Representative students from Confucian and Western cultures were 
selected from all countries and regions that completed both S01 and S02, 
with 1067 students from China (including Beijing, Shanghai, Jiangsu, 
and Zhejiang), Singapore, South Korea, and Japan representing the 
Confucian culture and 1820 students from the United Kingdom, the 
United States, Canada, and Australia representing the Western culture 
(Kiplinger & Linn, 1995). After deleting data for 28 students who were 
missing all the answers to the target items or whose total reaction time 
exceeded the normal range (3600 s), data for 2853 students remained. 
Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for all variables.
2.2. Measurement indicators
2.2.1. Behavioral effort and its process data indicators
As defined earlier, we developed the “behavioral effort” as a new 
way of assessing TTM. This concept is extracted from the PISA 2018 log 
file, where we recorded four types of process data related to test-taking 
effort (the extracting method is shown in the “Data Analyses” section). 
These types of process data are time to last visit, time to first action, 
number of actions, and user missingness. (i) Time to last visit indicates 
the time in seconds from the beginning of presenting an item to the last 
visit to the item; (ii) Time to first action indicates the time from the 
beginning of an item to the first action (in second units); (iii) Number of 
actions indicates the number of actions that students performed in 
interacting with a particular item; (iv) User missingness indicates the 
number of invalid missing (out-of-range responses) and no response 
missing (random blanks in the middle of the test) during the response 
process (see Liu and Hau (2020)‘s definition). In particular, since the 
item format affects the values of the above indicators, we separated MC 
and CR items. This resulted in four types of process data for MC items 
and four types of process data for CR items, respectively. These eight 
types of process data (indicators) were standardized before subsequent 
analyses.
2.2.2. Self-reported measure
To validate the effectiveness of behavioral effort, we selected both 
subjective and objective measures as the criteria. As for the subjective 
measure, we used self-reported effort and achievement motivation. Self- 
reported effort in PISA 2018 includes one self-reported item, where 
students rate the effort they expended on the PISA test on a scale of 1 to 
10. The item was “How much effort did you put into this test?”. Higher 
scores indicate greater effort.
The achievement motivation referred to the degree to which a stu­
dent wanted to perform well in school work in terms of the approach- 
oriented achievement motivation (Elliot & McGregor, 2001; Mur­
ayama & Elliot, 2012). In the PISA framework, achievement motivation 
encompasses two dimensions: competitiveness and work mastery, where 
competitiveness (the dispositional desire to outperform others) consists

2.2.4. Science academic performance
Academic performance is indicated by several professional qualities 
in a specific area. The PISA 2018 science test measures “an individual’s 
ability to explain phenomena scientifically, to evaluate and design sci­
entific investigations, and to interpret data and evidence scientifically” 
(OECD, 2019a). The PISA summarizes achievement scores using a latent 
regression two-parameter model with a mean of 500 and a standard 
deviation of 100, and results in 10 plausible scores (using OECD coun­
tries and territories as a constant model, see OECD, 2019a for details). In 
the present study, we chose the first plausible score; detailed imputed 
results from 10 plausible scores are shown in the online Supplemental 
Materials (see Tables S1-S3).
2.2.5. Controlling variables and cultural differences
Background information was controlled for as in previous research 
(Cheryan, Master, & Meltzoff, 2015; Nagengast et al., 2011). We 
included socioeconomic status (SES, standardized scores, see OECD, 
2019a for details) and gender (coded as 0 for males and 1 for females). 
As achievement motivation is related to dispositional motivation com­
ponents rather than the state-like motivation, competitiveness and work 
mastery were also controlled in the following analyses.
Previous research has confirmed differences in the relationship be­
tween test-taking motivation and academic performance across cultures 
(Liu & Hau, 2020; Marsh et al., 2021; Nagengast & Marsh, 2012; Silm 
et al., 2020), so culture (coded as 0 for Western and 1 for Confucian) was 
additionally included as a moderator variable to be analyzed in the 
model to differentiate motivational effects across cultures.

2.3.1. K-means clustering
In the present study, K-means was used to obtain groups of different 
behavioral effort levels, mainly because K-means is effective in mining 
the behavioral patterns of individuals on computerized tests 
(Goldhammer, Martens, & Lüdtke, 2017; He, Liao, & Jiao, 2019). Before 
clustering, Yeo-Johnson power transformation was used to transform 
the eight process data to improve the normality (Yeo & Johnson, 2000), 
and then the variables were standardized (G´eron, 2019).
Subsequently, the number of groups was determined based on two 
indicators: the elbow rule and the silhouette coefficient (Sugar, 1998). In 
the elbow plot, the optimal number of clusters is typically identified at 
the point where the rate of significant decrease in within-cluster sum of 
squares begins to slow down, corresponding to the “elbow” in the graph. 
The K value prior to this point is usually considered the optimal number 
of clusters, as it represents a balance between model complexity and 
explanatory power. The silhouette coefficient ranged from −1 to 1, the 
value closer to 1 indicates better clustering quality.
2.3.2. Multilevel regression model
In the PISA sampling design, students are nested within different 
schools across various countries, which necessitated the use of multi­
level modeling (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). The intra-class correlation 
coefficients (ICCs) for the null model were 0.317 at the school level and 
0.098 at the national level. This indicates the importance of accounting 
for variations among schools and countries. Given the limited sample 
size at the country level (Level 3), which consists of only eight units, we 
analyzed the all variable at the individual level (Level 1) to obtain more 
accurate estimates. We only controlled for higher-level unit differences, 
resulting in a null model at both Levels 2 and Level 3. The conditional 
model is defined as.

represents the constant, which is decomposed to the group mean β000,

representativeness of our estimates, we incorporated the sampling 
weights provided by the PISA datasets into all multilevel analyses.
Since PISA data has an inherently large sample size, effect sizes for 
the multilevel model were calculated using d = 2β × SD/σ (Cohen’s d, 
Melhuish et al., 2008), where β is the parameter estimate, SD is the 
standard deviation, and σ is the individual-level standard deviation in 
the null model. A commonly used interpretation is to refer to effect sizes 
as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d = 0.8) based on 
benchmarks suggested by Cohen (1988); however, in educational set­
tings, the average effect size (Cohen’s d) in meta-analysis of motiva­
tional factors and engagement is around 0.43–0.48 (Hattie, 2009).
In particular, to compare the empirical prediction effects, the present 
study established three multilevel regression models with behavioral 
effort (Model 1), self-reported effort (Model 2), and RTE (Model 3), 
respectively. Continuous variables were standardized before being 
entered into the model.
2.3.3. Statistical software
The data cleaning, preprocessing, and variable creation for this study 
were conducted using R version 4.1.0 (R Core Team, 2021). K-means 
clustering was performed utilizing the built-in function from the R 
package (Hartigan & Wong, 1979). Descriptive statistics, correlation 
analysis, and t-tests were carried out using SPSS version 20.0. Addi­
tionally, multilevel regression analysis was performed with Mplus 
version 8.11.
3. Results
3.1. Behavioral effort characteristics
Correlations between process data and the related variables are 
shown in Table 1. The results showed that self-reported effort was 
significantly negatively correlated with user missingness (rMC = −0.14, 
p < 0.05; rCR = −0.25, p < 0.01) and was significantly positively 
correlated with the time to last visit (rCR = 0.21, p < 0.01) but was not 
significantly related to the time to first action or the number of opera­
tions. Response time effort was significantly positively correlated with 
time to last visit, time to first action, and number of operations, ranging 
from 0.17 to 0.38 (p < 0.01), and was significantly negatively correlated 
with user missingness (rMC = −0.44, p < 0.01; rCR = −0.58, p < 0.01) but 
was not significantly related to the time to first action in MC.
The results showed that academic performance was significantly 
positively correlated with the time to last visit (rMC = 0.25, p < 0.05; rCR 
= 0.47, p < 0.01), time to first action (rCR = 0.26, p < 0.01), the number 
of actions (rMC = 0.13, p < 0.05), and was significantly negatively 
correlated with user missingness (rMC = −0.31, p < 0.01; 0.47, rCR =
−0.47, p < 0.01). Academic performance showed higher correlation 
coefficients with all constructed-response item indicators than with 
multiple-choice item indicators, except for the number of actions.
The elbow rule showed no obvious cutoff point in the graph and 
suggested the presence of 2 or 3 groups (see Fig. 1). The silhouette co­
efficients suggested 2 categories. We finally chose 2 groups as the best 
solution.
The first group, defined as the low effort group (1013, 35.5 %), was 
characterized by a shorter time to last visit, shorter time to first action, 
fewer number of actions, and more user missingness, reflecting low test- 
taking motivation. The second group is thus defined as the high effort 
group (1840, 64.5 %), was characterized by a longer time to last visit, 
longer time to first action, more actions, and less user missingness, 
indicating that individuals consumed more cognitive resources and 
spent more time thinking about the item (Table 2). Regarding the 
number of actions, the low-effort group showed marginally higher 
values than the high-effort group for multiple-choice items, but 
demonstrated substantially lower values for constructed-response items.

The t-test and Cohen’s d results also showed significant differences be­

3.2. Relationship of behavioral effort to self-reported measure and 
response time effort
As for the extracted groups, behavioral effort was positively corre­
lated with self-reported effort and response time effort with a correlation 
coefficient of 0.132 and 0.326, respectively. At the same time, 
competitiveness and work mastery as two dimensions of achievement 
motivation were significantly but small positive correlated with 
behavioral effort, with a correlation coefficient of 0.044 and 0.063, 
respectively. To verify the criterion validity, t-tests were conducted for 
self-reported effort, competitiveness, work mastery, and response time 
effort. The results showed that the self-reported effort of the low effort 
group (M = 7.53, SD = 2.24) was significantly lower than that of the 
high effort group with a small effect (M = 8.05, SD = 1.57; t(2851) =
−6.21, p < 0.001, d = 0.27); the response time effort of the low effort 
group (M = 0.93, SD = 0.12) was also significantly lower than that of the 
high effort group with a large effect (M = 0.98, SD = 0.04; t(2523) =
−14.25, p < 0.001, d = 0.62); competitiveness of low effort group (M =
0.08, SD = 1.07) was significantly lower than that of the high effort 
group (M = 0.17, SD = 0.93; t(2679) = −2.30, p < 0.05, d = 0.09); work 
mastery of low effort group (M = 0.01, SD = 1.05) was significantly 
lower than that of the high effort group (M = 0.14, SD = 0.94; t(2667) =
−3.257, p < 0.001, d = 0.06) (Table 2). Results indicate the effectiveness 
of the behavioral effort measure; the effect size, when compared to the 
RTE measure, showed the greatest, while for achievement motivation, 
the lowest.
3.3. The effect of effort on academic performance and its cultural 
differences
After controlling for background variables, behavioral effort, self- 
reported effort, and response time effort were all found to significantly 
and positively predict science academic performance, of which behav­
ioral effort had the largest influence indicating a large effect (Est. behavior 
= 87.338, p < 0.001, d = 1.083; Est. SR = 17.604, p < 0.001, d = 0.448; 
Est. RT = 32.449, p < 0.001, d = 0.773) (see Table 3). In addition, we 
found that the predictive effects of behavioral effort, self-reported effort, 
and response time effort on academic performance were moderated by 
cultural variables (Est. Behavior = −31.565, p < 0.05; Est. SR = −14.768, p 
< 0.05; Est. RT = −10.775, p = 0.083). The cultural interactive effect 
showed that for Western culture, TTM would increase performance with 
a larger effect than that in Confucian culture (Fig. 2).
4. Discussion
The main purpose of the present study was to use process data to 
assess test-taking motivation and to explore the relationship between 
test-taking motivation and academic performance. The research results 
showed that this method has good validity and could promote academic 
performance.
4.1. Effectiveness of the process data
As validated by self-reported measures and RTE, behavioral effort, 
extracted from the eight process data, has moderate-level criteria val­
idity. The combination of eight indicators would be reasonable since 
they all have associations with the criterion. Among the process data, 
user missingness had the largest correlation with motivation measures, 
indicating that the lower the level of missingness is, the higher the test- 
taking motivation is, which has been confirmed by previous research 
(Liu & Hau, 2020). This association was even larger when correlated 
with RTE than for self-reported effort. The time to last visit was the next 
strongest; the time to first action and number of actions were the 
weakest. These results show that user missingness and time to last visit 
are core indicators and play a key role in measuring test-taking 
motivation.
In addition, time to first action and number of actions, while not as 
directly related to RTE as the first two indicators, also provide additional 
information in measuring test-taking motivation. For example, time to 
first action reflects thinking time, including reading comprehension, 
information processing, and answer formulation, which indicates high- 
quality solution behavior (Bergner & Von Davier, 2019; Jiao & Lissitz, 
2018). The number of actions could also reflect rapid guessing behavior 
and would be useful if combined with response time (Sahin & Colvin, 
2020). The lower relevance of these indicators may partly be due to their 
nonlinear relationship with test-taking motivation, where there may be 
an “optimal behavioral point”, before which they are positively corre­
lated with test-taking motivation and after which the correlation be­
comes negative (Goldhammer et al., 2017; Greiff et al., 2016; Greiff, 
Wüstenberg, & Avvisati, 2015; Zhang, Wan, Lyu, Li, & Liu, 2021). The 
above results also provide an important foundation and evidence for 
further establishing a complete test-taking motivation assessment sys­
tem and assigning weight to behavioral effort.
Finally, the behavioral effort correlates low with competitiveness 
and work mastery, which are measures of achievement motivation dis­
positions (Elliot & McGregor, 2001; Murayama & Elliot, 2012). Such a 
low correlation probably indicates that the construct extracted from 
process data assesses somewhat different components from those the 
traditional dispositional assessment. Although studies have shown that 
test-taking effort and engagement can be considered as reflections of 
examinee motivation (e.g., Ivanova et al., 2020), there is a discrepancy 
between the measures of “effort” and “motivation”. Such a discrepancy 
shows the different focus of dispositional measures and situational 
measures.

4.2. The impact of test-taking motivation on academic performance
Test-taking motivation is significantly associated with academic 
performance outcomes. Results indicate that behavioral effort is a strong 
predictor of academic performance. Students with high test-taking 
motivation tend to take exams more seriously, exert greater effort, 
engage in more problem-solving behaviors, and ultimately achieve 
better academic results. These students are less likely to guess quickly, 
skip items, or provide superficial responses (Ulitzsch, von Davier, & 
Pohl, 2020; Wise & Gao, 2017; Wise & Kong, 2005). Furthermore, 
behavioral effort has a larger effect size (much larger than the d = 0.48 
found in meta-analyses) in predicting academic performance. In com­
parison, response time effort has a medium effect size, while self- 
reported effort has the smallest one. These results suggest that behav­
ioral effort is a more direct measure of an individual’s implicit psycho­
logical traits, as interactions with testing items happen naturally and 
unconsciously. Additionally, behavioral effort provides a wider range of 
clues about performance than self-reported effort and response time 
effort. Among the various indicators associated with academic perfor­
mance, constructed items show a stronger correlation with outcomes 
than multiple-choice items. This may indicate that individuals need to 
invest more cognitive and behavioral resources to interact with con­
structed items, such as typing in answers rather than simply clicking 
buttons to select an option.
The study revealed that cultural factors moderate the relationship 
between TTM and academic performance. The results indicated that 
Western students may derive more benefits from higher levels of TTM 
compared to their peers from Confucian cultures. This difference could 
stem from the observation that Confucian students often demonstrate a 
higher baseline level of TTM in assessments; their performance remains 
at a high level regardless of the strength of their motivation level. This 
phenomenon is likely connected to cultural values that emphasize 
obedience to authority figures (Ho & Hau, 2008; McInerney & King, 
2017). Students are likely to obey the requirements of their teachers and 
parents, thus perform well whether the test is of high-stakes or low- 
stakes. However, the interaction term was not significant in the RTE 
model (Model 3), suggesting that cultural influences may operate 
differently depending on how effort is measured. Since the level of RTE 
is relatively high (M = 0.96; SD = 0.08, see Table 1), its skewed distri­
bution may undermine the effect.
5. Limitations and future directions
The present study provides a new assessment approach for 
measuring test-taking motivation from “behavioral effort”. However, 
since different behavioral effort indicators correlate with test-taking 
motivation and academic performance differently, future research can 
further identify the specific relationship between each key process data 
and behavioral effort and quantify the behavioral effort. For example, 
studies could determine the weight of each indicator, the linear or 
nonlinear relationship for each indicator, and how to transform the 
grouping effort variable into a continuous latent variable. The personal 
fit index, the total person correlation coefficient, and extracting ap­
proaches such as machine learning can be considered to quantify TTM 
(Man, Harring, & Sinharay, 2019).
Second, since the process data recorded in our study are based on the 
PISA data, there should be other types of process data that have not been 
considered. Other process data indicators, such as visual fixation points, 
eye-tracking information, and thinking-aloud information, can also be 
incorporated into the behavioral effort model as an extension of this 
study to develop more efficient and closer process indicators (Azevedo, 
2015; Zhan, Man, Wind, & Malone, 2022). Meanwhile, other charac­
teristics of items, such as the number of response options, length of the 
item, position within the test, and even whether an item has a corre­
sponding graph, figure, or image, are not controlled due to the avail­
ability of the original items. Future research could consider more 
characteristics from the item to the individual and even to the situa­
tional context. Furthermore, since the validation of the present study 
only focuses on the provided information from the PISA 2018, other 
criteria would be included to enrich the results and their application, 
such as students’ GPA, educational/career aspirations, future occupa­
tions, work performance, etc.
