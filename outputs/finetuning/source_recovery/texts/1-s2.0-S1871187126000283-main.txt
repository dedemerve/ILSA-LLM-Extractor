Computer-based assessments can capture data on students’ response processes when completing
creative thinking tasks, which helps explore their varying levels of task engagement and disengagement. The large amount of contextual information collected by large-scale assessments also
contributes to examining the background factors related to it. Therefore, this study aims to
identify students’ distinct engagement and disengagement patterns in multiple creative tasks and
the most important factors to predict task engagement and disengagement. Based on the process
data from the creative thinking test in the PISA 2022 data of Canada (n = 6,794), Germany (n =
1,692), and Hong Kong (n = 1,661), we identified six engagement patterns (i.e., insightful innovators, divergent explorers, elaborative strategists, disfluent performers, hasty attempters, and
passive learners) and three disengagement behaviors (i.e., rapid, relatively rapid, and nonresponding). Patterns were summarized across all creative tasks. It was found that students
tend to adopt the strategy of taking fewer actions in a short period of time to generate solutions
for most creative tasks. To predict engagement and disengagement, an XGBoost classifier optimized with SMOTE and class weighting was employed. The model demonstrated robust predictive performance across three economies. SHAP analysis identified the top 10 predictors, which
were predominantly cognitive and academic factors. By translating behavioral logs into interpretable profiles and transparent prediction rules, this study moves beyond a “black box” to offer
actionable, evidence-based insights for fostering engagement in creativity education.

1. Introduction
Creative thinking is a higher-order thinking skill that involves complex thinking processes of generating innovative ideas or solutions to solve problems effectively, and further evaluating ideas and developing new insights from multiple perspectives (Hernandez
Sibo et al., 2024; Lucchiari et al., 2019; Xiong et al., 2022). It contributes to students’ learning, problem-solving, and metacognition
skills, thus promoting their holistic development (OECD, 2023a). Through continuous exploration and discovery in complex thinking
processes, students can be well prepared to face the multifarious problems in the future society (Lucchiari et al., 2019). As one of the
key skills required for the 21st century, creative thinking has long been explored by researchers and practitioners in education (Chen
et al., 2025; Yildiz & Yildiz, 2021). To guide research, pedagogies, and policies on adolescents’ creative thinking, the Programme for

International Student Assessment (PISA) conducted an international large-scale assessment (ILSA) of creative thinking in the 2022
cycle. The computer-based assessment (CBA) can capture information on students’ response processes when completing creative
thinking tasks. This massive amount of data enables researchers to better explore test takers’ behaviors, for instance, the engagement
patterns of students in processing multiple creative thinking tasks, rather than just focusing on the final scores.
Based on process data, previous studies have mostly focused on examining test-taking behaviors such as distinguishing between
students’ engaged and disengaged behaviors in completing tasks (e.g., Sahin & Colvin, 2020), response-time effort (e.g., Michaelides
et al., 2020), and action sequences (e.g., He et al., 2021). However, identifying disengaged behaviors through isolated tasks is
insufficient for understanding students’ creative thinking in multiple tasks, as item and individual differences may cause changes in the
extent of engagement (Wang et al., 2023). The exploration of test-taken behaviors from the perspective of assessment can indeed
provide insightful views on students’ engagement in completing creative thinking tasks. A more fine-grained exploration of
engagement patterns beyond the binary difference between engagement and disengagement is also needed to help us understand how
different students put effort into processing multiple tasks. Furthermore, although some research has investigated how contextual
factors relate to educational outcomes, it remains to be explored what factors would affect students’ engagement in creative tasks.
The rapid development of educational data mining (EDM) and machine learning (ML) has provided a novel methodological
perspective for mining ILSA data. Given the widespread application and remarkable performance of ML in data mining, integrating ML
in EDM offers great potential for generating valuable insights, ultimately supporting evidence-based policy decisions in line with
EDM’s core mission (Alvarez-Garcia et al., 2024). Despite its popularity and immense potential, there is a growing appeal to uncover
the “black box” of ML models, that is, to make the inner workings of models more transparent and explainable to stakeholders.
Consequently, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), a rising field for ensuring transparency and interpretability of results, has been
introduced to EDM (Alvarez-Garcia et al., 2024; Guleria & Sood, 2023; Livieris et al., 2023). Leveraging XAI techniques, this study
bridges the identified research gaps through the application of both supervised and unsupervised learning methods. Specifically, the
study focuses on the following research questions:
1) What engagement patterns can be identified from multiple creative tasks?
To address Research Question 1, we first distinguish disengagement from engagement patterns. We also expect to find more varied
engagement patterns based on the process data that reflects response time and action effort.
2) What are students’ most common engagement and disengagement patterns when completing multiple creative tasks?
With Research Question 2, we investigate students’ most common engagement patterns in multiple tasks instead of identifying their
behavioral patterns in single or several isolated tasks. It has the potential to avoid the randomness of engagement patterns and
behavioral characteristics that depend on specific tasks. Moreover, the changes in their engagement patterns in different tasks can be
explored. As the level of student engagement may vary across different tasks, students may exhibit multiple common engagement
patterns.
3) What are the most important individual-level predictors for students’ engagement and disengagement in creative tasks?
This study employs supervised learning, i.e., building predictive models, to explore the most important factors that distinguish
students’ engagement and disengagement in creative tasks. By doing so, accurate interpretations can be provided to different stakeholders, enabling effective decision-making in cultivating students’ creative thinking.
2. Literature review
2.1. Creative thinking
The conceptualization of creative thinking reflects its multidimensional nature, with different theoretical models highlighting
particular aspects. But overall, it refers to an individual’s ability to generate new concepts or solutions (De Bono, 1970). Creativity was
typically categorized into two distinct types: “big-C” creativity and “little-c” creativity (Csikszentmihalyi, 2013; Simonton, 2013). The
former focuses on creative outcomes that require expertise in a given context. While the latter refers to the general creativity that all
individuals can exhibit in daily life (e.g., arranging photos, decorating rooms). Unlike the eminent outcomes in “big-C”, this everyday
creativity can be systematically developed and honed through sustained practice and education (Kaufman & Beghetto, 2009). According to Guilford (1957), creativity stems from the interplay of two cognitive abilities: divergent thinking and convergent thinking.
Divergent thinking requires an individual to be capable of generating diverse and creative ideas (Cropley, 2006), while convergent
thinking involves the process of evaluating ideas and making decisions on the best solution (Reiter-Palmon & Robinson, 2009). Both
thinking skills are widely recognized as critical to creative thinking. Research on creative thinking has been devoted to investigating its
conceptualization (Hadar & Tirosh, 2019; Runco & Jaeger, 2012), cultivation across disciplines and educational stages
(Jarutkamolpong & Kwangmuang, 2025; Paz-Baruch et al., 2025), possible influential factors (Suherman & Vidákovich, 2024),
assessment and mechanisms through advanced deep learning techniques (Ghosh & Konar, 2024; Ghosh et al., 2020, 2025).
The PISA 2022 cycle defined creative thinking as “the competence to engage productively in the generation, evaluation and
improvement of ideas that can result in original and effective solutions, advances in knowledge and impactful expressions of imagination” (OECD, 2023a). The corresponding cognitive test assesses students’ ability to utilize both divergent and convergent cognitive
processes to generate diverse and creative ideas, evaluate and improve ideas to complete tasks that are set in everyday contexts. Most
tasks in the test require students to generate written or visual artefacts. Thus, to successfully demonstrate proficiency in creative
thinking, students need to exhibit some level of task engagement, i.e., investing time and effort in understanding task prompts and
materials, and elaborating creative outputs in the required format (OECD, 2023b).

2.2. Task engagement and cognitive flexibility
The tripartite model of student engagement posits engagement as a multifaceted construct critical for understanding academic
achievement, motivation, and learning (Fredricks et al., 2004). It conceptualizes engagement as a dynamic state comprising three
distinct, yet interrelated, dimensions, i.e., behavioral, emotional, and cognitive engagement. Behavioral engagement refers to the
observable, outward manifestations of participation in academic or task-related activities. Emotional engagement encompasses students’ affective reactions to their learning context, such as feelings of interest, enjoyment, belonging, and conversely, anxiety or
boredom. Cognitive engagement involves the mental effort and cognitive investment students make in the learning process. Among
three dimensions, behavioral engagement serves as the critical, observable foundation of the engagement construct (Xie et al., 2025).
In cognitive tests, the quality of engagement in tasks serves as the vital requirement for interpreting the validity of test scores (Wise,
2017; Wise & Gao, 2017). However, test-takers may show different extents of engagement and even be disengaged when completing
tasks (Wise & DeMars, 2005). Such heterogeneity can be observed at both the individual and item levels (Wang et al., 2023).
One important aspect of human cognition, cognitive flexibility, has been regarded as closely intertwined with how individuals
engage in creative tasks (Arán-Filippetti & Krumm, 2020; Chen et al., 2014), as it allows individuals to address problems from different
angles, think outside the box, and generate novel solutions. Cognitive flexibility is a core executive function defined as the mental
ability to adaptively switch between different concepts, tasks, or behavioral strategies in response to changing environmental demands, rules, or goals (Diamond, 2013; Ionescu, 2012). It transcends mere shifting and involves the dynamic reconfiguration of
thinking and the inhibition of prior mental sets to generate, evaluate, and apply novel, appropriate approaches (Kloo et al., 2010). It is
not simply being creative but is the cognitive control mechanism that enables creative problem-solving, learning transfer, and resilience in complex, non-routine situations. Thus, cognitive flexibility and creativity share similar cognitive processes (Khalil et al.,
2019). According to the dual pathway to creativity model (Nijstad et al., 2010), creative cognition can be achieved through two
fundamental pathways, flexibility and persistence. The flexibility pathway permits switching fluidly between states of attention and
viewpoints. In contrast, the persistence pathway relies on sustained and focused cognitive effort. Creative outputs arise from the
complex interplays of both flexibility and persistence, processes that are fundamentally associated with cognitive flexibility
(Beversdorf, 2019).
The heterogeneity of behavioral engagement could be observed through the extent of students actively participating in tasks. In
CBAs, the wealth of available process data allows for its sophisticated operationalization. Its true explanatory power can be unlocked
when it is analyzed not as an end in itself, but as the behavioral trace of underlying motivational and cognitive processes demanded by
the task itself, guiding researchers and educators to discern productive effort from mere activity. Furthermore, cognitive flexibility is
intrinsically linked to the quality and sustainability of task engagement, particularly in complex, ill-structured, or creative tasks
(Ionescu, 2012). In a sequence of creative tasks, where demands, problems, and success criteria may evolve, it is the linchpin of
adaptive engagement. Cognitive flexibility theory helps interpret why some learners maintain high-quality engagement across tasks
while others frequently disengage.
2.3. Process data for identifying task engagement and disengagement
In CBAs, rich process data can be stored and generated, including time-related data (e.g., total time, time to first action) and actionrelated data (e.g., frequency of actions, action sequences). These data allow researchers to examine students’ test-taking processes,
moving beyond traditional outcome-based accuracy measures (Wise, 2017). Especially in open, constructed items used to assess
higher-order competencies, process data has enormous potential to improve test design and construct validity (Sireci & Zenisky, 2006;
Zumbo et al., 2023) and dig into how test-takers engage in complex tasks (Goldhammer et al., 2016; Wise & Gao, 2017).
Over the past two decades, one key research focus has been detecting disengaged or non-effortful test-taking behaviors using
process data, specifically, identifying unmotivated test-takers who may disengage during an assessment and respond to items with
minimal effort (Wise & Gao, 2017; Wise, 2020). Some studies have examined several types of disengaged behaviors and methods for
detecting them. In a test event, disengaged test-takers would rarely engage in the items and thus may omit the items in a very short time
(rapid omitting), provide quick unrelated keypresses (rapid perfunctory answer), or quickly enter responses in hopes of getting answers correct by chance (rapid guessing) (Sahin & Colvin, 2020; Wise & Gao, 2017). Due to different types of test items, some variants
of these disengaged behaviors were also studied. For instance, rapid guessing was more suitable for closed-ended items, as in
open-ended and constructed items, test-takers could not make rapid guess. The PISA creative thinking test requires students to generate
creative outputs. Such a constructed-response format inherently prevented students from employing rapid guessing strategies to
generate correct answers through chance. Therefore, PISA identified task disengagement with three indicators, including rapid
responding (i.e., rapidly moving through the item without giving a valid response), relative rapid responding (i.e., rapid responding
relative to other students), and non-responding (i.e., not submitting a valid response after spending any length of time) (OECD, 2024).
These indicators were identified based on responses and processing time. Processing time, or the time spent on individual test items, is
a common and promising measure for detecting disengagement. The underlying assumption is that engaged test-takers invest a
baseline amount of time (e.g., to read instructions and process the stimulus) before responding, whereas disengaged test-taking
behavior means taking less time or guessing rapidly (Goldhammer et al., 2016). Thus, response time has been used to identify
diverse thresholds to separate engaged and disengaged test-taking behaviors at the level of individual items, such as, visual inspection
method (Schnipke, 1995), visual inspection with response accuracy (Lee & Jia, 2014), and P + > 0% (Goldhammer et al., 2016). For
instance, the P + > 0% method was proposed to detect task disengagement by examining the shortest response time associated with a
proportion correct (P +) greater than zero, based on the problem-solving tasks in PIAAC (Programme for the International Assessment

Research on the detection of task disengagement has achieved certain significant results. However, fine-grained exploration of
engagement in multiple tasks is still limited. Regarding task engagement, based on process data, several studies examined test-takers’
behavioral patterns in completing complex tasks, which delved into the level of task engagement in different subgroups. For example,
using log data from the PISA 2012 cycle, Hahnel et al. (2022) explored students’ different behavioral patterns in several digital reading
tasks. The study identified five clusters based on the indicators from navigation and timing data. Using general log data of the U.S.
PIAAC 2012 problem-solving assessment, Wang et al. (2023) distinguished between engagement and disengagement, and further
explored fine-grained clusters of engaged behavioral patterns in multiple tasks. Unlike the navigation and action sequence data that
belong to specific tasks, general log data, including processing time, orientation time, and action count, can be compared across
different tasks. These indicators also contain rich information about whether test-takers consistently keep motivated and engaged in
multiple tasks (Wang et al., 2023). Furthermore, navigation and action sequence data may contribute little to the exploration of
creative tasks, as these tasks require students to create rather than follow certain effective steps. Therefore, these general log data,
which reflect how students invest time and effort, can be used to mine valuable information about student engagement in creative
tasks.
2.4. Key factors affecting creative thinking
Creative thinking, characterized by its multidimensional and complex nature, is influenced by diverse individual, cognitive, and
environmental factors (Vincent et al., 2002; Hernandez Sibo et al., 2024). Personality traits significantly affected creative achievement. For instance, the Big Five trait openness to experience correlated with creativity across domains (Kaufman et al., 2016; Xu et al.,
2021). Intrinsic motivation and creative self-efficacy would enable sustained engagement in creative work (Cromwell et al., 2023;
Hallak et al., 2018). Some studies also have reported associations between cognitive abilities and creativity, such as fluid intelligence
(Frith et al., 2021), working memory (Beaty & Silvia, 2012), and metacognitive skills (particularly evaluative monitoring of ideas)
(Urban & Urban, 2024). Regarding socio-environmental aspects, factors such as supportive climates and cultural values were also
contributing to creative attitudes and behaviors (Hoffmann et al., 2025; Li & Li, 2025).
In PISA 2022, apart from three core domains (reading, mathematics, science), the innovative domain assessed was adolescents’
creative thinking. To better understand this concept and guide the collection of contextual information, PISA 2022 conceptualized
three interconnected dimensions, i.e., internal resources (e.g., knowledge, skills, attitudes), external factors (e.g., cultural norms and
expectations), and creative engagement (e.g., creative expression), that support students’ creative thinking in the classroom (OECD,
2023a). Based on creative thinking data, only a few studies have been conducted to investigate the influential factors of students’
performance in the creative thinking test. For example, in Zeng (2025), several non-cognitive factors, including growth mindsets, ICT
self-efficacy, creative environments, ICT availability, and ICT information practices, were found to be significantly correlated with
creative self-efficacy and creative thinking. The exploration of the possible influential factors for predicting creative thinking performance from a large amount of contextual factors is still scarce, especially for student engagement in completing creative tasks.
Therefore, one research question of this study is to build predictive models to predict student engagement and disengagement in
creative tasks based on students’ background information collected in PISA 2022.
2.5. Explainability
In response to the challenges of lacking transparency due to the black-box nature of complex models, explainability has emerged as
a critical element in bridging the gap between model performance and human understanding when employing AI algorithms (Minh
et al., 2022). XAI approaches have been used in various domains to facilitate human decision-making in the EDM field (e.g., Alvarez-Garcia et al., 2024; Kruger et al., 2023). For instance, Kruger et al. (2023) applied ML algorithms to predict students’ dropout and
techniques of model explainability (e.g., result visualization and SHAP) to understand why students may drop out. Using a large
amount of mixed-type contextual data from PISA 2022, Alvarez-Garcia et al. (2024) employed an explainable cluster analysis approach
to uncover complex student profiles. The explainable techniques have exhibited great potential in enhancing the transparency of
models and the interpretability of results. For problems in the educational field where interpretability is more important than predictive accuracy, this transparency builds trust and makes educators more likely to act upon the insights effectively. Thus, in the
present study, using explainable techniques can help understand complex student engagement profiles and the influential contextual
factors of their engagement and disengagement in multiple creative tasks.
3. Methods
From both person-centered and variable-centered perspectives, this study employed ML approaches, including supervised learning
and unsupervised learning algorithms, to identify distinct student engagement patterns and the potential influential factors to predict
engaged and disengaged students.
3.1. Sample
In the PISA 2022 cycle, 15-year-old students from 81 OECD member and partner economies participated in PISA. Among them,
about 28% of the sample students from 64 countries and economies took the creative thinking cognitive test. The creative thinking test

points on the creative thinking test. This study selected students from three economies as research samples, including Canada, Germany, and Hong Kong. The reasons are as follows. First, all three economies are economically developed. Second, the three economies

3.2. Analytical framework
The data used in the current study includes the process data obtained from the PISA 2022 creative thinking test and the contextual
data from the student questionnaire. The process data was used to identify students’ distinct engagement patterns. The contextual data
was used to predict whether students were mostly engaged or disengaged in multiple creative tasks. The overall analytical framework
is presented in Fig. 1.
3.3. Cluster analysis
3.3.1. Data preprocessing for cluster analysis
Four features from process data were extracted for cluster analysis, including 1) scored response, the credits achieved by students
on the item; 2) action count, the number of actions taken by the student in interacting with the item (e.g., clicks, drag/drop events); 3)
orientation time, the interval between item display and the first system-recorded action; 4) processing time, the total time spent on the
item. Data was preprocessed through the following steps. First, valid cases in three economies were extracted. Second, three disengaged behaviors were identified according to PISA’s criteria for task disengagement (OECD, 2024) and the P + > 0% method
(Goldhammer et al., 2016). Rapid responding behaviors indicate that students quickly move on to the next item without submitting a
scoring response within 30 seconds. The identification of relative rapid responders is the time interval between 30 seconds and the
shortest time of the scored answer given in this item (if this time was above 30 seconds). Non-responding behaviors represent that
students do not submit a valid response after spending any time on an item. Third, the remaining data that represents engaged behaviors was transformed into z-scores.
3.3.2. Clustering algorithm
To examine RQ1, task-specific k-means clustering was performed utilizing four features from process data. K-means clustering is a
commonly used unsupervised learning algorithm used to group unlabeled data based on similarity (MacQueen, 1967). The number of
clusters (k) was determined by R package NbClust (Charrad et al., 2014). The NbClust package evaluates 30 clustering validation
indices simultaneously (e.g., silhouette, elbow) and provides the best solution according to the majority rule.
3.3.3. Data summarization
To address RQ2, students’ distinct engagement patterns obtained from cluster analysis and their disengagement patterns were
integrated and summarized based on mode (i.e., the most frequent value in a dataset).
3.4. Predictive analysis
3.4.1. Data preprocessing for predictive analysis
Students were classified into two groups, i.e., engagement and disengagement. Disengaged students were identified if their most
common patterns were all disengaged behaviors. The remaining were identified as engaged students. This binary variable was set as
the target variable in predictive models (1 = disengagement, 0 = engagement). For each economy, the contextual data (see supplementary material, Table A.1) was integrated with the target variable as a new dataset for predictive analysis. The missing data rate of
each variable was shown in Table A.2 (see supplementary material). Variables with a missing data rate greater than 30% were
considered unable to provide information and were deleted. Regarding missing values in other variables, the significant results of
Little’s test for missing completely at random (MCAR) versus missing at random (MAR) indicated that for all three territories, MCAR
was not preferred, and the MAR assumption was reasonable. Therefore, hot deck imputation with KNN (K-Nearest Neighbors) was used
to impute missing values (Andridge & Little, 2010). The algorithm identifies the k most similar complete records (nearest neighbors)
based on the available data. For continuous variables, it can use the mean/median of the k neighbors. For categorical variables, it
typically uses the mode (most frequent value) of the k neighbors. Finally, the number and proportions of engaged and disengaged

3.4.2. ML techniques
In order to select the most valuable features for predicting engaged and disengaged students, this study employed the ML algorithm,
lasso, for feature selection. Then, the classification models were built by the following steps. First, data of each economy was randomly
split into the train set (80%) and test set (20%). Second, to mitigate the extreme class imbalance (engagement ~95% vs. disengagement ~5%), we employed a dual strategy exclusively on the training data: (a) synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE), and (b)
algorithmic class weighting. Third, the optimal hyperparameters (e.g., number of estimators, learning rate, maximum tree depth; see
Supplementary Table A.7) were determined via an exhaustive grid search coupled with 10-fold cross-validation on the training set. The
configuration that maximized the average ROC-AUC score across all validation folds was selected. Finally, this study built XGBoost
models to make predictions. As the class in the dataset is imbalanced, multiple metrics for evaluating the performance of classification
models were considered, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, specificity, and AUC. Especially, recall, F1 score, specificity,
and AUC should be considered as the main evaluation metrics. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) provides a unified framework
for interpreting ML models by assigning each feature an importance value for a given prediction based on cooperative game theory
(Lundberg & Lee, 2017). In this study, the SHAP bar plot, SHAP beeswarm plot, and dependence plot with LOWESS (Locally Weighted
Scatterplot Smoothing) were used for global explainability (i.e., how the model makes decisions across the entire dataset). LOWESS is a
non-parametric regression technique that can be used to explore and display possible non-linear relationships.
4. Results
4.1. Engagement and disengagement patterns
Regarding task disengagement, rapid responding and non-responding behaviors were observed in all tasks across three territories.
The proportion of rapid responding behaviors was below 10.00% in most tasks. The proportion of non-responding behaviors was below
10.00% in all tasks, except for one task, which had a rate of 20.95% in Canada. The relative rapid responding behaviors were only
observed in 5 tasks in Canada, but 17 in Germany, and 15 in Hong Kong. It indicates that for each task, a small portion of students were
unable to provide scored answers within 30 seconds, or even provide valid answers far beyond that time frame. The relative rarity of
rapid responders indicates that some students, especially Canadian students, can provide scored answers within 30 seconds.
Cluster analysis was employed to examine distinct engagement patterns across different creative tasks. Six heterogeneous patterns
of task engagement were uncovered. Not all patterns were found in every task. To statistically validate the distinctness of the
engagement patterns identified within each creative task, we conducted separate one-way MANOVAs for each of the tasks across the
three economies. For each task, the clusters served as the independent variable, with the four process features as dependent variables.
Across all tasks, the multivariate effects were consistently significant (all p < .05 after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons),

confirming that within each task context, the clusters represent statistically distinct behavioral patterns. Post-hoc analyses (Tukey’s
HSD) further confirmed that the clusters differed significantly on all features within each task (all p < .05). This statistical validation
demonstrates that the clustering solution captures robust, separable behavioral patterns. Fig. 2 displays the z-score differences of the
engagement patterns in an example item. Descriptive statistics and proportions of all patterns were provided separately for Canada,
Germany, and Hong Kong (see supplementary material, Table A.3-5). Fig. 3 shows the proportion of students’ engagement and
disengagement patterns in different creative tasks.
The identification and interpretation of the engagement patterns followed a theory-informed, yet data-grounded approach. First,
each pattern was described based on the degree to which four process metrics show noticeable differences compared to other clusters
in each task. Subsequently, these patterns were labeled through a dual lens, combining the observable dimension of behavioral
engagement with the theoretical principles of creative cognition. In almost all tasks, a cluster of students was found to achieve full or
almost full credits within a short time, as well as fewer actions on these tasks. This indicates that they can complete creative tasks
efficiently. We labeled this cluster as insightful innovators. In the following analysis, we used this pattern as a reference to explain our
labeling rationale for the other clusters in each task. Fig. 3 shows that a high proportion of students exhibited this efficient pattern in
completing most creative tasks (17.20% - 68.91% in Canada, 16.69% - 58.02% in Germany, 12.41% - 57.02% in Hong Kong). Some
students also achieved high credits but took more actions to complete the tasks. We labeled them as divergent explorers, as this cluster
embodies the characteristics of divergent thinking. They quickly generated a large number of ideas and sought innovative solutions
through extensive exploration. The exploring pattern also has a relatively high proportion in most tasks in Canada and Germany, but a
smaller proportion in Hong Kong. We assigned the elaborative strategists label to students who achieved high credits but took a longer
time to initiate their first action compared to their efficient peers. This pattern occurred relatively infrequently and only appeared in
several tasks (10 in Canada, 8 in Germany, 16 in Hong Kong). In addition to these patterns with higher scores, two patterns with lower
scores were also identified. One pattern is that students have made a lot of attempts or spent a lot of time thinking about how to start
the first step. They put much effort into creative tasks but performed unsatisfactorily. Therefore, we labeled this cluster as disfluent
performers. This pattern was only found in 8 tasks in Canada, 8 in Germany, but 18 in Hong Kong. The proportion of this pattern varies
greatly. The other pattern is that students spent a shorter time thinking and taking the first action, fewer attempts, and a shorter total
time to complete the task. They reflected a tendency towards hastily completing tasks. Therefore, we labeled this cluster as hasty
attempters. Similar to the efficient pattern, the hasty pattern occurred in most tasks and accounts for relatively large proportions. The
last pattern was labeled as passive, where students behaved similarly to the hasty pattern but had no scores. Compared to task
disengagement, the passive pattern exhibited slightly more effort in tasks but was still inefficient. This pattern was only found in 6 tasks
in Canada, 6 in Germany, and 10 in Hong Kong.
4.2. The most common engagement and disengagement patterns
Table 2 summarizes the most common engagement and disengagement patterns among students when completing multiple creative tasks. Among all three economies, a relatively large proportion of students commonly performed efficiently in these tasks (II in
Table 2) (33.24% in Canada, 21.98% in Germany, 37.69% in Hong Kong), indicating that their relatively high cognitive flexibility
enabled them to successfully complete creative tasks multiple times. The hasty pattern was another common pattern with Canada at

5. Discussion
5.1. Engagement and disengagement in creative tasks
This study analyzed three disengaged behaviors, i.e., rapid, relatively rapid, and non-responding. The reasons behind task
disengagement differed between high-stakes and low-stakes assessments (Wise, 2017). In high-stakes assessments, test-takers may
quickly omit items or provide rapid, perfunctory answers as a strategic way to manage time and complete the test. In contrast, in
low-stakes assessments, where test-takers face no consequences for their performance, disengagement reflects their lack of motivation
when completing tasks (Wise & Kong, 2005). Especially for creative work, intrinsic motivation has been proven to be one of its
strongest predictors (Liu et al., 2016).
Our cluster analysis, based on behavioral metrics from the PISA creative thinking tasks, revealed six distinct patterns of student
engagement. It is crucial to emphasize that these metrics are direct, observable indices of behavioral engagement, which quantify the
“what” and “how much” of participation, such as persistence and time investment. They do not directly measure the internal, strategic
processes that define cognitive engagement. At the behavioral level, the observed patterns were similar to prior findings on digital
reading (Hahnel et al., 2022) and problem-solving tasks (Wang et al., 2023). However, guided by the multidimensional engagement
model and the nature of creative cognition involved in tasks, specific configurations of these behavioral patterns can serve as proxies
for inferring probable differences in underlying cognitive strategies and motivational states during creative problem-solving.
For insightful innovators, they can quickly understand the essence of the problem, skip tedious trial and error, and directly generate
novel and useful ideas. It suggests high motivation and cognitive efficiency. The pattern implies rapid problem structuring and
effective strategy retrieval, which is a possible marker of well-developed prior schema or high cognitive flexibility in applying them.
Divergent explorers exhibited the characteristic of generating a multitude of ideas. Students tested these ideas, leading to relatively
high creative performance. It exemplifies the generate-explore cycle of creative cognition and implies students’ sustained cognitive
effort and investment. While a small portion of students demonstrated the opposite engagement pattern (i.e., elaborate strategists).
Corresponding to the incubation and elaboration stages in the creative process, they spent a lot of time in deep thinking and internalization. Although they took fewer actions afterwards, each one was carefully evaluated and refined, resulting in relatively high
creative performance. It can be inferred as deep cognitive engagement in internal planning, mental simulation, and refinement.
Disfluent performers were students who either attempted much or were unable to take effective action due to overthinking. Ultimately,
it led to a mismatch between effort and output. It highlights that behavioral effort, without adaptive metacognitive control and
cognitive flexibility, can lead to unproductive struggle. A considerable number of students were hasty attempters who reflect superficial behavioral engagement. They might quickly try some ideas they thought of, lacking deep processing and persistence, and fail
to invest in true creative exploration. The minimal investment infers a lack of intrinsic motivation and deep cognitive engagement. In
some creative tasks, a portion of students demonstrated a passive engagement pattern with minimal ineffective motivational and
cognitive investment. This pattern can be inferred to stem from overwhelming task demands, a complete lack of intrinsic motivation,
or very low perceived competence.
Elaborate strategists, disfluent performers, and passive learners only existed in some tasks, which might be due to task characteristics (e.g., difficulty) and individual factors (e.g., intrinsic motivation). The fact that these patterns were task-contingent underscores a core tenet of engagement theory. Engagement is a dynamic state that can be influenced by the interaction between
individuals and their contexts, encompassing psychological processes that evolve over time (Reschly & Christenson, 2022; Salmela-Aro
et al., 2016). For instance, a task perceived as overly difficult (low perceived competence) may push a student from exploration into
disfluent performance or passive engagement. Consequently, an individual’s pattern of engagement is not fixed but can vary across
different tasks and situations, a premise critical to interpreting various common patterns in multiple creative tasks.
5.2. The most common patterns in multiple creative tasks
Multiple engagement patterns across a series of creative thinking tasks reflect that students exhibited different preferences for how
to engage in tasks (Cromwell et al., 2023). The creative thinking tasks involve multiple dimensions, incorporating two cognitive skills
(convergent and divergent thinking), three facets (generating creative ideas, generating diverse ideas, and evaluating and improving
ideas), and four domains (visual and written expressions, social and scientific problem-solving). Therefore, to succeed in creative tasks,
students need to demonstrate greater cognitive flexibility, i.e., adapting behaviors and exploring different strategies between different
tasks (Diamond, 2013). Students’ engagement heterogeneity reflects their varying levels of cognitive flexibility. The frequency of six
engagement patterns indicated that most students adopted a strategy of taking fewer actions in a short period of time to generate
solutions for most tasks, with half of them able to come up with original and diverse solutions and achieve high scores. For highly
creative individuals, cognitive flexibility strengthens their semantic memory networks, promoting innovative ideation and enhancing
creative potential (Kenett et al., 2018). The passive pattern only occurred in several tasks, but with high proportions, especially in
Hong Kong, indicating that some students’ cognitive flexibility is insufficient for them to complete these tasks.
Students need to continuously generate creative output in a series of creative tasks within a certain period of time, which requires
students to demonstrate flexibility and sustained motivation between tasks. As an ability to transition between different tasks, rules, or
strategies, cognitive flexibility enables individuals to easily adjust their attention and viewpoints (Ionescu, 2012). A considerable

number of students are able to engage steadily in creative tasks, completing efficiently, investing more effort, or adopting a quick
reaction strategy in most tasks. However, the tension between time management and cognitive engagement would lead students to
exhibit distinct behavioral patterns. For instance, the switch between exploring and hasty patterns reflects students’ diminished
flexibility and motivation during task switching. This is evident in three to four common engagement patterns, especially in the
transition between disengagement and engagement patterns, although this occurs in a small percentage of students.
5.3. Prediction of engagement and disengagement
Our predictive analysis reveals that engagement in creative tasks is not determined by singular factors but arises from a complex,
non-linear interplay between individual capabilities and contextual influences.
First, the analysis revealed that cognitive and academic factors, specifically, core academic competencies (reading and mathematics) and self-reported effort on tasks, emerged as robust predictors of disengagement. This can be empirically supported by ongoing
research on the associations between domain competencies and creativity (e.g., Lin et al., 2025; Wicaksono et al., 2026). The findings
indicate that the foundation of academic mastery and task-specific confidence is crucial for students to willingly invest effort in the
novel and uncertain demands of creative thinking. It was aligned with self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1995), which posits that mastery
experiences are the most potent source of efficacy beliefs. While self-efficacy is domain-sensitive, competence and confidence built in
core academic domains such as mathematics and reading can serve as a crucial psychological resource. It may foster a more generalized sense of agency, making students more willing to engage in novel challenges such as creative thinking, where they perceive a
lower risk of failure.
Second, apart from linear relationships, SHAP dependence plots also revealed the pervasive non-linearity, including widespread
threshold effects (e.g., mathematics self-efficacy in Germany) and complex curvilinear patterns (e.g., reversed U-shaped relationships
for mathematical anxiety in Canada). Threshold effects suggest that interventions may require a minimum dosage to catalyze a significant shift in task engagement. The reversed U-shape relationships imply that both very low and very high levels can be beneficial
for task engagement. For instance, regarding mathematical anxiety in Canada, the reversed U-shaped relationship implies that
disengagement occurs in an intermediate zone. This complexity explains why simple correlational analyses are insufficient and
highlights the value of machine learning models capable of capturing these intricate interactions.
Third, SHAP dependence plots also revealed cross-cultural divergence in key predictors. Mathematics-related factors can function
in opposite ways across economies. In Canada and Germany, strong math performance served as a straightforward resource for
engagement. In Germany, however, intensive exposure to advanced mathematical tasks predicted disengagement from creative
thinking. This paradox suggests that a curriculum overly focused on structured, domain-specific reasoning may inadvertently cultivate
cognitive styles or allocate motivational resources that compete with the open-ended exploration required for creativity. Therefore, a
factor is not universally positive or negative; its effect might be context-mediated. Therefore, predicting and nurturing engagement in
creative activities cannot rely on universal factors but should account for how these factors are embedded within specific educational
and cultural contexts (Lubart, 2010).
6. Limitations and future research
There are several key limitations of this study that need consideration. Firstly, this study analyzed general log data from ILSA.
Future research could incorporate other data sources, such as eye-tracking technology, to dig into students’ engagement from a
cognitive perspective. Secondly, while the dependence plots with LOWESS revealed complex relationships between some background
factors and creative engagement, the non-linear patterns and potential threshold effects warrant further investigation. Finally, while
our data-mining approach identified cross-country differences in predictors of task disengagement among students from three
countries, a dedicated comparative analysis is needed to explicitly explore these variations.
7. Conclusion
The present study explored students’ distinct engagement and disengagement patterns in multiple creative tasks, examined the
most common patterns, and developed predictive models to identify the most important predictors for task engagement and disengagement. Unsupervised clustering analysis was used to accurately identify various engagement patterns. Simple behavioral metrics
were transformed into interpretable, high-level engagement signatures through the lens of engagement theory and models of creative
cognition. This theory-informed, data-grounded approach offers a scalable framework for moving beyond surface-level behaviors to
integrate behavioral traces with the inherent demands and nature of the tasks themselves. Furthermore, the patterns were summarized
across multiple creative tasks. The results contribute to our understanding of students’ varied levels of engagement and the switch of
engagement patterns across tasks. The subsequent predictive analysis provided significant insights into the relationships between the
contextual factors and task engagement. The most important factors were cognitive and academic factors across three economies. Our
findings also highlighted their complex relationships. The explainability of predicted results plays a crucial role in providing accurate
information within the “black box” rather than just demonstrating predictive performance. Such strengths can offer actionable insights
to support stakeholders in making evidence-based decisions about fostering engagement in creativity in educational settings.
