Teacher collaboration and professional development activities are core issues of teacher professionalisation. The
purpose of this explorative study is to explore patterns of professional development participation, teacher collaboration and teaching practices using multiple correspondence analysis. Feedback provided to teachers and class
characteristics were also included in the analysis. Therefore, secondary analysis of lower secondary teacher data
from the OECD Teaching and Learning International Survey 2018 was conducted. The explorative analyses show
that teachers who are not participating in collaboration activities are less likely to use teaching practices like
clear instruction and cognitive activation. Feedback and class characteristics also show small but notable connections to collaboration and teaching practices. Teacher working with challenging classes (e. g. high number of
children with problem behaviour) frequently use cognitive activation and clear instruction practices but also less
collaboration and participation in professional development.

1. Introduction
Teacher classroom practice plays a crucial role in student learning
and achievement (Boonen, Van Damme, & Onghena, 2014; Cordero
& Gil-Izquierdo, 2018). To improve teaching quality, teacher growth,
teaching practices and student learning, professional teacher development activities (PD) (Kalinowski, Egert, Gronostaj, & Vock, 2020; Opfer
& Pedder, 2011; Parkhouse, Lu, & Massaro, 2019), diﬀerent forms of
teacher collaboration (Bryk, Camburn, & Louis, 1999; Doğan & Yurtseven, 2018; Hargreaves, 2019; Vangrieken, Dochy, Raes, & Kyndt,
2015) and feedback provided to the teachers (Hellrung & Hartig, 2013)
are widely considered to be essential. In fact, research often focuses on
the eﬀects of speciﬁc programs on teacher or student outcomes.
Taking into account that teaching is contextually situated
(Anderson, Greeno, Reder, & Simon, 2000; Opfer & Pedder, 2011),
this study explores if speciﬁc teaching practices are used more often
in classrooms with speciﬁc challenging characteristics (e. g. classes
with high number of students with problem behaviour, children from
socio-economically disadvantaged homes, low-achievers) and if teacher
working in challenging classes show diﬀerent collaboration and PD
patterns than teachers working with less challenging classes. Especially
when teaching in challenging classes intensive collaboration, feedback
practices and wide range of PD activities are beneﬁcial to prepare
teachers to foster childrens´ learning and development. Therefore,

The aim of this study is to explore patterns of professional development (PD) activity participation, teacher coordination, collaboration,
and teaching practices. Further, feedback provided to the teachers and
environmental factors like class characteristics will also be taken into account. For this purpose a correspondence analytic approach was used,
which allows us to explore and visualize structures and patterns in large
complex datasets (Hjellbrekke, 2019). This approach facilitates the simultaneous study of diﬀerent PD, collaboration-, teaching practices,
feedback practices and classroom characteristics.
Research Questions:
• Which collaboration and PD activities are associated with diﬀerent
teaching practices like cognitive activation, disciplinary practices,
goal setting etc.?
• Which teaching practices are frequently used by teachers working
with challenging classes (e. g. high number of low achievers or children with problem behaviour)?
• Which teaching practices are frequently used by teachers who receive diﬀerent types of feedback (e. g. observation, self-assessment)?
• Do teachers working with challenging classes show diﬀerent collaboration and PD participation patterns than teachers working with
less challenging classes.

Yet, considerable ambiguity with regard to eﬀective teacher collaboration in speciﬁc contexts still prevails. Teacher collaboration can also
lead to negative consequences (e. g. teacher competitiveness, conﬂicts,
social loaﬁng), especially when the implementation of these collaborations is restricted (e.g. by conditions in school which do not support opportunities to collaborate, insuﬃcient meeting time, negative attitudes
of teachers towards collaboration) (for a review see Vangrieken et al.,

Professional development (PD) refers to “formal and informal learning opportunities that deepen and extend teachers’ professional competence, including knowledge, beliefs, motivation and self-regulatory
skills” (Richter, Kunter, Klusmann, Lüdtke, & Baumert, 2011, p. 116).
PD also includes various formal and informal activities like workshops,
courses, coaching, mentoring, conferences, etc. (Parkhouse et al., 2019;
Postholm, 2012). The diversity of teacher development programs has
led to a body of research aiming to identify the characteristics of highquality PD activities (Blank & de Las Alas, 2009; Desimone, 2009;
Kennedy, 2016; Sims & Fletcher-Wood, 2020). Previous research suggests that PD should focus on teacher’s interests, needs, prior knowledge and real-life contexts. Building subject knowledge and pedagogical skills (in contrast to general pedagogical techniques) through active
methods can provide meaningful learning experiences which are related
to teachers’ daily work in the classroom (Blank & de Las Alas, 2009;
Desimone, 2009; Dunst, Bruder, & Hamby, 2015; Patton, Parker, &
Tannehill, 2015). Further, knowledge sharing in teacher collaboration
activities (professional learning communities, community of practice,
learning communities of educators) is beneﬁcial for improving teachers’ practice (Bryk et al., 1999; Doğan & Yurtseven, 2018; Vescio, Ross,
& Adams, 2008) and student outcomes (McLaughlin & Talbert, 2006;
Patton et al., 2015; Vangrieken et al., 2015; Vescio et al., 2008). Ongoing and sustained over time activities including follow-up sessions
should also be considered when conceptualizing PD activities (Blank &
de Las Alas, 2009; Desimone, 2009; Dunst et al., 2015; Patton et al.,
2015). In follow-up sessions, experiences ensuring the transfer of such
practice into daily work can be discussed and reﬂected upon. The change
of knowledge, attitudes, beliefs and skills in PD and collaboration activities should lead to changes in teaching practices which should improve
student learning (Desimone, 2009). Results from the TALIS 2013 data
show that curriculum-based PD is related with the use of instructional
teaching methods. Teachers’ participation in PD activities focusing on
teacher collaboration and active learning methods are more likely to use
these strategies in their teaching (Barrera-Pedemonte, 2016).
However, enhancement of skills etc. does not always lead to changes
in teaching practices. The complexity of real-life teaching and learning environments has to be considered because teaching and learning is contextually situated (Anderson et al., 2000; Opfer & Pedder, 2011). Various approaches to teacher learning such as situated
learning (Billett, 1996; Cobb & Bowers, 1999; Kwakman, 2003), schoolbased learning (Parise & Spillane, 2010; van Velzen & Volman, 2009),
communities of practice (Kimble, 2008; Wenger, McDermott, & Snyder,
2002), professional learning communities and other forms of teacher
collaborations (Hargreaves, 2019; Lomos, Hofman, & Bosker, 2011;
McLaughlin & Talbert, 2006; Muckenthaler, Tillmann, Weiß, & Kiel,
2020; Vangrieken et al., 2015; Vescio et al., 2008) address the importance of learning through social practices and social interactions (Liou
& Canrinus, 2020).
It has been demonstrated that teacher collaboration is related to various positive outcomes like teacher-student interaction (Egert, Dederer,
& Fukkink, 2020), teachers’ job satisfaction (Duyar, Gumus, & Sukru Bellibas, 2013), teachers’ positive emotions (Owen, 2016), teacher engagement (Owen, 2016), student performance (Dunst et al., 2015;
Lomos et al., 2011; Moolenaar, Sleegers, & Daly, 2012), innovation in
schools (Vangrieken et al., 2015), classroom practices (Doğan & Yurtseven, 2018; Dogan, Pringle, & Mesa, 2016; Kalinowski et al., 2020),
teachers’ self-eﬃcacy (Choi & Kang, 2019; Duyar et al., 2013), teachers’ collective eﬃcacy (Moolenaar et al., 2012), teachers’ motivation
and a feeling of not being isolated (for a review see Vangrieken et al.,
2015). For example Doğan and Yurtseven (2018) show that professional
learning communities have a stronger eﬀect on teachers’ instructional
quality than diﬀerent forms of PD activities. “If teachers work in an environment or a community where reﬂective and collaborative practices

1.2. Teaching practice
The major aim of PD and teacher collaboration is to foster eﬀective teaching practices (Doğan & Yurtseven, 2018), which should enhance student learning and achievement (McLaughlin & Talbert, 2006).
The relationship between diﬀerent teaching practices in the classroom
and student achievement is complex. For instance, students not only
beneﬁt from strong pedagogical teacher knowledge, individual learning support, active learning (Baumert et al., 2017), learner-centred
teaching and eﬃcient classroom management practices (Opdenakker &
Van Damme, 2006), but also from traditional teaching strategies (e.g.
to build factual knowledge) (Bietenbeck, 2014).
Traditional teaching strategies (e.g. teacher-centred strategies, mastery learning) as well as modern constructivist based teaching strategies (learner-oriented teaching, self-regulated approaches) seem to be
beneﬁcial for students learning in diﬀerent contexts (e.g. mathematics, spelling and reading achievements; diﬀerent socioeconomic backgrounds of the students) (Bietenbeck, 2014; Boonen et al., 2014; Cordero
& Gil-Izquierdo, 2018; Gao, 2014; Hattie, 2019). As previously stated,
such recent evidence highlights the signiﬁcant inﬂuence of context on
the eﬀectiveness of various teaching strategies. Thus, an environment
characterised by teacher collaboration (e.g. professional learning communities, co-teaching etc.) can be seen as a way to improve teaching effectiveness by sharing knowledge through discussion and reﬂecting on
diﬀerent teaching strategies under diﬀerent circumstances. In addition,
taking part in collaboration activities helps to build a wide repertoire of
diﬀerent teaching strategies.
Based on the assumption that the implementation/change of diﬀerent teaching practices in the classroom may be fostered or hindered by
variables such as participating in formal PD activities, diﬀerent forms
of teacher collaboration, feedback provided to the teachers and environmental aspects like class characteristics, this paper aims to explore
patterns of professional development participation, teacher collaboration and teaching practices in lower secondary teachers using data from
the TALIS 2018 survey (OECD, 2019a).
2. Method
2.1. Data source and sample
Secondary analysis was conducted using the data of the Teaching
and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 conducted by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and
the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA). TALIS, the largest ongoing international survey of teacher
and school leaders and school learning environments, oﬀers teacher
and school level data for research and educational policy making
(OECD, 2019a, 2019b). Detailed information on the data collection can
be found in OECD (2019c). The lower secondary (ISCED level 2) teacherlevel data from participating OECD countries (Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada-Alberta, Chile, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Hungary, Israel, Italy, Japan, Korea, Latvia, Lithuania,
Mexico, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Portugal, Slovak Republic, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Turkey, United Kingdom, United States)
were used for all analysis. Data from OECD partner countries where not

2.2. Measures2
2.2.1. Professional development (PD)
Teachers were asked whether they had participated in diﬀerent
PD activities (question 223 : e.g. courses, online courses, conferences,
teacher networks) in the last 12 months.
2.2.2. Teacher coordination and collaboration
To gain information about teacher collaboration, the teachers were
asked to rate the frequency (6-point scale: never, once a year or less, 2–4
times a year, 5–10 times a year, 1–3 times a month, once a week or more)
of diﬀerent collaboration activities they had participated in (question
33: e.g. observe other teacher in class, joint teaching, joint activities
across diﬀerent classes and ages, exchange of teaching materials).
2.2.3. Feedback about teaching
Teachers were asked to note the types of feedback received on their
teaching activities (question 29: e.g. observation of classroom teaching,
student surveys). Furthermore, they mentioned from whom they had
received this feedback (external individuals, school principal or members of the school management team, colleagues within the school, no
feedback).

Teachers were asked to focus on a particular class they teach and

3.2. Supplementary points: target class characteristics
To analyse the links between the supplementary variables and the
dimensions, the deviation between the coordinates of two categories on
a dimension are used (Le Roux & Rouanet, 2010). Deviations greater
than 1 illustrate large and greater than 0.5 notable connections of the
supplementary variable to this dimension (Le Roux & Rouanet, 2010;
Le Roux, Rouanet, Savage, & Warde, 2008).
Dimensions one and two are related to classes with high vs. low number of students with behavioural problems in the class (deviations between coordinates of the extreme modalities of the variable: dimension

The results also show that feedback practices in the school are important to teachers’ classroom practices. Teachers who receive feedback

linked to a lack of participation in collaborative activities among people

These results highlight the importance of diﬀerent teacher collaboration, PD activities and feedback practices in relation to teaching
practices. In line with current research (Doğan & Yurtseven, 2018;
Dunst et al., 2015; Kalinowski et al., 2020; Vescio et al., 2008) fostering
teacher collaboration, feedback practices and PD should help teachers
to build a repertoire of diﬀerent teaching practices (like cognitive activation practices). A school culture characterized by both feedback and
collaboration seem to be beneﬁcial.

Taking into account that teaching is contextually situated
(Anderson et al., 2000; Opfer & Pedder, 2011), the results of the
present paper show that in classes with a high number of students with
problem behaviour, teachers are more likely to show clear instruction,
cognitive activation, and everyday-life relation in their teaching. The
stronger link to dimension two of the MCA indicates, that in these
classes disciplinary classroom management strategies are used more
frequently than in classes with fewer students with behavioural problems. Teachers who work in classes with a high number of refugees or
students from socio-economically disadvantaged homes also frequently
use clear instruction, cognitive activation and the use of everyday-life
related problems. Further in classes with a high number of low achievers, teachers are more likely to use disciplinary classroom management
practices and participate less in professional collaboration than teachers
in classes with a low number of low achievers.
Even though teachers in classes with high number of students with
problem behaviour, low achievers, refugees, or students from socioeconomically disadvantaged homes show a wide range of positive teaching practices like cognitive activation and clear instruction, they show
less collaboration activities and PD participation. This should be intensiﬁed.
