Abstract
Background: Teachers’ team innovativeness—the collective capacity of teaching
teams to generate, adopt, and implement new ideas and practices—is increasingly
recognized for its role in enhancing instructional methods and fostering school innovation. However, empirical research identifying its predictors remains limited, particularly
within large-scale educational contexts. Prior studies often analyzed a restricted set
of variables using traditional multilevel models, which may not fully capture the complexity inherent in such data structures.
Methods: This study utilized data from the Teaching and Learning International
Survey (TALIS) 2018, encompassing responses from 2895 Korean middle school teachers and 163 principals. To address the multilevel structure of the data and perform
comprehensive variable selection, we employed glmmLasso—a penalized regression
technique that integrates generalized linear mixed models with LASSO penalties. This
approach allowed for the identification of important predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness across 571 explanatory variables. Additionally, a simulation study was conducted to evaluate the performance of glmmLasso in comparison to traditional LASSO
and elastic net (Enet) methods regarding predictive accuracy and variable selection.
Results: The application of glmmLasso resulted in a more parsimonious model, selecting considerably fewer variables while maintaining predictive performance comparable to that of LASSO and Enet. Fourteen key predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness
were identified, predominantly exhibiting positive associations and encompassing
factors such as teacher collaboration, self-efficacy, and supportive school climate.
Notably, the variable representing shared responsibility for school issues demonstrated
a negative association with team innovativeness. The simulation study further supported glmmLasso’s superior accuracy in identifying true predictors and reducing false
positives, particularly at the school level.
Conclusions: This study contributes both methodologically and substantively
by demonstrating the efficacy of glmmLasso in analyzing large-scale, nested educational data to uncover important predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness. The
findings offer actionable insights for educational policymakers and school leaders
aiming to cultivate innovative teaching teams through strategies that enhance teacher
collaboration, bolster self-efficacy, and foster a supportive school climate. Future
research should explore the applicability of glmmLasso across diverse educational

Introduction
As education continues to evolve in response to the demands of a rapidly changing
world, innovation in education becomes increasingly important. Innovation in education plays a crucial role in enhancing learning outcomes, promoting equity and equality,
improving efficiency, and driving the necessary adjustments to meet changes in society (OECD, 2014). The more innovative a school is, the better the outcomes, such as
increased teacher collaboration, higher job satisfaction, more innovative teaching practices, and greater cognitive activation of students (Blömeke et al., 2021). When teachers
work in a culture of innovation, they are more likely to collaborate with each other, sharing their strengths and expertise with one another. Furthermore, an innovative school
climate enhances teacher job satisfaction and supports the development of innovative
teaching practices.
Most meaningful challenges, especially those in education, necessitates collaborative, interdisciplinary approaches. Teachers are increasingly expected to work in teams
to design curriculum and assessment, respond to diverse, complex students’ needs,
and implement comprehensive school reforms. As such, team-based collaboration has
become not only common but essential across various educational contexts. In the
framework, teachers’ team innovativeness, which involves collective professional development and the implementation practices not just in an individual teachers’ classroom,
but across educational teams (Jang et al., 2024), has been gaining attention, especially in
enhancing professional practices such as their instructional methods (Atasoy & Yalçın,
2023). Therefore, deepening our understanding and fostering teachers’ team innovativeness is crucial for driving systemic change and improving educational outcomes. One
effective way to achieve this is by exploring the factors that influence teachers’ team
innovativeness.
Despite its importance, research on the factors influencing teachers’ team innovativeness remains limited. Previous research primarily examined the relationship between
teachers’ team innovativeness and some selected variables such as school leadership
or teachers’ professional practices (e.g., Blömeke et al., 2021; Hsieh et al., 2024; Xia &
O’Shea, 2024). For example, Hsieh et al. (2024) investigated the impact of school leadership and teacher self-efficacy on teacher innovativeness, employing multilevel modeling.
However, their study included only a small set of variables in the analysis. Multilevel
models often face nonconvergence problems when overparameterized; this limits the
scope of variables analyzed. Given the paucity of prior research, there is a clear need
for exploratory research into the factors that influence teachers’ team innovativeness.
Exploring the variables that affect teachers’ team innovativeness from large-scale assessment data could provide valuable insights for policy makers or school principals on ways
to improve it.

For exploratory research, employing machine learning techniques, particularly penalized regression, can effectively identify predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness.
By imposing constraints, penalized regression explores numerous potential predictors
while minimizing issues of nonconvergence and overfitting. Additionally, it offers the
distinct advantage of producing interpretable predictive models, enhancing its relevance
and usability in educational research, where explanation has been valued over prediction (Yoo, 2024). When combined with large-scale data, penalized regression facilitates
the discovery of previously unexamined key predictors or relationships among variables
within a single statistical model, aligning with the sparsity assumption that only a relatively small number of predictors have non-zero coefficients (Immekus et al., 2022; Lim
et al., 2022; Yoo & Rho, 2020).
Furthermore, to address the nested structure inherent in large-scale educational
assessment data, we applied glmmLasso (Groll & Tutz, 2014), which integrates the Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) with penalized regression. Prior studies analyzing large-scale educational datasets (e.g., Immekus et al., 2022; Lim et al., 2022; Yoo,
2018; Yoo & Rho, 2020) has employed techniques such as Least Absolute Shrinkage and
Selection Operator (LASSO) (Tibshirani, 1996), elastic net (Enet; Zou & Hastie, 2005),
and Mnet (Huang et al., 2016). However, these approaches have largely overlooked the
multilevel structure resulting from multistage sampling designs. Given this, models such
as the GLMM are necessary to appropriately model such hierarchical data. Nonetheless,
the GLMM does not support variable selection. In contrast, glmmLasso incorporates the
LASSO penalty within a GLMM framework, executing variable selection. Accordingly,
we employ glmmLasso to explore key predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness—a
construct newly introduced in the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS)
2018.
In addition to the empirical data analysis, we conducted a simulation study to examine the applicability of glmmLasso in the context of large-scale educational data. The
simulation data were generated to reflect the structure of the empirical dataset, and
the performance of glmmLasso was compared to those of LASSO and Enet across both
empirical and simulation datasets, focusing on the predictive accuracy and variable
selection capabilities. In summary, this study aims to explore the predictors of teachers’
team innovativeness using TALIS 2018, while also assessing the applicability of glmmLasso for analyzing large-scale educational datasets through simulation.

Key predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness
Teachers’ team innovativeness refers to the collective capacity of teacher teams to
generate, adopt, and implement new ideas or practices in schools. It comprises collective teacher innovativeness (Park & Jang, 2021) and an innovative school climate
(Lee & Kim, 2020), highlighting how teachers collaborate, explore new approaches to
teaching and learning, and embrace openness to change (Meirink et al., 2010). Understanding what drives this innovativeness is important not only for improving educational outcomes but also for fostering better school environments. For instance, team
innovativeness among teachers plays an important role in enhancing professional
practices, which in turn significantly improve their instructional methods (Atasoy
& Yalçın, 2023). Teachers’ team innovativeness emerges from a complex interplay

of factors at multiple levels. Previous research across various countries highlights a
blend of school-level factors (e.g., school climate, leadership) and teacher-level factors (e.g., teachers’ self-efficacy, cooperation) that predict teachers’ team innovativeness. The key predictors at the school- and teacher- levels, based on empirical studies
and theoretical frameworks, are discussed below.

School‑level predictors
School climate

School climate and social capital (e.g., informal cooperation and trust among stakeholders in schools) are influential factors affecting team innovativeness (De Dreu & West,
2001; Lecat et al., 2018; Lee & Kim, 2020; Moolenaar et al., 2014; Park & Jang, 2021; Parlar et al., 2019; Taştan, 2013; Widmann et al., 2016; Xafakos et al., 2020). Various types of
school climates, such as innovation-friendly, participatory school climates have mainly
explored in previous studies. For instance, schools rated high in innovativeness by
teachers show greater collaboration, more frequent innovative classroom practices, and
higher job satisfaction (Blömeke et al., 2021). This implies that an innovative school climate not only sparks new ideas but also reinforces positive outcomes. Some studies (e.g.,
Park & Jang, 2021) have shown that a participatory school climate positively impacts
collective teacher innovativeness. For example, the more educators intentionally shape
their networks, the more they perceive their school’s climate as innovative. Collaborative
networks within a school have a substantial impact on teachers’ innovativeness. The network of school teachers and the support they receive can predict an innovative school
climate within and among schools (Xafakos et al., 2020). Additionally, a culture of academic optimism, which includes collective efficacy and trust in supporting innovation,
predicts school innovation (Schwabsky et al., 2020). In such cultures, teachers believe in
their collective ability to overcome challenges and feel pressure to continually improve,
fostering an innovative environment. However, Xafakos et al. (2020) did not observe any
statistically significant correlation between innovative school climate and teachers’ perceived innovativeness.
Leadership

Leadership plays a crucial role in shaping the conditions for teachers’ team innovativeness. School leaders who are supporting, trusting, and empowering can strongly influence a team’s willingness to innovate. For example, principals’ inclusive leadership,
characterized by behaviors that encourage teachers in the decision making, positively
predicts innovative behavior (Bao, 2024). Specifically, the effect of inclusive leaders on
teachers’ innovation is partially mediated by teachers’ psychological empowerment
(sense of autonomy and impact) and foster a school innovation climate, which in turn
drive innovation. Similarly, distributed leadership, which means shared, participatory
leadership roles with teachers, have been lined to higher teacher innovativeness (Hsieh
et al., 2024; Lin, 2022; Xia & O’Shea, 2024). When teachers feel trusted by their principal in their professionalism, their collective innovativeness increases (Schwabsky et al.,
2020).

Teacher‑level predictors
Teacher empowerment and teachers’ self‑efficacy

Teacher empowerment in decision making, as an aspect of leadership, and teachers’
self-efficacy are crucial for improving teachers’ team innovativeness. Participation in
decision-making is positively linked to innovation (De Dreu & West, 2001), with teacher
involvement in school decisions being a key predictor of both individual and team innovativeness (Xia & O’Shea, 2024). That is, empowered teachers, those who have a voice in
school decisions and autonomy in their classrooms, are more likely to show greater team
innovativeness.
The positive relationship between teachers’ self-efficacy (i.e., the belief in one’s ability or confidence to teach effectively and make a positive impact on students) and team
innovativeness has been investigated in previous research (e.g., Woo & Kim, 2020; Xia &
O’Shea, 2024). Particularly, team innovativeness is primarily associated with innovation
self-efficacy (teacher’s self-efficacy with helping students think critically) (Xia & O’Shea,
2024) and self-efficacy relating to student engagement and instructional strategy (Woo &
Kim, 2020). When teachers are confident in their abilities to engage students and implement instructional strategies, they are more likely to exhibit greater team innovativeness
and innovative behaviors.

Teacher collaboration and professional development

Teachers’ innovative behaviors are positively related to sharing activities with colleagues,
colleagues’ and students’ support, and work motivation (Asbari et al., 2019; Cai & Tang,
2021; Suharyati, 2017). Particularly, high levels of teacher collaboration and cooperation are linked with greater teachers’ team innovativeness (Park & Shin, 2020). In other
words, teachers who engage more in collaboration and cooperation are more likely to
perceive higher levels of teachers’ team innovativeness. Collaboration enables teachers
to share ideas and co-develop new practices, fostering a synergy through diverse perspective or peer feedback. Innovative school environments are related with more frequent teacher collaboration and exchange (Blömeke et al., 2021).
Professional preparedness and practice are key predictors to teachers’ team innovativeness. Innovation-related teacher preparedness (e.g., teaching cross-curricular skills)
and teacher professional practices (e.g., exchanging materials, engaging in discussion,
team teaching, peer observation), and innovative classroom practices have a positive
effect on teachers’ team innovativeness (Blömeke et al., 2021; Xia & O’Shea, 2024). However, the impact of innovation-focused professional development—such as training in
information and communication technology (ICT) skills for teaching and instructional
innovation—on teachers’ team innovativeness remains inconclusive. Some studies (e.g.,
Xia & O’Shea, 2024) have reported no significant effects.

The early penalized regression, ridge, was developed to solve the multicollinearity
problem (Hoerl & Kennard, 1970). Specifically, ridge adds a penalty term to the diagonals of Xk T Xk matrix, resulting in an invertible matrix. Due to the penalty term, ridge
also shrinks the regression coefficients. However, ridge does not perform variable selection; all predictors remain in the model, even though their coefficients may be reduced
to values very close to zero.
Later penalized regression techniques such as LASSO, Enet, and glmmLasso executes variable selection, as they shrink the coefficients of less important predictors
to exact zero. Especially in large-scale data analysis involving hundreds or thousands of variables, variable selection is highly desirable, not least for its contribution to interpretability.
Group LASSO and group Enet

LASSO, developed by Tibshirani (1996), is the first and most popular penalized regression
method for variable selection but does not handle multicollinearity. Enet addresses multicollinearity by incorporating a ridge penalty into the objective function (Zou & Hastie, 2005).

LASSO and is typically set to 0.5 (Hastie & Qian, 2016).

Materials and methods
Data
Study I: TALIS 2018

The data employed in this study contains a total of 2,931 Korean middle school teachers’ and their 165 principals’ responses who participated in TALIS 2018. On average,
schools had 17.98 teachers (SD = 4.78), with a minimum of 4 and a maximum of 29. We
employed a scale score, teachers’ team innovativeness (T3TEAM), as the response variable of this study (mean = 11.41, SD = 2.11). The TALIS measures teachers’ team innovativeness using items scored on a 4-point Likert-like scale. The items are “Most teachers
in this school strive to develop new ideas for teaching and learning (TT3G32A),” “Most
teachers in this school are open to change (TT3G32B),” “Most teachers in this school
search for new ways to solve problems (TT3G32C),” and “Most teachers in this school
provide practical support to each other for the application of new ideas (TT3G32D).”
Then, the TALIS generates the scale score, T3TEAM, derived from confirmatory factor analysis of the combined responses to these items; the standardized factor loadings
for the scale T3TEAM of Korea are 0.824, 0.847, 0.911, and 0.814 (OECD, 2019a). The
TALIS 2018 data is available at https://​www.​oecd.​org/​educa​tion/​talis/​talis-​2018-​data.​
htm.
The 493 teacher variables and 448 principal variables of the TALIS dataset were
cleaned as follows. First, variables that were irrelevant to our analysis were removed,
including ID, weighting, standardized scores, and administration (e.g., IDTEACH,
PISASCHOOLID, IDLANG, IDCNTRY, SCHWGT, and VERSION). Next, variables
with 20% or higher missingness were removed from further analyses, as they could have
impaired subsequent missing data imputation (Rho & Yoo, 2019; Yoo & Koo, 2021).
Categorical items from the TALIS questionnaires were dummy-coded as 1’s and 0’s.
Aforementioned, predictors of three or more categories were treated as a group in the
analysis after dummy coding, and there were three such items: TC3G10, TC3G34, and
TT3G37. For instance, TC3G10 (school location description) had five response categories: (1) a village, hamlet, or rural area, (2) a small town, (3) a town, (4) a city, and (5) a
large city. The first dummy of TC3G10, (1) a village, hamlet, or rural area, was removed,
and four dummy variables were created by using dummy_cols function of fastDummies
package in R (Kaplan, 2020).
The item’s original coding was 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 and was recoded with four dummy variables. The newly created dummy variables replaced the original item of multiple categories and were selected or removed as a group in glmmLasso, LASSO, and Enet. After
merging the teacher and principal datasets, the final dataset comprised 571 explanatory
variables, including dummy-coded variables, of 2895 middle school teachers and 163
principals. Lastly, the k-nearest neighbors algorithm was employed to handle missing
data. In particular, the study used the Gower distance—recommended for mixed-format
data (Gower, 1971), with 25 as the value of k calculated from 644 complete observations
(Immekus et al., 2022; Lim et al., 2022; Yoo et al., 2022).

Model selection: Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion

In this study, the three penalized regression models, LASSO, Enet, and glmmLasso,
were employed. In model comparison and selection with penalized regression, the
regularization parameter  (in Eqs. (3), (4), and (6)) is tuned to yield predictive models. In glmmLasso, information criteria such as the Akaike Information Criterion
(AIC) (Akaike, 1973) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Schwarz, 1978) is
typically employed, and the tuning (or regularization) parameter , which minimizes
AIC or BIC is identified. Equations (9) and (10) explain AIC and BIC, respectively. In
these equations, L, W, and n denote the maximized value of the likelihood function
of the model, the number of parameters estimated by the model, and the number of
observations, respectively.

As smaller AIC and BIC are deemed better, the number of parameters is a penalty
for both AIC and BIC, whereas the number of observations acts as a penalty only for
BIC in model selection. Particularly when n is sufficiently large, BIC not only raises
the penalty for the increased complexity of a model (Kuha, 2004) but also results in
consistent model selection (Shao, 1997). For the aforementioned reasons, BIC was
employed as a model selection criterion, and the  of the lowest BIC was selected to
establish the final model.

Model assessment
Test data root mean square error

To control the bias resulting from data, data splitting and model fitting were iterated
(Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010). The model selection and assessment steps were as
follows. First, the data were split with a ratio of 7 to 3, comprising training and test
data, respectively. The ratio of 7 to 3 followed the convention of social science largescale data studies (e.g., Immekus et al., 2022; Lim et al., 2022; Yoo & Rho, 2020, 2021).
The training set is employed to fit a model, and the test set is used to estimate prediction error for assessing the final model. Second, for  in range, the BIC was measured
using the training data from Step 1. As a result, the  of the lowest BIC was identified
as well as the W, the number of parameters. This step was repeated independently
for each of the penalized regression methods. Of note, glmmLasso, unlike LASSO or
Enet, allows the user to specify the  range, specifically the minimum and maximum
values of  . For a thorough search of optimal  , the value that shrinks the coefficients
of all predictors to zero served as the maximum, and the value of zero as the minimum. Third, the  value identified in Step 2 was applied to the test data from Step
1, yielding prediction errors measured as root mean square error (RMSE). The three
steps were iterated 1,000 times each for glmmLasso, LASSO, and Enet. All the programs were written in R. Specifically, the glmmLasso package (Groll, 2017) was used

Variable selection

The selection or non-selection of each predictor across iterations were summarized as
the selection counts. The number of variables selected by one or more, 25% or more,

Discussion
Unlike extant studies on large-scale survey data that have mostly neglected the nested
data structure, we utilized generalized penalized regression, specifically glmmLasso,
to account for the multilevel data structure of TALIS data. Our analysis explored predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness in TALIS 2018, and identified predictors that
can shed light on designing and implementing effective school policies to improve
teachers’ team innovativeness. Additionally, a simulation study was conducted to
deepen the understanding of the empirical research, reflecting the structure of
TALIS 2018. Consistently across both studies, our methodological findings show that
glmmLasso produced more stable and parsimonious results than LASSO and Enet. It
is also notable that glmmLasso demonstrated more effective variable selection than
LASSO and Enet, particularly for school-level predictors. To summarize, glmmLasso
appears a promising technique in large-scale survey data with a nested structure.
Detailed discussions and future research topics are followed.
Predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness

The key predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness encompass both those identified in
previous studies and new factors explored in this research. Specifically, teacher self-efficacy related to student engagement and instructional strategies has been shown to positively correlate with teachers’ team innovativeness, consistently with previous research
(e.g., Woo & Kim, 2020). The more teachers feel confident that they can motivate students with low interest and use various assessment strategies, the higher their team
innovativeness. Teachers’ self-efficacy has well known predictor of team innovativeness
in previous research (Woo & Kim, 2020; Xia & O’Shea, 2024). Hence, motivating teachers to be more confident to make a positive impact on students is important. For example, empowering teachers, providing teachers with opportunities to participate, making
teachers involved in decision-making may help enhance their team innovativeness, as

(2025) 13:19

teacher empowerment, participation in decision-making, and teacher involvement were
crucial factors for improving teachers team innovativeness (De Dreu & West, 2001; Xia
& O’Shea, 2024).
By employing machine learning methods, new predictors were explored that have not
yet been investigated in previous research with conventional statistical methods (Shmueli, 2010). Interestingly, teachers in schools that emphasize shared responsibility report
low recognition of their team innovativeness. While shared responsibility is negatively
correlated with team innovativeness, a collaborative school culture–characterized by
mutual support and shared beliefs about teaching and learning among staff—is positively associated with team innovativeness. Prior studies have identified a participatory
school climate, along with inclusive and distributed leadership, as key factors in fostering team innovativeness (Bao, 2024; Blömeke et al., 2021; Hsieh et al., 2024; Lin, 2022;
Xia & O’Shea, 2024). However, when responsibility is widely shared, individual teachers
may be less inclined to engage in innovative behaviors or may experience a diminished
sense of ownership over changes. Consequently, shared responsibility may act as a barrier to innovation. Teachers’ team innovativeness is conceptualized as an overall climate
attribute, rather than an individual trait or attitude (Blömeke et al., 2021). Nevertheless,
the relationship between a school climate characterized by shared responsibility and an
innovative school culture remains unclear and requires further investigation.
While previous studies primarily focused on the positive effects of a participative
school climate and teacher cooperation on team innovativeness (e.g., Park & Shin, 2020;
Taştan, 2013), we specifically explored additional predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness: teacher-student relations, stakeholder participation, and the frequency of
engagement in professional practices. Based on the new key predictors investigated in
this study, detailed school policies can be suggested based on the results of the study.
For instance, the frequency of teachers’ joint activities was a newly identified variable
from the subsection on teacher cooperation. Compared to other items in the subsection, increasing the frequency of teachers’ joint activities is easier to operationalize and
implement. In addition, when teachers perceive greater agreement that their school
offers students opportunities to participate in decision making, they tend to experience
a stronger sense of team innovativeness. While previous research (e.g., De Dreu & West,
2001; Xia & O’Shea, 2024) have highlighted the impact of teacher empowerment, fostering a participatory school climate that involves students in decision-making is also crucial. Likewise, other newly investigated variables can shed light on school policy, leading
to evidence-based research in enhancing teachers’ team innovativeness.
To the best of our knowledge, little empirical research has explored predictors of
teachers’ team innovativeness. More empirical research is required. Particularly, the
relationship between teachers’ team innovativeness and the newly identified variables by
glmmLasso requires further investigation, which will contribute to the existing body of
literature. Important predictors of teachers’ team innovativeness also should be identified and explored with other TALIS samples. While we only analyzed Korean teachers
and their schools in this study, other countries’ data should be analyzed to expand our
results and suggestions. In particular, teachers in European countries, including Belgium
and Portugal, displayed low team innovativeness in TALIS 2018 (OECD, 2019b). Further machine learning studies based on different TALIS samples may help accumulate

knowledge on teachers’ team innovativeness, potentially increasing teachers’ team innovativeness and school innovation.
Using glmmLasso for multilevel data

The original contribution of this study lies in the application of glmmLasso, a penalized
regression algorithm that accounts for the multilevel data structure of TALIS data. Given
the hierarchical nature of the dataset, employing multilevel modeling is both appropriate
and necessary. Specifically, the ICC of teachers’ team innovativeness was 0.094. As demonstrated in previous research (e.g., Hedges & Hedberg, 2007; Peugh, 2010), ICC values between 0.05 and 0.20 are common in cross-sectional multilevel modeling in social
science studies. Furthermore, the application of glmmLasso yielded a more parsimonious model by selecting considerably fewer variables, while maintaining predictive performance comparable to that of LASSO and Enet across both empirical and simulation
studies.
Notably, glmmLasso exhibited superior performance in variable selection compared to
LASSO and Enet, particularly with respect to level-2 (school-level) variables. This finding aligns with the results of Koo and Yoo (2021), who reported that glmmLasso more
accurately identified true level-2 predictors than its counterparts in a simulation study
using multilevel large-scale data. Although glmmLasso explicitly accounts for the multilevel structure, it did not select any school-level variables in the empirical analysis,
whereas both LASSO and Enet selected one. Given the simulation findings, glmmLasso
does not appear to select a greater number of school-level predictors than LASSO or
Enet. Rather, LASSO and Enet demonstrated a tendency to select a higher number of
false-positive predictors in comparison. As a result, the school-level variable commonly
selected by both LASSO and Enet is likely to present a false-positive predictor rather
than a true contributor to teachers’ team innovativeness.
Further simulation studies are warranted to investigate the optimal selection count
thresholds across methods and to determine the appropriate ICC levels for applying
penalized regression techniques that account for multilevel data structures. Additionally, penalized regression employing nonconvex penalties such as Mnet (Huang
et al., 2008) deserves greater attention and should be included in the future comparative analyses. Nonconvex penalty methods are known for producing consistent coefficient estimates (Huang et al., 2016). However, linear mixed modeling has not yet
been extended to accommodate nonconvex regularization; both theoretical foundations and algorithmic implementation remain underdeveloped. We strongly suggest
advancing penalized regression methods by extending nonconvex penalties to the
mixed modeling framework.
