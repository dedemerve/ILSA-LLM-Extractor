The primary purpose of this study was to use visual analytics to examine the context in which U.S. schools can
excel concerning science literacy. The school context was assessed using 49 various student-, teacher-, and
principal-reported variables related to school resources, characteristics of the teaching force, and student dispositions regarding science learning at a school level. Using multidimensional scaling preference analysis as a
visual analytical method of data mining, we examined the data of 238 U.S. schools collected from the Program
for International Student Assessment in 2015. The visual analytic results suggested that the context in which
schools can achieve better science literacy includes high- quality science teachers and favorable student dispositions toward science learning. The analysis of variance further supported the results. The results were discussed
concerning the implications for school leaders for setting program priorities.

Introduction
It is well understood that science literacy has become a fundamental
skill in an increasingly complex world, and enhancing students’ science
literacy is a core focus in public education (Anderson et al., 2007).
Success in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) is
also a national focus, and many efforts and resources have been dedicated to student achievement in STEM (Young et al., 2018). In the
United States, schools face significant pressure regarding the continual
improvement of science achievement in all students (Every Student
Succeeds Act, 2015), and schools are expected to carry out
scientifically-based interventions. Schoolwide approaches to enhancing
student performance are being explored, particularly in STEM education
(Bybee, 2018).
Because school science literacy achievement is a function of various
characteristics of school context, such as the teaching process, school
resources, and student’s backgrounds, research in this area is concerned
with the potential variables that are related to science learning outcomes
(e.g., Holzberger et al., 2020). Examining the relationships between
school science literacy and school context as assessed by various factors
related to students, teachers, and administrators can reveal how these
variables are associated and operate as a system for schools to achieve
better. As macro learning environments, schools encompass administrative functions, the teaching force, the classroom practices, student
characteristics (including attitudes, ambitions, and mental well-being),

and the overall school climate. The research on school science literacy
achievement has recently been increasing (e.g., Beese & Liang, 2010;
Belland et al., 2017; Holzberger et al., 2020).
The No Child Left Behind Act of 2002 required that states conduct
annual assessments of learning outcomes and that schools be evaluated
based on the results of these assessments. The Common Core standards
in mathematics and Next Generation Science Standards are national
standards for teaching mathematics and science in the United States.
Through this coherent framework, programs are designed to generate
greater interest in science learning among students (e.g., National
Research Council, 2013). Although assessment outcomes may not be the
best indicators of school achievement level (Chingos & West, 2015),
schools still attempt to improve these outcomes to enhance public perceptions of school quality (Dee, 1998; Heck, 2000). The Every Student
Succeeds Act of 2015, which was the successor of the No Child Left
Behind Act, includes support for STEM education. As a result, 48 states
and the District of Columbia include, or plan to include, learning outcomes in their school accountability system (Data Quality Campaign,
2019), although significant differences exist concerning the relative
emphasis placed on these outcomes (Achieve, 2019). According to Next
Generation Science Standards Lead State (2013), “Science is at the heart
of this country’s ability to continue to innovate, lead, and create the jobs
of the future. All students—whether they become technicians in a hospital, workers in a high-tech manufacturing facility, or Ph.D. researchers—must have a solid K-12 science education” (p. xiii). Understanding

the context in which schools can excel in science literacy is a matter of
great importance for schools in the United States.
Scientific literacy is considered a critical competency for students not
only in the U.S. but also around the globe. As Marginson et al. (2013)
indicated, STEM education is globally recognized as a national interest
for economic competitiveness and social well-being. Governments
worldwide expand STEM policy and programs to enhance STEM
(Freeman et al., 2015). Freeman et al. (2019) reported that participation
in STEM was highest in some western European countries (e.g., United
Kingdom, Germany, Sweden) and East Asian countries (e.g., Singapore,
China, South Korea). Many high STEM performance countries have
national STEM policies addressing economic needs and international
competitiveness. For example, the United Kingdom developed Science &
Innovation Investment Framework 2004–2014 (HM Treasury, 2004)
and Industrial Strategy: Building a Britain Fit for the Future (HM Government, 2018).
Similarly, Australia developed the policy Australia 2030: Prosperity
Through Innovation (Innovation & Science Australia, 2017, 2018).
Western European countries (e.g., France, Ireland, and Spain) adopted
STEM policies to address school-based mathematics and science teaching, participation, and performance (Freeman et al., 2019). Similar
policies have also been developed in East and South East Asian countries
(e.g., China, Japan, and South Korea), such as Science and Technology
Basic Law in Japan and Science and Technology Development Goal in
China. All these examples illustrate that government STEM policy in
different nations aims to focus on investment in global science by
reforming the curriculum, pedagogy, and learning environment of science and mathematics education in K-12 schools.
From a broader perspective on the importance of science literacy,
Schaefer (1998) stated in his keynote address at the Second International IPN-Symposium on Scientific Literacy: "Taking into account the
growing complexity of our world, caused by the opening of national
borders and by almost infinite electronic communication, and the
increasing speed of global ecological, economic and political changes,
number 1 of the educational challenges of the next century seems to be:
high flexibility, both in storing and using knowledge, and in international communication." Indeed, life in the 21st century provides challenges for education, with various information widely spread. For
instance, the crisis due to the COVID-19 pandemic revealed many inequalities and vulnerabilities in education (Valladares, 2021). UNESCO
(2020) stated nine big ideas for this post-pandemic education, with one
of these ideas related to scientific literacy: “…Ensure scientific literacy
within the curriculum. This is the right time for deep reflection on
curriculum, particularly as we struggle against the denial of scientific
knowledge and actively fight misinformation…” (p. 6). There have been
calls for increased science literacy in recent years, motivated by concerns about misinformation and conspiracy theories contradicting the
scientific evidence (e.g., global climate change) (Howell & Brossard,
2021). Therefore, science literacy has become more critical than ever in
today’s world.
Because students, teachers, and school administration are all part of
the school system, school leaders may want to understand how these
factors are related to promoting a school context associated with strong
science learning outcomes. From the pragmatic perspective of school
policy and practice, school administrators and teachers may be interested in knowing the association between school context and science
literacy achievement. Although such data do not account for student
differences in science learning outcomes, they can be used to examine
the relationships among various schoolwide factors concerning school
science literacy outcomes. Because there are many variables involved in
school success, the primary purpose of this study was to use a visualization method, a machine learning approach, to depict a comprehensive
picture for pattern classification and prediction of school science literacy
outcomes in U.S. schools.

Literature review
The use of data in educational decision-making has been a common
theme of educational reform agendas in many countries (Schildkamp &
Lai, 2012). Around the globe, data-based decision-making has been
linked to school accountability systems, which shape decisions on using
the data (Datnow et al., 2018). Because school achievement outcomes
are affected by teachers and leadership, school climate, resources, as
well as student dispositions in terms of social and emotional health
(Schneider, 2017), many studies have examined these factors as predictors of student science learning outcomes, particularly concerning
individual variations in science learning between students. For instance,
Caro et al. (2016) suggested that students with positive dispositions (e.
g., highly motivated, interested in science, and positive attitude toward
tasks) have a higher level of science literacy. Student science
self-efficacy is a critical student disposition variable that can be a vital
factor in facilitating science learning (Dang & Nylund-Gibson, 2017;
Hyytinen et al., 2018). Tytler (2007) asserted that science education
should not ignore the realities of students’ interests and feelings. A
positive disposition has been found to contribute to student enjoyment
of science learning (Alsop & Watts, 2003; Bybee & Mccrae, 2011; Ma,
2018; Renninger & Hidi, 2011). Research suggests that different
emotional states can enhance or disrupt information processing (Pekrun
et al., 2002). Negative emotions, such as anxiety, can lead to debilitating
thinking; positive emotions, such as enjoyment, can facilitate science
learning by supporting an exciting science learning environment
(Zeidner, 1998; Zembylas, 2004).
Having recognized the importance of student dispositions toward
science learning outcomes, researchers have explored the factors
contributing to positive dispositions toward science learning (Linn et al.,
2012). Hasher (2010) suggested that student learning involves teaching
and learning practices that draw out various emotional states, such as
frustration or happiness. Other studies have found that teacher support,
instruction strategies, and inquiry-based hands-on activities are positively associated with student enjoyment, motivation, and satisfaction
(Bulunuz et al., 2012; Renninger & Bachrach, 2015). Indeed, classroom
environment and teacher characteristics are commonly examined in the
research on learning. For instance, some researchers argue that
inquiry-based teaching is most effective when it involves a higher level
of feedback or guidance (e.g., Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007; Lazonder &
Harmsen, 2016), whereas others contend that teacher-directed instructions are more beneficial to science learning (Rosenshine, 2012;
Zhang, 2016). Teacher collaboration has been found to be positively
associated with math and reading achievement in schools (Goddard
et al., 2007). Dorman & Fraser (2009) examined ten dimensions of the
classroom environment (e.g., teacher support, involvement, task orientation, and computer use) and found that enhancing the classroom
environment can improve student outcomes. In discussing possible
reasons for the low science literacy of Slovak students, Bellová et al.
(2018) found that one reason was that teachers did not help students to
develop non-traditional solutions due to a lack of sufficient knowledge
in natural science subjects. Studies conducted over the past 30 years
have provided clear evidence that the classroom environment is an
important predictor of student learning (Dorman & Fraser, 2009; Kerr
et al., 2006).
Another line of research that aims to identify predictors of student
science learning achievement has focused on teacher beliefs and perceptions regarding the school environment; these beliefs and perceptions shape instructional strategies, which in turn influence the learning
experiences of students (Holzberger et al., 2014; Huppert & So, 2013;
Woolfolk et al., 2009). It has been found that teacher perceptions
regarding school are linked to teacher well-being, motivation, job
satisfaction, and organizational commitment, each of which is associated with dedication and effective teaching (e.g., Collie & Martin, 2015;
Holzberger et al., 2014; McLean & McDonald Connor, 2015). In addition, teacher contextual beliefs, such as perceived autonomy support,

Learning outcomes have also been found to be associated with teacher
qualifications; the most effective teachers have high academic degrees,
teach in the areas in which they are trained (Fitchett & Heafner, 2018;
Vesterinen & Aksela, 2013), and participate in professional development

In light of a literature review, there seem to be three common
themes. First, most studies focused on predictors of student science literacy achievement, suggesting that school science literacy achievement
may be connected to factors on students, teachers, leadership, and
school resources. Second, these studies are typically theory-driven and
use a hypothesis testing approach by including a few theoretically
relevant variables. This approach provides a limited view of school as a
macro learning environment. Third, few studies use machine learning
methods incorporating many variables in the analysis to provide a more
comprehensive view of complex relationships among various factors.
More specifically, few studies have used visual analytics in educational
research. This particular type of data analytics may provide an alternative mechanism for investigating education issues of interest. Vieira
et al. (2018) suggested that traditional data visualization methods, such
as the scatter (or correlation) plot, histogram, and boxplot, are most
commonly used; more advanced or novel visual analytic methods are
rarely utilized. Thus, using more advanced visual analytics in assessing
school science literacy outcomes based on high-dimensional data may
aid school leaders and teachers set priorities for program design and
development.

Methodological framework
Within educational research, visual analytics based on highdimensional data has not gained much attention as a component of
multivariate data analytics. However, similar in business and medicine,
there has been significant growth in applying artificial intelligence
within the education field in recent years (Chen et al., 2020; Roll &
Wylie, 2016). This includes intelligent tutoring (Hwang et al., 2020),
machine learning as a classification system (Musso & Cascallar, 2009),
and the use of machine learning as a predictive system (Musso & Cascallar, 2009; Musso et al., 2020). Visual analytics is becoming an
essential part of data analytics and machine learning. Visual analytics of
high-dimensional data can help enhance the interpretability of results
from the data analytics, which provides a convenient way for school
administrators and teachers to understand the relationships among
variables in the data. Visually interpreting the results also aids in understanding complex data and allows for quick identification of patterns
for classification and prediction.
Thus, visual analytics can help educational researchers and practitioners quickly present large data sets more simply, creating a pictorial
story to understand complex issues. Thomas & Cook (2005) define visual
analytics as the science of analytical reasoning via visual interfaces.
Similarly, Vieira et al. (2018) define visual learning analytics as visualization techniques to understand educational phenomena. It provides
a common language and platform among various educational stakeholders, allowing for easier and more efficient data discussions. Visual
analytics can help schools accelerate stakeholders’ understanding of the
data, gain more insights, and enable school leaders to make quicker
decisions. As the adage says, “A picture is worth a thousand words.”
Data visualization can help research consumers grasp the key content of
the study’s results and remember the information more quickly ( From
the editors, 2018). Therefore, with the rapid evolution of visual analytics and data science, using advanced methods to visualize quantitative data in education combined with traditional statistical methods is
imperative. Identifying patterns using visual analytics for classification
and prediction could provide information for positive intervention and
aid in determining the teaching practices that positively impact student
learning and school achievement (Musso et al., 2013).
An important contribution of visual analytics, a form of artificial
intelligence and machine learning, is its ability to build visual models of
the context in which schools can achieve better outcomes. One such
visual analytics method is MDS preference analysis.1 It is a visual analytics tool that can investigate relationships among variables and differences in preferences for these variables. It also provides a visual
analysis of many variables when there is a small ratio of cases to variables. Nevertheless, a lack of conceptual and methodological understanding of machine learning hinders its use among educational
researchers. Machine learning has its own conceptual and methodological framework that can be useful for looking more effectively at
one’s data. In a recent article, Alyahyan & Düstegor (2020) proposed a
data analytics framework for applying data mining techniques.
Following this framework, the present study used a visual analytics
technique to investigate and make sense of complex educational data for
teachers and school administrators.

Theoretical framework
From the framework of educational theories, this study is motivated
by Lewin and Murray’s work and social cognitive theory (Bandura,
1997). Lewin (1936) indicated that the interaction between the environment and personal characteristics of individuals determines human
behavior so that B = f(P, E). This Lewinian formula stresses the need for
research that considers behavior, B, as a function of the environment, E,
and the individual, P. Following this approach, Murray (1938) suggested
a needs-press model, in which personal needs, such as motivational
characteristics, represent tendencies to achieve specific goals, and the
environmental press provides conditions that either support or hinder
the expression of personal needs. Similarly, the social cognitive theory
focuses on learning and teaching through different forms of social
interaction, which is highly affective. Students, teachers, and schools
must interact to achieve the optimal outcomes of science education.
These theories imply that students’ cognitive and affective learning
outcomes are associated with their perceptions of the psychosocial
characteristics of schools or classrooms. For instance, after reviewing 40
previous studies on science education, Fraser (1994) indicated that an
association between learning outcomes and perceptions of the learning
environment had been replicated with various cognitive and affective
measures, learning environments, and samples. Based on these theoretical frameworks, it is possible to conduct visual analytics to explore
whether schools can achieve higher learning outcomes when there is a
stronger fit between the learning environment and what is required for
schools to achieve better. That is, science literacy outcomes depend not
only on the school learning environment but also on the match between
students’ preferences and the learning environment. For instance, if
schools want to achieve high-level science literacy, this would not occur
if the learning environment in schools does not provide any efforts to
stimulate students’ self-efficacy or enjoyment of science learning. In a
sense, there is a lack of fit between what is required for better science
literacy and the school environment.

variables about science learning (Soltani, 2020), the effects of a few
specific variables (e.g., motivating science classes) may not provide the

learning environment as possible, particularly if school administrators
need to consider many factors when making decisions about the school
environment to achieve the best science learning outcomes. In doing so,
we hoped to determine the most salient components congruent with
science literacy outcomes. We recognized that science literacy
achievement is an individual student characteristic, and most variation
is between students in the same school. However, there is also substantial, reliable variation between schools (Ma et al., 2018). From a
policy perspective, actions can be taken to improve science literacy
outcomes in a school. Second, of particular interest is that this study
used visual analytics to provide a visual analysis of school contexts in
which science literacy outcomes are enhanced.

Method
The design of this study was based on the framework developed by
Alyahyan & Dustegor (2020). This section describes the six proposed
stages and explains the design decisions made during each visual analytics step.
Data source
The data used in this study were drawn from the PISA 2015. PISA
operates on a 3-year cycle, and achievement areas are assessed in each
cycle, but one specific area is emphasized each cycle. In 2015, science
performance was the focus of the assessment. According to PISA, science
literacy is “the ability to engage in reasoned discourse about science and
technology, which requires the competencies to: explain phenomena
scientifically, evaluate and design scientific enquiry, and interpret data
and evidence scientifically” (OECD, 2017a, p22). In this study, 2015
PISA data from 238 U.S. schools were used. PISA administered student,
teacher, and principal questionnaires in addition to assessment data.

the student, teacher, and principal questionnaires of PISA 2015. The
variables were categorized in the following manner: variables derived
from the school principal questionnaire, variables derived from the

questionnaire included: school resources, school climate, school leadership, the quantity of teaching staff, school type, school responsibility,
and extra-curricular activities. Variables derived from the teacher
questionnaire included: educational resources, job satisfaction, school
leadership, teaching and assessment practices, and teaching and teacher
collaboration. Variables derived from the student questionnaire
included: student disposition toward collaborative work, interest in

Data processing and statistical analysis
We first aggregated the student and teacher data to the school level
to create school-level variables in processing the data. We then checked
for outliers in the data to ensure all variables’ values were within a
reasonable range. No outliers were found in these variables. Next, all the
continuous variables were standardized with a mean of 0 and a standard
deviation of 1 to be on the same scale. Finally, all continuous variables
were scored with a high score indicating a higher level of characteristic
as assessed by that variable. For example, a high score for inquiry-based
teaching as reported by students indicated a higher level of average
inquiry-based teaching perceived at the school level. A high score for job
satisfaction reported by teachers indicated a higher level of average job

Multidimensional scaling (MDS) is an advanced data visualization
method that visualizes proximity data among variables in a twodimensional space. With the advent of data mining and data analytics,
MDS is considered part of visual analytics and machine learning and
works as a nonlinear dimension reduction technique (Buja et al., 2008).
Given high-dimensional data v1, ….., vN €ℝk, MDS attempts to find the
variable configuration in a low-dimensional Euclidean space by
embedding variables v = 1, ….., N, to a configuration in such a way that
estimated distances from the MDS model approximates observed distances among variables.
One of the MDS visual analytics is MDS preference analysis, which is
a visual method of investigating relationships among variables as well as
individual (or school) differences with respect to its “fit” or “preference”
for those variables. These relationships are visualized by plotting both
relationships among variables and individuals (or schools) on the same
plot, allowing us to visually examine the relational patterns embedded
in the plot. MDS preference analysis is closely related to biplot (Gower
et al., 2011), a joint display of rows and columns in a two-dimensional
space. The row coordinates are plotted as points in a biplot, and the
column coordinates are plotted as vectors. The biplot is derived from
matrix decomposition. The data matrix Y(n,m) can be decomposed into
matrix A(n,q), a matrix of n variable coordinates with q dimension, and
matrix B`(q,m), a matrix of m subject coordinates with q dimension. When
q = 2, the data matrix is exactly the scaler product of AB`. When q > 2,
AB` is approximately equal to the data matrix, and the plot is an
approximate biplot since the first two columns of A and B best approximate the high-dimensional data in two-dimensional space (Gabriel,
1981).

Results
The results of the biplot from the MDS preference analysis are shown
in Fig. 1. Examining the graph, we can identify the following patterns.
First, schools were well distributed around the four quadrants, with
some schools being further from the center than others. This indicated
that these schools were more closely matched with features represented
by variables in that quadrant. For example, the schools outside the circle2 in Quadrant 1 were more reflective of variables in the same quadrant, such as more shortages of educational staff, educational material
shortages, or teacher behaviors hindering learning.
Similarly, schools outside the circle in Quadrant 4 were more
reflective of higher science literacy outcomes, student science selfefficacy, or the proportion of science teachers. Second, variables were
well distributed amongst the four quadrants. However, the variables in
Quadrant 3 did not show any vector length, suggesting that these variables may not fit the model well. In contrast, variables in Quadrants 1, 2,
and 4 had relatively longer vector lengths, indicating that these variables fit the model relatively well, particularly variable “school science
literacy outcome” in Quadrant 4. In addition, the variables in the same
quadrant tended to be more associated with each other than those from
other quadrants, notably the opposite quadrants. For instance, higher
levels of individualized teaching, the proportion of science teachers,
student science self-efficacy, creative extra-curricular activities, and
enjoyment of science learning was more associated with school science
literacy outcomes.
All these results suggest that schools with more characteristics represented in Quadrant 4 tended to have higher levels of positive science
literacy outcomes than schools in other quadrants, particularly those in
Quadrant 1. To verify this finding, we first classified each school into one
of these four quadrants. The result of this classification is shown in
Table 2. One school did not belong to any quadrant group, suggesting
that this school was at the center of the plot. Then the one-way analysis
of variance (ANOVA) was performed on school science literacy outcomes, with school quadrant grouping as the independent variable. The
results of the ANOVA indicated that school groupings using the quadrant
could account for 46% of the variance in school science literacy outcomes. There was a significant quadrant effect, F(4233) = 50.24, p <
.001, suggesting that school science literacy outcomes differed due to
their quadrant location on the graph. The results of multiple comparisons with adjusted p values indicated that schools in Quadrant 4 differ
significantly from schools in other quadrants on science literacy (ps <
0.001), particularly schools in Quadrant 1. Also, schools in Quadrant 1
differ significantly from schools in Quadrants 2 and 3 (ps < 0.001). One
school at the center of the plot did not significantly differ from other
schools in science literacy outcomes. These results are shown using the
mean plot with a 95% confidence interval in Fig. 2. These findings
support the conclusion drawn based on visual analysis concerning
school science literacy outcomes.

Quadrant 0 indicates the center of the plot, and the circle indicates the mean
score with a 95% confidence interval.

Discussion
The primary aim of this study was to use visual analytics to examine
the school context in which schools can achieve enhanced science literacy outcomes in the United States. This paper reported an attempt to
systematically use visual analytics to map school science literacy outcomes in the learning environment. Visual analytics is part of machine
learning and data science, and it has increasingly become an important
machine learning tool to gain insights into processes and mechanisms of
learning outcomes. The main research question the current study
attempted to address was, “Under what conditions do schools achieve
better learning outcomes concerning science literacy?” Based on results
of visual analytics using MDS preference analysis, the key findings were
that schools that were more reflective of a higher level of individualized
teaching, student science self-efficacy, student achievement motivation,
student enjoyment of science, creative extra science activities, and
qualified teaching force (i.e., teacher who majored in science, fully
certified science teachers) had better science learning outcomes. In this
study sample, only 28% of schools were classified in this group. This
group of schools had much higher levels of science literacy than the rest
of the schools, particularly the schools in Quadrant 1. Findings from the
analysis of variance further confirmed this result.
The findings of this study highlight three key points when discussing
science literacy outcomes in schools. First, although student science
learning outcomes are associated with student-level predictors (e.g.,
student-reported learning environment) (Soltani, 2020), teacher-level
predictors (e.g., teacher characteristics) (Rubie-Davies et al., 2012),
and school-level predictors (e.g., pupil-teacher ratio) (Dearden et al.,
2002), factors related to student motivation, enjoyment of science, and
science self-efficacy provide an essential context for schools to achieve
better. While this finding is consistent with previous evidence on the
importance of cognitive and emotional factors (Krapp, 1999; Pekrun
et al., 2002; Ustun et al., 2022), it further suggests that K-12 education is
often emotional in nature (National School Climate Council, 2015). For
instance, Ustun et al. (2022) found that students’ enjoyment and interest
in science are the most influential predictors of science literacy in
Finland and Singapore. In addition, a quality science teaching force (e.
g., more science teachers who majored in science) tended to enhance
student dispositions toward science learning. This finding is also
consistent with previous studies (e.g., Bellová et al., 2018; Ustun et al.,
2022), indicating that teacher quality and fairness are the most prominent predictors. Thus, school leaders and teachers should pay particular
attention to students’ emotional and motivational aspects of science
learning. They should, for example, hire more quality science teachers
and implement programs that enhance student cognitive and emotional

Second, theoretically, these findings are consistent with the Lewinian
formula and Murrays’ needs-press model that considers behavior as a
function of the environment and the individual. These theories imply
that school science literacy outcomes are associated with schools and
students’ characteristics. The findings suggest that individualized instruction, extra science curriculum activities, a qualified teaching force,
and favorable student dispositions towards science learning are directly
related to better science literacy outcomes. These factors are more
closely related to better science literacy outcomes than other factors,
suggesting that these factors are the necessary school characteristics for
schools to achieve better science outcomes. However, this does not mean
that other factors are not important. Instead, it only suggests that in
comparison with these factors, the other factors may be secondary or
indirect conditions for schools to achieve better science literacy
outcomes.
From the perspective of social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1997), the
findings provide evidence about the need for joint responsibility from
students and teachers in science content learning. Students, teachers,
and the learning environment must interact together to achieve optimal
outcomes of science literacy. Since teaching and learning occur through
different forms of social interaction, learning is likely to be highly
emotional and experiential. Although science learning instructions have
commonality across schools, schools that can stimulate and sustain
students’ affective state concerning students’ self-efficacy, enjoyment,
and motivation for science learning may pave the way for obtaining a
better school science literacy outcome.
Given that research in STEM education is increasing internationally
(Li et al., 2020), the context for better school science literacy in the U.S.
may be used as a reference for designing school STEM curricula and
resourcing schools with quality teachers and materials. Since STEM
education has been an international topic over the past decade,
improving teaching and learning STEM for better science literacy has
become an economic factor around the globe (Kennedy & Odell, 2014).
Although STEM education is not new, increased efforts by policymakers
and educators reflect the need for more explicit STEM curricula in
schools around the globe to improve student achievement in STEM. For
example, student characteristics (e.g., emotions and other dispositions)
are consistently found to be related to school-level science literacy in
studies around the globe. Schools should systematically incorporate
these elements into the STEM curriculum to boost student engagement
and provide relevance to learning. Thus, all students should be part of
the STEM vision (Kennedy & Odell, 2014).
Third, as part of visual analytics and machine learning, MDS preference analysis provides an alternative way to identify patterns for
classification and prediction. As conducted in this study, the MDS
preference biplot establishes a topological map of school science
learning outcomes and their direct correlates. As a graphical representation of relationships, it captures key aspects of complex interactions
between variables; particularly, it displays the context under which
schools achieve high science literacy outcomes. Although we tend to use
mathematical equations first (e.g., hierarchical linear modeling) to
study the complex relationships among variables, this process can be
reversed with alternatives (Butner et al., 2014). Visual analytics can be
the first step used in model building. We can test the results or hypotheses derived from visual analytics with traditional statistical
modeling. For example, from our MDS preference plot, we can hypothesize that schools in Quadrant 4 would do better in science literacy
achievement than those in other quadrants. The results of the analysis of
variance supported this expectation. Thus, visual analytics can aid in
testing one’s theory via topological plotting or mapping.

Limitations
The current study supports visual analytics as a machine learning
approach in educational research. Findings from the data analysis provide a better understanding of conditions under which schools can
achieve better science literacy outcomes in the U.S. However, there are
limitations in the study that should be acknowledged. First, school science literacy outcomes only showed relationships with factors that fit
the first two dimensions well. That is not to say that the other factors are
unnecessary; they may fit well in other dimensions and show relationships among them. However, that does not directly impact relationships
with school science literacy outcomes. Thus, the first two dimensions are
the most important visual representations of key relationships. Second,
although the current study included many variables based on principal,
teacher, and student questionnaires, we could have used more variables.
While machine learning may offer the greatest advantage in classification and prediction, it is also important to stress the role of theory in
developing complex visual representations.
Conclusion
Using visual analytics of MDS preference analysis as an unsupervised
machine learning approach, we investigated the context in which
schools can achieve better science literacy outcomes. Methodologically,
it is a novel application of MDS modeling of school science education
outcomes based on high-dimensional data. It effectively communicates
research results via a visual technique to teachers and school leaders.
