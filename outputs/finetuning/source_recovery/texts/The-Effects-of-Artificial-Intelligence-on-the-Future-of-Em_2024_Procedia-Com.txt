The Effects of Artificial Intelligence on the Future of Employment: 
Looking for a Trend from a Literature Review 
Hicham Sadoka, Hasna Chaibib, Abdellah Chehric,*, Rachid Saadaned 
a Mohammed V Univerity,  Rabat, Morocco.  
b  Polydisciplinary Faculty, Errachidia, Molay Ismail University Morocco.

Abstract Each new wave of technological progress sparks debates about the effects of automation on the future of 
employment. Current debates on artificial intelligence (AI) and employment are reminiscent of those raised by 
mechanization in the 19th century, the generalization of electricity, and the introduction of computers in the 20th 
century: some consider new technologies as a way to relieve workers of the most challenging tasks, and others are 
alarmed by the imminent threat to employment. This article aims to contribute to the ongoing debate on the potential 
changes that may arise from the recent emergence of Generative AI in job markets. It is based on a historical 
analysis of technological revolutions and a literature review of technology's impact on employment. The purpose of 
this study is not to gather general statistics but rather to analyze potential changes and help design suitable policy 
responses. This analysis will also consider the possible impact on job quality. The study emphasizes the potential 
implications for various professional categories but does not predetermine the outcomes of technological 
transition. The decision to incorporate such technologies is driven by humans, and it is their responsibility to guide 
the transition process.

1. Introduction 
In the wake of the Dartmouth conference in 1956, the field of artificial intelligence has undergone a series of 
phases characterized by both fervor and disillusionment, persistently challenging the boundaries of human 
capabilities. Through the persistence of its initial endeavor, the field of artificial intelligence (AI) has yielded 
tangible achievements, including triumphs in chess, go, and the comprehension of natural language.  
Defining artificial intelligence is not easy. Since its origins, it has always constituted a border, constantly pushed 
back. In fact, it designates less a well-defined field of research than a program, based around an ambitious objective: 
understanding how human cognition works and reproducing it; create cognitive processes comparable to those of 
humans. The field is therefore naturally extremely vast, both in terms of the technical procedures used and the 
disciplines involved: mathematics, computer science, cognitive sciences, etc.   
The assessment of the effects of this technical progress on employment has long been a controversial subject 
among economists [1]. We can distinguish two main categories of studies: The first essentially includes 
macroeconomic work, which is based on the fact that technology contributes to increasing productivity by 
substituting capital for labor, which has a negative influence on the quantity of employment, but on the other hand, it 
also contributes to reduce costs, expand markets and increase the diversity of products and services, which has a 
positive influence on growth and employment. From this perspective, the results in terms of employment will be 
positive provided that the indirect effects on growth outweigh the direct effects of productivity gains. In the current 
context of sluggish growth and stagnant productivity gains, it is not surprising that this category of studies no longer 
appears at the forefront in debates on the future of employment [2];  
In the second category of studies, technology in general, AI in particular, is no longer considered as a black box 
in which we are only interested in the inputs and outputs. It is a matter of opening and exploring this black box, of 
being interested in the construction of innovations and technological trajectories [3]. It is in this category that we can 
place the evolutionary perspective, which is interested in long cycles in economic development and is based on the 
concept of a technoeconomic paradigm [4]. This article is situated in this evolutionary perspective. After this first 
introductory section,  
We briefly explore in Section 2 the highlights of previous technological revolutions in an attempt to draw some 
points of reference from the current one. Section 3 presents the current technological transition as a tipping point in 
the techno-economic paradigm, and in section 4 we synthesize a literature review on the effects of technology on 
employment. Finally, in section 5, we draw up an overall trend of the impacts of AI on employment before being 
able to conclude.  
2. Literature Review and Lessons from the Past  
The research on the effects of technology on employment gained momentum in the early 1980s, a period marked 
by the aftermath of the oil crises of 1973 and 1974 and the advent of personal computers and microprocessors in 
industrial systems. This era saw the publication of a seminal study that analyzed the crisis of Fordism, a theory 
developed by the school of regulation [5]. 
It constitutes a break with the classic approach of the spill theory of Alfred Sauvy [6]. The authors distinguish 
several significant families of technological innovations and study which branches of activity will be affected and to 
what degree, which qualifications are threatened or strengthened, and the foreseeable impacts on employment. 
According to their estimates, the direct effects on employment are generally negative; a positive effect can only be 
expected from indirect effects, such as creating new economic activities based on the latest technologies. The main 
criticism addressed to the estimation method developed by these authors lies in the establishment of a direct causal 
link between, on the one hand, the potential of technologies and, on the other hand, the effects on qualifications and 
employment. This same criticism can be leveled at the study by Frey and Osborne [7].  Brainard and Fullgrabe's 
review of research [8] yielded three key conclusions: 
• Many studies overestimate the rate of technology diffusion and its productive efficiency while 
underestimating the social and organizational constraints that can slow down or mitigate its effects [9]. 
• The real impact of technology on employment is likely to be small compared to other factors, such as growth 
fluctuations and changes in world trade [10].

Technology can generate employment opportunities in specific industries and professions while causing job 
losses in other sectors, though varying degrees across different regions or countries. Ciarli et al. [12] conducted a 
more comprehensive study that confirmed these conclusions. In the 1990s, there was a shift in focus from studying 
the impact of technology on employment to examining the organizational changes that occurred as information and 
communication technologies became more prevalent. This shift focused on flexibility, skill development, work 
intensification, and restructuring [13]. The advent of the "new economy" in the early 2000s sparked a surge in 
research on digitalization and employment. During that period, the rapid spread of the Internet in businesses 
represented a significant technological advancement [14].  
The European Commission conducts interdisciplinary research to assess the social impacts of digital technologies 
[15]. In conclusion, digital media serves as an "amplifier of trends." He highlights that for technological investments 
to impact productivity positively, they must be accompanied by effective organizational changes. These changes 
should primarily focus on worker participation, decentralization of responsibilities, and autonomy in work.  
It was also in the 2000s that the controversy over the link between the development of digital technologies and 
the increase in productivity was revived [16]. This link was called into question in 1987 by the American economist 
Robert Solow and his famous formula, the Solow paradox: "We see computers everywhere, except in productivity 
statistics" [17].  
Today, the controversy is based on observing an enormous gap between the productive performances attributed to 
digital technologies, considered in isolation, and the effective measurement of the productivity gains they would 
bring [18].  
Over the past twenty years, productivity gains have been very modest in OECD countries despite significant 
investments in innovation. Several explanations can be put forward. The time lag between the start of the 
implementation of innovations and the moment when they reach their optimal efficiency regime is 
underestimated mainly by technology designers, as is the importance of implementing organizational 
changes. Current methods of measuring productivity are also ill-suited to an increasingly immaterial economy and 
new business models. Furthermore, the literature on the digital revolution maintains confusion between productive 
efficiency from a performance perspective and productivity from an economic perspective. In any case, the extent of 
productivity gains expected from the new generation of digital technologies remains uncertain [19].  
The spill theory is an economic theory in which technical progress improving productivity generates a transfer 
(spill) of jobs from one sector of activity to another. This thesis is formulated by the French economist and 
demographer Alfred Sauvy (1898-1990).  
3. Technological Transition: Tipping Point in a Techno-Economic Paradigm  
There are alarmist predictions about the effects of AI on employment that still rely on a simplistic understanding 
of work. In this scenario, the concept of continuous socio-economic development regimes, characterized by 
transition phases between different paradigms, highlights the need to consider long cycles deterministically. It also 
emphasizes the connection between technology's impact on employment and the significance of tensions and 
political decisions during transition periods [20].  
This techno-economic concept was developed in Brighton (Science Policy Research Unit, SPRU) and Maastricht 
(Maastricht Economic Research Institute on Technology, MERIT) in the 1980s under the aegis of Christopher 
Freeman, Luc Soete, and Carlota Perez, mainly. This cannot be reduced to tasks that can be more or less replaceable 
by intelligent machines. It is the result of organizational choices determined by political choices, social relations, the 
strategies of economic actors, the functioning of the labor market, and the adaptation of institutions. Neither the 
outcome nor the transition duration is predetermined by the technologies [21].  
Carlota Perez proposed a reformulation of this concept [4]. She is particularly interested in the specific role of 
speculative bubbles and financial crises in developing techno-economic paradigms, which she also calls "great 
surges." The novelty of this approach lies in the succession in each large wave of periods of installation of tipping 
points and moments of deployment. Figure 1 describes the stages of a great wave more precisely and allows us to 
interpret better what we today call the emergence of AI.

In the techno-economic paradigm based on AI, the initial trigger is the appearance of the computational power of 
microprocessors drilling big data from the beginning of this millennium. Still, we must wait until the second decade 
of this millennium to see this new wave burst into economic activity. This irruption phase rapidly spreads to all 
branches of activity and affects a growing proportion of jobs. Then comes a phase of excitement, euphoria, and even 
frenzy, with innovations going in all directions, which are accelerating and benefitting from a context of financial 
speculation and market deregulation, linked to the weakening of States in the face of a new environment that they no 
longer control. In this context, a first warning shot comes to temper the frenzy, with the bursting of an 
overestimation of the effects of AI, a harbinger of a financial crisis following the bubble that has formed around it. 
This crisis, separated by a brief renewal of frenzy, marks the tipping point between the period of installation and the 
period of deployment of the technical-economic paradigm, between an artificial and bubbling prosperity and more 
stable prosperity, which synergizes the potential of technologies and the transformation of society. The duration of 
the shift depends much more on socio-political conditions than on the performance and availability of new 
technologies.   
The AI revolution is not simply a new industrial revolution. Instead, it represents a complex and uncertain 
transition from the installation to the deployment of the technico-economic paradigm. The institutional, political, and 
social conditions of this transition are not yet perfectly aligned, making it difficult for the excitement surrounding AI 
to be fully realized in the socio-economic domain. The current forecasts on the impacts of AI on employment often 
fluctuate between contrasting perspectives, which is why. They refer to research conducted in the 1990s that focused 
on examining the connections between computerization and employment.   
4. The Effects of Technology on Employment: A Literature Review  
The current estimates regarding the impacts of technology on employment cover a wide range of perspectives. 
Frey and Osborne [7] predict that approximately 50% of jobs will face significant threats in the next twenty years. 
According to Arntz, Gregory, and Zierahn (22), approximately 10% of jobs would face threats, while half of the 
current jobs would undergo significant transformations without completely disappearing. Other recent studies lie in 
the middle ground, but they are not as pessimistic as the most extreme estimates (refer to the Table 1). The 
significant gap can be attributed to significant differences in how technology and work transformations are analyzed, 
as well as in how work content and professions are represented [23].

Most of the studies carried out on the relationship between technology and work raise the hypothesis that the 
exponentially increasing performance of technologies suggests, over one or two decades, the possibility of 
computerizing and robotizing not only routine tasks, manual or intellectual, but also non-routine tasks, comprising 
an essential cognitive dimension or an intuitive dimension [24]. However, the technical possibilities of substitution 
are limited by what the authors justify as the existence of "engineering bottlenecks," which are the tasks for which 
the technology is not yet competitive enough compared to human beings. They thus identify three types of 
bottlenecks: unstructured or poorly predictable perception and manipulation tasks in singular, cramped, or disordered 
environments; creative intelligence tasks, where robots enrich human creativity without replacing it; social 
intelligence tasks: relational or emotional tasks, negotiation, persuasion, personal care and non-codifiable 
conversation [25]. The researchers calculate the probability of substitution of human labor by technology, taking into 
account, in each profession, the proportion of substitutable or nonsubstitutable tasks [14]. Nonsubstitutable tasks 
exist for at least one of the three engineering bottlenecks. The more interchangeable tasks a job has, the higher its 
probability of disappearance.   
Except for a literal interpretation of Frey and Osborne's estimates [7], none of the studies on this theme indicate a 
significant decrease in job opportunities. However, all indications point to substantial changes in professions and 
careers. Is this something new? When examining the research findings on the correlation between work and 
technology since the early 1980s [15], it becomes evident that the idea of replacing humans with machines has 
emerged multiple times and subsequently diminished. In light of the emergence of AI, what vision of work emerges 
from these studies?   
5. The Effects of AI on Employment: Global Estimate  
There is a divergence of opinions regarding the rise of artificial intelligence and its impact on the job market. The 
scholarly works analyzed on this theme above do not perceive these technologies as a genuine threat. The United 
Nations' International Labor Organization (ILO) has conducted a study on the impact of the latest wave of generative 
AI, specifically ChatGPT. According to their estimates, the majority of jobs and industries will only experience 
partial automation. According to the report, the primary effect of this technology is not expected to be job

The ILO study was conducted in 59 countries, and the analysis reveals significant variations among countries at 
various stages of development. Therefore, it is evident that generative AI has the potential to impact 5.5% of total 
employment in affluent countries, whereas in low-income countries, this percentage increases to a mere 0.4%. The 
ILO has studied all countries and found that AI is expected to create jobs, regardless of their level of development. 
This increase in job creation is connected to the existing economic contexts and technological gaps in these 
countries.

Despite these significant differences, the analysis of the ILO study reveals an almost equivalent potential number 
of jobs created by AI in all these countries. This suggests that with the right policies in place, this new wave of 
technological transformation could offer significant benefits to developing countries. Regarding job titles, the 
professions most impacted by these platforms will be office workers, with almost a quarter of tasks considered very 
exposed and more than half presenting an average level of exposure. In other professional categories, including 
managers, professionals, and technicians, only a small part of the tasks will be affected, while around a quarter have 
a medium degree of impact. This effect becomes even more evident when we present the effects of AI on jobs for 
each gender. As shown in the Figure 2, in high-income countries, jobs likely to be affected by AI constitute 8.5% of 
women's employment, compared to 3.9% of men's employment. In addition, the share of jobs with high automation 
potential is visibly higher among women than men, regardless of income bracket.  
Considering the limitations of the data, it is important to interpret the specific numbers provided in the Fig.3 as 
representations of a broader pattern in the impact of AI on employment. The global estimate derived from microdata 
analysis of specific countries aligns with the trends already observed in scholarly literature on this matter. To clarify, 
it can be said that richer countries are more likely to experience both greater disruptions caused by the technological 
transition resulting from AI, as well as greater overall benefits from this process. The global estimates also support 
the significant gender effect observed in the microdata. When we analyze the estimate by dividing it into

The development of generative AI is unpredictable, but discussions about its impact on employment primarily 
focus on its current capabilities and future prospects. Some skeptics argue that AI is merely a "stochastic parrot" 
[27]. However, recent literature indicates that there is a growing capacity to carry out new and complex tasks, which 
demonstrates certain types of early reasoning [28], [29].  
The phenomenon under examination extends beyond a mere replacement of occupations by intelligent, material, 
or virtual machines. Rather, it encompasses a profound metamorphosis of employment structures, which 
is accompanied by noteworthy societal concerns.

6.  Conclusion   
The evolutionary approach makes it possible to characterize the current period of "AI transition" not as a new 
technological revolution but as the tipping point between the establishment phase and the deployment phase of the 
techno-economic paradigm based on information digitized and networks. The deployment of this paradigm is 
conditioned by the implementation of significant transformations in society, particularly in the organization of work 
and the labor market; it must lead to a new era of shared prosperity.  
Recent studies evaluating AI's future impacts on employment refer to the employment relationship model 
constructed during the previous paradigm. However, AI contributes to the erosion of this model by favoring the 
multiplication of new forms of employment. Its socio-economic impacts will largely depend on the way in which its 
dissemination is managed: questions of the balance of power, the voice of workers affected by labor market 
adjustments, respect for existing standards and rights, and Adequate use of national social protection and vocational 
training systems will be crucial elements in managing the deployment of AI in the workplace. Without appropriate 
policies, there is a risk that only certain well-positioned actors and countries will be able to benefit from the 
transition, while the costs for affected workers could be brutal.
