A B S T R A C T
Effective use of technology is central to digitally mediated education. Using cross-sectional PIRLS 2021 data from 
a nationally representative sample of 1657 U.S. fifth-grade students across 78 schools, this study examines how 
school-level digital support factors predict and moderate reading achievement. Results indicate that teacher 
technological support is positively associated with reading achievement, whereas greater school internet access is 
negatively associated when instructional use is unstructured. However, early instruction in locating information 
within texts moderates this relationship, enabling students to benefit more from internet access. Results further 
suggest that the effectiveness of school digital resources is contingent on early digital literacy development and 
aligned parental support. Overall, the results imply that digital access alone is insufficient to improve literacy 
outcomes; coordinated instructional practices, early skill development, and coherent home–school engagement 
are essential for advancing digital equity in elementary education.
1. Introduction
Equitable access to technology has become a central concern in 
contemporary education, particularly in the wake of the COVID-19 
pandemic, which accelerated the shift toward digital and blended 
learning environments. This transition has not only highlighted the 
potential of educational technologies to support learning but has also 
exposed longstanding disparities in students’ access to and engagement 
with these tools (UNESCO, 2021; Kaden, 2020). Research demonstrates 
that achieving digital equity extends beyond providing technological 
infrastructure; it also requires attention to students’ and teachers’ digital 
competencies, the quality of instructional support, and the involvement 
of families in the learning process (Di Pietro and Casta˜ 
no Mu˜ 
noz, 2025). 
These dimensions interact in complex ways to shape foundational aca-
demic skills such as reading, where students’ ability to navigate both 
digital and traditional texts can significantly influence their learning 
outcomes.
The Progress in International Reading Literacy Study (PIRLS) 2021 
offers a valuable lens through which to examine these dynamics. By 
assessing fourth grade reading outcomes across multiple countries, 
including the U.S., PIRLS 2021 allows for an exploration of how home-
and school-based digital factors contribute to student achievement.
While previous research emphasizes that the way teachers and students 
use educational technology has a stronger influence on engagement and 
achievement than mere access (Balalle, 2024; Teng and Wang, 2021; 
Timotheou et al., 2023), the effectiveness of these technologies depends 
critically on how they are integrated into instruction. Ng (2012) and 
Fraillon et al. (2020) argue that teacher technological support and 
competency—defined as the ability to deploy digital tools purposefully 
in teaching—are most effective when aligned with students’ digital and 
cognitive readiness. Similarly, school-level factors such as internet 
connectivity, although essential for access, may inadvertently promote 
superficial information processing or distraction if not accompanied by 
meaningful instructional guidance (Dontre, 2021).
Student-level digital literacy further shapes reading achievement in 
digitally mediated environments. Skills such as locating, evaluating, and 
synthesizing information from digital texts are particularly critical for 
effective learning (Guan and Lin, 2025; Leu et al., 2015). For example, 
Guan and Lin (2025) demonstrate that students’ engagement with dig-
ital texts is influenced by task instructions and perceptions of text 
credibility, with proficient navigation and critical evaluation skills 
enabling deeper information processing and higher reading perfor-
mance. Yet, the ways in which these student-level competencies interact 
with school-based technological support, and the potential moderating

3.3. Population and sampling
The PIRLS 2021 dataset comprises information from 400,000 stu-
dents spanning 57 countries and eight benchmarked education systems. 
This offers a robust and comparative data source on educational inputs 
and outcomes (Mullis et al., 2023; IEA, 2023). In the U.S., PIRLS 2021 
deviated from the traditional fourth-grade target due to 
COVID-19-related disruptions. Instead, the U.S. administered the 
assessment to fifth-grade students in the fall of 2021, revising the sam-
pling frame and assessment design accordingly (Mullis et al., 2023). 
PIRLS 2021 employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design 
in the U.S. to ensure national representativeness and statistical effi-
ciency (IEA, 2023). In the first stage, schools were selected using 
probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling based on the 2021 Na-
tional Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) school frame. Strat-
ification was applied across key variables, including U.S. Census region, 
school type (public or private), religious affiliation, school poverty level, 
locale (urban, suburban, rural), racial or ethnic composition, and state. 
In the second stage, an equal-probability sample of two classrooms 
per selected school was drawn. Very small classrooms (fewer than 10 
students) were combined into “pseudo-classrooms” to stabilize sampling 
weights, and all students in the selected classrooms or pseudo-
classrooms were assessed. This rigorous sampling framework ensured 
that PIRLS results for the U.S. would be generalizable to the national 
population of fifth-grade students and supported the estimation of valid 
population-level statistics (IEA, 2023). Although this design aimed for 
representativeness, the U.S. used fifth instead of fourth graders, and 
some schools were unable to participate due to logistical, administra-
tive, or COVID-19-related constraints (IEA, 2023; Mullis et al., 2023), 
which may introduce minor selection bias. However, the nationally 
representative U.S. sample consisted of 1657 students from 78 schools, 
with comprehensive contextual data collected through standardized 
instruments.
3.4. Measures
3.4.1. Outcome variable
The outcome variable for this study is student reading achievement, 
measured through five plausible values provided in the PIRLS 2021 
dataset. These values represent random draws from a posterior distri-
bution of students’ latent ability, based on their item responses and 
background characteristics (Mullis et al., 2023). All five plausible values 
were used in the analysis following recommended procedures for 
large-scale assessment data (Mullis et al., 2023). PIRLS reading 
achievement is assessed along two broad purposes: reading for literary 
experience and reading to acquire and use information, and four 
comprehension processes: (1) retrieving, (2) inferring, (3) interpreting 
and integrating, and (4) evaluating content (Mullis et al., 2023). Scores 
are reported on a scale from 300 to 700, centered at 500, which corre-
sponds to the international average in 2001. This allows for comparisons 
across assessment cycles and education systems (Mullis et al., 2023).
3.4.2. Predictor variables
Three school-level predictors were examined to understand student 
reading achievement. The key predictors were a) teacher technological 
support (ACBG10AG), b) school internet connection (ACBG10AJ), and 
c) technologically competent staff (ACBG10AF). These variables were 
derived from the PIRLS 2021 principal questionnaire and reflect re-
sponses to the extent to which instructional quality is affected by 
shortages in technological resources. Response categories ranged from 
“Not at all (1) to A lot (4)” and were aggregated into three interpretive 
categories: “Not Affected, Somewhat Affected, and Affected A Lot”. 
These predictors are theoretically grounded in research on school-
level digital capacity and its relationship with student academic 
achievement. For example, reliable school internet connectivity is 
essential for enabling access to digital reading platforms and supporting
technology-integrated instruction (Reich, 2020). Moreover, technolog-
ical support for teachers, such as ongoing training and technical assis-
tance, is strongly associated with the effective use of digital tools in 
classrooms (Tondeur et al., 2017; Tate and Warschauer, 2022). The 
presence of competent IT staff reduces instructional disruptions and 
facilitates seamless technology integration (McKnight et al., 2016), 
reflecting the school’s technological infrastructure, which is particularly 
salient in the wake of increased digital learning during and after the 
COVID-19 pandemic (Ruloff and Petko, 2021).
3.4.3. Moderator variables
Two moderators were included in the model to investigate how the 
relationship between digital school resources and reading achievement 
is influenced by instructional practices and family engagement. These 
were grade level of teaching locating information within the text 
(ACBG14F) and school perception of parental involvement in school 
activities (ACBG11F). The variable locating information within the text 
reflects the grade level at which this skill receives major instructional 
emphasis. It was measured on an ordinal scale from “First Grade or 
Earlier to Not in These Grades”, based on principal responses. This skill 
is fundamental to reading comprehension, particularly in informational 
texts, and has been shown to significantly predict reading performance 
when introduced early (Kim et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Pearson et al., 
2020).
School perception of parental involvement in school activities was 
derived from the PIRLS school emphasis on academic success scale and 
measured principals’ perceptions of parental participation in school 
activities. This variable was categorized into “Very High, High, and Me-
dium Emphasis”, using cut scores from principal ratings. Numerous 
studies affirm the positive association between school-based parental 
engagement and student academic outcomes, especially in elementary 
education (Castro et al., 2015; Jeynes, 2018). Moreover, in digitally 
supported learning environments, strong family–school partnerships 
can enhance students’ literacy development and mitigate equity gaps 
(Fan, Williams, 2009; Shi et al., 2024). The two moderators align with 
ecological models of literacy development that emphasize the interac-
tion of school, family, and instructional practices in shaping achieve-
ment (Bronfenbrenner and Morris, 2006; Smith et al., 2021).
3.4.4. Analytical approach
A multiple linear regression model was performed in R Studio 
(version 4.5.1) to examine how school-level technology resources, in 
interaction with instructional emphasis and school perception of 
parental involvement, predict student reading achievement. Multiple 
regression is well-suited for estimating the joint and interactive effects of 
several predictors on a continuous outcome variable (Fraillon, Roˇzman, 
2025; Hayes, 2017). The model included all main effects, two-way in-
teractions, and selected three-way interactions between the predictors 
and moderators. Interaction terms tested whether the effect of each 
technology predictor differed based on the grade level student locate 
information skills and school perception of parental involvement. Spe-
cifically, the following model was specified:

From Table 1, an unexpected finding emerges regarding school 
internet access. Higher levels of internet access show a significant 
negative association with reading achievement (B = –57.07, SE = 8.78, 
t = –6.50, p < .001), supporting H 2 . This suggests that students who 
engage with unstructured or ineffective internet resources may be hin-
dering rather than supporting learning. Each unit increase in internet 
access corresponds to an approximate 57-point decline in reading scores, 
indicating that access alone is not ensuring positive outcomes without 
guided instruction.
In contrast, teacher technological support demonstrates a strong 
positive effect on reading achievement (B = 58.37, SE = 12.12, t = 4.82, 
p < .001), supporting H 1 . Students benefit substantially when teachers 
provide technological guidance and scaffolding, with each unit increase 
in support corresponding to an approximate 58-point gain in reading 
scores. These findings reinforce the importance of teacher-mediated 
technology use over unstructured access.
Results in Table 1 shows that early instruction in digital literacy 
appears to be a critical moderator of technology’s effectiveness. School 
internet access interacts positively with the grade at which students are 
being introduced to locating information within texts (B = 25.28, SE = 
3.52, t = 7.19, p < .001), supporting H 3 . Students who learn these skills 
earlier utilise internet resources more effectively, achieving approxi-
mately 25 additional points. At the same time, teacher technological 
support interacts negatively with the timing of instruction in locating 
information (B = –19.33, SE = 5.49, t = –3.52, p < .001), supporting 
H 4 , indicating that delayed instruction reduces the benefits of teacher 
support, likely because students have less time to practise and apply the 
skills.
Table 1 shows school perception of parental involvement shows 
complex effects. High parental involvement (level 4) interacts nega-
tively with teacher technological competency (B = –32.77, SE = 16.39, 
t = –2.00, p = .046), supporting H 5 . This suggests that overlapping or 
conflicting guidance from home and school diminishes the positive 
impact of teacher expertise, highlighting the need for coordinated sup-
port between home and school.
Finally, the significant three-way interaction indicates that reading
achievement reaches its highest levels when school internet access, early 
instruction in locating information, and active parental involvement all 
occur together (H 6 ). This combined model explains over 21 % of the 
variance (see Table 1), emphasising that students benefit most when 
individual, familial, and institutional supports are aligned.
The results show a nuanced pattern: students benefit most when 
teacher guidance and early digital literacy instruction combine with 
structured home support, whereas unmediated access or excessive 
parental involvement reduce effectiveness. Coordinated and timely in-
terventions across school and home drive optimal reading achievement 
in digitally mediated learning contexts.
5. Discussion
The findings of this study illustrate that digital equity in U.S. primary 
education extends far beyond technological access. It depends on the 
interplay among teacher support, student digital literacy skills, parental 
involvement, and the effective use of internet connectivity. Drawing on 
PIRLS 2021 data and grounded in ecological systems theory, the study 
offers a nuanced view of how these interrelated factors shape fifth-grade 
reading outcomes.
The strong positive effect of teacher technological support on 
reading achievement reinforces the critical role of educators in inte-
grating technology into instruction. As Tamim et al. (2011) and Balalle 
(2024) argue, the educational value of technology lies not in access 
alone, but in its pedagogical use. Yet in many U.S. schools, especially 
those with limited resources, professional development in digital 
pedagogy remains uneven (Patrick et al., 2021). Salmer´ 
on et al. (2023) 
found that instructional scaffolding and feedback via digital tools 
significantly improve reading comprehension, further emphasizing the 
importance of teacher competence in purposeful technology use. Situ-
ated within Bronfenbrenner’s ecological framework, this confirms the 
role of the classroom microsystem in supporting literacy and identifies 
teacher training as a practical policy lever.
Conversely, the negative association between school internet access 
and reading achievement shows a recurring paradox in digital educa-
tion: connectivity without instructional alignment can hinder rather 
than support effective digitally-mediated learning. Research by Dontre 
(2021) and Clinton (2019) cautions that unstructured internet use may 
lead to distraction or shallow engagement. Reich (2020) and van Dijk 
(2006) similarly warn that infrastructure improvements alone, which is 
part of the “first-level digital divide” are insufficient for promoting 
educational equity. In many districts, broadband expansion has out-
paced teacher readiness and curricular integration (Connected Nation, 
2023; Gomez, 2024), illustrating the need for a more coordinated 
approach that integrates access with instructional strategy.
The timing of instruction in locating information within texts 
emerged as a critical moderating factor. Students introduced to these 
skills in early grades were better able to leverage internet access for 
reading gains, which highlights the importance of digital literacy over 
mere connectivity (Hargittai, 2002; Selwyn, 2016). For example, Leu

et al. (2015) and Guan and Lin (2025) found that students with stronger 
navigation and evaluation skills engage more deeply with digital texts, 
resulting in improved comprehension. Given the variability in how these 
skills are taught across U.S. districts, embedding locating instruction in 
second or third grade could strengthen students’ capacity to use digital 
environments productively. This aligns with research on metacognitive 
strategy development (Rawson et al., 2000; Haverkamp and Bråten, 
2022), which suggests that such instruction is most effective when 
introduced prior to the “reading to learn” phase of upper elementary 
grades (Denton et al., 2015).
Importantly, the interaction between locating skill timing and 
teacher technological support revealed that digital pedagogy is most 
effective when matched with students’ developmental readiness. 
Teacher-led technology use had reduced impact when students lacked 
foundational navigation skills. This reflects Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) 
principle of proximal processes, where educational interventions are 
most powerful when aligned with learners’ capabilities. The finding is 
particularly relevant for one-to-one device programs, where insufficient 
attention to student skill levels may blunt the benefits of digital 
investment.
A more complex picture emerged in the interaction between parental 
involvement and teacher technological competence. Unexpectedly, high 
levels of parental involvement diminished the positive effects of teacher-
led digital instruction. This indicates that parental involvement can play 
both supportive and conflicting roles, depending on alignment with 
teacher practices and the quality of digital guidance provided at home. 
For example, parents encouraging reading apps in ways that differ from 
classroom instruction may unintentionally confuse students. As Borup 
et al. (2020) and Knopik et al. (2021) suggest, home–school alignment is 
crucial in digital contexts. Without coordination, even well-intentioned 
parental support can create cognitive or instructional dissonance. In the 
U.S., where parents’ digital literacy varies widely (Hou et al., 2024; 
Neumann and Neumann, 2015), families may unintentionally interfere 
with or contradict school-based practices. This highlights the impor-
tance of the mesosystem, interactions between home and school, and the 
need for clear communication, parent training, and shared strategies to 
ensure consistency in digital learning environments.
Perhaps the most compelling finding is the three-way interaction 
among internet access, early instruction in locating information, and 
parental involvement. Students who had early instruction and sup-
portive home environments experienced the greatest benefit from school 
internet access. This alignment across microsystem and mesosystem 
levels exemplifies ecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner, 2006), 
reinforcing the idea that educational outcomes depend on the coordi-
nation of multiple subsystems. Reich (2020) and Gottschalk and Weise 
(2023) argue that digital equity materializes when infrastructure, 
pedagogy, and support systems function cohesively. Although systemic 
coherence is challenging in a decentralized education system, this study 
demonstrates that targeted alignment among instructional practices, 
family engagement, and technology use can produce meaningful literacy 
gains.
These findings reaffirm that digital equity is not merely a matter of 
distributing devices or expanding connectivity. As Virella and Cobb 
(2021) emphasize, equitable digital learning requires investment in 
infrastructure, the development of teacher and student competencies, 
and robust family involvement. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated 
existing disparities in these areas, particularly for students from his-
torically underserved backgrounds. In this context, multi-tiered strate-
gies, detailed further in the implications section, are essential for 
mitigating gaps and promoting digital inclusion.
Moreover, this study refines our understanding of developmental 
timing. It is not only whether students learn digital literacy skills that 
matters, but when they do. Early acquisition of locating skills equips 
students to engage more meaningfully with digital tasks later on. This 
calls for intentional curriculum sequencing, ensuring that foundational 
strategies precede complex digital reading expectations. The negative
main effect of internet access and the moderated interactions between 
teacher support and parental involvement highlight the limitations of 
fragmented interventions. Infrastructure investments, if not matched by 
instructional and familial support, risk producing disjointed learning 
experiences. These findings speak to broader implementation challenges 
in U.S. education, where governance fragmentation and uneven capacity 
hinder systemic coherence (Gomez, 2024; IEA, 2023; Mullis et al., 2023; 
National Center for Education Statistics, 2024; National Education As-
sociation, 2020). Yet the evidence of synergistic effects, where aligned 
systems produce enhanced outcomes, offers a hopeful path forward: 
interventions must be context-sensitive, developmentally appropriate, 
and integrative across school, home, and digital domains.
6. Conclusion and implications for policy and practice in digital 
literacy education
We examined the influence of school-level digital support and home 
factors on reading achievement in the PIRLS 2021 U.S sample. We found 
that improving digital equity in U.S. primary education requires more 
than distributing devices or expanding internet access. This study shows 
that technology enhances reading achievement only when supported by 
coordinated efforts across classrooms, curricula, and home. The most 
effective strategies focus not just on access, but on building the human 
infrastructure needed to use digital tools meaningfully.
This study provides evidence-based insights into the role education 
plays in human development. It focuses on how educational resources 
influence human development and social equity in elementary educa-
tion. To achieve this, we demonstrate a clear link to feasible public 
policy regarding the ‘digital divide’ and educational inequality. This is 
further demonstrated by examining how teacher technological support 
and home factors moderate reading achievement, contributing to the 
critical debate on how education can promote human capability and 
reduce inequality in an increasingly digitised global context. Given that 
the interplay of local and national dynamics in digital technology is 
essential, we use a representative U.S. sample to challenge the notion 
that digital access alone improves outcomes. Instead, we highlight that 
without coordinated instructional practices and digital literacy, tech-
nology can entrench existing differences. This focus on ‘digital support’ 
as a driver of resilience and academic well-being aligns with the broader 
definition of human development. By using quantitative analysis to shed 
light on this key challenge of educational policy, we offer actionable 
evidence that informs how developed and emerging democracies can 
better structure school-level support to ensure technology serves as a 
tool for social justice and productivity rather than a source of further 
exclusion. Specifically, the following implications are made to support 
policy and practice in blended and digital literacy education.
First, teacher capacity is a crucial driver of educational success. 
Teachers should be trained not only in how to use technology, but in 
how to integrate it into reading instruction in ways that support 
comprehension. Professional learning should include strategies such as 
modelling how to navigate online texts, using digital scaffolds, and of-
fering real-time feedback through educational platforms. For example, 
literacy coaches can run workshops on how to guide students through 
digital articles using annotation tools or structured online discussions. 
States should also revise teacher certification standards to include dig-
ital pedagogy as a core competency, ensuring that new educators enter 
the field ready to support digital reading.
Second, school internet access must be paired with instructional 
alignment. The finding that broadband alone may negatively affect 
reading reveals the risk of unstructured technology use. Without clear 
guidance, students may use internet time passively or for off-task be-
haviors. Schools should develop digital learning plans that define how 
and when technology will be used to meet reading goals. Teachers might 
use online articles during nonfiction units, explicitly teaching students 
how to skim for key ideas or evaluate the reliability of sources.

Fourth, the study also reveals that parental involvement in digital

practices. Schools should communicate clearly with families about how

help ensure consistent support. For example, a school might send home a

tional practice, and family support work in sync. Schools can extend 
impact by partnering with libraries or community centers to reinforce 
digital learning outside of school hours. Including family engagement 
staff in tech planning ensures strategies remain coherent across envi-
ronments. With intentional planning, early instruction, and cross-system 
collaboration, schools can ensure that digital tools are used effectively to 
advance reading achievement for all students.
Limitations and future directions
PIRLS 2021 data are based on self-reported data from principals, 
teachers, and parents, which may be influenced by social desirability or 
recall bias. The measures used in PIRLS may lack the granularity 
required to fully capture the complex nature of digital competence or 
parental engagement, especially in culturally specific U.S. contexts. U.S. 
schools vary widely in technological infrastructure and instructional 
practices, but PIRLS data do not provide detailed school- or district-level 
information. The focus on fifth-grade students offers only a snapshot of 
reading development, limiting broader generalizations across grade 
levels or developmental stages. A significant deviation of using fifth 
instead of fourth graders in 2021 affect inferences on fourth graders. 
Finally, given the nested structure of PIRLS data (students within 
classrooms and schools), multilevel modelling would be methodologi-
cally more appropriate than single-level multiple regression. However, 
substantial missing data across several key variables limited the feasi-
bility of estimating more complex models. As a result, not all potentially 
relevant school, classroom, and home factors could be simultaneously 
controlled. Consequently, observed associations should not be inter-
preted as causal or exhaustive, as other unmeasured or omitted variables 
may also influence reading achievement. Findings should therefore be 
interpreted with caution and generalized only within contexts compa-
rable to those examined in this study.
Despite these limitations, the study remains highly relevant for the U. 
S. elementary education. It sheds light on how key ecological factors 
such as digital access, instructional support, and family engagement 
interact to shape reading outcomes. This contributes to fresh and 
evidence-based insights that align with ongoing national discussions 
about digital equity and literacy development in primary education. 
Future research should build on these findings using longitudinal 
data and multi-level modelling to track how home-school dynamics in-
fluence reading growth over time. Mixed-methods studies could provide 
deeper insights into how specific instructional strategies and parental 
behaviours interact with digital tools in diverse classroom settings. U.S. 
specific studies with more detailed contextual variables like socioeco-
nomic status, teacher training in technology use, and district-level policy 
implementation would help refine and expand on PIRLS-based findings. 
Finally, exploring student voices and experiences with digital learning 
could also showcase the mechanisms through which technology and 
family engagement support or hinder literacy development, especially in 
historically marginalized U.S. communities.

established by the International Association for the Evaluation of 
Educational Achievement. Participation in PIRLS is voluntary and con-
