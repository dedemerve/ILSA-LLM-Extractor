Abstract
As the student population continues to become more culturally diverse, it is
imperative for teachers to cultivate greater self-efficacy in their instructional
practices. To gain a clearer picture of the association between teacher
education and multicultural self-efficacy, we conducted a multilevel modeling
analysis on data from TALIS 2018. Our study revealed a strong correlation
between teachers’ self-efficacy in general and their multicultural self-efficacy
while highlighting significant differences between the two constructs. We
also found that teachers who received multicultural education during their
initial teacher education and professional development demonstrated

Introduction
Globalization, migration and educational internationalization have led to
more cultural and linguistic diversity in classrooms worldwide, presenting
significant challenges for teacher education. To better prepare teachers for
culturally diverse students, many scholars have emphasized that the pedagogical approach of culturally responsive teaching should be incorporated
throughout initial teacher education (ITE) and professional development
(PD) of teachers (e.g., Bonner et al., 2017; Gay, 2018; Ladson-Billings, 2021;
Sleeter, 2001). This approach values and utilizes culturally diverse students'
backgrounds and experiences to enhance their academic achievement and
promote educational equity (Gay, 2018). In addition, social cognitive theories
suggest that teachers’ self-efficacy (TSE), which mediates the effect of teachers’ knowledge on actions, cannot be ignored (Bandura, 1997; Pajares, 1996).
TSE refers to “individual teachers’ beliefs in their ability to plan, organize,
and carry out activities that are required to attain given educational goals
(Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2010, p. 612),” which has been believed to reach
maturity after decades of development (Henson, 2002). In contrast, teachers’
multicultural self-efficacy (TMSE) is still an emerging field that has not been
well conceptualized yet. In this study, we defined TMSE as teachers’ beliefs
in their capacities to execute teaching practices related to culturally responsive teaching (Mahali & Sevigny, 2021; Siwatu, 2007), which is consistent
with the data we used. Although conceptualizing TMSE is not the primary
aim of this study, we believe that a clear relationship between TSE and TMSE
would contribute to the further development of research in TMSE.
Teacher education (including ITE and PD), which aims to prepare teachers for culturally different students, often resort to multicultural education
as an element in the training process or project. Multicultural education is
an idea, an educational reform and a process that promotes equal learning
opportunities for all students (Banks & Banks, 2019). In practice, it

accentuates curriculum and pedagogy that include multiple perspectives
and engage students’ voices to facilitate learning (Bonner et al., 2017). To
tackle the challenges teacher education faces, it can serve as a good starting
point to examine the association between TMSE and multicultural education implemented in ITE and PD.
Therefore, this article intended to provide empirical evidence of linkages
between TMSE and two other concepts: TSE and multicultural education in
teacher education. More specifically, we used data from the Teaching and
Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018 and adopted a cross-national
perspective. Two multilevel models were applied to analyze specific correlations. Hopefully, this study will provide more evidence underpinning prior
assumptions and offer implications for teacher education, researchers and
educational policymakers.

Literature Review
An overview into the literature can provide us with a deeper understanding of
the current knowledge about multicultural self-efficacy of teachers. This section aims to outline the fundamental constructs, namely TSE, TMSE, multicultural education in ITE and PD, by drawing on the literature from the
domains of teachers’ self-efficacy and teacher education.

Teachers’ Self-Efficacy and Multicultural Self-Efficacy
TSE refers to teachers’ beliefs in their abilities to execute behaviors and organize activities required to attain a specific educational goal in certain contexts
(Skaalvik & Skaalvik, 2010; Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998). It includes
teachers’ beliefs in three aspects (Tschannen-Moran & Hoy, 2001): classroom management (maintaining an orderly classroom environment), instructional strategies (applying proper teaching strategies and employing various
assessment measures) and student engagement (motivating students and fostering critical thinking). Teachers with firmer self-efficacy tend to set challenging goals and spend more effort on their pursuits (Coladarci, 1992; Evans
& Tribble, 1986). They constantly learn new instructional strategies and are
less likely to refer struggling students to special education (Malo-Juvera
et al., 2018; Muijs & Reynolds, 2015).
TMSE, by contrast, has yet to be clearly conceptualized. In OECD (2019a),
TMSE refers to the extent to which teachers feel confident about teaching in
a culturally diverse classroom. Similar to TSE, TMSE is multidimensional
yet contains four aspects of beliefs in their capacities to: (a) utilize students’
various cultural backgrounds and facilitate their learning; (b) align the

66

learning environment with minority students’ cultures; (c) measure students’
learning outcomes in numerous methods; (d) balance mainstream and minoritized cultures in the classroom (Siwatu, 2007).
The conceptual relevance between TSE and TMSE seems complicated by
the differences shown from their sub-dimensions, which leaves their relationship subject to debate. It is apparent from the above conceptualization that the
student population is the key distinguishing factor between TSE and TMSE.
Multicultural education emphasizes that teaching culturally diverse students
requires teachers to possess specific pedagogical knowledge and skills,
including the ability to apply culturally responsive teaching practices (e.g.,
Banks & Banks, 2019; Gay, 2018). This context-specific attribute of teaching
diverse students is the theoretical foundation for many researchers in defining TMSE as a distinct concept from TSE (e.g., Choi & Lee, 2020; DjonkoMoore, 2020; Siwatu, 2007). Supporting this context-specific attribute of
TMSE, studies found that high self-efficacy in teaching mainstream students
does not necessarily lead to an equivalent level of self-efficacy in teaching
culturally diverse students (e.g., Geerlings et al., 2018; Romijn et al., 2020).
Although it is more or less clear that TMSE is distinct from TSE, it would
further clarify their relationship by examining to which extent those two concepts are different. By measuring TSE and TMSE among in-service teachers
in the US, Mulder (2010) found that TSE and other teacher demographics
only explain 21% of the variance in TMSE. Nevertheless, she cautioned
about the small effect size of specific variables in her study and appealed for
more research on the relationship between TSE and TMSE. To validate and
expand previous research findings, the current study would take on the challenge of testing the association between TSE and TMSE in cross-national
contexts.

Multicultural Education in Initial Teacher Education
Zeichner and Hoeft (1996) called attention to the educational revolution in
curriculum and instruction to empower culturally diverse students against all
odds and put forward comprehensive strategies for implementation. They
believe it can be achieved by increasing preservice teachers’ awareness and
knowledge concerning culturally diverse students and cultivating a critical
stance toward mainstream cultures. In addition, courses that aim to provide
preservice teachers a preview of culturally different teaching environments
are recommended. ITE refers to various courses and field experiences that
support preservice teachers with knowledge, attitudes and skills demanded
for effectively addressing students in the classrooms, schools and communities (Cochran-Smith & Zeichner, 2005; Trif, 2019). As a result, ITE would be

Mou et al.

67

an optimal learning milieu for preservice teachers to learn how to teach culturally diverse students. Grounded on Zeichner and Hoeft’s (1996) theories,
the current study conceptualizes multicultural education in ITE as teaching
activities or structures targeting to boost preservice teachers’ ability to cope
with culturally diverse students.
The association between TMSE and multicultural education in ITE varies
across countries and depends on the way ITE involves multicultural education. In the United States (the U.S.), Bodur (2012) found that taking multicultural courses and having field experience in elementary schools with
culturally diverse students improved preservice teachers’ attitudes and beliefs
towards those students. Recent quantitative studies in Belgium revealed that
infused multicultural courses are positively related to preservice teachers’
knowledge (Dursun, Claes, &Agirdag, 2023) and beliefs (Dursun, Agirdag,
& Claes, 2023) regarding ethnic minority students. Based on a qualitative
study with 13 preservice teachers, Locke (2005) concluded that stand-alone
multicultural courses marginally affected preservice teachers’ attitudes
toward diversity and suggested that infused multicultural courses would be
more effective. Moreover, Thomassen and Munthe (2021) obtained comparable results in a quantitative study with 520 fourth-year preservice teachers
in Norway. In one teacher training program infused with multicultural education courses, they found that preservice teachers who took the compulsory
Norwegian language course exhibited higher TMSE than those who took
Norwegian as an optional subject. They explained that it might be because
preservice teachers could acquire more knowledge in language learning
while joining the Norwegian course.
However, in Canada, Mahali and Sevigny (2021) used surveys to test 110
preservice teachers and revealed that those who took more multicultural
courses did not differentiate themselves in terms of TMSE. They inferred that
the interplay of formal educational training and other factors might trigger
changes in TMSE. Research from Nadelson et al. (2012) corroborated the
study of Mahali and Sevigny (2021). Nadelson et al. (2012) conducted quantitative research with 88 preservice teachers in the U.S. and found a minor
impact of multicultural courses on TMSE. To some extent, their research
findings are partially consistent with Locke (2005)’s results vis-à-vis standalone multicultural courses.

Multicultural Education in Professional Development
PD can be defined as “any activity that is intended partly or primarily to prepare paid staff members for improved performance in present or future roles
in the school districts (Little, 1987, p. 491).” Numerous studies have

68

Education and Urban Society 57(1)

developed PD programs to promote the teaching and learning of culturally
different students by training participants in culturally responsive teaching/
pedagogy/instructions (e.g., Aujla-Bhullar, 2011; Bishop et al., 2009; Charity
Hudley & Mallinson, 2016). Therefore, multicultural education in PD can be
framed as activities of PD in which pedagogical approaches such as culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings, 2021), culturally responsive teaching (Gay, 2018) and culturally sustaining pedagogy (Paris, 2012) are
incorporated, although the characteristics of PD programs can vary (Choi &
Lee, 2020; Parkhouse et al., 2019).
The impact of multicultural education in PD on teachers' beliefs and
behaviors has been witnessed in piles of studies. For instance, Powell et al.
(2016) found that their PD program, which aimed to help elementary school
teachers implement culturally responsive instructions, increased teachers’
culturally congruent teaching practices. Timmons-Brown and Warner (2016)
found that teachers perceived higher levels of culturally responsive teaching
knowledge and practices by modeling optimal culturally responsive teaching
practices to math teachers in their mixed-method study. In PD aiming to help
teachers create and apply culturally responsive science teaching strategies,
Brown and Crippen (2016) found that their program benefited the TMSE of
high school science teachers.
Notwithstanding that most studies are in the context of the U.S., as shown
above, similar studies are performed in other parts of the world. In New
Zealand, Bishop et al. (2009) appealed to reform educational practices for
Māori students according to those students’ opinions and culture. Through a
PD project in which 422 secondary schoolteachers participated, they showed
that both teachers’ self-efficacy and competencies in teaching Māori students
improved. Research results of Choi and Lee (2020) suggested that, for both
the U.S. and South Korea, involving multicultural education in PD is conducive to TMSE.
Even though most studies showed a positive link between multicultural
education in PD and TMSE, most are small-scale qualitative studies or largescale quantitative studies confined by country/subject contexts. Thus, largescale international research is needed to update the association between
multicultural education in PD and TMSE.

The Present Study
The study aims to replicate and renew prior studies on whether TMSE is
related to TSE and multicultural education in ITE and PD. Although the
above-referenced studies provided evidence of the association between those

Mou et al.

69

variables and TMSE, some important limitations remain. First, the extant
research does not permit crystal clear empirical evidence of to which extent
TMSE is distinct from TSE. Second, those studies which proved TSE is weak
in predicting TMSE are limited in country contexts. Third, studies about the
linkage between TMSE and teacher education are mostly small-scale qualitative studies that lack generalizability in terms of research findings; and other
larger-scale quantitative research mainly concentrated on limited country
contexts or subjects. Last but not least, existing findings about the effect of
ITE and PD on TMSE are derived from separate studies with different
research aims, populations and methods, which might not be easy to compare
relative effects of those two sorts of teacher education.
In response, the present study used large-scale international data from
TALIS 2018, which investigated teachers’ learning opportunities in various
subjects. It mainly surveyed lower secondary education teachers and was the
first time that the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development
(OECD) added the theme of diversity and equity to TALIS (Ainley &
Carstens, 2018). Additionally, TALIS 2018 placed TMSE under the theme of
diversity whereas TSE was in teaching in general (OECD, 2019a), which is
convenient for examining the correlation between TMSE and TSE. As such,
our study might provide a nuanced look at the relationships among TMSE,
teacher education and TSE. Practically, various educational actors can draw
implications from this study to improve teacher training and education quality. The following research questions will guide the current study.

Research Question 1
To which degree is TMSE different from TSE?

Research Question 2
To what extent are multicultural education in ITE and PD related to TMSE?

Methods
To address the research questions, this study analyzed TALIS 2018 data from
OECD. The two-level Multilevel modeling (teachers at level 1 and countries
at level 2) was adopted in the current study as participants were nested within
their national units. IBM SPSS for Windows (Version 28.0.1.1) was used for
the analysis. The following sections will delineate data sources, participants,
statistical analyses and related variables.

Data and Participants
TALIS 2018 is an international survey concentrating on teachers’ learning
environments and working conditions. It covers over 260,000 teachers from
48 countries/economies, where teachers from lower secondary education
(ISCED level 2) are the main data sources (OECD, 2019b). Participants were
selected through random sampling in two sequential phases: first, 200 schools
were selected per country; second, 20 teachers were selected in each school
(OECD, 2019a). Then, selected teachers were administered to complete a
questionnaire of 58 multiple-choice questions in approximately 45 and
60 minutes. In our study, data from Russia, Spain and Iceland were excluded
from the analysis due to issues with questionnaire administration or data
availability (OECD, 2019c); and some teachers’ answers were not considered
because of incomplete responses. Additionally, our analytical sample was
significantly reduced since TALIS 2018 only surveyed the TMSE of teachers
who had previously taught in culturally diverse classrooms. Consequently,
our final sample size comprised data from 65,978 lower secondary education
teachers across 45 countries/economies.

Statistical Analyses
Before analyses, the listwise deletion was conducted to exclude missing values and the restricted maximum likelihood estimation method was applied.
During the analysis, we conducted two multilevel models (Model A and
Model B). In Model A, an intercept-only model (Model A0) was first tested
to analyze the variance components of TMSE at teacher and country levels.
Next, TSE was included in Model A1 and individual background characteristics were fitted in Model A2. In building Model B, we first performed an
unconditional model (Model B0) analogous to Model A0; then, elements of
teacher education and teachers’ background characteristics were included in
the following Model B1 and Model B2, respectively.

we described how variables in Model A and B were derived; then, a list of
control variables which were the same for both models was introduced. All
independent and control variables were grand mean centered before the analysis. Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of all variables.

The Dependent Variable. TMSE served as the dependent variable in Model A.
It was measured in the TALIS 2018 survey through five items: (1) coping
with the challenges of culturally diverse classrooms, (2) adapting teaching to
the cultural diversity of students, (3) ensuring students with and without
migrant backgrounds work together, (4) raising awareness for cultural differences among students, and (5) reducing ethnic stereotyping among students.
Teachers’ responses were recorded on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all,
2 = to some extent, 3 = quite a bit, 4 = a lot). Higher scores represent a higher
level of TMSE. The scale had a reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.85. One
composite variable of TMSE (mean = 2.91, SD = 0.66) was constructed by
computing the mean of those five items.
Independent Variables. TSE is the independent variable in Model A. It was
obtained from teachers’ responses to the 12-item version of TschannenMoran and Hoy’s (2001) Ohio State Teacher Efficacy Scale. In the TALIS
2018 questionnaire, it was adapted for the question in your teaching, to what
extent can you do the following? with items from A to L. Altogether, those
items have an internal consistency of 0.91. Teachers’ answers were reported
through a 4-point Likert form (0 = not at all, 1 = to some extent, 3 = quite a bit,
4 = a lot) and higher scores imply a higher level of TSE. A composite variable
(mean = 3.26, SD = 0.48) was produced on the basis of the overall mean of
teachers’ responses.

Model B has two independent variables: multicultural education in ITE
(TT3G06F2) and multicultural education in PD (TT3G23J). For multicultural
education in ITE, participants were asked whether teaching in a multicultural
or multilingual setting was included in their formal teacher education and to
which extent they felt prepared. Teachers needed to choose among “1 = not at
all,” “2 = somewhat,” “3 = well,” or “4 = very well” to indicate their preparedness if multicultural education was included in their ITE. Moreover, high
scores indicate better preparation. Multicultural education in PD was measured by investigating if teaching in a multicultural or multilingual setting
was included in PD activities in the last 12 months. Teachers’ responses were
dichotomous (yes or no) and recoded as 1 = yes and 0 = no in this study.
Control Variables. Factors that might explain the self-efficacy of teachers were
included as control variables. Debates concerning what sorts of teacher background characteristics would influence self-efficacy have not settled in the
scholarship. For instance, some found that teachers’ gender, race/ethnicity,
teaching experience and advanced degree status (master’s degree) are unrelated to teachers’ self-efficacy (Gutentag et al., 2018; Nadelson et al., 2012;
Pas et al., 2012); whilst others asserted that demographic features such as
gender, teaching experience and formal education status of teachers are influential (Bullock et al., 2015; Choi & Mao, 2021; Lauermann & König, 2016).
As there is no available data for teachers’ ethnicity in TALIS 2018, we controlled for three background characteristics—gender, teaching experience
and degree status.
The gender (TT3G01) is a dichotomous variable (female = 69%) and we
included a dummy variable in which male was the reference group. For
teaching experience (TT3G11B), we focused on teachers’ total years of teaching experience without considering working places. In our dataset, the average teaching experience of teachers is 16 years, with a standard deviation of
10 years. The degree status (TT3G03) refers to the highest level of education
teachers complete. One dummy variable was created for degree status and
Bachelor’s and below (ISCED 2011 Level 3–6) (58%) is the reference group.
42% of our participants were in the Master’s and above group (ISCED 2011
Level 7–8).

Research Question 1
In the first research question, we intend to probe the extent to which TSE can
predict TMSE. In Model A0, an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of
16.59% was calculated for the gross country effect (cf. Table 2). According to
Cohen (1988), an ICC of 16.59% represents a high level of between-group
variability and multilevel modeling should be used. It indicates the significant effect of country context on TMSE and justifies our analyzing method of
multilevel modeling.
In Model A1, TSE was fitted to explain the variance composition of
TMSE. Table 2 presents that TSE is statistically related to TMSE (β = .473,
p < .001). Moreover, the Pseudo R2 of TSE is 40%, which is considered a
sizable effect (Cohen, 1988). However, there were still 60% of the variances
in TMSE could not be explained by TSE. In teachers’ background characteristics, female teachers are more self-efficacious than male teachers and teaching experience was negatively associated with TMSE. However, they
explained little variance composition in TMSE (cf. Table 2), which can be
neglected. Teachers’ educational degree was not related to TMSE in this
model. To conclude, TSE, together with teachers’ gender and teaching experience, left 60% of variances unexplained in TMSE.

Research Question 2
In this research question, we are interested in how multicultural education
in ITE and PD are associated with TMSE. Results of Model B1 showed that
both ITE (β = .129, p < .001) and PD (β = .123, p < .001) are positively
related to TMSE (cf. Table 3). Furthermore, ITE had a slightly more significant effect on TMSE than PD. The arithmetic result of Pseudo R2 in Model
B1, yet, made it appear that those two predictors merely explained 7.14%
of variances, which is a weak effect size according to Cohen (1988). In
Model B2, we took into account teachers’ characteristics. Results showed
that all control variables were related to TMSE and the gender of teachers
was the most significant factor among them. In detail, female teachers demonstrated higher levels of TMSE than male teachers. In Model B, teachers
with more teaching experience had firmer TMSE. And teachers with a master’s or above level degree showed more TMSE than those with a bachelor’s or lower-level degree. However, little variance composition has been
explained after fitting those control variables. To sum up, multicultural
education in ITE and PD are significantly associated with TMSE, yet their
effect sizes are small.

Discussion
Culturally and linguistically diverse classrooms have challenged teaching
and learning effectiveness in schools. A further empirical investigation of the
interlaced relationship between TSE and TMSE would add to the current literature on self-efficacy. Moreover, an examination of ITE and PD’s effects
on TMSE in a cross-national context, writ large, would renew and expand
previous research results obtained from certain countries. The following parts
discuss the research results, implications and limitations of the present study.
Our research findings showed that TSE left 60% of the variance in TMSE
unexplained, which indicates that TMSE, to a large extent, is a distinct concept from TSE. Yet, TSE’s sizable effect on TMSE showed their close association. Those two findings are not paradoxical and can be interpreted from
two angles. On the one hand, the significant difference between TSE and
TMSE corroborates with the domain-specific theory of self-efficacy
(Bandura, 1997). Additionally, it resonates with the theoretical foundations
of culturally responsive teaching that to teach in a culturally different classroom requires other specific pedagogical knowledge and skills than the general classroom (Dursun et al., 2021; Gay, 2018; Lucas & Villegas, 2013;
Sleeter, 2001).
On the other hand, the close association between TSE and TMSE can be
explained by the similarities between general and culturally different classroom teaching. Bandura (1997) pointed out that the predictive power of selfefficacy scales relies on the similarity of the compared contexts and the
foreseeability of task requirements. The difference between teaching students
in general and culturally different students cannot dismiss the fact that there
are shared commonalities. Also, the analytic teacher sample in our study was
teachers with experience in teaching culturally different classrooms. Thus, it
would be easier for them to imagine the demands of teaching culturally different students, contributing to the comparatively high level of linkage
between TSE and TMSE. This result is consistent with Crook’s (2010) study
on school counselors’ self-efficacy beliefs whereas contradicts research from
Mulder (2010). Overall, our research findings disentangled the interdependent yet independent relationship between TMSE and TSE. As for the control
variables, teachers’ gender and teaching experience were related to TMSE.
Specifically, female teachers exhibited higher levels of TMSE than male
teachers. Teachers with longer years of teaching experience were, however,
less self-efficacious in teaching cultural classrooms. In our study, those two
control variables explained little variance in TMSE and thus can be ignored.
As regards the connection between teacher education and TMSE, we
found that, for one thing, both multicultural education in ITE and PD are

positively related to TMSE; and ITE showed a slightly more considerable
influence compared with PD. For another, those two sorts of teacher education explained around 7% of variances in TMSE, arguably weak in effect.
Plausible reasons for our findings can be elucidated from three aspects.
Firstly, the positive relationship between ITE and TMSE indicates that
teachers who experienced multicultural education in their formal teacher
education would have higher TMSE. Generally, scholars encourage ITE to
involve multicultural education and assume it would end with more effective
instruction of culturally diverse students. Our research results echo these
theoretical assumptions in the scholarship (e.g., Gay, 2018; Lucas & Villegas,
2013; Sleeter, 2001). For instance, Lucas and Villegas (2013) emphasized
that learning to teach culturally different students should start from ITE and
proposed a systematic framework in which multicultural teaching is deeply
embedded. Additionally, the slightly more significant effect of ITE identified
in our research is compatible with the empirical results of Siwatu (2011) that
exposure to multicultural education in ITE equipped teachers with higher
TMSE.
Secondly, the positive association between multicultural education and
TMSE in PD resembles that in ITE. Thus, the theories that encourage implementing multicultural education in ITE also accommodate PD. Apart from
those theories, three possible reasons exist for the linkage between PD and
TMSE. To begin with, PD can provide a low-stake environment for teachers
to learn and practice culturally responsive teaching strategies which better
meet their daily teaching needs (Benegas, 2014; Norman, 2020; Smith &
Bahr, 2014). Besides, teachers can network with one another through PD
activities, which provides them with the opportunity for mutual learning
(Smith & Bahr, 2014; Timmons-Brown & Warner, 2016). Moreover, observing teaching and receiving feedback from colleagues can spur teachers to
reflect upon their teaching beliefs and practices (Norman, 2020; Smith &
Bahr, 2014). During mutual learning and reflection, teachers might deepen
their understanding of culturally responsive teaching and thus enhance
TMSE.
Lastly, the more or less mild effect of ITE and PD on TMSE can be
explained, first, from the perspective of how teacher education includes
multicultural education elements. The questionnaire of TALIS 2018 did not
provide information about whether multicultural education in both ITE and
PD was infused into the program or simply in the form of stand-alone
courses. Numerous studies showed that infused multicultural education
would significantly make a difference in TMSE and stand-alone multicultural courses showed marginalized effects (e.g., Bodur, 2012; Locke, 2005;
Nadelson et al., 2012). As a result, our research findings may have shown a

Mou et al.

77

combined effect due to the existence of different ways of incorporating
multicultural education across countries. Teachers’ background characteristics (i.e., gender, teaching experience and degree status) explained little
variance in the model though they were all statistically and positively significant in Model B.
A handful of practical implications can be drawn from the current study for
teacher education, researchers and educational policymakers. First, the effectiveness of both ITE and PD on TMSE confirmed that multicultural education
should be incorporated throughout all training phases of teachers to foster
more confident and competent culturally responsive teachers. In the second
place, the similar influence of ITE and PD on TMSE implied that PD can
serve as a complement to ITE where it is less successful in preparing culturally responsive teachers. Last, the unique relationship between TMSE and
TSE hinted that teacher education which aims to prepare more self-efficacious
teachers should ensure its precise goals align with student needs.
However, the current study is not flawless. There are three main limitations to our study. The first limitation is the cross-sectional study design of
TALIS 2018, which precludes the inference of causality among observed
relationships (OECD, 2019a). Secondly, we made a strong assumption that
included data were missing completely at random (MCAR) and used listwise
deletion in dealing with missing data, which might cause problems such as
inflated standard errors (Heck et al., 2014). Lastly, the relatively small effect
size in our research results also needs caveats. However, it is understandable
that elements of teacher education boost TMSE in concert with one another,
which means no single element alone can make a substantial difference in the
outcome (Martin et al., 2010). Many studies revealed that other elements in
ITE and PD, such as general pedagogy, pedagogical content knowledge and
training about communication with people from different cultures, effectively enhance teachers’ self-efficacy in specific country contexts or subjects
(e.g., Bradshaw et al., 2018; Juuti et al., 2018; Shi, 2016). Hence, more international quantitative studies like the current study are needed to examine
further and renew the linkage between teacher education and TMSE.

Conclusion
Many educationists introduced culturally responsive teaching to teach better
and empower socially and culturally minoritized students. However, pedagogical approaches alone are insufficient for preparing effective teachers in
teaching culturally diverse classrooms. This study investigated the relationships between TMSE and teacher education, TSE. By analyzing TALIS 2018
data, we identified two major findings: (a) TSE is correlated with TMSE, yet

78

Education and Urban Society 57(1)

TMSE remains an independent concept from TSE; (b) multicultural education in ITE and PD are positively associated with a higher level of TMSE, but
with a mild effect on TMSE. Our research advances research regarding
TMSE by renewing the generalizability of past evidence-based findings and
confirming prior theoretical understanding with data from 45 countries/economies without subject limitation.
