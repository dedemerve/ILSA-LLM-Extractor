A B S T R A C T  
During the early stages of the teaching profession, pre-service and beginning teachers often 
participate in individual support activities intended to develop their teacher self-efficacy. These 
individual support activities (modeling, feedback on lesson plans or lessons, and reflection) are 
provided as part of an intervention by a mentor, cooperating teacher, instructor, or supervisor. 
We conducted a meta-analysis to investigate the effectiveness of these interventions and the in­
dividual support activities for pre-service and beginning teachers’ self-efficacy. We found a 
medium-sized overall effect of interventions on teacher self-efficacy based on 28 studies (49 effect 
sizes) in our meta-analysis. Providing feedback on lesson plans amplified the intervention effect 
(large effect). However, this moderator effect slightly missed the significance level of 0.05. The 
moderator effect of providing feedback on lesson plans was stable after controlling for mastery 
experiences and methodological moderator variables. Implications for future interventions 
regarding teacher self-efficacy for early career teachers are discussed.   
1. Teacher self-efficacy in teaching situations 
Teachers’ beliefs about their competencies in teaching are pivotal for successful instructional behavior in classrooms. One moti­
vational belief that has been investigated for many years is self-efficacy. Self-efficacy is generally defined as “a belief in one’s capa­
bilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to produce given attainments” (Bandura, 1997, p. 3). Teacher 
self-efficacy is associated with many favorable outcomes such (pre-service) teacher commitment (Klassen & Chiu, 2011; Pfitzner-­
Eden, 2016a), instructional behavior (e.g., Holzberger et al., 2013; Lazarides et al., 2020), student engagement and achievement (for a 
review, see Bardach et al., 2021; for a meta-analysis, see Klassen & Tze, 2014). Hence, a high teacher self-efficacy is generally desirable 
and positively contributes to various educational outcomes. 
To develop teacher self-efficacy, four sources of self-efficacy come into play (Bandura, 1997): mastery experiences, vicarious ex­
periences, verbal persuasion, and physiological and affective states. According to Bandura (1977, 1997), mastery experiences have the 
largest impact on one’s self-efficacy beliefs for any given task. These experiences result from effective performance and can alter the 
level and strength of one’s self-efficacy beliefs. By acquiring new teaching experiences in real classrooms, pre-service teachers (PSTs) 
and beginning teachers (BTs) gain mastery experiences. This is one piece of information that is processed in the development of one’s 
teacher self-efficacy (Clark et al., 2014; Woolfolk Hoy & Burke Spero, 2005). We define mastery experience as the enactment of

offered during (new) teaching situations, such as the teaching practicum (Rupp & Becker, 2021; also known as the school internship or 
school placement; Lawson et al., 2015), teaching a specific science method in the classroom (Seung et al., 2019), or teaching one’s own 
students (Tschannen-Moran & Woolfolk Hoy, 2007). Thus, these teaching elements may be of relevance when promoting teacher 
self-efficacy. Bandura (1997) also stated that it is not only the performance itself that is primarily responsible for the development of 
self-efficacy beliefs but rather the reflection and resulting interpretation of one’s own performance. Each individual PST or BT can be 
supported in their reflection and interpretation of their teaching performance by a variety of support activities within teacher edu­
cation programs. These individual support activities can be linked to other sources of self-efficacy. For instance, verbal persuasion or 
vicarious experiences can be activated by the assistance of support persons (e.g., experienced mentors, cooperating teachers, or su­
pervisors) who give the PSTs and BTs individual advice and feedback on their teaching (Moulding et al., 2014; Wyatt, 2014) or model 
teaching practices and lesson plans (Mok & Staub, 2021; Seung et al., 2019). Moreover, individual support activities related to 
reflection and discussions promoting active learning are not only pivotal for learning in professional learning settings (Hill, 2004) but 
also for developing teacher self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997; Tschannen-Moran & McMaster, 2009). Consequently, mastery experiences 
can largely be informed and influenced by other sources (Bandura, 1997) and may serve as a mediator between these sources 
(Pfitzner-Eden, 2016b). 
To date, several intervention studies have been conducted to promote PSTs’ and BTs’ teaching skills (Ingersoll & Strong, 2011; Mok 
& Staub, 2021) and their teacher self-efficacy (e.g., Dicke et al., 2015; Seung et al., 2019; Voet & De Wever, 2018; Weber et al., 2019). 
However, the interventions vary considerably in terms of design, learning component(s), target group, and setting (university vs. 
school-based). The common element among many intervention studies is that they followed a mentoring, coaching, or supervision 
approach. In these approaches, support persons provide a variety individual support activities that guide and assist PSTs or BTs in 
learning how to teach (e.g., Chizhik et al., 2018; Hobson et al., 2009; Mok & Staub, 2021). These individual support activities are often 
combined in different ways within interventions designed to support teaching-related skills of PSTs and BTs. It is still unclear how 
effective these interventions are on teacher self-efficacy of PSTs and BTs and which individual support activities of these interventions 
are crucial for increasing teacher self-efficacy of PSTs and BTs. The present meta-analysis addresses these research gaps. 
1.1. Intervention effects on PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy 
1.1.1. Intervention effects on PSTs’ teacher self-efficacy 
Many of the interventions focusing to promote PSTs’ teaching are embedded in teacher education programs and provide teaching 
experiences (Mok & Staub, 2021), which also result in mastery experiences and therefore teacher self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). In­
terventions can take place in universities or schools. For PSTs interventions focusing on improvement of teaching skills can be uni­
versity based. For instance, a study by Seung et al. (2019) investigated the effects of a university-based intervention that focused on a 
science methods course for elementary school PSTs in a summer camp. Other learning elements integrated into the program included 
the modeling of inquiry-based approaches for science lessons by experienced teachers, developing and demonstrating inquiry-based 
lessons, peer team-teaching sessions, reflection, and feedback opportunities provided during mentoring sessions at the end of each day. 
In many European, Northern American, and Southeast Asian countries, the curriculum of teacher education programs for PSTs 
includes a school-based teaching practicum for few weeks, which allows authentic “clinical experience” in planning and teaching 
lessons (Ellis et al., 2020; Im et al., 2016). Other interventions focusing on the school-based teaching practicum for PSTs often involved 
different support persons for reflection and discussions on PSTs’ teaching experiences (e.g., Flores, 2015). School-based intervention 
elements are also sometimes combined with university-based elements. For instance, Weber et al. (2019) investigated the intervention 
effect of video-based and blended coaching approaches during the teaching practicum (compared to a face-to-face coaching as a 
control group) on PSTs’ teacher self-efficacy. PSTs in the face-to-face coaching group received class visits from their university su­
pervisors and their peer partners, which can be considered as common support during the teaching practicum. Based on these visits, 
PSTs received feedback, reflected on their teaching, and discussed their teaching experiences. All groups increased their teacher 
self-efficacy but the conditions that included blended coaching and/or expert feedback showed larger effects on teacher self-efficacy 
from the intervention as compared to the face-to-face coaching condition. In the intervention study by Chizhik et al. (2018) used 
professional learning communities (i.e., a university supervisor, classroom teacher, and peer students) integrated in a mentoring 
approach to discuss PSTs’ lesson plans and recorded lessons as well as to give feedback on them. The results showed that the op­
portunity to receive specific feedback on (video-taped) lessons (and plans) appears to be an intervention activity that fosters PSTs’ 
teacher self-efficacy. The control group followed a more traditional mentoring approach. Students in the control group were assisted 
only by a classroom teacher who provided guidance on lesson plans and instructions. 
1.1.2. Intervention effects on BTs’ teacher self-efficacy 
Most BTs across the globe are assisted in their first years in the teaching profession by mentoring arrangements (for an overview, 
see Hobson et al., 2009; Tynj¨al¨a & Heikkinen, 2011). As teaching in schools can be challenging especially for novice teachers, 
instructional support by experienced mentors is needed to develop teaching skills (Hobson et al., 2009; Ingersoll & Strong, 2011; Mok 
& Staub, 2021), which can also affect teacher self-efficacy (Moulding et al., 2014). Compared to teacher self-efficacy interventions for 
PSTs, the interventions for BTs are mostly school-based (e.g., Helms-Lorenz et al., 2012). For instance, a study by Dicke et al. (2015) 
focused on the effects of a classroom management skills training. The training included group discussions with an experienced teacher 
focused on real life cases, development of classroom management practices, and role playing of classroom management techniques. 
The intervention group was compared to a wait-list control group. BTs who participated in this classroom management skills training

1.1.3. Mentoring in interventions 
Despite the differences between the interventions for PSTs and BTs, there are also similarities across these interventions. Most 
interventions followed mentoring, coaching, or supervision approaches to support PSTs and BTs. Surprisingly, there are no clear 
definitions for these approaches (Aspfors & Fransson, 2015), so the terms mentoring, coaching, and supervision are often used 
interchangeably (Ambrosetti et al., 2014). Based on previous reviews (Ambrosetti et al., 2014; Aspfors & Fransson, 2015; Hobson et al., 
2009), it seems that mentoring is the most used term used for both PSTs and BTs. Hence, in the following we use only the term 
mentoring. Moreover, the perceived assistance from a support person has been found to positively affect PSTs’ and BTs’ self-efficacy. 
For instance, the received support by a cooperating teacher1 was positively correlated with teacher self-efficacy of PSTs (Moulding 
et al., 2014; Rupp & Becker, 2021). Similar to a cooperating teacher, received support from a university supervisor can also positively 
affect teacher self-efficacy (Woolfolk Hoy & Burke Spero, 2005). Although some mentoring elements have been established in existing 
teacher education programs and PSTs’ and BTs’ self-reported teacher self-efficacy are associated with attendance in these programs (e. 
g., Fives et al., 2007; Richter et al., 2013), there are constantly improvements in these programs that need to be explored in in­
terventions to test their effectiveness. 
In sum, most intervention studies focusing on the professional development of PSTs and BTs are aimed at improving instructional 
skills. Furthermore, the above-outlined intervention studies included various individual support activities. Importantly, these indi­
vidual support activities can be linked to sources of self-efficacy (i.e., mastery experiences, verbal persuasion, vicarious experiences, 
and psychological and affective cues) and create opportunities for PSTs and BTs to (re)interpret their pre-existing teacher self-efficacy 
beliefs, which may subsequently shape their teacher self-efficacy in future teaching situations (Bandura, 1997). The individual support 
provided by support persons within teacher education programs for PSTs and BTs can provide different forms of support and have been 
shown to have a strong impact on teaching practices (e.g., Lazarides et al., 2020) and teacher self-efficacy (e.g., Weber et al., 2019) 
particularly for PSTs and BTs. For instance, Tschannen-Moran and Woolfolk Hoy (2007) found that PSTs’ self-efficacy is affected to a 
greater extent by contextual factors such as available individual support given by other persons (e.g., established teachers). However, it 
remains unclear to what extent the various intervention elements that are not mastery experiences but instead focus on supportive 
activities provided by others (i.e., individual support activities such as feedback provision, guided reflection, and observation) 
contribute to the development of self-efficacy in PSTs and BTs. 
1.2. Individual support activities in interventions, mastery experiences, and their effects on teacher self-efficacy 
The collaboration between either a school-based cooperating teacher, university supervisor, or instructor (we refer to all these 
person groups as “support persons” from now on) and early career teachers such as PSTs and BTs is pivotal for providing important 
planning- and teaching-related learning opportunities (Clarke et al., 2014; Ellis et al., 2020). These learning opportunities are inte­
grated in individual support activities. Five common individual support activities provided by an experienced support person to 
improve young teachers’ instructional planning and teaching practices can be highlighted from previous reviews: modeling of lesson 
planning and teaching practices, giving feedback on a lesson plan, giving feedback on observed lessons, providing opportunities to 
observe teaching practices enacted by a support person, and guiding reflection processes (for reviews, see Ambrosetti et al., 2014; 
Clarke et al., 2014; Ellis et al., 2020; Spooner-Lane, 2017; for a meta-analysis, see Mok & Staub, 2021). These individual support 
activities are also in line with the suggested core elements of teacher education programs that are most effective for learning and 
implementing new teaching-related content (Desimone, 2009). In detail, offering active learning elements such as observing an expert 
teacher, receiving feedback, and participating in discussions are effective for learning and implementing teaching content (Hill, 2004). 
Thus, we suggest that such active learning elements based on reflection and interpretation of past teaching experiences can be found in 
individual support activities which might also improve teacher self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). Below, we focus on the following in­
dividual support activities: modeling, feedback on lesson plans, feedback on observed lessons, and guided reflection. 
1.2.1. Modeling 
Vicarious experiences are situations in which individuals can observe and learn from another person demonstrating a desired skill 
(Fives et al., 2007; Woolfolk Hoy & Burke Spero, 2005). This demonstration of a particular teaching skill is known as modeling (Collins 
et al., 1989). In teacher education, vicarious experiences (i.e., job shadowing, demonstrating teaching techniques) are often provided 
by experienced support persons. Especially modeling has been shown to be a very effective support activity to increase PSTs’ 
instructional skills (Liu, 2005; Mok & Staub, 2021). Support persons make use of modeling to demonstrate planning or teaching 
practices and to explain the reasoning behind the planned or selected practices (Collins et al., 1989). Thus, PSTs and BTs are exposed to 
vicarious experiences by observing and learning from support persons. This may explain why PSTs and BTs have reported increased 
teacher self-efficacy in studies using modeling as a support activity (Morris et al., 2017). 
1.2.2. Providing feedback

increased teaching experiences in schools, the instructional skills applied in lessons should be further developed (Tas et al., 2018; 
White, 2007). Hence, feedback on lesson plans and feedback on lessons taught can be helpful for less experienced PSTs, but also for BTs 
who will teach regularly at school. In general, having other people believe in oneself and speak well of one strengthens one’s 
self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). However, providing general feedback like “you can do it” when instructional skills are lacking may be 
less beneficial (Rupp & Becker, 2021), especially because PSTs and BTs have limited prior knowledge of the actual demands and 
complexity of the teaching task (Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998). As PSTs and BTs, there are teaching tasks and activities where un­

and BTs in ways that help them imagine how they can and will be successful, for example by discussing lesson plans, to foster their 
professional development as well as their teacher self-efficacy beliefs. 
1.2.2.1. Feedback on lesson plans. Lesson planning skills are especially important for less experienced teachers, as lesson planning is 
complex (K¨onig et al., 2017). Support persons can help PSTs improve their lesson planning skills (Mok & Staub, 2021). Some studies 
further suggest that providing feedback on lesson plans before PSTs teach the lesson can have positive effects on teacher self-efficacy 
(e.g., Chizhik et al., 2018). For BTs at the beginning of the induction phase, lesson planning is still a challenging task (K¨onig et al., 
2021). Hence, similar to the teacher self-efficacy effects found for PSTs from lesson plan feedback, we assume that feedback on lesson 
plans can also positively affect BTs’ teacher self-efficacy. 
1.2.2.2. Feedback on lessons. Feedback on observed lessons combines two individual support activities, namely letting a support 
person observe a lesson and receiving feedback on that lesson. Lesson feedback provided by a support person focusing on instructional 
behavior has been shown to improve PSTs’ teacher self-efficacy during the teaching practicum (Prilop et al., 2019) and BTs’ teacher 
self-efficacy during the induction phase (Richter et al., 2013). The feedback on observed lessons by a support person can also be seen as 
verbal persuasion, similar to feedback on lesson plans. Supporting this, research showed an association between feedback of one’s 
support person about one’s teaching performance and teacher self-efficacy (Tschannen-Moran & Woolfolk Hoy, 2007). 
1.2.3. Guided reflection 
Reflection about one’s own teaching practices is essential to developing better instructional skills and improving student learning 
(Sch¨on, 1983). The reflection process for teachers in PST education is often guided by a school-based cooperating teacher: For instance, 
dialogical pre-lesson conversations between a cooperating teacher and a PST or BT supported by a set of reflection questions to plan 
lessons can be helpful for improving instructional skills (e.g., Chizhik et al., 2018; Kreis & Staub, 2011). From the perspective of 
self-efficacy theory, guided reflection often in form of discussions might affect verbal persuasion and psychological and affective cues 
(Bandura, 1997), depending how the reflection is guided by a support person (e.g., Richter et al., 2013). 
Taken together, as teaching skills and teacher self-efficacy are strongly associated (e.g., Holzberger et al., 2013) and research 
suggests that learning based on mastery experiences in teaching situations and discussions as well as reflections with support persons 
can be important sources for PSTs’ and BTs’ development of teacher self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997; Fives et al., 2007; Moulding et al., 
2014; Woolfolk Hoy & Burke Spero, 2005), we assume that individual support activities by a support person such as guidance on 
reflection can also affect the teacher self-efficacy of both PSTs and BTs. 
1.2.4. Mastery experiences 
Since mastery experiences are considered to be the most impactful source of teacher self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997; Tschannen-­
Moran & McMaster, 2009), it is important to take mastery experiences into account when exploring the effect of individual support 
activities on teacher self-efficacy. Mastery experiences can refer to the mere opportunity to attempt and engage in a teaching task 
(Morris et al., 2017) as well as the interpretations individuals make of their past performance (Bandura, 1997). For example, the 
perception of successful teaching experiences during the school-based teaching practicum or microteaching sequences within a 
university-based course can be regarded as an opportunity to create teaching related mastery experiences and, therefore, enhancing 
teacher self-efficacy. In contrast, some teacher education programs may focus on developing specific skills, such as practicing digital 
skills by creating a learning video for students (e.g., Kiili et al., 2016). Such intervention elements are important aspects for developing 
teachers’ digital competence and achieving a professional use of technology (e.g., Instefjord, 2015). However, practicing specific skills 
which are not directly embedded in an authentic teaching situation (in the classroom) might be needed later in a teaching situation. In 
line with previous studies on the development of teacher self-efficacy (e.g., Flores, 2015; Klassen & Durksen, 2014; Tschannen-Moran 
et al., 1998), we focus on mastery experiences within a teaching-related situation, a teaching practicum, or field experience that closely 
resembles authentic teaching situations in schools. This aligns with Bandura’s (1997) description of the sources, according to which 
actual experiences provide more authentic evidence of one’s own teaching capabilities. 
1.3. Potential influence of methodological variables on teacher self-efficacy 
Previous teacher self-efficacy intervention studies were conducted in various contexts, and they might also vary with regard to the 
research design, measurement(s) of teacher self-efficacy, and the teacher group involved in the intervention. These variables should be

considered when examining the effects of interventions and their individual support activities. 
1.3.1. Research design 
Studies exploring effects of research designs have postulated that intervention effects are weaker in studies using a randomized pre- 
post-control group design compared to studies using simpler design such as pre-post design without any control group (Cheung & 
Slavin, 2016; Marsden & Torgerson, 2012). In one-group pre-post design studies, different factors can lead to an overestimation of the 
effects. For example, the potential effects might be affected by the contemporaneous influence of normal educational experiences or 
current innovations in practice that can account for the observed effect (Marsden & Torgerson, 2012). Moreover, learners might 
improve their outcomes in the educational context during a certain time period due to their increasing maturity (Marsden & Torgerson, 
2012). 
1.3.2. Teacher self-efficacy measurement 
Teacher self-efficacy has been assessed both by global measures of general self-efficacy and more specific measures of teaching- 
related self-efficacy (Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998; Wyatt, 2014). Various studies exploring global measures of self-efficacy found 
no associations with behavioral outcomes (for a review, see Zee & Koomen, 2016). To solve this problem, critical reviews have 
suggested concentrating on more teaching-specific forms of self-efficacy including domain- and/or task-specific aspects of self-efficacy 
(e.g., Wyatt, 2014). 
1.3.3. Teacher group 
Teacher self-efficacy is more malleable in PSTs than in more experienced teachers such as BTs, because once teacher self-efficacy 
beliefs are established in the early stages of one’s teaching career, teacher self-efficacy cannot easily be modified (Woolfolk Hoy & 
Burke Spero, 2005). Findings on which teacher group benefits more from the assistance of support persons are rather mixed. On the 
one hand, supporting was found to be more important for PSTs (less experienced teachers) than for BTs, who have more teaching 
experience and, therefore, mastery experiences (Tschannen-Moran & Woolfolk Hoy, 2007). On the other hand, BTs’ teacher 
self-efficacy has been shown to either increase slowly (Dicke et al., 2014) or decrease in the first year of teaching (Tynj¨al¨a & Heikkinen, 
2011; Woolfolk Hoy & Burke Spero, 2005), which suggests that BTs also need support to maintain or increase their teacher 
self-efficacy. 
Taken together, individual support activities vary between intervention studies, which also differ in terms of research design, 
teacher self-efficacy measurement, and target group of teachers included in the intervention. Confounding relationships between 
individual supported activities and the methodological variables might exist. 
1.4. The present study 
The goal of the present research is to conduct a meta-analysis of studies investigating the effects of university-based and school- 
based interventions on PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy. Based on our meta-analysis we evaluate three research questions (RQs). 
RQ1) How effective are interventions in improving PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy? 
RQ2) Is the overall effect of interventions moderated by the individual support activities (i.e., feedback on lesson plans, feedback on 
lessons, modeling, and guiding reflection) provided by support persons after controlling for mastery experiences? 
RQ3) Do the moderated effects remain after controlling for mastery experiences and methodological variables (i.e., research design, 
teacher self-efficacy measurement, and teacher group)? 
We expect that university-based and school-based interventions will show an overall effect in favor of the interventions on teacher 
self-efficacy across studies. The overall effect can be seen in PSTs and BTs showing higher teacher self-efficacy after an intervention (a) 
in comparison to PSTs and BTs with no (formal) intervention or (b) at the end of the intervention compared to before the intervention. 
Based on previous research on mentoring (Ellis et al., 2020; Ingersoll & Strong, 2011; Kraft et al., 2018; Mok & Staub, 2021) which 
showed improvements in the teaching skills of PSTs and BTs through the assistance of a support person – and findings reporting that 
teaching experiences affect teacher self-efficacy (Holzberger et al., 2013) – we assume that interventions to promote teaching skills 
that include mentoring can also positively affect teacher self-efficacy. Particularly, the individual support activities such as feedback on 
lesson plans, feedback on lessons, modeling, and guided reflections by support persons trigger pivotal self-efficacy processes to develop 
teacher self-efficacy (Bandura, 1997). Hence, we expect that these support activities will have additional influence as moderator 
variables on the overall effect of interventions on PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy. Finally, we expect that the moderator effects will 
remain stable after controlling for mastery experiences and relevant methodological variables. 
2. Method 
2.1. Literature search 
We conducted a systematic literature search in the databases Educational Resources Information Center (ERIC), PsycInfo, Web of 
Science, Scopus, and FIS Bildung. To increase the number of potential studies, we looked for peer-reviewed articles published in 
English and German. We used different combinations of search terms to identify relevant studies. In detail, to identify different

approaches to assisting and fostering PSTs’ or BTs’ teacher self-efficacy, we used the terms “coaching”, “mentoring”, “supervision”, 
“training”, “program”, “intervention”, or “professional development”. These terms for the approaches were linked with either the 
target group terms (“PSTs”, “student teachers”, “teacher candidates” for PSTs and the terms “beginning teachers”, “novice teachers” for 
BTs) or teaching phase terms (“field experience”, “practicum”, or “induction”; for a detailed key word combination syntax for the 
literature search, see Online Appendix A). 
2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria 
To be included in further analyses, studies had to investigate whether interventions such as coaching, mentoring, supervision, 
educational programs, university-based courses, or teacher professional development programs showed an increase, positive effect, or 
change in teacher self-efficacy. The dependent variable of the studies needed to be teacher self-efficacy. The target groups were 
restricted to PSTs or BTs who were in their first year of teaching. Studies also had to use a (quasi-)experimental pre-posttest control 
group design, pre-posttest design without a control group, or a posttest design with a control group. Accordingly, the studies had to be 
quantitative to be included in the meta-analysis. Studies were excluded from the analyses if they (a) focused only on students with 
special needs, sports education, or teaching in low-income settings, as the teaching content might deviate strongly from the other 
studies, or (b) did not provide sufficient quantitative data (e.g., means and standard deviations per experimental group) to calculate 
relevant effect sizes (despite requests sent to authors to share their data for the meta-analysis). 
2.3. Screening and coding of study characteristics 
Initially we identified 610 studies. The potential studies were screened and coded in two phases following the PRISMA guidelines 
(Page et al., 2021). In the first phase, the first author screened and coded all abstracts and title based on the inclusion and exclusion 
criteria. A second rater (i.e., the second author) was trained with 20 studies before double-coding 94 studies (approximately 15% of all 
studies) based on the abstracts and titles to identify relevant studies for further analyses (Cohen’s kappa = .796). Disagreements 
regarding the inclusion of studies were discussed and resolved. This process resulted in 34 studies with 63 effects entering the second 
screening phase. 
In the second screening phase, the characteristics and moderators of 34 studies were coded based on an analysis of the full-texts. A 
coding manual (for an overview, see Online Appendix A) was developed based on previous reviews describing significant learning 
activities offered by support persons (i.e., mentors, cooperating teachers, and supervisors; Ambrosetti et al., 2014; Clarke et al., 2014; 
Ellis et al., 2020; Mok & Staub, 2021; Spooner-Lane, 2017). These activities can be summarized in four main individual support ac­
tivities within collaborations between support persons and PSTs or BTs that also offer vicarious experience and verbal persuasion. We 
dummy-coded all moderator variables. Each of the individual support activities (i.e., feedback on lesson plans, feedback on lessons, 
modeling of teaching practices or planning, guided reflections) were coded with two codes: we assigned code 1 if a specific activity 
occurred in a study and code 0 if the specific activity did not occur. Mastery experiences in teaching situations as a control variable was 
also dummy-coded. Similarly, we dummy-coded the methodological moderator variables (research design: pre-posttest-control group 
design vs. pre-posttest design without control group; teacher group: BTs vs. PSTs, and the teacher self-efficacy measurement: global vs. 
domain- or context-specific self-efficacy measurement); the first category of each moderator was given code 0 and the second category 
was coded 1. All study characteristics (Table 1) and moderator variables (Table 2) were coded by the first and second author. Again, 
disagreements were discussed by both raters until they agreed on a solution. 
2.4. Meta-analytic procedures 
Before we synthesized effect sizes into one overall effect, we calculated the effect sizes for each study or transformed them into 
Hedges’ g using the metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010) and esc (Lüdecke, 2018) packages in R. For studies with a pre-posttest design without 
control group or posttest-control group design, we calculated the effect sizes by using the pooled pretest SDs (Morris, 2008). For studies 
with a posttest-control group design, we calculated the effect sizes if the means and standard deviations of the experimental (and 
control) group or F-statistics and the p-value of an ANOVA were reported (Borenstein et al., 2021). Then, we proceeded with the 
calculation of the overall effect across all studies for the meta-analysis with the R-package robumeta (Fisher et al., 2017). To assess 
publication bias, we used metafor and robumeta. Finally, the moderator analyses were conducted in robumeta. 
2.4.1. Meta-analytic aggregation 
For the overall effect, we estimated a random-effects model because the included studies assessed different school-based and 
university-based interventions of PSTs and BTs from various regions. Most of the included studies showed multiple effect sizes due to 
the measurement of different aspects of teacher self-efficacy (i.e., student engagement, instructional strategies, and classroom man­
agement self-efficacy). To address the dependency of effect sizes, we used robust variance estimation (RVE) adjusting for the standard 
errors of the effect sizes (Hedges et al., 2010; Tanner-Smith et al., 2016). The RVE procedure also applies a small-sample correction.

2.4.4. Analyses of moderators 
To investigate the moderator effects we used multiple meta-regression models (Tanner-Smith & Tipton, 2014). In Model 1, we 
included all individual support activities of support persons (feedback on lesson plans, feedback on lessons, modeling of teaching 
practices or planning, guided reflection of PSTs/BTs) and controlled for mastery experiences. In Model 2, we additionally included 
relevant methodological variables such as the research design, the teacher group, and the teacher self-efficacy measurement. More­
over, we tested whether the moderators (i.e., feedback on lesson plans, feedback on lessons, modeling of teaching practices or 
planning, guided reflection) differed significantly from the reference group (i.e., none of the individual support activities were used in 
the interventions2). To test these differences, we used the Wald test function in the R-package clubsandwich (Pustejovsky, 2022). The 
Wald test function examines the linear contrasts from a regression model by using a sandwich estimator for the variance-covariance 
matrix and a small sample correction for the p-value (Tanner-Smith et al., 2016; Tipton & Pustejovsky, 2015). 
3. Results 
3.1. Descriptive statistics 
All descriptive statistics regarding the study characteristics are shown in Table 1. We reported the descriptive statistics for all 
studies including the outliers, but the moderator coding information were reported without outliers (see 3.2.). The study character­
istics included the country in which the study was conducted, school level, research design, number of participants in the treatment 
and control group, outcome(s), and teacher self-efficacy measurement. The moderators (i.e., individual support activities) that 
occurred in each study are displayed in Table 2. Most of the coded individual support activities were rarely applied in the teacher self- 
efficacy interventions included in the meta-analysis: providing feedback on lesson plans (j = 5 studies, k = 7 effects), providing 
feedback on lessons (j = 8 studies, k = 12 effects), and modeling teaching or planning practices (j = 5 studies, k = 6 effects). One 
exception is the guided reflection as support activity (j = 11 studies, k = 17 effects). 
3.2. Results of the preliminary analyses 
In the preliminary analyses, we checked the group sizes of each study and ran an outlier analysis. Initially, we identified 34 po­
tential studies with 63 effect sizes. However, we detected three studies with small group sizes (n ≤10 participants per experimental 
group), which might not allow normal distributed testing of group differences and, therefore, were removed from our sample (Anthony 
et al., 2011; Fogg-Rogers et al., 2017; Vansteelandt et al., 2020). The sample for further analyses therefore consisted of 31 studies and 
53 effect sizes. The test of influence diagnostics revealed four outlier effects (Cinici, 2016; Goker, 2006; Kissau & Algozzine, 2015, 
Study 1; Saputro et al., 2020), each indicating an extremely high effect size. Consequently, we removed these four effects from our final 
sample (N = 28 studies, k = 49 effect sizes). 
3.3. Overall effect 
To answer RQ1, we showed a significant and medium-sized overall effect on teacher self-efficacy across all 28 studies (k = 49 effect 
sizes; Hedges’ g = 0.678 (SE = 0.064, p < .001, CI[0.547, 0.809], I2 = 90.133, Fig. 1). That means that interventions were more 
effective in improving PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy compared to the control group or compared to before the intervention. 
3.4. Results of the moderator analyses 
To answer RQ2, we investigated whether individual support activities of support persons moderated the overall effect of in­
terventions on PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy after controlling for mastery experiences. We included all individual support ac­
tivities (i.e., providing feedback on lesson plans, providing feedback on lessons, modeling teaching, or planning practices, and guiding 
reflection) and teaching experiences as mastery experiences in the model. The intercept refers to the reference group (i.e., none of the 
four individual support activities were applied within interventions). The results of Model 1 showed that if none of the four individual 
support activities were offered by support persons, the effect was medium-sized (g = 0.629, SE = 0.102, p < .001, CI[0.401, 0.856], 
Table 3a). The results further revealed that providing feedback on a lesson plan was the only moderator of the overall effect on teacher 
self-efficacy that was very close to the significance level of 0.05 and it showed a large effect (g = 1.033, SE = 0.273, p = .056, Table 3a). 
The results of the Wald test revealed a significant difference between the effect of the moderator providing feedback on lesson plans 
compared to the reference group (i.e., none of the four individual support activities are used; Wald = 23.8, df = 7.6, p < .001). This 
moderator effect means that teacher self-efficacy was significantly larger in interventions when support persons provided feedback on

3.5. Publication bias 
We also tested whether there was a publication bias in our sample. We found no evidence for an asymmetry of published studies 
based on the Egger’s regression test (b = 0.634, z = −0.115, p = .908, CI[0.409; 0.859]). We additionally conducted the conditional 
PET-PEESE estimator test (Stanley & Doucouliagos, 2014). The PET estimate was not significant: β1 = −0.0722, SE = 0.907, p = .938, 
CI[-2.025, 1.88]. The intercept reflecting the unbiased true meta-analytic overall effect was significant: Intercept β0 = 0.689, SE =
0.15, p = .00081, CI[0.358, 1.02]. Given the non-significant PET estimate, we used the PET estimate result as the conditional 
PET-PEESE estimate as recommended by Carter et al. (2019). In sum, the different tests for publication bias revealed that the adjusted 
and unadjusted overall effects produced comparable effect sizes: the dependency-adjusted Hedges’ g = 0.678 was comparable to the 
intercept of the PET-PEESE model β0 = 0.689 and the unadjusted Egger’s regression test b = 0.634. The test results suggest that there is 
no bias due to substantial publication bias (Carter et al., 2019; Stanley & Doucouliagos, 2014). 
4. Discussion 
The aims of the present meta-analysis were to investigate the effectiveness of interventions (including mentoring approaches) on 
teacher self-efficacy for teachers at the beginning of their career (i.e., PSTs and BTs) and to shed more light on the influence of in­
dividual support activities as moderators of these intervention effects. The results of the meta-analysis based on 28 studies (49 effects) 
showed a significant and medium-sized overall effect of interventions on teacher self-efficacy (Hedges’ g = 0.678; p < .001, CI[0.547, 
0.809]). The moderator analyses revealed that providing feedback on lesson plans is an important individual support activity that has 
the potential to increase teacher self-efficacy above and beyond the averaged intervention effect: the provision of feedback on lesson 
plans by support persons within interventions had a large effect on teacher self-efficacy. However, the moderator effect of feedback on 
lesson plans was not significant, as it slightly exceeded the significance level of 0.05. As teacher self-efficacy is positively associated 
with different teaching-related outcomes (e.g., Dicke et al., 2014; Lazarides et al., 2020; for a review, see Zee & Koomen, 2016), it is

especially important that the teacher self-efficacy of early career teachers like PSTs and BTs is supported by interventions. The findings 
of our meta-analysis contribute to the teacher education literature by systematically investigating the effectiveness of existing 
intervention studies promoting teacher self-efficacy (and teaching skills) and to what extent individual support activities additionally 
affect teacher self-efficacy of PSTs and BTs. 
Comparing the effect sizes of the findings on teaching-related skills is important because teachers’ own teaching experiences have 
been shown to affect their teacher self-efficacy (Holzberger et al., 2013). The overall effect in our meta-analysis of interventions on 
teacher self-efficacy also lines up with previous meta-analytic findings of interventions on teaching-related skills of (early career) 
teachers. For instance, meta-analysis by Kraft et al. (2018) on the effects of coaching in-service teachers found medium-sized effects on 
teacher instruction (effect size = 0.49 SD). A meta-analysis by Mok and Staub (2021) examining the effects of mentoring, coaching, and 
supervision during the teaching practicum on PSTs’ lesson planning and instructional clarity found only a small overall effect (Cohen’s 
d = 0.41). In contrast, our meta-analysis revealed a medium effect size across interventions on teacher self-efficacy. This difference in 
effect size for PSTs’ lesson planning and instructional skills (Mok & Staub, 2021) and our effect size for PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher 
self-efficacy is in line with research demonstrating that inexperienced PSTs score high on teacher self-efficacy after their first teaching 
practicum (for a review, see Ulrich et al., 2020). Furthermore, it is worth noting that the assessment of instructional skills in inter­
vention studies included in meta-analyses typically involves ratings of videotaped teaching lessons by independent raters (Kraft et al., 
2018; Mok & Staub, 2021), which likely provides more realistic estimations of actual teaching skills compared to self-reported teacher 
self-efficacy. 
While no previous meta-analysis has been examined the effects of interventions on teacher self-efficacy for PSTs and BTs, there 
have been correlational meta-analyses that have focused on the relationships between teacher self-efficacy and educational outcomes 
such as teacher commitment (Chesnut & Burley, 2015) and teaching effectiveness (e.g., Klassen & Tze, 2014). The overall effect from 
our meta-analysis of interventions on teacher self-efficacy is comparable to the findings of Klassen and Tze’s (2014) correlational 
meta-analysis, which demonstrated a medium-sized effect of teacher self-efficacy on evaluated teaching performance (r = 0.28). In 
contrast to Klassen and Tze’s (2014) meta-analysis, in our meta-analysis we provide robust evidence for the effectiveness of in­
terventions that positively affected teacher self-efficacy of PSTs and BTs. 
It is noteworthy that previous meta-analyses have predominantly focused on investigating effects on either specific teaching- 
related outcomes or teacher self-efficacy alone. However, longitudinal (non-meta-analytic) studies have shown an association be­
tween instructional skills and teacher self-efficacy over time (Hettinger et al., 2021; Holzberger et al., 2013). To date, no meta-analytic 
path model has been examined the intervention effects on both instructional skills and teacher self-efficacy simultaneously. Thus, it 
would be valuable for future research to explore these longitudinal associations between instructional skills and teacher self-efficacy in 
intervention studies at the meta-analytic level to learn more about these relationships and their potential causality. 
Our findings regarding the individual support activities provided by other persons are in accordance with Bandura’s (1997) 
argument that it is not the teaching performance per se that is the primary basis for the development of self-efficacy, but rather the 
reflection and interpretation of one’s own performance. The reflection and interpretation of PSTs’ and BTs’ teaching performance can 
be facilitated by individual support activities. In detail, several intervention studies included in the present meta-analysis used multiple 
sources of self-efficacy such as vicarious experiences through modeling and verbal persuasion through feedback on lesson plans and 
lessons to support teacher self-efficacy (in addition to mastery experiences through teaching students). This may at least partially 
explain the positive, moderate-sized overall effect of interventions on teacher self-efficacy in our meta-analysis. Some research 
especially emphasizes the indirect assistance of different support persons activating self-efficacy sources (e.g., verbal persuasion, 
vicarious experience; Clark & Newberry, 2019), combined with mastery experiences in teaching situations during the collaboration 
with a support person (Tschannen-Moran & McMaster, 2009). As a result, support persons may provide verbal persuasion to PSTs and 
BTs during their feedback sessions and stimulate reflection through dialogue. Thus, feedback from a support person inform PSTs’ and 
BTs’ perceptions of their teaching skills as well as provide encouragement and strategies for coping with situational demands in the 
future (Tschannen-Moran et al., 1998). Hence, the moderator effect of providing feedback on lesson plans is consistent with findings 
suggesting a positive impact of the assistance of support persons on PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy (Moulding et al., 2014; Rupp & 
Becker, 2021; Tschannen-Moran & McMaster, 2009). This feedback likely involves verbal persuasion by a support person, which serves 
as a source of self-efficacy for PSTs and BTs. However, the collaboration with support persons also involves other self-efficacy sources 
associated with teacher self-efficacy. For example, feedback from a support person can range from kind words of encouragement (i.e., 
verbal persuasion) to critical feedback (i.e., verbal evaluation by telling how to do something better or suggesting alternative actions) 
with scaffolding (Clark & Newberry, 2019). Previous studies have shown that maintaining a sense of efficacy is more challenging when 
support persons express doubts about one’s capabilities (Bandura, 1997) or engage in co-constructive lesson planning during lesson 
conferences (Rupp & Becker, 2021). As a result, supported persons can support or hinder positive changes in teacher self-efficacy for 
PSTs and BTs in different ways, but they undoubtedly play an important role in fostering he professional development of PSTs and BTs 
(Klassen & Durksen, 2014). 
Not only does a support person provide instructional support but also emotional support to facilitate PSTs’ and BTs’ professional 
learning (Ellis et al., 2020). Emotional support in feedback sessions is equally important to the professional development of PSTs and 
BTs as instructional and task-related support (e.g., Davis & Fantozzi, 2016; Hennissen et al., 2011). PSTs and BTs desire emotional 
support that focuses on creating a supportive, trusting, and caring environment that enables PSTs and BTs to move forward rather than 
being evaluated, which may also be related to building their teacher self-efficacy (e.g., Davis & Fantozzi, 2016; Hartl et al., 2022; 
Hennissen et al., 2011). This underscores the relevance of the source of self-efficacy advocated by Bandura (1997), affective cues, 
which are related to one’s physical and emotional responses to a particular task (Fives et al., 2007). Emotional support is also viewed as 
a crucial facet in the relationship between PSTs and their support person (e.g., Hascher & Hagenauer, 2016), determining whether

14
verbal persuasion is integrated into one’s own beliefs. Surprisingly, affective cues did not play a leading role in most of the analyzed 
interventions. Only two intervention studies concentrated on practicing specific skills such as stress management skills for teachers 
(Çelebi et al., 2014; Dicke et al., 2014). The lack of consideration of emotional aspects in the relationship between PSTs and support 
persons is consistent with findings from a review by Hagenauer and Volet (2014) pointing at this understudied research field. Future 
research should, therefore, explore affective cues in the relationship between PSTs or BTs and their support persons and their effects on 
teacher self-efficacy. 
No further moderator effects were significant in our meta-analysis. This was surprising, as previous findings hinted at potential 
positive moderator effects of other individual support activities which can be linked to self-efficacy sources. For instance, the feedback 
provided by a support person focusing on teaching behavior was related to PSTs’ teacher self-efficacy during the practicum (Prilop 
et al., 2019) and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy during the induction phase (Richter et al., 2013). There are different explanations as to why 
we did not detect additional moderator effects. First, the number of included studies (Table 2) might be rather small for investigating 
moderator effects with meta-regression models. Still, one individual support activity stood out in our moderator analyses: providing 
feedback on lesson plans can be highlighted as moderator that showed a large effect size, even though this moderator missed the .05 
significance level in Model 1 and 2. Thus, teacher education researchers need more intervention studies exploring the development of 
teacher self-efficacy in early career teachers to provide a solid base for future meta-analyses on teacher self-efficacy. Second, most 
intervention studies included in our meta-analysis focused primarily on the improvement of teaching skills in authentic teaching 
situations as mastery experiences. However, other variables such as individual competence variables might also influence PSTs’ and 
BTs’ teacher self-efficacy. Previous research, for example, found that PSTs’ and BTs’ level of content knowledge regarding their school 
subject plays a further role in the development of teacher self-efficacy (Swackhamer et al., 2009), and teacher programs for PSTs and 
BTs also affect content knowledge (Menon & Sadler, 2016). Little is known about the relationship between these two variables and 
teaching skills but based on Bandura (1997) it seems likely that content knowledge and teacher self-efficacy both mediate 
teaching-related intervention effects on teaching skills. Future intervention studies, therefore, should include PSTs’ and BTs’ content 
knowledge and teacher self-efficacy as potential mediators of the intervention effects on teaching skills. 
There are also methodological aspects that need to be discussed. Unlike general teacher self-efficacy measurements, task-specific 
teacher self-efficacy measures consider the context in which PSTs and BTs must perform. Recognizing the drawbacks of general 
measurements, researchers have begun to emphasize the context-specific nature of teacher self-efficacy (e.g., Thommen et al., 2022). 
Following Chesnut and Burley’s (2015) meta-analysis, we therefore used a combined variable for “specific teacher self-efficacy 
measurement” considering either a specific teaching context or situation (context specificity) or a specific school subject (domain 
specificity). However, given our sample size of 28 studies, we restricted our meta-regression models to as few control variables as 
possible to calculate reliable results. Consequently, we contrasted the combined “specific teacher self-efficacy measure” to a more 
general teacher self-efficacy measure. Future meta-analyses with a larger sample size should differentiate between domain and context 
specificity and explore them as separate categories. 
4.1. Limitations 
Our meta-analysis had several limitations. First, there might have been learning and support activities in interventions that were 
not captured in the present meta-analysis. Further support activities such as group discussions with peer students might be also 
interesting to explore, as feedback from peers may also be related to PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy (e.g., Prilop et al., 2021; Seung 
et al., 2019). However, the additional investigation of these activities is beyond the scope of this meta-analysis and would further 
restrict the statistical power for the meta-regression models. Second, as instructional skills are related to teacher self-efficacy it is 
surprising that most of the interventions included in our meta-analysis did not report an initial assessment of instructional skills or 
pedagogical content knowledge. Without a baseline assessment of instructional skills or pedagogical content knowledge, the 
self-reported teacher self-efficacy of less experienced PSTs or BTs might be overestimated at the beginning of their career. With little 
teaching experience, it may be difficult for PSTs and BTs to accurately judge their level of teacher self-efficacy because they might not 
see all relevant aspects regarding planning, instruction, and engagement of students during teaching. Thus, we recommend that future 
primary studies should report pre- and post-test measures of instructional skills or pedagogical content knowledge as well as teacher 
self-efficacy to compare the level of actual instructional skills and teacher self-efficacy. Third, we cannot rule out the possibility that 
the control groups in our intervention studies had some kind of formal traditional support by a school-based mentor, as this is 
commonly provided during teacher education programs, or minimal assistance by other persons (such as informal meetings). Some 
studies summarized the support received in the control group, including for example group discussions or self-reflection tasks; in other 
studies detailed information about the support received in the control group was lacking. Hence, these control groups in our sample 
might have received some support activities informally. Nevertheless, we found a large overall effect for teacher self-efficacy in favor of 
the interventions that implemented new elements compared to more traditional teacher education programs, which suggests that these 
programs can be improved, even if they already offer some support. 
Finally, we included studies from various countries embedded in different cultural contexts but did not assess differences between 
countries. It is possible that the cultural context plays a role in teacher self-efficacy. Previous studies have consistently found that 
teachers of East Asian countries report lower self-efficacy than teachers of Western countries (Ho & Hau, 2004; Klassen et al., 2009). 
Interestingly, teachers of East Asian countries often have high-performing students (e.g., OECD, 2010) and teachers with a high degree 
of professional knowledge. Hence, it seems that this low teacher self-efficacy combined with the high performance of students does not 
match well to the assumptions of social cognitive theory (Bandura, 1997) or to previous findings of positive relationships between 
teacher self-efficacy and student performance (Klassen & Tze, 2014). One possible explanation for this “mismatch” lays in different

cultural orientations (individualistic vs. collectivistic orientations; Vieluf et al., 2013). This is supported by findings from a secondary 
analysis of the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS; OECD, 2009) data based on 23 countries by Vieluf et al. (2013), 
which showed that collectivistic orientations were negatively associated with teacher self-efficacy. Unfortunately, we had only two 
studies from highly collectivistic-orientated East Asian countries (i.e., South Korea), which is not a solid base for a moderator analysis. 
Nevertheless, future meta-analyses with enough studies from different collectivistic-orientated countries should investigate cultural 
orientation as moderator of teacher self-efficacy effects. 
4.2. Conclusion 
Based on the findings of our meta-analysis, we can conclude that interventions are effective in improving the teacher self-efficacy of 
early career teachers (PSTs and BTs). On average, the interventions showed a medium effect size. This effectiveness was amplified 
when a support person (e.g., school-based cooperating teacher, mentor, or university supervisor) provided feedback on lesson plans. 
More experimental studies are needed to gain further insight into individual support activities and other potential moderators of the 
intervention effects on PSTs’ and BTs’ teacher self-efficacy. With a larger number of experimental studies and meta-analytic pro­
cedures, future research might explain in more depth the intervention design elements needed to maximize the effectiveness of in­
terventions for early career teachers.
