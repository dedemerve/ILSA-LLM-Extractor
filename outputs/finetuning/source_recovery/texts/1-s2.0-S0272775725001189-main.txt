Schools are often used as temporary shelters during calamities in many places around the world. This may
prolong calamity-induced school closures, which may contribute to learning losses. In this study, we combined
student assessment data from the Philippine round of the 2019 Trends in International Mathematics and Science
Study (TIMSS) with school administrative records and area-level typhoon warnings to assess the impact of short
school closures on learning outcomes. Results show that one school closure day induced by school-as-shelter use
depresses student achievement by 12 to 14 percent of a standard deviation, equivalent roughly to as much as a
full year’s worth of learning. We find limited evidence that this could be partly driven by a decline in student
interest, rather than by a contraction in the breadth of topics covered in class or by poorer teaching quality. These
findings highlight potential hidden disasters from seemingly benign but frequent hazards.

In many places around the world, schools are often used as temporary shelters before, during and immediately after calamities. The
ubiquity and strategic location of schools, with its many rooms and
amenities, makes them natural candidates for this purpose. On the one
hand, this may arrest further economic losses from disasters by
providing safe shelters for vulnerable populations during calamities. But
the secondary use of schools as shelters may encroach on its primary
function as dedicated formal learning spaces for children. This may have
important immediate implications on human capital acquisition and
therefore later future productivity.
Previous studies on the impact of natural disasters on school closures, school attendance and learning outcomes were largely based on
acute but generally infrequent shocks, such as pandemics, super typhoons and volcanic eruptions (c.f. Marin et al., 2024). The impact of
much benign but more commonly occurring hazards, on the other hand,
remains poorly understood (Cadag et al., 2017). Some exceptions
include Zivin et al. (2018) who document the negative effect of

The Philippines is one of the most typhoon-prone countries in the
world with about 20 typhoons entering its territory annually, of which
about nine make landfall and five likely to be destructive (Lapidez et al.,
2015). The economic losses from such typhoons are quite massive, with
Typhoons Ondoy and Pepeng, which hit in 2009, for example, costing
the country about 2.7 percent of its gross domestic product (GDP), with
other less destructive typhoons reducing economic activity by about one
to two percent (Strobl, 2019).
While Philippine public policy officially discourages the use of
schools as temporary shelters during such calamities, they are nonetheless often the de facto evacuation centers potentially due to its wide
availability across the country and the facilities it may offer (David et al.,
2018). In 2020, around 95 percent of barangays (villages) had access to
a school either within its boundaries or outside but within two kilometers from the village government center. Public community centers,
such as multipurpose halls, covered courts, etc., are less prevalent,
especially in less affluent areas.
Our analysis reveals that school closures induced by school-asshelter use could lead to substantial learning losses. We find that one
school closure day is associated with around 0.12 to 0.14 standard deviation (SD) decline in Mathematics and Science achievement, which
could potentially be as much as a year’s worth of learning (c.f. Avvisati
& Givord, 2023). These results are robust to different model specifications and data used in estimation.
We find limited indication that the learning loss may be partly due to
a decline in student interest, rather than by a reduction in curricular
topics covered in class or a deterioration in teaching quality one year
after the school closures. This may be related with previous evidence
suggesting increased psychological distress from traumatic events, such
as displacement because of calamities (Adams et al., 2023; Pfefferbaum
et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019). This underscores the multifaceted nature of learning and the need for multi-pronged interventions to arrest
learning losses from disasters.

data and methods that we employed. We present the results in Section 5.

3. Data
We used Grade 4 student-level Science and Mathematics assessment
data in the 2019 Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study
(TIMSS) in the Philippines. The 2019 TIMSS is the seventh cycle of the
international large-scale assessment, and the third instance that the
Philippines participated in TIMSS.
The TIMSS student assessment is based on a comprehensive test
framework co-developed with participating countries that focuses on
both subject content and student thinking process. The 2019 TIMSS for
Grade 4 covers three content domains in Mathematics (number, measurement and geometry and data) and in Science (life science, physical
science and Earth science), and include approximately 350 test items in
total. A subset of these items is provided to a random set of students. In
addition to the student assessment, TIMSS also collects contextual variables from students, parents, teachers and school heads that provide
information on students’ home and school learning environments.1
Many items in TIMSS are analyzed using response theory (IRT)
models. In such models, a single latent trait, e.g. academic skill, is
recovered based on responses to items designed to measure such trait
while accounting for each item’s properties, including its apparent difficulty and the strength of its correlation with the latent trait. Blocks of
contextual questions, such as on availability of learning resources,
experience of bullying and subject matter confidence, are usually summarized as indices based on partial credit IRT model.
Student assessment scores in Mathematics and Science are also
provided in IRT scales. Unlike other indices in TIMSS, however, five
imputed plausible values are provided for each student in each assessment subject to account for uncertainty associated with measuring latent
variables. Reported TIMSS scores are scaled relative to the distribution
of scores in the first TIMSS in 1995, centered at 500 points with a
standard deviation of 100 points.
We combined responses in TIMSS with information on school closures collected from public schools by DepEd, and tropical cyclone
warnings (TCW) from weather bulletins provided by the Philippine Atmospheric, Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration
(PAGASA).2 The tropical cyclone warnings were converted into typhoon
school days by cross-referencing days with area-level tropical cyclone
warnings with DepEd’s official school calendar for public schools.
Classes in elementary schools in the Philippines that are covered by any
tropical cyclone warning are automatically cancelled. Local government
chief executives may also declare class suspensions for localized extreme
weather disturbances that are not covered by TCWs. We aggregated
these variables as sums by school year and school.
The Philippine public basic education school calendar begins in early
June, coinciding with the start of the southwest monsoon season, and
lasts until early April of the succeeding year, before the peak of the
summer season. The Philippine round of the 2019 TIMSS was collected
in March 2019, towards the end of the school calendar. However, information on school closures from DepEd are only available for public
elementary schools for the prior school year, 2017–2018, and the succeeding school years starting in 2019–2020, skipping the 2019 TIMSS

2. Shelter-use and school closures in the Philippines
The Philippine government, as a national policy, discourages the use
of schools as temporary shelters during calamities. However, dedicated
structures for evacuation or temporary shelter are not always available
in local communities. Indeed, much of the evacuation centers are not
originally designed to be such, which include multipurpose halls, public
school buildings and covered basketball courts (Cajucom et al., 2019).
Fig. 1 shows the share of public elementary schools used as shelters
in each municipality in school year 2017–2018 based on information
collected by the Department of Education (DepEd) from public schools.
While only five percent of public elementary schools across the country
were ever used as evacuation centers during this period, the rates vary
wildly across locations, reaching as high as two-thirds of all schools in a
municipality. Schools in the eastern seaboard of the country appears to
be the most used as temporary shelters, which coincides with the usual
path and first landfall location of typhoons coming from the Pacific
Ocean.
The number of public elementary schools used as shelters seems to
follow the progression of the Philippine monsoon season that starts in
early June, and appears to correlate with the strength of the typhoon
passing the Philippines. The December 2017 peak shown in Fig. 2 (Panel
A) coincides with the back-to-back tropical cyclones Kai-tak and Tembin
that passed through the southern Philippine islands during this period.

The 2019 TIMSS implemented a two-stage random sampling design.
In the first stage, schools are randomly selected. From each of these
schools, one Grade 4 class section is randomly selected to take the student assessment. From the 180 randomly selected schools that participated in the TIMSS 2019, we excluded 23 private schools, which do not
report school closures to DepEd, and one public school, which has

problematic school closures information. This resulted in 156 public
schools left for analysis, of which nine schools have had been used as
shelters in school year 2017–2018. The final database included almost
5000 Grade 4 public school students whose TIMSS scores in Mathematics and Science averaged 287 and 236 points, respectively, which are
both slightly lower than the national average of 297 and 249 points.

We are interested in estimating the impact of school closures on
student learning outcomes. In an ideal scenario, school closures are
randomly assigned and that they are uncorrelated with other covariates
that also affect student outcomes. This may not be realistic as the decision to suspend classes may vary with attributes, such as leadership
style by school principals or exposure to natural hazards, that may also
influence student outcomes. Naïve comparison of average outcomes by
school closure days would generally be biased as a result.
In order to address this endogeneity issue, we use the number of days
a school had been used as temporary shelter to instrument for the
number of school closure days in a two-stage least squares framework. In
particular we, we specify our main estimating equation as

We instrument dcs by the number of days a school had been used as
shelter in the same school year. This technique critically relies on two
assumptions. First, the instrument, i.e., school-as-shelter days, should be
relevant in explaining the endogenous variable, i.e., school closure days.
Second, the instrument should have no direct effect on the outcomes of
interest, i.e., uncorrelated with the residual ϵis , and that it only influences the outcomes indirectly through the endogenous variable dcs .
The first assumption may be directly tested. As we have shown in
Table 1, schools used as shelters have longer school closure days. But
Table 1 also suggests that the use of schools as shelters is correlated with
exposure to natural hazards and school head characteristics, which have
been documented to be correlated with schooling outcomes. This
potentially violates our second assumption. Had these variables not been
measured and considered in our model, the estimates from the instrumental variable (IV) model are expected to be biased with generally
unknown direction and magnitude. Since these covariates are observed,
we can include them in our IV model to remove the bias that they may
introduce if they were excluded.
The second assumption is not testable. While we have included
additional covariates to remove bias from potential confounding, there
may still be other remaining unobserved variables that we cannot control for in our IV model. We perform several robustness checks to assess

The results in Table 2 suggest that school closures induced by schoolas-shelter use have a sizeable negative impact on learning outcomes. An
additional school closure day reduces students’ learning achievement as
measured in the 2019 TIMSS by as much as 12.7 points in Mathematics
and by 14.1 points in Science based on the full model. It is worth noting
that the point estimates are negative but much muted in the more basic
models, suggesting the importance of controlling for additional covariates that are correlated with both learning outcomes and the chosen
instrument. We provide the full set of estimates, including those for the
first-stage regression, in an Appendix. We confirm that school-as-shelter
use induces longer school closure days on a one-for-one basis.
As shown in Table 2, sample sizes decline markedly as additional
control variables are introduced, reflecting nonresponse patterns among
students and school heads. To assess potential biases arising from
varying samples, we re-estimated all models using the restricted sample
corresponding to the full specification. Results based on this common
restricted sample (not shown but available upon request) are nearly
identical, indicating that differences in sample composition do not drive
our findings.
Previous evidence from the literature show that natural disasters
may affect the education outcomes of boys and girls differently (e.g.
Bjorkman-Nyqvist, 2013; Takasaki, 2017). We explored this in our
analysis by re-estimating the IV models separately for boys and girls.
Results presented in Table 3 show that the impact of school closures on
learning outcomes appear to be similar for boys and girls in our study
sample.
The impact of school closures that we presented are on the extreme
negative end relative to the distribution of effect sizes in education reported in other recent studies. Evans and Yuan (2022), for example,
provide percentiles of standardized effect sizes on learning and access
based on education interventions in low- and middle-income countries.
They reported median standardized effect sizes on Mathematics
achievement of 0.05 for quasi-experimental studies and 0.07 for randomized control trials (RCTs). Our IV estimates are lower than their
reported tenth percentile of − 0.05 for RCTs and − 0.09 for

confounding. First, we estimated additional IV models that include
previously excluded covariates that may influence learning outcomes.
We also performed a placebo test using school closure information from
school year 2019–2020, i.e., the school year after the TIMSS 2019. We
expect that school closures that are yet to happen should have no influence on student achievements in the 2019 TIMSS, and that any estimated impact from this exercise reflects the potential bias from violating
the second assumption. Finally, we also estimated IV models using
student achievement in a national assessment taken by Grade 4 students
in October 2018 as additional robustness check.
Our identification strategy primarily relies on the variations in
school closure days induced by differences in the length of time that
schools had been used as shelters after controlling for the influence of
typhoon school days and other student and school characteristics. That
is, we purge the variation in school-as-shelter days of the systematic
influence of the mentioned variables, in effect leaving only random
variations that, in turn, drive the differences in school closure days. As
such, our estimates may be seen as a local average treatment effect
among students enrolled in a school that had longer school closure days
because of the school’s use as shelter.
As we have noted in the previous section, the 2019 TIMSS provides
five plausible values per subject tested for each student assessed. These
values are imputed based on random draws from the IRT model that
incorporates students’ responses in the assessment, their background
characteristics, and the psychometric characteristics of the assessment
items. Following the guidance by Rubin, 1897 and Beaton and Gonzalez
(1995), we estimated separate IV models for each of the five sets of
plausible values, and average the resulting point estimates, covariance
matrices, and test statistics.

Recent impact estimates of natural disasters on learning outcomes
based on reduced-form models are also economically significant but
relatively small compared to our estimates. Focusing on hydrological
calamities, Thamatanajit (2020) documented around 0.06SD drop in
national student assessment scores of Grade 6 and 9 students in Thailand
because of massive floods in 2011. Pelli and Tschopp (2025), on the
other hand, show that storm exposure in India leads to around 0.02SD
decline in academic performance among those in middle school. There
are limited studies looking at the impact of temporary school closures
related to hydrological events. An exception includes Morill and Westall
(2023) who explored the impact of extended school closures due to
Hurricane Florence in North Carolina in 2018. They document heterogeneous but relatively small impacts of up to 0.005SD in learning loss
per day of temporary school closure.
It may also be instructive to compare our results with estimated
learning losses from the extended school closures during the Covid-19
pandemic around the world. The Philippines had one of the most protracted pandemic-induced school closures around the world, lasting two
and a half years. During the pandemic, content delivery in public
elementary schools had shifted primarily to the use of learning modules,
which focused on the most essential learning competencies (Orbeta,
2022). Comparison of 2018 and 2022 PISA results show no deterioration
in average learning outcomes in the country (Avvisati & Ilizaliturri,
2023). We must emphasize, however, that the causal pathways linking
school closures and learning outcomes during rare pandemics may be
widely different from those experienced much more regularly from
weather disturbances.
Again, our estimates appear to be rather extreme when compared
with the global experience during the pandemic. Jakubowski et al.
(2023) estimated that reading literacy declined by nine percent of a
standard deviation among Grade 4 students in 2021 for every ten weeks,
i.e. about 0.18 percentage-point SD decline per day, of at least partial
school closure during the Covid-19 pandemic. Using learning achievement results of 15-year-old students in 2022, Jakubowski et al. (2024)
documented learning losses of as much as four percent SD decline in
Mathematics and Science scores for every ten weeks, i.e., 0.08
percentage-point SD decline per day, of school closure. Their results are
based on pooled multi-year international large-scale student assessments, i.e., PIRLS for those 10 years old and PISA for those 15 years old,

before and during the Covid-19 pandemic.
5.2. Robustness and falsification tests
In order to assess the sensitivity of our results to potentially
remaining unobserved confounding, we estimated similar IV models,
but using a more extensive list of covariates. Starting from the full model
in Table 2, we sequentially add explanatory variables that may be
correlated with school-as-shelter days that may also influence learning
achievement. We present the results in Table 4.
First, we add fixed effects for broad region and for schools in the
eastern seaboard. These fixed effects should be able to capture the
contribution of common conditions within region and in the eastern
seaboard on learning outcomes. For instance, schools in the eastern
seaboard are usually the most affected by typhoons from the Pacific and
may have developed specific coping mechanisms over the years.
Further, schools within broad regions (National Capital Region, rest of
Luzon, Visayas and Mindanao) may share more similarities, e.g. culture,
environment, weather patterns, etc., compared with schools across
regions.
Next, we control for longer lags of typhoon school days to account for
the potential effect of persistent exposure to extreme weather events. We
also have another model where we weighed typhoon school days by
typhoon-specific intensity as measured by the minimum wind speed for
a particular tropical cyclone warning.
Finally, we control for language homogeneity to proxy the ease of
implementation of the mother tongue-based multi-lingual education
(MTB-MLE), which uses local languages instead of exclusively English or
Filipino as medium of instruction. A language homogeneity index was
calculated as the sum of the squared shares of each mother-tongue by
population in each municipality. The Philippines has adopted MTB-MLE,
but experienced several implementation issues (Orbeta et al., 2021).
Having a more homogeneous mix of students by language use is expected to result in better implementation of MTB-MLE (e.g. Gupta,
1997).
The IV estimates presented in Table 4 appear to be robust to the
inclusion of the additional covariates in these alternative specifications.

As a final robustness check, we also performed a similar IV analysis
using school-level averages of the Early Language Literacy and
Numeracy Assessment (ELLNA) conducted in October 2018. The ELLNA
is a nationally representative student assessment that gauges students’
learning achievements acquired from Kinder to Grade 3. It is administered to a random sample of Grade 4 students, and covers topics in literacy in three languages (Filipino, English and a mother-tongue) and in
numeracy.

average to ease interpretation. The basic IV model controls for typhoon
school days and include school division fixed effects. We also estimated
a more elaborate model wherein we also control for student-teacher
ratio, student-classroom ratio and student drop-out rate, among
others. Similar to our main analysis, we used school closure days from
school year 2017–2018.

induced by school-as-shelter use reduce numeracy scores by about 0.12
SD. The impacts on different language literacy are also negative but are
much muted and measured with substantial uncertainty. It is noteworthy that the estimated impact on numeracy is very similar to our
earlier results using student-level TIMSS assessment data.

same vein, households may choose to recalibrate family expenses away
from longer-term investments, such as children’s education (Deuchert &
Felfe, 2015), towards more immediate concerns, such as for food consumption and reconstruction. Natural calamities may also inflict severe

First, we assessed whether school closures lead to curricular topics
not being taught. We conjecture that school closures reduce school
instructional days that directly impacts the breadth and depth of topics
covered in class. In its teacher module, the 2019 TIMSS directly asks
teachers the extent to which the topics included in the TIMSS assessment
have had been taught in class. The responses are summarized as separate
indices under broader headings.
Second, we checked whether school closures result in worse teaching
quality. Related to the above conjecture, teachers may choose to sacrifice exposition quality to be able to cover the breadth of the prescribed
curriculum. In the 2019 TIMSS, the student questionnaire asks students
to rate the degree of teachers’ instructional quality (e.g. “My teacher is
easy to understand”) separately for Science and Mathematics. These are
also summarized as indices.
Finally, we looked at students’ enthusiasm for Science and

Mathematics, which has been shown to correlate well with assessment
outcomes (Yin & Fishbein, 2020). The 2019 TIMSS asks students to rate
several statements on the degree that they like Science or Mathematics
(e.g. “I like any schoolwork that involves numbers”, “I like to do science
experiments”), which are also transformed by TIMSS into separate
indices.
We need to emphasize that the 2019 TIMSS was collected in March
2019, towards the end of the 2018–2019 school year in the Philippines;
however, we have no school closure information that matches this
school year. Instead, we use school closure information from school year
2017–2018. As such, impacts on curricular coverage, instructional
quality and student motivation may have already dissipated with time.

5.4. Cost implications
The practical implication of our results could be catastrophic. With
an average learning gain of one-tenth to one-fourth SD from one year of
schooling (Avvisati & Givord, 2023), a single day of school closure
induced by school shelter use can practically halve or even wipe out a
whole year’s worth of Mathematics and Science learning.
In turn, this leads to substantial foregone public education financing.
Using DepEd’s USD12 billion budget in 2023 as a hypothetical reference, with about five percent of students enrolled in public schools that
have had been used as shelters and the average school closure of about
six days among schools ever used as shelter (Table 1), as much as a third
of the government’s education budget or USD4 billion-worth of education financing per year could be rendered ineffective because of school
closures.
Following Jacubowski et al. (2023, 2024), we also calculated the
potential long-term economic loss from school closures. We similarly
assume that the drop in TIMSS score among Grade 4 students leads to a
proportional drop in PISA scores among 15-year-old students. This allows us to link PISA-based estimates to calculate implications for
long-term economic welfare. Using the 1.74 coefficient estimated by
Hanushek and Woessmann (2010) and our estimated 0.12SD learning
loss per day of school-as-shelter use-induced school closure, a one-off
nation-wide school closure day in the Philippines translates to about
0.2 percentage points drop in its long-term annual GDP. With the
Philippines’ 2023 GDP, this is roughly equivalent to a foregone one
billion USD for every day of nation-wide school closure.
Even if we apply this to only five percent of students and six days of
temporary school closures as described above, this translates to around
300USD million per year in foregone economic activity resulting from
extended school closures. Such amount is enough to finance the construction of about 6000 classrooms every year in the Philippines. With
the projected rise in the number and intensity of typhoons due to climate
change (Tsuboki et al., 2015), such economic losses from induced school
closures would likely increase in the future if effective mitigating measures are not put in place.

6. Conclusions
We combined student-level assessment data with official school
closure and typhoon warning information to assess the impact of school
closures on children’s learning outcomes. We leverage on school-level
differences in school-as-shelter days to provide causal estimates of the
impact of school closure on Mathematics and Science achievement.
Short school closures induced by school-as-shelter use result in large
learning losses. Mathematics and Science achievement of Grade 4
10

M.R.M. Abrigo et al.

Economics of Education Review 110 (2026) 102738

students in the Philippines decline by 12 to 14 percent of a standard
deviation on average for each day of school closure, which roughly
equates to about half to a whole year’s worth of learning. These effects
are larger than reported comparable per day learning losses from school
closures during the COVID-19 pandemic and closely approximates the
cumulative effects for countries with the longest pandemic-induced
school closures. Our estimates are robust to alternative model specifications and student achievement data employed.
Learning losses from school closures may be driven in part by a drop
in student interest, and not necessarily because of teachers skipping
topics in class or of educators teaching ineffectively. This resonates with
previous results documenting increased psychological distress among
displaced children after disasters, which highlights the need for postdisaster psycho-social interventions for children. However, such interventions may be outside of the purview of education sector managers
as may be traditionally imagined and would require the engagement of
the broader community.
We need to emphasize that there may be other causal pathways that
have been documented in the literature, but we did not consider in this
study due to data limitations. For example, children from more disadvantaged backgrounds may temporarily leave school to take up
employment as part of a broad set of household responses to shocks.
Alternatively, children from more affluent households may have difficulty coping with significant disruptions, such as when schools are used
shelters. We attempt to account for these possibilities in our study by
including different household- and school-level socio-economic measures. However, we are unable to estimate the degree to which these
other pathways contribute to our estimated learning losses.
Why are learning losses from short school closures so big? This may
be related to differences in responses implemented to address learning
losses from school closures. During the COVID-19 pandemic, public
school students in the Philippines were provided physical learning
modules based on key learning competencies that they may study while
sheltering in place. Additional supports were provided through recorded
lessons broadcasted over mass media and through direct interactions
with teachers using mobile communication. These were not available
prior to the pandemic. Instead, teachers provided learners with additional homework or held make up classes if at all since these interventions were not mandatory. These are conjectures that need to be
validated, which we reserve for future examination.
