A B S T R A C T  
The high educational achievement among East Asian nations in ILSAs like TIMSS and PISA is widely known. 
However, a comprehensive review of the research literature is lacking. The current study reviewed the literature 
in English between 1967–2020 with the aim to identify underlying explanatory factors and their relative 
importance with regard to educational achievement among East Asian schoolchildren (Grade 5–10) in Japan, 
China, South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore. N = 99 studies, and seven main themes with 53 corresponding sub- 
themes for these years were discerned. N = 56 studies emphasized cultural and educational quantity variables as 
pertinent for achievement among East Asian nations and populations. Cultural and educational quantity sub- 
themes such as more annual study hours, higher effort, and family expectations dominate as the explanatory 
variables, often emerging as a cross-national theme among East Asian countries. Another theme of significance 
are non-cognitive abilities such as math self-concept and math resilience. Other themes include higher-than 
average cognitive ability levels, although that has partly been contested, as well as curricular themes.   
1. Introduction 
For several decades scholars have noticed that East Asian school­
children and adolescents perform well in International Large-scale As­
sessments (ILSAs) like FIMS (First International Mathematics Study), 
TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study), PIRLS 
(Progress in International Reading Study), and PISA (Programme for 
International Student and Assessment) and cognitive ability tests and 
typically outperform the “white” European majority groups in the US, 
Europe and Australia (e.g., Tsuneyoshi, 2004; Jerrim, 2015; Lee & 
Zhou, 2015). Literature from earlier decades underscores stereotypes 
related to East Asian schoolchildren such as math excellence (Hus´en, 
1967), the “diploma disease” (e.g., Dore, 1976), high IQ (Lynn, 1982), or 
Confucian elements within the education realm (e.g., see Sorensen, 
1994, Yee, 1992, for critical reviews). Whereas the literature often 
suggests cultural and family characteristics as explanations for the high 
scholastic achievement among various East Asian groups (e.g., Hsin & 
Xie, 2014; Jerrim, 2015), a problem seems to be that many studies focus 
on small effects and differences in larger cross-national samples without 
cultural depth, do not differentiate particular national groups (e.g., 
Barro & Lee, 1993; Jerrim, 2015), or are merely based on East Asians in 
the United States (e.g., Wang & Lin, 2005; Lee & Zhou, 2015; Hsin & 
Xie, 2014) or Australia (Dandy & Nettelbeck, 1992; Jerrim, 2015). 
Although there is no shortage of case studies or comparative studies 
on particular East Asian nations like Japan (Lynn, 1988; Takayama, 
2008), China (e.g., *Liu et al., 2005; Wang & Lin, 2005), South Korea 
(Bermeo, 2014; Kim, 2010), Singapore (e.g., Soh, 2014), and Taiwan (e. 
g., Hung, 2007), an extensive overview of the research literature on East 
Asian schoolchildren’s educational performance is lacking. Such a 
research endeavor could aim to identify the explanatory factors or 
themes in the literature on East Asian schoolchildren’s achievement 
levels, as they have been assessed in mathematics, science and reading 
since the mid-1960s (Hus´en et al., 1967; Postlethwaite, 1967; Wolf, 
1971) or other achievement tests. Moreover, the extent to which 
curricular, cultural, cognitive, or non-cognitive factors matter requires 
further analysis. Furthermore, a rather broad geographical approach is 
useful in the pursuit of discerning general achievement factors for East 
Asians that may transcend space and perhaps also time. A comprehen­
sive thematic review of the literature may reveal the proportions of the 
weights ascribed the different factors or themes (e.g., cultural, cognitive, 
non-cognitive, teacher quality) among high-performing East Asian 
schoolchildren. A strong rationale for such a research endeavor, beyond 
mere scholarly curiosity, is that many school systems are trying to 
improve their scores in assessments such as PISA (e.g., see Feniger & 
Lefstein, 2014; Hannum et al., 2019; Ramirez et al., 2006; Stei­
ner-Kamsi, 2014; Sellar & Lingard, 2013; Waldow et al., 2014) for

The current article aims to fill this gap by examining the scholarly 
literature on East Asian schoolchildren (Grade 5–12) from 1967–2020 
and therefore complements earlier works within secondary analysis on 
similar subjects such as *Kim et al. (2019), Chen (2018), Holloway

reviews are more or less outdated (e.g., Holloway, 1988; Wang & Lin, 
2005; Yamamoto & Holloway, 2010; Yee, 1992), has less emphasis on 
East Asian schoolchildren (Chen, 2018), does only focus on socioeco­
nomic status among East Asian school systems (Kim, 2019), or countries 
outside the demarcation such as Vietnam (Kim et al., 2019). Thus, a 
comprehensive thematic review of the literature on the achievement of 
East Asian schoolchildren is needed. 
The review will focus on all East Asian countries – Japan, China, 
South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore – and try to identify the factors that 
have caused them to often outperform their Western counterparts, 
particularly on international scholastic tests. Due to its substantial 
portion of Chinese inhabitants (75%) and strong cultural ties to China, 
Singapore was considered a part of East Asia despite its geographical 
position in Southeast Asia. The exclusion of borderline cases such as 
Vietnam and East Asians in other countries is also related to space 
limitations, as an all-encompassing study could have gargantuan 
proportions. 
The study aims to address the following research question: 
RQ1: What factors explain the high educational attainment in ILSAs 
like FIMS, TIMSS and PISA among East Asian schoolchildren? 
The article proceeds with a theoretical framework section, a method 
section that describes the overall procedure, inclusion criteria, search 
strategy and thematic analysis, a results section that presents, summa­
rizes, and critically discusses the included studies, and ends with a dis­
cussion section which recapitulates the results and discusses the study’s 
implications, limitations, and suggests some directions for future 
research. 
2. Theoretical framework 
A general theory linked to academic performance is the input- 
process-output model, which is a part of a school effectiveness frame­
work (*Ning et al., 2015; Ma, 2008; Perelman & Santin, 2011). This 
implies that students bring with them for example their cognitive and 
non-cognitive abilities, cultural background, and parents’ expectations, 
and in tandem with various school factors (e.g., teacher competence, 
curriculum, school material) processes occur that lead to achievement 
output of various degrees (*Ning et al., 2015). Such achievement is 
linked to for example PISA tests and pupils’ performance (Ma, 2008). Ma 
(2008, pp. 441–442) stresses: 
School effectiveness research employs an input-process-output 
model to describe how school experiences influence educational out­
comes of students with differing family backgrounds. Inputs such as 
family characteristics, home influences, and family social and cultural 
values are what students bring into their schools. Schools, with different 
contexts and climates, then channel (or process) students with different 
inputs into different categories of educational outcomes (outputs). 
In this regard, schools play an important role in how for example 
boys and girls are treated and perform, but also the home environment, 
culture, students’ cognitive ability, and family characteristics matter as 
inputs into the school process (Ma, 2008; Rindermann, 2018). Further­
more, Perelman and Santin (2011) underscore that in quantitative 
research which builds on individual and classroom data, both inputs and 
school factors might be captured. They (2011, p. 32) assert: 
However, disentangling student and school sources of poor perfor­
mance is an identification issue. Several factors could be responsible for 
observed differences in performance – among them the effort and 
motivation put into education by teachers on the one hand, and by 
parents and students on the other. Other factors relate to the overall role 
of institutions, including main pedagogical choices, organizational 
structure and incentive schemes, among others. 
In other words, it can be difficult to identify and differentiate be­
tween student and school characteristics. Nonetheless, this broad and 
general framework allows for a plentitude of factors to potentially 
contribute to school achievement without considering specific school 
process factors in each study’s context. Thus, it informs the research 
questions which are open for several factors (themes). In particular, 
when interpreting studies related to schoolchildren’s outcomes or out­
puts (e.g., test scores) the author has paid attention to inputs such as 
individual abilities, family characteristics, social and cultural values or 
other factors that may be highlighted in the academic literature. How­
ever, some factors are more related to the school process such as teacher 
skills or curricular constitutions, as emphasized by Perelman and Santin 
(2011). 
This does also seem to be largely congruent with the so-called Hey­
neman and Loxley effect, which implies that school process factors are 
typically more important in developing countries than in developed 
countries in which virtually all students have access to schools with 
adequate resources (school buildings, teachers, materials and so on) 
(Baker et al., 2002). In developed countries, socioeconomic status (SES) 
differences are instead more strongly associated with student achieve­
ment. Because most East Asian countries and urban regions were 
considered developed by the 1990s when ILSAs become more wide­
spread, SES might play a more important role for achievement in this 
region. However, because this review does largely focus on 
cross-national differences and ‘comparative advantages’ in the educa­
tional realm (Boman, 2020) it is still likely that for example cultural 
factors and school process factors are more important than SES for high 
achievement in ILSAs. 
3. Method 
3.1. Methodological approach 
The current study constitutes a thematic literature review, partly in 
accordance with Moher et al. (2015; see also Torgerson, 2003). In 
relation to the dependent variable, TIMSS, PISA, SAT (Student 
Achievement Test) scores, and in some cases other school achievement 
test scores or grades, there is a rather rich literature that criticizes the 
PISA surveys as a measure of educational quality (e.g., Prais, 2004; 
Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2016; Sjøberg, 2015), or the weight ascribed to 
rather small differences in performance (Soh, 2014). However, several 
scholars still suggest that PISA measures quality in mathematics, sci­
ence, and reading literacy (e.g., *Hanushek et al., 2019; Sahlberg, 2007) 
or at least is still useful in a comparative respect (Cheung, 2017; Sjøberg, 
2015). This is, in part, due to large sample sizes, typically more than 
5000 participants in each country (e.g., OECD, 2017). Similarly, the SAT 
scores in the United States have been criticized (e.g., Heckman & Kautz, 
2014a) but are nonetheless pertinent as a way to measure school 
knowledge and related skills. 
3.2. Inclusion criteria 
Only peer-reviewed journal articles, books, and book chapters were 
included to increase quality, reliability, and consistency. Books and 
book chapters might not be reviewed to the same extent as peer- 
reviewed articles in academic journals but are typically so by at least 
an editorial referee. They were included if they focused on the factors 
that underlie educational achievement among East Asian schoolchildren 
(Grade 5–10) in Japan, China, South Korea, Taiwan, and Singapore, and

Hong Kong and Macau were considered urban regions of China rather

Whereas this study does not focus on specific effect sizes, the analyzed

The age span is important for two major reasons: it is mainly Grade 5 
(age 10–11) and Grade 8 or 9 in most countries (15-year-olds) that 
participate in ILSAs like TIMSS and PISA. Moreover, the attainment of 
young schoolchildren is more malleable and higher or lower cognitive 
ability levels may be washed out at later stages of development (Heck­
man & Kautz, 2014a; 2014b). Moreover, specific personality dimensions 
are typically not understood and therefore inapplicable to young chil­
dren (Andersen et al., 2020). 
The independent or explanatory variables are those that are identi­
fied in the literature and relate to academic achievement among 
schoolchildren in these five East Asian countries. With regard to study 
design, both quantitative, qualitative and review designs were included. 
Only sources in English were included for consistency and to limit the 
scope of research. 
3.3. Search strategy and coding procedure 
Below, I describe the search strategy and coding procedure, which 
also is manifested in the flowchart figure (see Fig. 1 and Table 1), partly 
in accordance with Moher et al. (2015). Four databases were searched in 
March 2020: ERIC, Google Scholar, PsycINFO, and Web of Science as 
these are some of the most important for educational research and 
related fields within the social sciences. The targeted years for publi­
cation were 1967–2020. The search words were similar for consistency 
(e.g., “Educational achievement East Asia”, “Educational achievement 
Japan”).

3.4. Thematic analysis 
Inspired by Krippendorff (2018) and Fereday and Muir-Cochrance 
(2006), the included studies were also coded in regard to themes and 
sub-themes based on each manuscript’s constitution (e.g., title, theo­
retical framework, results, discussion). Supplementary sources related 
to methodology, argument, and critique, as well as those that only 
included dependent variables were not coded. All studies that fitted the 
inclusion criteria have been marked by an asterisk (*) in the reference 
section. 
Fereday and Muir-Cochrance (2006) emphasize the consistency be­
tween the researchers typifications and “actors understanding in 
everyday life”, in the present study implying that a non-specialist should 
often be able to grasp the meaning of the themes and sub-themes. Thus, 
as Ratner (2002) underlines, qualitative methods such as textual anal­
ysis should aim to reflect rather than distort reality. The themes iden­
tified in the included studies are mainly descriptive and closely situated 
in each study’s text as they were interpreted by the author. As such it 
may regarded as a thematic content analysis (Krippendorff, 2018; see 
also Kim, 2019). The semantic classification is also inspired by Snow 
et al. (2005) in that the themes constitute hypernyms (overarching 
concepts or themes) and the sub-themes hyponyms (semantically sub­
ordinated concepts or themes). For instance, under the broader category 
Non-cognitive abilities, math self-concept, math resilience and test anxiety 
are situated. This is evident when coding Cheung (2017), a research 
article which includes several variables and themes in the analysis that 
are situated under the broader theme non-cognitive abilities. Another 
example is *Lynn et al. (2016), whose results show that urban regions in 
China such as Shanghai and Beijing have remarkably high cognitive 
ability levels. This is the only factor emphasized and thus its only theme. 
Many categories are also affected by previous research (e.g., *Lu et al., 
2011; Heckman & Kautz, 2014b) which requires a certain degree of 
specialization to grasp. As stated in the results section, a single study 
could include several themes and sub-themes, which also solves some of 
the problems related to subjective semantic classification and categori­
cal overlap (Krippendorff, 2018). 
Moreover, studies were also coded in regard to study design (e.g., 
regression, narrative review) and number of countries, as shown in 
Table 2. That was considered sufficient and therefore the potential in­
clusion of additional descriptors such as qualitative/quantitative was 
considered redundant. If one–five countries were included, these were 
named. If more than five countries were included in a study, such studies 
were labelled “multiple”. Apart from the thematic tables, the results 
section does also include narrative overviews of the included themes.

4.1.2. Cognitive ability 
The latest PISA survey (OECD, 2019) showed that B-J-S-Z (Beijing, 
Jiangsu, Shanghai, Zhejiang) outperform all other participating coun­
tries and urban regions, even Singapore and Macau. Moreover, 
compared to the Beijing-Jiangsu-Shanghai-Guangdong scores in 2015 
(OECD, 2018b, 514.33) the results have increased with almost 55 
average points (i.e., more than half of an international standard devia­
tion, see Jerrim, 2015). It is puzzling why this has not occurred before as 
the IQ data from *Lynn et al. (2016) were collected in 2005–2006 and 
Guangdong does only have one IQ point (106) below the substitute re­
gion, Zhejiang (107). Be that as it may, it is crucial to accentuate that 
this does not represent China in its entirety since the cognitive abilities 
differ throughout its many regions. In that regard, cognitive ability 
research can be helpful in the pursuit of comparing and contextualizing 
intranational differences, which in turn affect international differences 
in surveys like PISA. In *Lynn et al. (2016), the vast regional differences 
in China in regard to cognitive ability are noticeable. B-J-S-Z have the 
highest cognitive ability scores, and cognitive ability (IQ) and cognitive 
skills (PISA) are correlated above .8 in bivariate correlations (which has 
led many researchers to even use PISA scores as proxies for IQ scores, e. 
g., Rindermann, 2018). It seems that in many other Han-dominated 
regions, such as Chongqing, Fujian, Jilin, Hubei, Hunan and Shan­
dong, the IQ scores are almost as high as in the B-J-S-Z cluster, but Anhui 
(98.2), Gansu (96.9), Hainan (90.7) and Henan (95.4) score less well 
(*Lynn et al., 2016). The overall average country score, if one excludes 
Tibet, is 99.4, approximately the same as in most of Europe (Rinder­
mann, 2018). However, IQ does only account for most, not all, of the 
variability in relation to specific countries. If these regions and those 
inhabited by a substantial portion of minorities, such as Ninxia Hui 
Autonomous Region and Qinghai Province, were included in PISA the 
scores would likely decrease because the average scores are dependent 
of all regions that are included. A mix of high-, middle- and 
low-performing regions does therefore provide the most representative 
picture of a country’s cognitive skill level. Moreover, school knowledge 
is also affected by the quality and competence of teachers (*Hanushek 
et al., 2019), family expectations (*Lu et al., 2011), school climate 
(*Ning et al., 2013), and the conscientiousness (Heckman & Kautz, 
2014; MacCann et al., 2009; Rindermann & Ceci, 2009) and resilience of 
pupils (Cheung, 2017). Some of these underlying non-cognitive, 
educational, and cultural variables must have affected the massive 
gains of more than half a standard deviation in reading between 2015 
and 2018 in the B-J-S-Z cluster. The gain has likely little if anything to do

It is quite possible that the overall educational achievement in China 
is lower than in Western countries. The reason for this might be the 
relationship between selective internal migration and stronger school

college entrance examination (gaokao), Chinese students with higher 
cognitive and non-cognitive skills may migrate to urban areas where 
their children – who then possess both favorable genes and environ­

Zhou & Hui, 2021; see also *Lynn et al., 2016; Pan, 2017). Hence, 
scholars might be particularly hesitant to accept the notion that Chinese 
students constitute a high-performing national group. 
Based on a large sample, *Lynn et al. (2011) found that Taiwanese 
schoolchildren had an IQ of 109.5 around 2005 and 2006 when the 
Raven’s Progressive Matrices test was administered. This might explain 
a large portion of the high TIMSS and PISA performance in several 
surveys. However, *Chen et al. (2017) found lower scores on the

dysgenic fertility, associated with low fertility rates among higher-SES 
families in the country. Ning (2019) analyzed micro data from PISA

*Lynn et al. (2011) and *Lynn et al. (2016) there is actually a 5.8 dif­
ference IQ difference between Shanghai and Taiwan. As some have 
underlined, PISA scores may even be regarded as a cross-national 
cognitive ability test. Thus, psychometric g, the general factor of intel­
ligence, is the dependent variable and what is being measured by PISA 
tests according to this viewpoint (Nyborg, 2007). This perspective is also 
taken by for instance Burhan et al. (2017) and Rindermann (2018) who 
either use PISA data as proxies for “national IQ” or mix the two measures 
into one composite cognitive ability score. Educational researchers have 
made similar observations with regard to PISA tests as akin to IQ tests 
(Sellar & Lingard, 2013; Sjøberg, 2015). However, if IQ is understood as 
the independent variable and PISA scores the dependent variable, 
research like Ning (2019) fails to account for cognitive ability 
differences as these are not part of the PISA micro data sets. On the other 
hand, the problem of comparability emerges when PISA data are aligned 
with cognitive ability studies because these are based on different 
samples. Hence, high cognitive ability as measured by standardized IQ 
tests may be regarded as a “comparative advantage” that might explain 
some of the PISA achievement but should be treated with great caution 
(Boman, 2022). Schoolchildren’s IQ seem to be an important input in the 
school system but other inputs such as family expectations and effort 
(which will be discussed in the next section) might mediate such re­
lationships. Moreover, school process factors like the curriculum and 
teacher quality matter as well (e.g., Dandy & Nettelbeck, 1992; Flynn, 
2012; Rindermann, 2018). 
4.1.3. Culture and educational quantity 
Sorensen (1994) noticed that Korean schoolchildren had the highest 
mean scores in science and math in the International Assessments of 
Educational Progress (IAEP) in 1988 and 1991. The author notices that 
early political reforms in the 1960s can hardly be responsible for this 
because they mainly focused on citizenship, anti-communism, and 
patriotic loyalty. Neither does Korea have a strong historical tradition 
among any of these subjects. Instead, the modern trajectory is related to 
the school development. 
Entrich (2014) compared shadow education (also labelled private 
supplementary education or cram schooling) in relation to Japan and 
Germany’s PISA (2009) performance and found that it was a significant 
determinant in the survey. Moreover, Entrich stresses, ‘In general, we 
find positive statistically significant effects of out-of-school lessons on 
performance for Japan only. Enrichment lessons in Japan result in 
higher performance ranging from 8 (MATH) to 23 (READ/SCIENCE) 
points.’ The author also underscores that cram schooling as a form of 
enrichment does only improve schooling in urban areas in Japan, which 
is why out-of-school classes are mainly enrichment-oriented for 
higher-SES pupils whereas in Germany they are typically remedial for 
low-performers (Entrich, 2014). Hence, there are some cross-cultural 
differences between Japanese and German schoolchildren in that re­
gard. Moreover, it shows that students do not simply bring in their 
educational quantity into the school system but also their SES. However, 
there are also intra-regional differences in that respect. Cheo and Quah 
(2005) examined private tutoring in Singapore but overall parents 
deemed it counter-productive due to increased burden of children 
already committed to much schoolwork. This indicates that the private 
tutoring market is less competitive in Singapore compared to for 
example Hong Kong (Cheo & Quah, 2005). 
Komatsu and Rappleye (2018) found that the “exam hell” notion, 
launched by for example Dore (1976), is an outdated stereotype in re­
gard to Japan. Japanese students tend to study less compared to many 
Western counterparts. Out-of-school learning on upper-secondary level 
has substantially decreased since 1980, yet Japan continues to perform 
well in TIMSS and PISA (Komatsu & Rappleye, 2018; see also Cave, 
2001; Smith & Gorard, 2012). However, the study does not account for 
other likely explanatory factors such as teachers’ cognitive skills 
(*Hanushek et al., 2019), classroom environment, curricular content, 
and number of schooldays (e.g., see NCEE, 2018). *Hanushek et al. 
(2019) show that Japanese teachers’ cognitive skills are among the 
highest in world. Depending on the ranking in the PISA tables, raising 
teachers’ cognitive skills to the same level as top performers might in­
crease PISA performance with .10–.15 of a standard deviation (i.e., 
10–15 points) (*Hanushek et al., 2019). Thus, Japan’s high results in for 
instance PISA 2012 might partly be attributable to competent teachers, 
likely in tandem with beneficial classroom environments (OECD, 2014, 
p. 65), and a long schoolyear (NCEE, 2018). 
Inputs such as family expectations and the level of effort among the 
schoolchildren are closely related to the school system where other 
process factors such as annual teaching hours and annual number of 
schooldays are displayed. Or in other words, the school systems in East 
Asia reflect the broader macro culture in each country, which also

4.1.4. Curriculum 
*Stigler et al. (1982) investigated achievement differences in math­
ematics among American, Japanese, and Taiwanese schoolchildren and 
linked those to curricular differences: elementary schoolchildren in 
Japan were introduced to relatively complex mathematical content at an 
earlier age compared to their counterparts in the US and Taiwan. 
Overall, Japanese textbooks included items that were not found in the 
US and Taiwan. Among the more complex and difficult statistical con­
cepts, sampling and probability stood out in the Japanese textbooks and 
were introduced in practice at a significantly later stage among the 
comparative nationalities. In the constructed mathematical test, US 
pupils in grade 1 and grade 5 scored significantly lower than Taiwanese 
and Japanese students. Japanese students outperformed Taiwanese 
students. In the fifth grade, 10% of the students could not divide 42 by 6 
compared to only 4% in Taiwan and 6% in Japan. Overall, factors 
beyond curriculum seem to account for the Taiwanese performance as 
the Taiwanese elementary school curriculum at that time was relatively 
unadvanced. The in-school mathematics quantity explained some 
portion, whereas homework quantity likely contributed even more to 
the observed differences at that time as Taiwanese schoolchildren had 
the highest rates in that respect. Thus, both curricular and quantitative 
measures might be considered in high-quality empirical studies in the 
present. Yet again, the inputs and school processes are often closely tied. 
*Mayer et al. (1991) compared mathematics test results between 
American and Japanese fifth-graders. While Japanese schoolchildren 
obtained higher average overall scores, their American counterparts 
were superior in problem-solving tasks. The results do likely reflect the 
extent to which these children have been exposed to different types of 
mathematical content, which signifies a curricular theme. Hence, the 
school processes are important and might be understood in tandem with 
the abilities and efforts of the schoolchildren. However, these studies 
(*Stigler et al., 1982; Mayer et al., 1991) are about 40 or 30 years old. 
Hence, future studies could use similar designs but with large samples 
and more sophisticated statistical techniques. 
4.1.5. Economic development 
As a distinctive theme, different from socioeconomic status among 
families and individuals, economic development (i.e., GDP per capita) is 
a differentiating factor among TIMSS 1999 countries (Chen, 2001). 
However, this theme is only identified in one study at the cross-country 
level. Leung (2002) explains why economic development, as well as 
educational spending, per se appears to be an unreasonable explanation 
for the high results among East Asian schoolchildren in TIMSS 1999. 
Moreover, wealth differences within East Asian countries like China 
might affect the selection of students and hence skew PISA results up­
wards (*Lynn et al., 2016). While some education economists are 
skeptical about the impact of economic development on school outputs 
(i.e., cognitive skills in assessments such as TIMSS or PISA, see Hanushek 
& Woessman, 2020) this relationship could be further analyzed using 
larger sample sizes and more recent data sources. 
4.1.6. Educational quality 
After a strong PISA performance, Shanghai has become one of the 
world’s reference regions in this regard (Komatsu & Rappleye, 2018). 
*Ning et al. (2015) underline, ‘The quality of educational materials 
significantly predicted reading achievement, but the richness of 
extra-curricular activities, the proportion of qualified teachers, and the 
condition of teacher shortage did not significantly predict reading 
achievement. Inputs such as school appreciation, teacher-student re­
lations, and reading atmosphere statistically predicted reading 
achievement, but disciplinary climate and supportive climate did not 
significantly predict reading achievement.’ However, the authors were 
bound to the explanatory variables that are embedded in the PISA sets. It 
might be that educational quantity and cognitive ability affect the PISA 
scores, which constitute both inputs and school processes, but such 
measures are only found in other data sets. 
As said, *Hanushek et al. (2019) stress that if other countries would 
raise their teachers’ average cognitive skills to the same level as Finland, 
the PISA scores would increase with 10–15 points, although depending 
on comparative strengths and weaknesses in that regard and their place 
in the league tables. The average teacher cognitive skill in the OECD, 
based on the Programme for the International Assessment of Adult 
Competencies (PIAAC) scores are 259 points in numeracy and 268 
points in literacy. PIAAC measures each skill according to a 500-point 
scale. The result for Singapore’s teacher pool is 303 in numeracy and 
300 in literacy, slightly below the top performer Finland. Thus, Japan 
and Singapore have some of the most high-skilled teachers among 
developed nations. On the other hand, there is a lack of description and 
explanation in regard to how these relatively higher cognitive skills 
among teachers affect students’ cognitive skills. Moreover, reflections 
on teachers’ perceptions of academic achievement is important to 
consider. Are they similar in Finland as in Japan or China? For example, 
are Japanese teachers more achievement-oriented than their Finnish 
counterparts and how does that affect the actual achievement in ILSAs? 
This dimension is typically lacking in both the qualitative and the 
quantitative studies, although there is some evidence that suggests that 
teachers in East Asia are achievement-oriented (e.g., Sorensen, 1994: 
Teng et al., 2019). That might imply that both teachers and parents are 
driven by the same test-driven and achievement-oriented educational 
culture. Hence, there is a relationship between culture, family, schools, 
and teachers in that regard, and as such between educational quality and 
quantity, if by quality an ability to drive students towards test scores is 
implied. Because ILSAs are considered low-stakes tests the good results 
may be a spillover from the general achievement-orientation as regards 
the education systems in East Asian countries. 
There are other educational quality factors that appear to be related 
to high performance in surveys such as PISA. However, principal 
decision-making and leadership is mainly associated with Singapore 
(*Huang et al., 2019; Deng & Gopinathan, 2016), whereas instructional 
competence is linked to several countries (*Akiba et al., 2007; *Huang 
et al., 2019; Bermeo, 2014; Kang & Hong, 2008). On the other hand, 
*Hanushek et al. (2019) suggest that the cognitive skills among teachers 
in South Korea are substantially lower than their Japanese and Finnish 
counterparts and data for Taiwan are missing. 
As said above, school process factors might be difficult to separate 
from the students’ inputs (Perelman & Santin, 2011) but didactic 
competence, teachers’ cognitive and non-cognitive skills, and princi­
pals’ decision-making, as well as curricular dimensions, might be clearly 
separated from the student inputs. The problem might be to model these 
various inputs and school process features in tandem with more quali­
tative school process factors such as curricular documents. 
4.1.7. Non-cognitive abilities 
*Ho et al. (2000) found that among 671 sixth-grade students in 
China, Taiwan and the US, math anxiety (the affective type in particular) 
was significantly negatively correlated with mathematical achievement. 
Two effect sizes were identified for the Taiwanese, Affective-> Mathe­
matics achievement (-.54) and Cognitive-Mathematics achievement 
(.25). Chao and Sung (2019) analyzed differences in math anxiety and 
educational quantity (time spent on homework and cram schooling) and 
found that Taiwanese students at the middling level of the academic 
performance spectrum had higher test anxiety and spent more hours at 
cram schools compared to high-performing students.

Chen and Zimmerman (2007) compared self-efficacy beliefs related 
to a mathematics test similar to TIMSS 1995 among Taiwanese sixth 
graders (N = 188) and American (N = 107) seventh-grade students. 
They found that the Taiwanese sample scored higher and these students 
were more accurate in relation to their self-stated math self-efficacy and

more calibrated self-concept which matches their performance. A large 
congruence between self-concept and performance is desired, although a

imbedded in for example the PISA data (e.g., Cheung, 2017; see also

Ceci, 2009). Moreover, larger sample sizes and multivariate models 
which consider both students’ cognitive abilities and teacher compe­
tence may provide more reliable results. 
4.1.8. Socioeconomic status 
Socioeconomic status consists of a composite of parental education, 
parental income, and occupational position (e.g., Sackett et al., 2009). In 
some contexts, such as PISA, the number of books at home are used as an 
indicator of SES (e.g., Tan, 2015), although research points to that 
economic resources are becoming increasingly more important (e.g., 
Wiberg & Rolfsman, 2021). Both cultural (i.e., cultural resources) and 
economic capital indicators such as average income are associated with 
SES in most national contexts (e.g., Kim et al., 2019; Sirin, 2005; Tan, 
2015). It is a general pattern that high-SES students outperform their 
lower-SES peers (e.g., Kim, 2019; Kim et al., 2019). That might occur 
because high-SES parents are more involved in their children’s lives and 
have higher aspirations as regards their children’s future trajectories in 
education and the labor market. In the home environment, high-SES 
parents spend more time reading with and to their children which 
likely stimulates cognitive growth and learning (e.g., Myrberg & Ros´en, 
2009). Moreover, children whose parents have higher SES do generally 
develop higher cognitive ability levels which are then transmitted to 
their children through both genes and the environment (Falk et al., 
2021; Turkheimer et al., 2003). Conversely, the lower degree of nurture 
and support among lower-SES children can affect the cognitive ability 
levels, and hence outputs in for example school tests, negatively (Flynn, 
2012; Sackett et al., 2009; Turkheimer et al., 2003). In East Asian con­
texts, such as South Korea, a higher level of SES might influence 
educational achievement through investment in out-of-school learning. 
In other words, parents with a relatively higher level of average income 
can afford to place their children in private tutoring classes whereas that 
might be too extensive for households with a lower income level (e.g., 
Kim, 2010; Oh, 2010). 
Hence, SES is an important predictor in relation to the output in 
ILSAs, which is also the case among East Asian school systems. However, 
there are some distinctive differences between, for example, East Asian 
countries and western countries in this respect (c.f., Kim, 2019; Sirin, 
2005; Tan, 2015). With regard to the relationship between SES and 
educational achievement there are also some differences among some of 
the East Asian school systems. For example, Huang (2009) found that 
the variability in TIMSS (1999) scores was higher in Taiwan than in 
many other East Asian countries, ‘In Taiwan, students in the 95th 
percentile of math achievement have a score as high as 739, but their 
counterparts in Hong Kong and Japan have a score of only 693 and 
702...’ Thus, cram schools and vast differences in SES in Taiwan 
(Chuang, 2012) might exacerbate patterns also found in South Korea 
(Kim, 2010) and Japan (Entrich, 2014). In this regard it is typically SES 
within East Asian countries which is highlighted and not differences 
between ethnic groups or countries in that respect. However, a general 
interpretation is that test scores differences among East Asian school­
children are smaller compared to their European and North American 
counterparts (e.g., Kang & Hong, 2008; Kim, 2019). Nevertheless, the 
overall data support that high-SES and medium-SES families in East 
Asian countries take advantage of after school classes and cram schools 
in ways that improve school knowledge acquisition and hence test 
scores. For instance, in Germany students do typically use cram schools 
for remedial reasons rather than to boost test scores from an already 
high level whereas the opposite is the case in Japan (Entrich, 2014). 
Similar patterns are found in Taiwan (Chuang, 2012). 
Overall, the SES–school achievement nexus is well-researched as 
regards East Asian schoolchildren and school systems (e.g., Kim, 2019; 
Tan, 2015). This line of research is likely more meaningful if it combines 
data on other inputs such as cognitive and non-cognitive abilities (c.f.., 
*Lu et al., 2011; Guez et al., 2018). Hence, more integrative approaches 
are called for and also more unified measurements of SES among East 
Asian school systems (e.g., Geesa et al., 2019; Kim, 2019). 
5. Discussion 
5.1. Conclusive summary 
The review included N = 99 studies and found seven themes with 53 
corresponding sub-themes for the years 1967–2020. Cultural and 
educational quantity sub-themes such as more annual study hours, 
higher effort, and family expectations dominate as the explanatory 
variables, often emerging as a cross-national factor within and between 
countries and populations associated with East Asia. Some of these are 
mediated by cram schooling (i.e., shadow education), although the 
picture is a bit a fuzzy as regards the distinctions between remedial and 
enrichment in relation to cram schooling. Moreover, out-of-school 
learning understood as cram schooling or after school classes were not 
deemed the same way in Singapore as in Hong Kong (Cheo & Quah, 
2005). It might also be the case that excessive cram schooling will 
diminish in South Korea as it partly has in Japan (Komatsu & Rappleye, 
2018). Thus, many important factors constitute student level inputs 
rather than school process factors (see Ma, 2008; Perelman & Santin, 
2011). However, some of these inputs at the micro level are tied to 
broader macro cultural factors. The cultural stereotype of Confucianism 
might not be present to the same extent because it does not constitute a 
construct that can easily be measured, even though many researchers 
accentuate the significance of Confucianism for the sociohistorical and 
sociocultural context among East Asian countries and populations. N =
56 studies emphasized cultural variables as pertinent for achievement 
among East Asian nations and populations. Whereas some of these tend 
to overlap, and all cultural patterns are not identified in all localities as 
some are context-specific (e.g., han in Korea), it is still a substantial 
cluster of themes that emerges in the literature. Moreover, these are 
often related to higher educational quantity. 
A second theme of pertinence are non-cognitive abilities such as 
math self-concept, math resilience, math anxiety, test anxiety, and self- 
efficacy, which are underscored in 18 studies. Some of these are more 
recent constructs, resilience in particular, which are related to the PISA 
surveys (Cheung, 2017). However, it is a bit unclear if this constitutes a 
“comparative advantage” (see Boman, 2020) in relation to non-East 
Asian schoolchildren. For example, it would be important to tease out 
if East Asian schoolchildren on average are more conscientious than, for 
example, their Western counterparts. Whereas personality traits like 
conscientiousness are difficult to compare across cultures due to refer­
ence bias (Heckman & Kautz, 2014b¸ pp. 352–355; Rindermann & Ceci, 
2009), there is some additional evidence from other research contexts 
(e.g., see Hsien & Xie, 2014; Dandy & Nettelbeck, 1992; Jerrim, 2015; 
Lee & Zhou, 2015) that indicate that that is, indeed, the case. However, 
at the intra-national level such comparisons may be applicable even in 
cross-ethnic research endeavors. Moreover, it is quite possible to study 
educational quantity indicators among schoolchildren based on both 
annual number of school days, annual number of study hours, and 
annual number of out-of-school learning (i.e., an indication of indus­
triousness, see e.g., Boman, 2020, Ziegeler et al., 2014; see also Komatsu 
& Rappleye, 2018). In accordance with the relationship between

schoolchildren’s’ various inputs (e.g., Ma, 2008; Perelman & Santin, 
2011), and the school processes, it would be interesting to examine 
classrooms and school discipline indicators with greater accuracy. 
Indeed, not all South Korean classrooms are discipline-oriented (*Kwon 
et al., 2017) but on average there might still be substantial differences. 
Cognitive ability at the same level or above Westerners constitutes a 
third theme of chief significance, although less pronounced as a final 
underlying achievement factor, whether within or between countries. 
This may reflect that IQ is affected by culture, family, and school process 
factors (Rindermann & Ceci, 2009) or downplayed already in the 1980s 
and in more recent research (Hsien & Xie, 2014). As the findings of 
*Lynn et al. (2016) show, however, higher than average cognitive ability 
levels in some urban localities of China may nonetheless partially 
explain the high PISA achievement. On the other hand, as many 
cognitive ability studies are quite outdated (see Rindermann, 2018) new 
research is required. If some countries or urban regions in East Asia 
really have higher cognitive ability levels than for example European 
countries, then representative and comprehensive studies should sup­
port such claims. 
A fourth theme are competent and highly paid teachers as related to 
the hypernym educational quality. However, the pattern is far from 
conclusive since many Western nations have high teacher skills 
(*Hanushek et al., 2019). A fifth theme of salience is linked to socio­
economic patterns, typically that higher SES predicts higher achieve­
ment in within-group comparisons. Some variables like democratization 
do only have a far-fetched longitudinal effect but are still important for 
educational attainment in an historical context in localities like Taiwan 
(Hung, 2007). Democracy is not an input per se but it might affect the 
school system and therefore the school process factors, as well as the 
curricular material (Sorensen, 1994). However, authoritarian rule has 
not undermined achievement in China, perhaps due to competitive, 
capitalist-inspired structures within these systems since 1978 (China). 
This might also suggest that cultural and school process factors are more 
important than political factors, both for cognitive ability and scholastic 
achievement. Economic development is associated with high PISA per­
formance in some East Asian countries and urbans like Hong Kong, 
Japan, and Singapore (Chen, 2001). 
Whereas curriculum is only underscored in a few studies, this is a 
promising field of research (see especially Mayer et al., 1991; Jerrim & 
Choi, 2014) because when specific key curriculum content is imple­
mented in practice at different age levels in different school systems does 
likely affect learning and even cognitive growth as measured by IQ tests 
and scholastic aptitude surveys. Hence, national curricular documents 
should not naively be taken at face value but analyzed in close alignment 
with observational studies, textbooks and other resources, as well as 
psychometric testing. Information about the totality of study hours, 
within and outside schools, constitute important control variables as 
regards both inputs and school processes. To the extent that stakeholders 
related to other school systems may study East Asian schoolchildren 
more closely, a policy recommendation may be to focus on translations 
of curricular documents, and to make visits to schools and 
out-of-learning contexts in several countries. Interviews with East Asian 
families might also be a cue, and as such comparisons with the earlier 
interview data. 
On a more general note, the cited literature shows that many of the 
stereotypes of East Asian school systems and hence East Asian school­
children tend to be incorrect or are grossly simplified. For instance, the 
modern Japanese society fosters creativity (Torrance, 1980a) and has 
decreased out-of-school learning at the upper-secondary level since the 
1980s (Komatsu & Rappleye, 2018). A substantial portion of East Asians 
lack a ‘growth mindset’ (meaning a belief that cognitive ability is 
malleable and can be improved, see Cheung, 2017), which is another 
salient albeit benign stereotype (see Lee & Zhou, 2015). IQ scores tend to 
be similar as those of Western countries in large representative samples 
(e.g., *Lynn et al., 2016; Georgas et al., 2003). A substantial portion of 
Japanese and Chinese students have reading difficulties (*Stevenson 
et al., 1982). On the other hand, if IQ scores are being isolated for 
particular urban regions or countries such as Shanghai and Taiwan these 
might substantially correlate with PISA and TIMMS scores (*Lynn et al., 
2016; *Lynn et al., 2011; Rindermann, 2018). 
However, the average math excellence stereotype persists; it is just 
that the underlying factors are hard to identify and disentangle and may 
even transcend factors like weekly or annual study hours. The combi­
nation of curricular emphasis, competent math and science matchers, 
family expectations, classroom discipline, competition, and effort seem 
to explain these international differences, although Japan is deviating 
from such a pattern (Komatsu & Rappleye, 2018). A complex and 
multivariate approach may identify more specific patterns and the re­
lationships between inputs, school process factors, and outputs in ILSAs. 
Currently, too much research has a rather narrow and univariate or 
bivariate focus (e.g., see Guglielmi & Brekke, 2017). 
5.2. Implications, limitations, and directions for future research 
All in all, this thematic literature review indicates what seems to 
matter more or less in regard to the high achievement among East Asian 
schoolchildren in ILSAs and in regard to other achievement tests and 
assessment contexts. The most reasonable overall indication is that 
cultural and learning quantity factors underpin the high achievement 
levels. However, it seems that both cognitive, non-cognitive and cur­
riculum factors matter as well. In the Chinese context, it is possible that a 
combination of college entrance exams and selective international 
migration to the most affluent cities skews the educational performance 
upwards. That in tandem with unrepresentative samples in assessments 
like PISA make it likely that China in its entirety is not as strong as the 
rest of other East Asian school systems like Singapore. On the other 
hand, Singapore might also be affected by selective migration (e.g., 
Boman, 2020). Thus, the nexus between both internal and international 
selective migration, SES, cognitive skills and favorable macro and micro 
environments should be further researched. 
As said, many of the important school achievement factors are most 
likely quite interrelated, such as that the mathematics curriculum in for 
example Japan influences earlier cognitive growth patterns, and high 
levels of out-of-school learning and parental aspirations facilitate 
industrious and assiduous learning patterns. There is some evidence that 
suggest that out-of-school learning has decreased, yet Japan performs 
well in PISA (e.g., Komatsu & Rappleye, 2018). Therefore, the trade-offs 
and cut-offs for optimal levels of in-school learning and out-of-school 
learning might be studied with greater accuracy in future research. 
The input–school process–output nexus becomes particularly useful if 
many factors are accounted for and they are measured in a meaningful 
way. Perhaps curriculum content and standards, instructional quality 
and teacher competence are important mediating factors in that regard 
(e.g., *Stigler et al., 1982; Mayer et al., 1991; *Hanushek et al., 2019). 
Policy implications might be more nuanced, detailed and sophisticated 
than to merely focus on more quality and effort such as more annual 
hours dedicated to mathematics. Perhaps, it may also be important to tie 
a higher degree of effort with cultural characteristics from each country. 
Perhaps it is also pertinent to stress that some cultural characteristics are 
hard to emulate, or not even beneficial to emulate (e.g., Feniger & 
Lefstein, 2014). With regard to non-cognitive abilities, these are more 
malleable than cognitive skills, especially at earlier ages (Heckman & 
Kautz, 2014), even though cognitive tests are influenced by incentives, 
effort, motivation, and concentration (Duckworth et al., 2011). Hence, 
policy recommendations may focus more on non-cognitive skill di­
mensions and how it can be improved both inside and outside the school 
context. Such efforts might be focused on low-performing groups within 
East Asian countries and in other national and regional contexts. 
This study has several limitations. For instance, it did only include 
elementary and lower-secondary education, although some studies focus 
more on the overall national or cross-cultural context without age- 
specific analyses. Thus, future research may focus on pre-school

education, upper-secondary, and higher education. Meta-analytical re­
views and multivariate analyses (for recommendations, see *Lu et al., 
2011; Guglielmi & Brekke, 2017) may identify and unravel both 
context-dependent and context-independent factors as many assump­
tions tend to signify a large portion of the literature (i.e., the importance 
of Confucianism). However, a narrower scope than the one used in the 
current article is probably required to pinpoint effect sizes regardless of 
age and national context. In that regard, PISA surveys may be a fruitful 
focal point for meta-analysis. Moreover, the qualitative research design 
makes replicability more difficult in regard to the analytical processes. 
As said in the method section, categorial overlap might be a related issue 
in that regard. In addition, the current study has not compared differ­
ences between different study designs and single-country designs 
visa-a-vis multiple-country designs. However, there is no reason to see 
any particular design as necessarily more important or fruitful than the 
other. As new evidence and data accumulate, it might be pertinent to 
focus on both case studies, multilayered cross-national studies, and 
single-country multivariate research in the future. 
Moreover, both peer-reviewed articles and dissertations in East Asian 
languages may further deepen and broaden the scope linguistically as 
the present study was limited to refereed literature in English. Research 
on East Asians in North America, Europe, Oceania, and Latin America 
could be further investigated (for a discussion, see Lee & Zhou, 2015). 
Gender effects in relation to East Asian countries and populations might 
also be further explored. In most research, large sample sizes, that match 
those of TIMSS and PISA as much as possible, are desirable for broader 
generalizations. 
Furthermore, the author cannot claim to have included all eligible 
studies. Nevertheless, a substantial portion of important earlier research 
and analyses were included in this rather broad thematic literature re­
view. While perhaps few definitive answers are provided, there are clear 
indications of the relative weight ascribed to different factors as regards 
the typically high achievement of East Asian schoolchildren.
