This study aimed to 1) identify groups of students with different expectancy, intrinsic and utility value, and cost
profiles in biology and physics, and 2) to examine group differences in sources of beliefs (gender, students’
perceptions of their biology and physics teachers’ need-supportive and need-thwarting behavior) and students’
learning behavior (reported persistence and use of learning strategies). In total, 1502 eighth and ninth grade
students from Estonia filled in questionnaires. With latent profile analysis, four general (Indifferent, Maladaptive,
Moderate, and Adaptive) and two subject-specific (Biology-Adaptive and Physics-Adaptive) motivational profiles
were identified. These groups differed in gender composition, students’ perceptions of their teachers’ needsupportive and need-thwarting behavior, task-persistent learning behavior, and reported use of deep learning
strategies. The findings suggest that motivation may develop differently within each subject. Examining motivation across two or more subjects adds valuable information about individual differences and has practical
implications for supporting motivation in various subjects.

Educational relevance statement

1. Introduction

This study contributes to the field of science education by examining
groups of students with different patterns of motivational beliefs in
biology and physics. While students mostly exhibited either low, moderate, or high motivation in both subjects, a number of students showed
differentiated motivation in biology and physics. Students with low
motivation in both subjects, who demonstrated the least adaptive
characteristics, may benefit from complex interventions tapping both
motivational support and learning strategies. The findings suggest that
teachers should also reflect on how students perceive their teaching
practices and behavior. Our findings indicate that students whose
motivation differs considerably between the two distinct but interconnected science subjects may be especially sensitive to their subject
teachers’ need-supportive and need-thwarting behavior, particularly in
the subject they do not identify with as strongly as the other.

The need for skilled professionals in the STEM (Science, Technology,
Engineering, Mathematics) area, and students’ simultaneous low motivation to study these subjects, has resulted in numerous studies on
STEM-related motivational beliefs in students (Murphy et al., 2019).
Many studies have been based on (situated) expectancy-value framework (further SEVT, Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). The comprehensive SEVT
model of achievement choices (Fig. 1 in Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) integrates individual (e.g., gender, earlier achievement-related experiences) and contextual factors (e.g., cultural milieu, parents’ and
teachers’ beliefs and behaviors). These factors, as perceived and interpreted by learners, guide their goals, memories, and motivational beliefs. Motivational beliefs, encompassing expectations/expectancies of
success and subjective task values (attainment, intrinsic, utility value,
and cost), as the most proximal cognitive aspects of achievement-related
choices and performance, have been extensively studied. Meanwhile,
contextual and situational factors have received less attention (but see
Parrisius et al., 2022).

Eccles and colleagues (e.g., Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) argue that, as
students mature, they develop increasingly distinct competencies and
values regarding different subjects and tasks (henceforth, we use EV to
refer to expectancies and positive values, while EVC also includes
negative values, i.e. cost). Such differentiated EVC regarding various
activities are characterized by hierarchies – the relative ordering of EVC
– within individuals: students prefer tasks and subjects that are relatively higher in their personal hierarchy over tasks that are lower in this
hierarchy. In school students may be more engaged in lessons of subjects
that are higher in their EVC hierarchies. Such hierarchies of EVC can be
studied via person-oriented analyses (see Wigfield & Eccles, 2020).
While a growing amount of studies have examined EVC hierarchies in
subjects that are taught in separate lessons already from the beginning of
school (e.g., math, language; see Chow et al., 2012; Lazarides et al.,
2021), less attention has been paid on students’ EVC hierarchies in
subjects that are taught in an integrated manner in primary school, but
as separate subjects in middle school or later. These include disciplines
like biology and physics that are taught in general science lessons in
primary school, but as separate subjects during later school years.
Depending on students’ career aspirations, there may be differences in
their priorities regarding the knowledge and skills in biology and
physics. To the best of our knowledge, motivational profiles across
several science subjects like biology and physics in parallel, their prerequisites and outcomes have not been previously examined. Nonetheless, such studies are important for several reasons. Such research adds
theoretical knowledge concerning if and how students’ motivational
beliefs are situated in and between closely related subjects that they
have only recently started studying as separate subjects. From a practical
side, subgroups characterized by different motivational profiles may
require different types of support to foster their achievement or help
them make further career choices. Such support may include interventions tapping students’ motivational beliefs, learning strategies
and behavior, but considering the importance of contextual factors in
students’ motivation, may also target teacher practices.
Prior studies have focused on the role of gender in the development
of EVC and their associations with learning behavior and career choices.
Gender issues also concern biology and physics as boys tend to be more
interested in physics and girls in biology (Gaspard et al., 2017; Jansen
et al., 2014). Person-oriented studies, though, have not shown clear
gender differences regarding motivation in physics (Bөe & Henriksen,
2013) and, to our best knowledge, person-oriented studies have not
examined gender differences regarding motivation in biology. Concerning other potential influences on students’ EVC, in the SEVT
framework there has been less empirical research done on students’
perceptions of their socializers’ need-supportive practices as emphasized in self-determination theory (SDT, Ryan & Deci, 2020; but see
Parrisius et al., 2022). Nonetheless, teachers might interact differently
with students characterized by different motivational profiles. Knowledge about how subgroups of students with distinct patterns of motivation differ in their perceptions of their teachers’ behavior would be
important so that teachers could adjust their practices for all students to
feel supported.
EVC, in turn, may influence students’ learning behavior, the amount
of effort students exert and the learning strategies they use (Vu et al.,
2021). While several studies have examined motivational profile differences in completing STEM courses and college persistence (Fong
et al., 2021; Perez, Wormington, et al., 2019), there are few studies
inspecting profile differences in persistent learning behavior in middle
school. Comprehending complex topics and the large amount of new
information in biology and physics lessons in the end of middle school is
successful when using deep learning strategies (Ardura & Galán, 2019).
So far, relations with the use of learning strategies have rarely been
studied (but see Kwarikunda et al., 2021). Information about motivational profiles and their differences in learning behavior – including
between-subject differences in such behavior – has practical value for
designing interventions and supporting students’ learning.

The aim of our study was to identify groups of students with different
expectancy, intrinsic value, utility value, and cost profiles in biology and
physics. The study was carried out in Estonia in the end of upper middle
school. In Estonia, these are the school years when biology and physics
are first taught as separate subjects and by different subject teachers,
while having before been taught by a single teacher as an integrated
science subject. We also aimed to examine profile differences in the
potential sources of these beliefs (gender, student perceptions of their
teachers’ need-supportive and need-thwarting behavior) and students’
learning behavior (reported persistence and use of learning strategies).
2. Literature review
2.1. Motivational beliefs
SEVT differentiates two types of motivational beliefs: expectancies
and values (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Students’ expectancies – their
beliefs about how well they will do in future tasks in a specific field – are
closely related to self-efficacy (Bandura, 1993). Attainment value is the
perceived personal importance of achievement and is related to one’s
identity (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Intrinsic value refers to interest, or
inherent enjoyment gained from a given task. Utility value is the
perceived relevance or usefulness of a given task or subject area
regarding students’ current or future plans. Cost is generally conceptualized as the time and energy that one must give up in order to engage in
a given task, but it also involves the negative emotional or psychological
consequences of this engagement (Perez, Dai, et al., 2019). So far, cost
has been less studied, partly because of the confusion with its conceptualizations (e.g., dimensions of cost and their relations with emotions;
see Song et al., 2023) and due to the lack of good assessment measures
(Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).
We examined expectancy, interest, utility value, and cost of studying
biology and physics. Interest was included as an emotional engagement
dimension of value that has been shown to be low in the end of middle
school (e.g., Steidtmann et al., 2022; Tire, 2016) and utility value and
cost as the facets of value that are the most easily alterable, hence carrying more educational relevance (for intervention studies, see Nagengast et al., 2017; Rosenzweig et al., 2020). We did not include
attainment value as this value component has been shown to be less
responsive to educational interventions (e.g., Robinson et al., 2019),
likely due to being most strongly related to students’ deep-seated
identity processes (Eccles, 2009; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Although
both biology and physics are science disciplines, the topics in these
subjects differ a lot: biology encompasses living organisms, whereas the
topics in physics include laws of nature, matter, and universe. Students’
interest may vary between these topics, but also, the perceived utility of
different topics for future careers varies (e.g., Gaspard et al., 2019). In
general, biology is perceived as an easier discipline than physics (Wong
et al., 2022). Also, compared to biology, physics is considered to be more
math-intensive (Ceci et al., 2009). The role of these motivational beliefs
in supporting (expectancies, interest, utility value) or inhibiting (cost)
learning in the science domain has been repeatedly demonstrated (see
below), but motivational beliefs in biology and physics have not been
concurrently studied.

henceforth primarily describe studies including science motivation but
refer to studies done in other subject domains if these include science or
concern areas that have not been studied in relation to motivational
profiles in science (e.g., student perceptions of teacher behavior). Appendix A (Tables A1 and A2) gives an overview of studies including the

All seven studies that examined motivational profiles in one single

profiles as adaptive and maladaptive is justified when considering profile differences in achievement. Namely, students in adaptive profiles
tend to have significantly higher grades than students in maladaptive
profiles (Hong & Bernacki, 2022; Perez, Wormington, et al., 2019; Watt
et al., 2019). Findings regarding students in moderate profiles are not as
clear, but their achievement tends to fall between that of adaptive and
maladaptive profiles. Watt et al. (2019) additionally identified a Struggling Ambitious profile, where students reported high EVC, but whose
grades were lower than those of the adaptive and higher than those of
the maladaptive profile.
Prior studies involving other subjects besides science (Chow et al.,
2012; Fong et al., 2021; Lazarides et al., 2021; Oppermann et al., 2021;
Snodgrass Rangel et al., 2020) have not included cost. Based on EV, they
have identified subject-general (similar for both or all subjects) adaptive
(24 %–66 % of students), moderate (44 %–48 %), and maladaptive (11
%–27 %) motivational profiles (Table A2). Additionally, focusing on
multiple subjects simultaneously has allowed researchers to identify
subject-specific profiles. For instance, Fong et al. (2021) and Snodgrass
Rangel et al. (2020), focusing on science and math, identified both
science-adaptive (higher EV in science and lower EV in math; 7 % and
22 % in studies by Fong et al., 2021 and Snodgrass Rangel et al., 2020,
respectively) and math-adaptive (higher EV in math and lower EV in
science; 6 % and 40 % in studies by Fong et al., 2021 and Snodgrass
Rangel et al., 2020, respectively) profiles. These studies found differences in achievement between the adaptive and maladaptive profiles
similar to those described earlier. Studies with more subjects (science,
math, language, arts) have found at least one subject-specific profile
(Table A2).
To summarize, studies including EVC have identified adaptive,
moderate, and maladaptive motivational profiles as well as profiles with
high EVC. Studies across science, math, literature, and arts have
consistently identified subject-general profiles, as well as subjectspecific profiles. However, findings differ in the number of profiles
and the distribution of students in these profiles. These differences are
likely related to differences in the designs of these studies (e.g., different
age groups, cultural context, studied constructs). Thus far, motivational
EVC profiles across multiple science subjects and including cost have not
been examined. Therefore, our study focuses on cross-subject motivational profiles in biology and physics.

2.4. Motivational beliefs, profiles, and perceptions of teacher behavior
According to the comprehensive SEVT model, individuals’ motivational beliefs are shaped by how they perceive and interpret their socializers’ behavior (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). There appears to be a
growing consensus among theorists from various theoretical perspectives that, in the classroom context, specific teaching behaviors,
perceived by students as more or less supportive of their basic psychological needs – as outlined in SDT (Ryan & Deci, 2020) –, are consistently
associated with students’ motivational beliefs (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).
In addition to need satisfaction, need frustration explains additional
variance in outcomes as indicated by research done within the SDT
framework (Bartholomew et al., 2011). Teachers may either support or
thwart the satisfaction of students’ basic psychological needs, which, in
turn, influences students’ motivational outcomes (Bartholomew et al.,
2018; Jang et al., 2016). Concerning STEM-related subjects, ninth
graders’ higher EV and lower cost in math have been shown to associate
with perceptions of their math teacher behaving more in ways that
support the satisfaction of students’ need for autonomy (Parrisius et al.,
2022).
Only a few of the studies on students’ motivational profiles in science
have examined profile differences in aspects of the teachers’ behavior.
Watt et al. (2019) found that students in the adaptive profile perceived
their science teacher to emphasize mastery in the classroom more than
students in less adaptive profiles (d = 0.47 and 0.61). They also found
that struggling ambitious students (high EVC) perceived their teacher to
emphasize high performance in the classroom more than did students in
the other two profiles (d = 0.78 and 0.68). Fong et al. (2021) examined
profile differences in teachers’ own reports of their behavior and found
high-school students’ math- and science-related motivational profile
membership not to be associated with teacher reported emphasis on
increasing student interest in these subjects. Nonetheless, both SEVT and
SDT emphasize the importance of students’ perceptions of contextual
factors, such as teacher behavior concerning their motivation (Deci &
Ryan, 2000; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). The effects of teacher behavior on
student motivation have been shown to be mediated by student perceptions of such behavior (Rubach et al., 2023).
Consistent with this, Lazarides et al. (2020) showed that students
who perceived higher fairness and friendliness of math teachers in Grade
7 were buffered against falling from a math-related adaptive motivational profile to a moderate one (OR = 0.46). Additionally, Lazarides
et al. (2022) showed that 1) ninth graders who perceive greater support
from their math teachers (e.g., teachers explain the material well) are
more likely to belong to an adaptive motivational profile in math
compared to less adaptive profiles (OR = 3.33 and 1.92), and 2) ninth
graders’ perceptions of more student-oriented teaching behaviors (e.g.,
differentiation of learning tasks) buffer their transition to a somewhat
less adaptive profile (high self-efficacy, moderate utility, and low interest value in math) in Grade 10 (OR = 0.43).
While earlier studies have shown motivational profiles to differ in
perceived teacher behavior, such student perceptions have not been
examined in studies focusing on cross-subject motivational profiles. In
this study, we inspect cross-subject motivational profile differences in
students’ perceptions of the behavior of their subject teachers of the
closely related subjects of biology and physics. Moreover, we examine
profile differences in students’ perceptions of both the need-supportive
as well as need-thwarting behavior of their teachers.

2.3. Motivational beliefs, profiles, and gender
Prior studies in SEVT have focused on gender role socialization and
its effects on the development of EVC (Eccles et al., 1983; Eccles &
Wigfield, 2020). Women are underrepresented in science careers,
particularly in math-intensive fields like physics (Ceci et al., 2009), and
it is more typical for males to be drawn toward topics of physics, engineering, and math, whereas females lean more toward topics of health
and the human body, hence preferring subjects like biology and chemistry (Bөe & Henriksen, 2013; Nagy et al., 2007).

Students’ EVC guide their interpretations of learning situations,
which can trigger and enhance the learning process (Eccles & Wigfield,
2020). Motivational beliefs may support learning and outcomes via two
pathways: 1) engagement and persistence and 2) using higher quality
learning behaviors (Vu et al., 2021). Task-persistent learning behavior
(i.e., sustained engagement when facing difficulties in learning; Zhang
et al., 2011) plays a larger role when solving complex problems as
compared to easy problems (Cai et al., 2018; Kikas et al., 2020).
Of the studies on motivational profiles, only Watt et al. (2019) specifically tapped effort exertion in science – an aspect of learning
behavior closely related to task persistence. They found that students in
the adaptive profile showed the highest, and students in the maladaptive
profile the lowest effort (d = 1.51). Other studies have not explicitly
examined between-profile differences in learning behavior, but their
findings refer to such differences implicitly. First, as students with
higher persistence tend to have higher achievement (Cai et al., 2018),
earlier described findings on achievement may also indicate betweenprofile differences in task-persistence. Second, between-profile differences in course completion also point to possible differences in persistence. For instance, Fong et al. (2021) found that, compared to the
adaptive profile, students in the science-adaptive (OR = 0.37) and
moderate profiles (OR = 0.69) had significantly lower odds of persisting
in college three years after high school. Moreover, the adaptive profile
had the highest levels of such persistence (ORs = 1.59–2.62). Additionally, Perez, Wormington, et al. (2019) found that students in the
maladaptive science profile completed fewer STEM courses than students in the adaptive (d = 0.78 and 0.96) and moderate profiles (d =
0.54 and 0.65) after one and four years of college.
What students remember and how well they understand new material is related to their cognitive processes during learning (Van Meter &
Campbell, 2020). Learning complex topics is enhanced by using deep
learning strategies (e.g., integrating information from text and drawings) rather than surface learning strategies (e.g., simple rehearsal and
memorization; Ardura & Galán, 2019; Kikas et al., 2020). Compared to
surface learning strategies, deep strategies are more difficult and timeconsuming (Seufert, 2020) and thus presume higher motivation
(Kikas, Silinskas, & Härma, 2023).
Only a few studies have examined relations between motivational
profiles and the use of learning strategies. Kwarikunda et al. (2021;
Table A1) found that students in the Primarily Intrinsically Motivated and
Adaptive profiles used organization (differences compared to other
profiles: ds = 0.62 to 8.00), critical thinking (ds = 1.07 to 7.94), elaboration (ds = 1.88 to 14.46), and metacognition (ds = 11.03 to 21.31)
learning strategies more than students in other profiles. Hong et al.
(2020) identified four motivational profiles (High Goals and Values and
Mastery-Driven, High Cost, Moderately Motivated) of EVC and
achievement-goals (Table A1) and used computer logs to examine students’ metacognitive behavior: 1) monitoring learning through selfassessment quizzes, 2) performance by checking grades, and 3) planning by using review papers or blueprints. Using these computer logs,
they identified three metacognitive strategy-use profiles: Infrequent
Metacognitive Processing, Planning and Self-Evaluation (strong use of tools
to check grades and make learning plans, but infrequent selfassessment), and Monitoring via Self-Assessment (frequent use of selfassessment quizzes). Students in the Infrequent Metacognitive Processing
profile showed lower achievement than the other two profiles.
Compared to the Mastery-Driven profile, students in the Maladaptive
profile (High Cost) more likely belonged to the Infrequent Metacognitive
Processing group relative to the Planning and Self-Evaluation group (OR =
4.15).

2.6. Cultural-educational background of the study
Cultural background is regarded as a situational component according to SEVT (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). The current study was carried out in Estonia, a small country that was part of the Soviet Union
from 1940 to 1991 and has since undergone rapid social changes.
Estonian students show strong scientific knowledge and skills in international comparisons, consistently ranking at the top of PISA (Programme for International Student Assessment) results in all subjects
(math, science, reading) since 2006, when Estonia first participated in
the survey (OECD, 2007, 2023). Similar to students in other countries,
the vast majority of Estonian students see the utility value of science
knowledge, but only 17 % in 2006 and 25 % in 2015 indicated interest in
working in science-related fields (Tire, 2016).
Although several education reforms have been implemented in
Estonia since 1991, teachers differ in their use of teacher-directed didactics and constructivist approaches (Kikas, Eisenschmidt, &
Granström, 2023). As a result, teachers may differ in how they support
students’ basic psychological needs. PISA results also show that Estonian
students report receiving less support from their teachers than students
in other OECD countries (OECD, 2019).
In Estonia, students learn science topics as a single integrated subject
with the same teacher until Grade 7. Biology is taught as a separate
subject with a separate subject-teacher starting from Grade 7, and
physics from Grade 8. Our study focuses on grade levels during which
students learn physics and biology as separate subjects, taught by
different subject-teachers. Grade 9 marks the end of compulsory education in Estonia, with students facing the decision about whether to
continue their education (secondary or vocational school) or join the
workforce. These decisions are likely guided by students’ motivational
beliefs.
3. Aims and hypotheses
The present study aimed to examine what kind of subject-general
and subject-specific motivational profiles could be identified based on
expectancy, intrinsic value, utility value, and cost of learning biology
and physics among upper middle school students, who have only
recently started studying these two subjects in separate lessons taught by
different subject teachers. Additionally, the aim was to inspect profile
differences regarding the potential sources and outputs. Our research
questions and hypotheses were as follows:
First, how do students’ motivational profiles differ between biology
and physics? We expected to find students with adaptive, moderate, and
maladaptive motivation in both subjects, but also students with adaptive
motivation in only one subject (cf. Fong et al., 2021). Moreover, we
expected the majority of students to belong to subject-general profiles
(cf. Chow et al., 2012; Oppermann et al., 2021). As Estonian students
have also consistently performed well in PISA studies (OECD, 2023), we
expected the majority of students to belong to adaptive and moderate
profiles.
Second, how do students’ motivational profiles differ in gender
composition? Based on earlier research (Gaspard et al., 2017; Jansen
et al., 2014; Nagy et al., 2007), we expected to find more girls in biologyadaptive profile and more boys in physics-adaptive profile.
Third, how do students with different motivational profiles differ in
perceptions of their biology and physics teachers’ need-supportive and
need-thwarting behaviors? We expected students with biology− /physics-adaptive motivational beliefs to perceive their biology/physics
teachers’ behavior to be more need-supportive and less need-thwarting
than other students, while expecting students in the more adaptive
subject-general profiles to perceive both of their teachers to be more

need-supportive and less need-thwarting compared to students in a
maladaptive profile (for variable-oriented results, see Bartholomew

Fourth, how do students with different motivational profiles differ in

4.2.2. Student perceptions of teachers’ need-supportive and need-thwarting
behavior
On a scale from 1 (do not agree at all) to 5 (completely agree), students
indicated how much they agreed with six statements describing teachers’ practices reflecting either supporting or thwarting students’ basic
psychological needs.
CFA with four factors (need-supportive and need-thwarting behavior
of the biology and the physics teacher) showed good model fit, χ2(44) =
391.87 (p < .001); CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.073, 90 % C.I.
(0.066, 0.079), SRMR = 0.040. Three items loaded on the needsupportive, and three items on the need-thwarting factor. Hence, four
mean scores were used. Cronbach’s α was acceptable (0.78/0.79 for
need-supportive behavior in biology/physics; 0.74/0.72 for needthwarting behavior in biology/physics).
4.2.3. Task persistence
Task persistence in biology/physics was assessed using three items
based on the Behavioral Strategy Rating Scale (Zhang et al., 2011; for the
Estonian version see Kikas et al., 2020). Students indicated how much
they agreed with the statements on a scale from 1 (do not agree at all) to 5
(completely agree). Two items related to task persistence and one to task
avoidance.
CFA with two factors (task persistence in biology and physics)
showed good model fit, χ2(7) = 37.44 (p < .001); CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.92,
RMSEA = 0.054, 90 % C.I. (0.038, 0.071), SRMR = 0.014. Cronbach’s α
was acceptable (0.70/0.69 for biology/physics).

4. Method
4.1. Participants and procedure
Participants included 888 eighth-grade and 614 ninth-grade students
(1502 total; 786 boys) from 34 schools in different parts of Estonia. Of
these students, 871 studied in Estonian-speaking schools, 633 studied in
Russian-speaking schools. The mean age of participants was 14.94 years
(minimum 13 years; maximum 17 years; SD = 0.73).
Student assessment was part of a larger project: “Developing tools for
assessing science-related motivation”. In March 2022, the Education and
Youth Board invited schools to participate in the study, after which
specific instructions about the assessments were given to teachers.
Although Institutional Review Board approval was not required for
research, we carefully followed the APA ethical guidelines. Students and
their parents were informed of the content of the assessment, and students were told they could withdraw at any time. Students were assessed
in April–May 2022, with participants from the Estonian- and Russianspeaking schools assessed in Estonian and Russian, respectively.
Teachers chose a suitable day within a predetermined time period.
Students were assessed in computer labs during regular school days and
were supervised by their teachers. The assessment took approximately
45 min.

4.2.4. Reported use of learning strategies
All tasks and questions were developed specifically for this test based
on earlier scenario-based assessment tools (Granström & Kikas, 2023).
Students first read the following description of a hypothetical learning
task: “In two months, there will be a challenging biology exam covering
the entire textbook. Try to learn as much as possible and get a good
grade on the exam”. The task was biology-specific because there are no
complex dual-subject integrated tests in Estonian middle school. We
chose biology as it includes a wider range of theoretical material than
physics. Subsequently, students were presented with eight learning
strategies and asked to evaluate the effectiveness of each learning
strategy (not used in this study). Next, students were again asked to
think about the earlier-described learning task and by being presented
with pairs of strategies, each of which included one deep and one surface
learning strategy, they were asked to mark which of the two strategies
they would use. The sum of deep learning strategies selected formed the
reported use of deep learning strategies. The Kuder-Richardson indicator
KR20 was acceptable (0.68).

4.2. Measures
Items of used measures are presented in Appendix B. Confirmatory
factor analysis (CFA) was used to inspect the factor structure of the
measures. While CFI and TLI values close to and higher than 0.95 are
preferrable, values above 0.90 alongside RMSEA and SRMR values
below 0.10 are considered to be indicative of an acceptable model fit
(Hu & Bentler, 1999; Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017).

4.3. Data analysis strategy
Preliminary analysis of measurement invariance across the Estonianand Russian-speaking students was carried out with lavaan package
(Rosseel, 2012) in R version 4.2.3 (R Core Team, 2023). Full invariance
of the factor structure and loadings of all the measures was established
(Appendix C). Additionally, full scalar invariance of motivational beliefs
measures with acceptable model fit was established, although TLI of the
scalar model dropped to 0.905. As to all other measures, partial scalar
invariance was established with three single items showing variance in
intercepts, although, again, TLI of this partial scalar model decreased to
0.908. Nonetheless, the indices still indicated acceptable model fit and
model comparisons indicated full and partial scalar invariance of the
measures (Awang, 2015). Therefore, Estonian- and Russian-speaking
students were subsequently examined together.
The main analyses were conducted with Mplus version 8.8. To
answer the first research question, latent profile analysis (LPA) with the
specification TYPE = COMPLEX using “classroom” as the cluster was
conducted with motivational beliefs in physics and biology as indicator
variables (Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017). Solutions with two to eight

5.2.1. Gender
Configural Frequecy Analysis (Table 3) showed significantly more

5.2.2. Perceived teacher behavior
There were significant differences between the profiles in students’

these differences). Indifferent and Maladaptive profiles perceived lower

profile did not differ from the Moderate profile. As to perceived needsupport of physics teachers, the Indifferent, Maladaptive, and BiologyAdaptive profiles perceived lower need-support from their physics
teachers than other profiles. Compared to other profiles, the PhysicsAdaptive and Adaptive profiles perceived higher need-support from their
physics teachers.
The Maladaptive profile perceived higher need-thwarting behavior
from their biology teachers compared to other profiles. The Adaptive
profile perceived lower need-thwarting behavior from their biology
teacher compared to other profiles, except for the Biology-Adaptive
profile, which, in turn, did not differ from the Indifferent profile. The
Physics-Adaptive profile did not differ from the Indifferent and Moderate
profiles regarding perceptions of biology teachers’ need-thwarting
behavior. The Indifferent, Physics-Adaptive, and Adaptive profiles
perceived lower need-thwarting behavior from their physics teachers
compared to other groups. The Maladaptive and Biology-Adaptive profiles

5.1. Latent motivational belief profiles
Based on fit indices of the LPA solutions (Table 1), theoretical
interpretability, and sizes of profiles, the solution with six latent profiles
was chosen for further analyses. Although AIC, BIC, and aBIC decreased
with additional profiles, solutions with more profiles resulted in multiple small subgroups as well as lower entropy. The six-profile solution
had the highest entropy and, compared to the five-profile solution,
included an additional profile with a pattern of motivational beliefs that
clearly differed from other groups.
Descriptive statistics of latent profiles are provided in Table 2 and
illustrated in Fig. 1. The largest group, Moderate, included 618 students

Adaptive, included 455 students (30 %), with high EV but low cost in
both subjects. The third-largest group, Maladaptive, included 135 students (9 %), with low EV but high cost in both subjects. Two groups
included students with distinguishable motivational beliefs in biology
and physics. The Biology-Adaptive group included 131 students (9 %),
with high EV and low cost in biology; and low EV and high cost in
physics. The Physics-Adaptive group included 120 students (8 %), with
low EV and high cost in biology; and high EV and low cost in physics.
The smallest group, Indifferent, included 43 students (3 %), with low
evaluations of all indicators.

6.3. Profile differences in perceptions of teachers’ behavior
As expected, motivational profiles differed in perceptions of teacher
behavior, mostly with medium to large effect sizes regarding needsupportive behavior and small to medium effect sizes for needthwarting behavior. We found that 1) students with high motivation
in both subjects perceived their biology and physics teachers to behave
in more need-supportive and less need-thwarting ways; 2) students with
low motivation in both subjects perceived both teachers to behave in less
need-supportive and more need-thwarting ways; and 3) students with
moderate motivation perceived both teachers’ behavior somewhere in
the middle. These results are consistent with earlier research on students’ and young athletes’ motivation and perceptions of their teachers’
and coaches’ need-supportive (Lazarides et al., 2020, 2022; Parrisius
et al., 2022) and need-thwarting (Bartholomew et al., 2011) behavior.
The fact that Fong et al. (2021) did not find similar differences may be a
result of assessing teacher behavior via teacher reports. The differences
between these findings point to the importance of students’ perceptions
and interpretations of contextual factors, such as teacher behavior
concerning their motivation.
Furthermore, we demonstrated that profiles with subject-specific
motivation were associated with perceptions of subject teachers’
behavior. Namely, students in the Biology-Adaptive and Physics-Adaptive
profiles perceived one of their teacher’s behavior (the teacher of the
subject where they had high motivation) to be more need-supportive
and less need-thwarting, while simultaneously perceiving the other
teacher’s behavior (the teacher of the subject where they had low
motivation) to be less need-supportive and more need-thwarting. This
highlights the importance of subject teachers’ behavior as perceived by
students for students’ subject-specific motivation.
It is probable that, as students perceive more need-supportive
behavior from their teacher, they experience higher satisfaction of
basic psychological needs (Bureau et al., 2022) and consequently
develop more adaptive motivational beliefs regarding that specific
subject (Parrisius et al., 2022). Students may simultaneously become
less motivated in other subjects if they feel that their psychological
needs are not being met. Our findings indicate that there are students
whose motivation differs considerably even between science subjects
and that those students may be especially sensitive to their subject
teachers’ need-supportive and need-thwarting behavior.

6.2. Between-profile differences in gender composition
The well-known gender gap in math and science related areas has
prompted researchers and practitioners to seek explanations and ways to
decrease it (Ceci et al., 2009). Researchers have focused on gender differences in motivational beliefs about these subjects (e.g., Bөe & Henriksen, 2013; Kwarikunda et al., 2021). As expected (Gaspard et al.,
2017; Jansen et al., 2014; Nagy et al., 2007), we found more girls with
high motivation in biology (but not physics), and more boys with high
motivation in physics (but not biology). Similarly, Bөe and Henriksen
(2013) found that girls were underrepresented in the Intrinsic physics
profile. This finding may be related to the higher importance of math in
physics than biology, which has been thought to be one reason behind
the gender gap between physics-related career choices (Ceci et al.,
2009). Students in the Physics-Adaptive profile were characterized by the
highest physics expectancy. Compared to girls, boys tend to develop
higher competence beliefs regarding math already in primary school
(Herbert & Stipek, 2005). As boys were overrepresented in the PhysicsAdaptive profile, it is possible that the students in this profile were
mainly boys with high math competence beliefs, which in turn influenced their physics expectancy due to the significant role of math in the
subject. Still, there were some girls with high motivation in physics (but
not biology) and boys with high motivation in biology (but not physics).
We found only one gender difference among subject-general profiles:
boys were overrepresented in the Indifferent profile. Although this group

6.4. Profile differences in task persistence and reported use of learning
strategies

This study has several limitations. First, participation was voluntary,
and the participating schools are likely to be more interested in supporting students’ learning competence. Second, only self-report measures were used, and each scale had relatively few items. While similar
motivational measures have been used in prior SEVT studies (Wigfield &
Eccles, 2000), and studies have shown the advantages of student perceptions of teacher behavior, in the future, at least learning behavior
should be examined with different methods (e.g., teacher-reports, observations; Zhang et al., 2011). Third, the learning task to assess the use
of deep learning strategies was biology-specific as there are no complex
science tests in Estonia starting from Grade 8. Other methods for
studying learning strategies should be used in the future. Fourth, we did
not assess all dimensions of values (i.e., attainment value or different
facets of utility and cost). Fifth, the data was drawn from a longer
questionnaire. As the questions about motivational beliefs were at the
end of the questionnaire, it is possible that the participants were
somewhat tired and not fully engaged in their responses. Sixth, the study
was carried out in Estonia, where students show strong scientific
knowledge as compared to other countries. Thus, we cannot generalize
these findings to other countries or age groups. Seventh, while full scalar
invariance of motivational beliefs measures was achieved across the
Estonian- and Russian-speaking students, there were a few items from
the task-persistence and perceptions of teacher behavior scales that had
varying intercepts, resulting in only partial scalar invariance of these
measures. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution.
In conclusion, most students belonged to subject-general profiles,
which indicates that most students at this age have not formed hierarchies of EVC between physics and biology. Thus, most students tend to
be engaged in learning both science subjects in the end of middle school.
This is in line with Estonian students’ top results in PISA studies (OECD,
2023). The most vulnerable students in our study belonged to the Maladaptive and Indifferent profiles with not only low motivation but also
low task persistence and application of deep learning strategies. We may
hypothesize that the Maladaptive and Indifferent profiles may include
students with lower abilities who need complex interventions, including
both motivational support and explicit instruction on how to learn.
Previous studies have shown the effectiveness of interventions which tap
utility value and cost (Nagengast et al., 2017; Rosenzweig et al., 2020).
However, these students may additionally require explicit instruction on
learning strategies (Seufert, 2020). Observational studies have shown
that teachers rarely talk about memory and learning processes explicitly
(Granström et al., 2023; Ornstein et al., 2010), which is especially
detrimental for the learning of students with lower abilities (Ornstein
et al., 2010). Our findings suggest that, in addition to teaching about
learning strategies, teachers should reflect on how students perceive
their teaching practices and behavior. When teaching and interacting
with students, teachers should aim to satisfy their students’ basic psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, while
maintaining an awareness of behaviors that students might experience
as need-thwarting.
We also found two smaller subject-specific groups that also differed
in their gender composition, with girls being overrepresented in the
group preferring biology and boys in the group preferring physics. This
may indicate emerging gender differences. Namely, girls may start to
believe that they are not suited for physics (similar to the concept of not

students’ task persistence differed between profiles with mostly medium
to large effect sizes. Differences were similar across both subjects. Students who have higher self-efficacy, are interested in the subject, see its
value outside of the classroom, and do not perceive learning too timeconsuming and demanding, are more likely to take on challenges and
engage effortfully during learning. While about a third of the participants valued both subjects highly, a smaller number of students had
clear priorities for learning either biology or physics. Exerting greater
effort is one of the two pathways through which motivational beliefs
support learning outcomes (Vu et al., 2021), is especially important
when learning complex topics (cf. Cai et al., 2018), and may fuel future
career decisions. However, both maladaptive motivational beliefs (Hong
& Bernacki, 2022; Watt et al., 2019), and low task persistence (Jõgi &
Kikas, 2016) may be related to lower knowledge and skills. Thus, we
may speculate that students in Maladaptive and Indifferent profiles have
low knowledge and skills in biology and physics and therefore struggle
with learning.
Another pathway from motivation to learning outcomes is the use of
complex learning strategies that support knowledge integration and
deep learning (Vu et al., 2021). Research has shown that applying deep
learning strategies supports learning, while applying surface strategies
inhibits learning (Ardura & Galán, 2019; Kikas et al., 2020). As expected, reported use of deep learning strategies differed between the
motivational profiles, with the effect sizes being mostly small to medium. Although reported use of strategies was not assessed separately
for biology and physics, the presented strategies were general deep
learning strategies: planning, distributed learning, using visuals, systematizing, and summarizing information. These are valuable for
learning all subjects. Studies indicate that middle school students tend to
already have generalized metacognitive skills (van der Stel & Veenman,
2013). Our findings are consistent with this as biology- and physicsspecific profiles did not differ in the reported use of learning strategies, but important differences were found between the Adaptive, Maladaptive, and Indifferent profiles.
Students in the Adaptive motivational profile stood out from students
in other groups by reporting to use a greater number of deep strategies.
We may speculate that these students had generalized knowledge of
learning strategies and the skills to apply them. Similar to the relationship between motivation and task persistence, motivational beliefs and
use of learning strategies may be mutually supportive. High motivation
supports learning with understanding, which is achieved via planning
and distributing learning over time, searching for means to integrate
knowledge, and properly integrating and systematizing this knowledge.
In other words, mastery of a subject implies knowing about and applying
deep learning strategies (Brod, 2020; Van Meter & Campbell, 2020).
In contrast, the Indifferent and Maladaptive motivational profiles reported using a smaller number of deep learning strategies. Similarly,
Kwarikunda et al. (2021) showed that students with low and gradeintrojected motivation mostly use surface learning strategies, while
students with high and primarily intrinsic motivation use deep learning
strategies. Many students do not acquire complex learning strategies
independently; instead, students typically require explicit instruction

less effort into learning physics. This may impact their future career
opportunities (e.g., in Estonia, passing a physics exam is required to
study medicine). On the other hand, students with higher motivation
and persistence in one of the subjects may benefit from more challenging
tasks and guidance from teachers to study that subject more deeply and
systematically (e.g., teachers can recommend participating in out-otschool science courses).
Our results shed light on hierarchies of expectancies and values, even
between closely related subjects. Still, further studies with different
specific subjects (e.g., chemistry), age groups, and data-collection
methods (e.g., teacher interviews, classroom observation) are needed.
Longitudinal studies could examine the development of motivation in
the classroom context. Future studies could also examine the development of motivational beliefs in multiple science- and math related
subjects as students transition from grades where they are only taught
general science lessons by a single teacher to grades where they are
taught multiple science subjects separately and by different teachers.
