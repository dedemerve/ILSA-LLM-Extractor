Abstract
This quantitative comparative study looks at cross-national variations in the regulation of the
teaching profession and the way they shape how teachers perceive the value of their profession,
feel about their employment conditions, work environment, and profession, and their attrition
intention within the next 5 years. It draws on a theoretical typology that distinguishes four models
of profession regulation and tests their effects with a sample of 16 countries having participated in
the TALIS 2018 survey. While results highlight cross-country differences; comparisons between
models of regulation add highly significantly to the prediction of these outcomes.
Keywords
Teacher policies, value of the profession, teacher satisfaction, teacher attrition, comparative
education

these can be detrimental to educational quality and economic resources (García and Weiss, 2019).
Teacher attrition and retention are “thorny issues” considered as two side of the same coin, attrition
referring to “teachers leaving the profession [. . .] retention to keeping teachers into teaching”
(Kelchtermans, 2017: 962), whereas turnover is understood as leaving teaching for another job
(which may be in another school). As we discuss later, first the literature on teacher retention
(Guarino et al., 2006) and attrition (Borman and Dowling, 2008; Ingersoll, 2001; Nguyen et al.,
2020) has examined the role of personal and school factors (Kalogrides et al., 2013). Research
covering teacher-level factors such as demographic characteristics (e.g. age, gender, race, family
variables) has shown that some teachers are more prone to turnover or to leave the teaching profession. It also stresses that school-level factors such as organizational characteristics (e.g. school
leadership, professional collaboration, and shared decision-making), resources for teaching and
learning, and student-body characteristics (Borman and Dowling, 2008; Brantlinger and Grant,
2024; DeAngelis and Presley, 2011) matter for understanding retention and attrition phenomena.
Second, research has studied influences from external/policy factors (Nguyen et al., 2020), investigating how teacher policies related to salaries and compensation (Springer and Gardner, 2010),
teacher preparation (Ingersoll et al., 2014), induction programs for new teachers (Helms-Lorenz
et al., 2016)), or teacher evaluation and accountability (Rodriguez et al., 2020) influence teacher
and/or human resources (HR) decisions, and therefore, have an impact on labor market outcomes.
This literature has made a major contribution to understanding teacher labor markets and their
impact on several labor market outcomes, in particular on teacher attrition, retention, and turnover. However, literature on teacher policy still suffers from limitations that this study aims to
address. We investigate how teacher policies impact the way teachers perceive the value granted
to their profession, express satisfaction with their employment conditions, working environment
and profession, and intend to leave over the next 5 years. However, rather than estimating the
causal effect of specific policies, we study how comprehensive models of teacher policies contribute to explaining an important source of variation in teacher labor market outcomes (i.e.
between-country differences) that has been neglected by most of the studies given their withincountry design. Our contribution to the current literature is thus twofold.
First, we integrate country-level variations into the analysis of the determinants of teacher labor
market outcomes. Studies in comparative and international education have consistently demonstrated cross-country differences in several key dimensions such as teacher certification (Akiba
et al., 2007), teacher preparation (Tatto et al., 2018), teacher accountability (Verger et al., 2019),
and teacher labor markets (Dumay and Burn, 2022). However, the way they could shape teacher
labor market outcomes has been neglected. On the other hand, the study of determinants of labor
market outcomes such as teacher retention or attrition has mainly adopted a within-country perspective ignoring an important source of variation both in prediction factors (i.e. cross-country
variations in teacher policies) and in outcomes. Our work thus takes into account the influence of
personal and school-level factors much studied in the literature, and examines the role of teacher
policies in shaping these country differences.
Second, we suggest encapsulating policies related to teacher education, teacher labor markets
and careers, and teacher accountability into models of regulation, in line with previous studies that
have adopted a comprehensive perspective on teacher policies (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017;
Tatto, 2007; Voisin and Dumay, 2020) and argue that teacher policies are not independent of one
another. They reflect choices, path dependencies and worldviews (on education and teachers) that
organize systems of policies and practices (Helgetun and Dumay, 2021). We use a typology that
classifies countries according to three categories of teacher policies (Voisin and Dumay, 2020):
(1) teacher education; (2) teacher labor markets and careers; and (3) teacher accountability. This
typology distinguishes four models of regulation, namely the Market, Bureaucratic, Training, and

The next sections of this paper are organized as follows. We start by reviewing the literature on

paper. We then present our methods before presenting our main results, which are discussed in terms
of their contribution to the scholarship on teacher labor market outcomes and teacher policies.

Literature Review and Problem Statement
Teacher labor market outcomes
Teacher labor market outcomes have been analyzed through the actual proportion of teacher shortages or retention/attrition rates using data from administrations at district or national levels (Nguyen
et al., 2020); or using proximal predictors of teacher attrition such as job satisfaction or intention
to quit (Madigan and Kim, 2021). As we used TALIS 2018 data to obtain comparable measures
between countries, we selected five related concepts documented in TALIS 2018 as proxies to
operationalize teacher labor market outcomes. We therefore enquire about teachers’ satisfaction
with employment conditions, work environment, and the profession, as well as teachers’ perception of the value of the profession, and teachers’ intention to quit within the next 5 years, which has
been extensively used as a proxy for attrition (Madigan and Kim, 2021).
Teacher job (dis)satisfaction is indeed considered strongly related to teacher attrition. A recent
meta-analysis showed that teacher job satisfaction had a strong negative relationship with teachers’
intention to quit (Madigan and Kim, 2021). While teacher job satisfaction with their profession is
often only related to attrition, job (dis)satisfaction may stem from the work environment rather
than the profession, which, in turn, is related to the phenomenon of teachers leaving their school,
but not necessarily the profession (Qin, 2019). TALIS 2018 survey includes satisfaction both with
the profession and with the work environment. Teacher satisfaction with employment conditions is
also reported as highly related to teacher attrition (García and Weiss, 2019). Such employment
conditions as satisfaction with salary have been shown to influence retention (Ashiedu et al., 2012),
especially in the case of experienced teachers (García et al., 2022). The perceived value of the
profession by teachers, which tend to be aligned with the perception of the larger society (Akiba
et al., 2023), has been described as related to teachers’ satisfaction with their job, teachers feeling
undervalued (by society, the media) being less likely to report being satisfied with their job (Akiba
et al., 2023; OECD, 2019b) and, in turn, potentially more prone to leave the profession. Some
researchers also argue that the low perceived status of the profession also impacts the profession’s
attractiveness (Cushman, 2007) and, in turn, the recruitment of teachers (Podolsky et al., 2016).

Personal, school and policy factors as determinants of labor market outcomes
As for the impact of distinct factors on these labor market outcomes, a recent meta-analysis highlighted three categories of factors having an impact on these outcomes: personal factors, school
factors, and policy factors (Nguyen et al., 2020).
Literature has documented the impact of age, gender, race, qualification, percentage of working hours, or qualification on teacher attrition, focusing on a demographic perspective (Brantlinger

and Grant, 2024; Nguyen and Redding, 2018). It stresses that women, teachers from ethnic minority backgrounds, younger teachers and close to retirement age are more likely to leave the profession, and that teachers with standard certification (vs alternative certification) are less likely to
leave it (Borman and Dowling, 2008; Ingersoll, 2001; Nguyen et al., 2020; OECD, 2019b;
Renzulli et al., 2011).
In addition, a great number of studies have also considered organizational/school factors such
as the characteristics of the school (number of students, students’ SES, type of education, etc.) or
the organizational routines (teacher collaboration, principal leadership, etc.) (Nguyen et al., 2020),
highlighting that collaboration, supportive communication with school administrators, greater
resources for teaching as well as higher achievement level and school socioeconomical status may
reduce attrition (Borman and Dowling, 2008; Nguyen et al., 2020; OECD, 2019b).
Moreover, a growing number of studies have evaluated the impact of teacher policies on teacher
labor market outcomes. Among the studies examining the effects of teacher policies on teacher attrition, those on the effects of teacher evaluation and accountability systems are the most developed.
Studies have focused either on teacher attrition at the school level in relation to evaluation and
accountability pressures felt by teachers or on variations in accountability policies and their effects.
For example, research on school variations has unsurprisingly found that accountability made teacher
retention more difficult in low-performing schools (Clotfelter et al., 2004; Reback et al., 2014).
Studies on teacher education policies and retention cover a broad set of related questions about
the effect of teacher certification and degree status, entry pathways into the profession, types of
education programs, and early career and induction (Cochran-Smith et al., 2011). Research on
degree status offers little support for the contention that teachers with greater academic competency persist in the profession longer (LaTurner, 2002). Benefits of alternative entry pathways (i.e.
alternative certification programs) or partnership models of teacher preparation (i.e. Professional
Development Schools—PDS) are a much more debated topic. Emerging and still inconsistent
trends showed lower rates of attrition for PDS programs, but higher levels of attrition for alternative certification programs, especially after more than 1 year in the profession (Zhang and Zeller,
2016). Studies have also pointed to consistent positive effects of teacher induction practices on
teacher turnover and attrition (Ronfeldt and McQueen, 2017
Finally, the extensive literature on teachers’ salaries (Nguyen et al., 2020) has shown that a
salary increase slightly reduces teacher turnover (Hanushek et al., 2004). Moreover, research on
country differences in the level of teacher salaries has provided evidence that differences in the
economic status of the teaching profession help to explain the variations in the profession’s attractiveness (Park and Byun, 2015), but to our knowledge, no study has tested the impact of betweencountry differences in the profession’s attractiveness on teacher retention.

Comparative studies of teacher policies
Literature on teacher labor market outcomes has focused mainly on a single-nation case, predominantly the USA, which is surprising given the growing need for global efforts to improve teacher
retention. However, comparative case studies and international studies on teachers and the teaching
profession (i.e. TALIS, TIMSS, TEDS-M) have provided strong evidence regarding cross-country
differences in teachers’ satisfaction with their working environment and the profession, and the
prestige of the profession (OECD, 2019b), as well as teacher retention and turnover, certainly pointing out to the importance of teacher policies to understanding labor markets outcomes.
In this vein, international studies have stressed cross-country differences in teacher preparation and development (Tatto et al., 2018), teacher certification (Akiba et al., 2007), cultural roles
and identities of teachers (Baker and LeTendre, 2005), and teacher knowledge (Blömeke and

206

European Educational Research Journal 25(2)

Delaney, 2012). Comparative and international education scholarship has also clearly identified
various models of teacher accountability. Verger et al. (2019) identified at least three different
models: New Public Management Marketizers (England, Chile, USA), Neo-Weberian States
(Denmark or Sweden) and countries with a Napoleonic administrative tradition (in France and
Southern European countries). Although fewer studies have examined international variations in
teacher labor markets and careers, systematic differences whether in employment status, career
structures or criteria for professional advancement have been stressed (Bertron et al., 2024;
Mathou et al., 2022).
In addition, a smaller number of comparative studies have gone one step further by looking at
patterns of teacher policies in distinct educational systems (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Tatto,
2007). Spanning three continents and five countries, Darling-Hammond et al. (2017) examined
seven jurisdictions that have developed comprehensive teacher policies systems (i.e. systems of
teacher recruitment, teacher preparation, career, and professional development): Singapore and
Finland, the states of New South Wales and Victoria in Australia, the provinces of Alberta and
Ontario in Canada, and the province of Shanghai in China. They found that selection practices that
make it possible to recruit the best candidates for entering the profession, strong teacher preparation along with induction and mentoring programs, continuous professional training, and career
opportunities as well as a strong emphasis on teacher evaluation not only ensure quality teaching
but are also critical drivers for professionalizing teachers.

Models of teaching profession regulation
The comparative and international literature therefore suggests not only that teacher policies vary
across countries, but also that categories of teacher policies are interrelated within given educational systems to form patterns that we refer to as models of regulation (Maroy, 2009; Voisin and
Dumay, 2020). However, to our knowledge, few studies have examined the effects of such patterns
of teacher policies on teacher outcomes.
The pioneering work of Price and Weatherby (2018), however, based on the OECD TALIS 2013
data, provide evidence on such between-country variations in the proportion of teachers who consider the teaching profession to be of little or no value in their societies. Interestingly, they also
found relationships with level of professionalism, characterized broadly in terms of the levels of
professional benchmarks, professional discretion, room for promotion, and the quality of workplace conditions. These findings, although of crucial importance, only considered limited range of
dimensions relating mainly to teacher knowledge and education, and to a lesser extent to teacher
accountability and teacher labor markets.
Akiba et al. (2023) through their comparative study using the PISA 2018 data and building on
Voisin and Dumay’s typology (2020) go beyond these limitations. They stress that policy contexts—that is, teacher education, labor market and accountability, not only create great disparity in
teacher candidate qualifications, distribution of qualified teachers, and degree of professional control, but also generate important within-country variations in teachers’ perception of occupational
value and working conditions.
As regard policy contexts, drawing on Eliot Freidson’s work on professionalism (2001), Voisin
and Dumay (2020) developed a theoretical typology of teacher policies encompassing teacher
education, teacher labor markets and careers, and teacher accountability policies. They identified
four models of profession regulation embedded in a market-oriented, bureaucratic, or professional
approach on which we have built our study (see Table 1 for a detailed presentation).
The Market model, within which Voisin and Dumay (2020) situate England, Australia, USA,
New Zealand, and Chile, is characterized by the diversification of teacher education providers and

teachers, and schools. While labor market regulation is based on the principles of openness, flexibility, and competition for jobs, professional and performance standards serve as benchmarks,
stressing the importance of monitoring the quality of the teaching workforce along the way. Various
accountability schemes imply that teachers enjoy a relatively low level of professional autonomy,
and a strong regulation of their core work. As for the effects of such a model, we hypothesis that
low teacher autonomy coupled with increased control in the form of accountability schemes may
create a sense of lack of trust in teachers (Ingersoll and Collins, 2017; Price and Weatherby, 2018).
Conversely, more flexible regulation of the labor market and the (relative) ability to choose one’s
work environment could contribute to greater satisfaction with working conditions (OECD,
2019b). However, we posit that teachers’ satisfaction with working conditions could be altered by
the severe pressure undergone by teachers facing accountability mechanisms (OECD, 2019b;
Parcerisa et al., 2022; Smith and Holloway, 2020) and by significant variations in teachers’ employment conditions (García and Weiss, 2019; OECD, 2019b).
The Bureaucratic model, in which Voisin and Dumay (2020) have situated countries such as
France, Italy, Portugal, and Spain, corresponds to a drastically different logic stemming from the
central definition of bureaucratic rules and impersonal criteria that govern teachers’ allocation to
schools, employment status, and career trajectories based on flat career structures that provide few
opportunities for professional advancement. While subject-matter expertise is the key foundation
of teachers’ autonomy exercised within state guidelines regarding educational curricula, this autonomy goes hand in hand with a relatively low level of teacher accountability. While the literature has
highlighted the strikingly low proportion of teachers who agree that teaching is a valued profession
in countries like France and Spain (OECD, 2014), we hypothesis that employment conditions
guaranteed by a highly regulated status and a high level of teacher autonomy contribute positively
to the overall sense of satisfaction with the profession. However, we posit that limited opportunities for career development or vertical and horizontal mobility could negatively affect teachers’

level of satisfaction with their employment and working conditions and, more broadly, the feeling
of valuation of the profession by society (Kim and Loadman, 1994; OECD, 2019b; Price and
Weatherby, 2018).
The last two models are arguably different versions of a professional model (Freidson, 2001),
emphasizing selective and high-quality initial teacher education and professional training as key
foundations of teacher professionalism. The main characteristic of the Training model (in Finland,
Denmark, and Norway) is a long-term university-based teacher preparation serving as a key foundation of teachers’ professional expertise and autonomy. This model encompasses a flexible division of labor based on professional autonomy and expertise where teachers enjoy a high degree of
freedom. Finally, the Professional skills model (in Japan, Singapore, Shanghai, and Korea) also
includes selective long-term initial teacher education. However, unlike the Training model, it relies
on a mix of bureaucratic rules and professional standards, and the definition of hierarchical career
ladders that include professional-development and performance-based elements. It also combines
various forms of accountability (managerial, hierarchical, and professional) along with a tight
monitoring of teachers’ and students’ performance. In both the Training and the Professional skills
models, whereas the historically high status granted to the profession has been largely maintained
(Price and Weatherby, 2018), we hypothesis that the pressure undergone by teachers facing multiple accountability mechanisms in the Professional skills model and performance-driven education
systems such as Singapore (Katsuno, 2012; La Londe and Verger, 2022) could lead teachers to a
greater sense of dissatisfaction with their work environment (OECD, 2019b).

This study
This study looks at cross-national variations in the regulation of the teaching profession and the
way they shape how teachers perceive the value of their profession, feel about their employment
conditions, work environment, and profession, and the intention to quit it within the next 5 years.
Based on empirical evidence from the comparative and international literature emphasizing that
cross-national variations in these key variables persist after controlling for key factors (personal,
school, and policy) (Akiba et al., 2023; OECD, 2019b, 2020; Price and Weatherby, 2018), we analyze the role of profession regulation models in shaping these country differences. We therefore ask
the two following research questions:
RQ1a. Are the country differences in professional value perceived by teachers, their satisfaction
with employment conditions, work environment and profession, and their intention to quit within
the next five years, related to the different models for regulating the teaching profession?
RQ1b. How do the relationships between models for regulating the teaching profession and
the dependent variables, resist to identifiable country-level confounders such as the economic
status of the teaching profession, cultural orientations of countries, and regional effects?

Methods
Sample and design
These research questions are answered using TALIS 2018 datasets. TALIS involves data collection
from a nationally representative sample of teachers, principals and schools using a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design in a set of 48 countries with approximately 200 schools sampled for
each participating country and 20 teachers for each school (OECD, 2020). The primary data used
for this research were from the 2018 TALIS “core” study, which included two datasets that

collected self-reported survey data from principals and teachers in lower secondary education (i.e.
grades 7, 8 ,9) respectively. Despite some limitations (see Ulferts, 2021), the TALIS data provides
rich comparative standardized information on teachers’, headteachers’, schools’, and participating
education systems’ characteristics.
From the 48 participating countries at the lower secondary level, we select a sample of 16 countries based on the previous classification of these countries into the four models of the teaching
profession regulation (for a detailed discussion of the classification process, see Voisin and Dumay,
2020). These 16 countries represent 53,178 teachers working in 3076 schools. The classification
was developed using the TALIS 2013 dataset complemented with other sources of comparative
documentation produced by international organizations and reports produced by national governments and agencies. It is indeed excessively difficult and risky to include the whole TALIS sample
given the lack of reliable comparable information on the different dimensions used to classify
countries. The limited number of countries that we selected in our sample is therefore justified by
the maximization of the reliability and the validity of the classification we used, even if this entails
a small n and a limited statistical power for testing effects at the country level.

Analytical strategy
Analyzing the link between models of teaching profession regulation and teacher labor market
outcomes poses several methodological challenges. The first is related to the TALIS study design
and related opportunities to apply cross-country analyses. Although multi-level analyses at three
levels (teachers, schools, and systems) might address our research objectives, Price and Carstens
(2020: 59) note:
a multilevel modelling of TALIS teacher data where the country/economy is one of the levels presents the
important drawback of assuming that country differences are random. That is, a three-level modelling
where the country/economy was declared as level-3 with schools nested within country/economy at level-2
and teachers within schools at level-1 would assume dependent error structures between country/economy
and school (Bryk and Raudenbush, 1992, p. 101), which is not likely a uniform assumption across TALIS
countries and economies.

We therefore analyze the data using two-level models (for each country) to adjust for differences across countries according to control variables measuring differences between teachers and
schools, and then test whether models of teaching profession regulation are related to these adjusted
mean differences in value of the profession, satisfaction with profession, employment conditions
and work environment, and intention to quit the profession. These two-level models do not mean
we assume a uniform dependent error structure between country/economy at level-2 and school or
teacher at level-1. Instead, we run models for each of the 16 countries to adjust for individual and
school characteristics within countries, and then descriptively compare countries before running
multivariate models using country dummy variables and country-level confounders as covariates.
The second methodological challenge is that such cross-country analyses need to demonstrate
the scalar invariance of the scales to be compared across countries (Price and Carstens, 2020). The
TALIS 2018 analytical report suggests that the comparison of the mean score of the scale is meaningful across groups only if scales would reach scalar invariance, which is only partially the case
for the scales of our depending variables (see Table 2). We discuss this point more extensively in
the section about our variables and measurement model.
The third and most critical methodological challenge is the potential for omitted variable bias in
the analysis. Our strategy for limiting these risks is to identify relevant country-level confounders

Variables and measurement model
All measures (see below) were taken from the TALIS 2018 teacher or principal questionnaires
(OECD, 2019a). Both observed and latent variables were included in our prediction models. As 16
units at level 3 were insufficient to model our latent construct at this level, we do not implement a
full latent structural equation model, but we instead extract latent factor scores of our dependent
latent constructs. Latent factor scores incorporate a partial correction for measurement error and
conserve the properties of the measurement models (Morin et al., 2020).

Dependent variables
The measurement model for four of the dependent variables was estimated at the teacher level. We
modeled a four-factor Exploratory Structural Equation Model (ESEM) to extract latent factor
scores of our four related constructs (see below for the specification of the model). The choice of

included four main items (TALIS2018 labels: TT3G53A, TT3G53B, TT3G53D, TT3G53F); job
satisfaction with work environment included three main items (TT3G53C, TT3G53E, TT3G53G);
satisfaction with employment conditions included two main items (TT3G54A, TT3G54B); and
value of the profession included four main items (TT3G54C, TT3G54D, TT3G54E, TT3G53H)
(for the full list of items, see online Supplemental Material). Following the ESEM procedure, all
items were allowed to load on each factor with loading close to null for their non-related factors.

p < .00). It is worth mentioning that two pairs of these variables showed quite high correlation

Since our dependent variables are used to compare groups of countries, we tested the measurement invariance of our measurement model for the 16 included countries. A detailed description of
the procedure can be found in the online Supplemental Material. As shown in Table 2, configural
and metric invariance were achieved. Full scalar invariance was not met, but partial scalar invariance was. Reaching only partially the scalar invariance step does not invalidate cross-country
comparisons. Indeed, Robitzsch and Lüdtke (2023) recently argued that full scalar invariance is
almost impossible to achieve with a database containing an important number of groups (>10) and
demonstrated that “(. . .) the violation of measurement invariance typically does not threaten validity (. . .) measurement invariance has only minor relevance for ensuring meaningful and valid
group comparisons” (Robitzsch and Lüdtke, 2023: 860). Furthermore, our model demonstrated
better invariance properties than the TALIS2018 invariance models—which only achieve configural to metric invariance for our four latent constructs (OECD, 2019a)—reinforcing its suitability
for our analysis.
In addition to these four latent variables, we included as dependent (observed) variable, the
intention to quit the profession within the next 5 years (TT3G50). To control for the higher propensity of teachers to leave the profession at the end of their career, which can be a confounder
for country differences in teacher demographics, we restricted the sample (for this variable only)
to teachers having less or equal to 25 years’ seniority. As expected from the literature on teacher
attrition (see Table 3), the intention to quit shows negative correlations with satisfaction with
profession (r = −0.296, p < 0.01), workplace (r = −0.200, p < 0.01), and employment conditions
(r = −0.170, p < 0.01). Interestingly, the correlation with the perceived value of the profession is
significant too, but the smallest (r = −0.100, p < 0.01).

Level-1 (teacher) covariates (for within-countries two-level models)
Considering existing meta-analyses on teacher attrition and turnover (Borman and Dowling, 2008;
Nguyen et al., 2020) and available variables in TALIS 2018, we included the following level-1
control variables: teacher gender (TT3G01),1 with female coded as 0 and male coded as 1; teaching
as choice of career (TT3G08) with first choice coded as 0 and second choice coded as 1; employment status as a teacher at this school (TT3G09) with permanent employment coded as 0, and
fixed-term contract coded as 1; working time at this school (TT3G10A) with full-time (or more
than 90% of full-time hours) as 0, and part-time as 1; experience as a teacher at this school
(TT3G11A) and in the teaching career (TT3G11B) measured by the number of years and centered
on the country means; and the number of schools teachers are working in with teachers in one
school coded as 0, or more coded as 1 (TT3G12).

Level-2 (school) covariates (for within-countries two-level models)
We included level-2 variables shown to be related to our set of dependent variables based on the
same meta-analyses (Borman and Dowling, 2008; Nguyen et al., 2020). Effective professional
development, stakeholder involvement, team innovativeness, disciplinary climate, and teacherstudent relations, were respectively operationalized with the standardized scores from the mean
of the corresponding items: effective professional development (TT3G26A to D), stakeholder
involvement (TT3G48A to E), team innovativeness (TT3G32A to D), disciplinary climate
(TT3G41A to D), teacher-student relations (TT3G49A to D). Based on the principal questionnaire,

214

school leadership was operationalized with standardized score from the mean of the corresponding
items (TC3G22D to F) (for the full list of items of these variables, see online Supplemental
Material). Reliability coefficients for each of these scales, calculated by country, are available in
the TALIS 2018 technical report (OECD, 2019b). In addition, a standardized composite score of
the school social composition based on three items (percentage of students whose first language

by the principal (TT3G29A) were included, coded 0 for principals reporting no teacher shortages,
and 1 otherwise.

Country-level covariates (confounders)
We identified three categories of confounders based on the comparative education scholarship
(Klassen et al., 2010; Park and Byun, 2015) and the cross-cultural psychology of work (Hartung
et al., 2010; Hofstede, 2006). The first is the economic status of the teaching profession measured
by the standardized mean salary for lower secondary teachers teaching in general programs from
PISA 20182 (mean from starting salary, salary after 10 years of experience, salary after 15 years of
experience, and salary at top scale); and the standardized ratio of salaries after 15 years of experience to per capita GDP lower secondary typical training from PISA 2015.3 The second are cultural
differences between countries, which we approach using Hofstede’s system of cultural dimensions, in particular the individualism-collectivism scale (Hofstede, 2006). This dimension, which
has repeatedly found to be associated with work values and career planning and decision-making
(Hartung et al., 2010), measures the degree of interdependence that a society maintains among its
members. The standardized mean of the individualism scale is used as covariate. Higher scores on
this variable indicate countries with a more individualistic orientation, which are expected to correlate negatively with the value of the profession. The third are regional (fixed) effects, which
captures all unobserved characteristics associated (other than the profession’s economic status
and countries’ cultural orientations) with the regional dimension. The difficulty, however, was to
find a definition of regional categories that does not strictly overlap with the categories of our
typology of profession regulation models. Indeed, the countries of the Bureaucratic model are
largely concentrated in continental Europe, those of the Training model in Northern Europe, and
those of the professional skills model in Southeast Asia. Only the Market model countries are
more widely scattered across different regions of the world (North and South America, Europe,
Asia/Oceania). We therefore define the regional categories as follows: America (=0), Europe (=1),
and Asia/Oceania (=2). Data on covariates by country are available in the Online Supplemental
Material.

Independent variables
The main independent variables of interest are the four models of profession regulation (the
Market, Bureaucratic, Training, and Professional skills models) coded as dummy variables.

Discussion
This paper examined the role models of profession regulation in shaping teachers’ perceptions of
the value of their profession, teachers’ satisfaction with their employment conditions, work environment and profession, and their intention to leave the profession within the next 5 years. Based
on analyses of TALIS 2018 data on a sample of 16 theoretically selected countries, we developed
an approach to the determinants of teacher labor market outcomes that considers country-level differences and the role of models of profession regulation.
Results indicate that models of profession regulation contribute to shaping labor market outcomes, thereby adding to the emerging literature in comparative education on institutional

and the literature on labor markets for teachers that has largely neglected this level of analysis
(Nguyen et al., 2020). The analyses show how countries embedded in different types of regulation
models not only have different levels of professional value, employment, profession, and work
environment satisfaction, but also how their institutional characteristics shape these differences

Analyses point to some important results in this regard.
First, teachers in bureaucratic oriented regulation (in countries such as France, Italy, Spain, and
Portugal) feel their profession is less valued than in the Market and the Professional skills models
and appear to be less satisfied with their employment conditions than teachers in the Market
and Professional skills models. Based on previous studies (Kim and Loadman, 1994; Price and
Weatherby, 2018), those results draw attention to what may be the negative effect of career trajectories, based on flat career structures that provide few opportunities for professional advancement
that characterize the Bureaucratic model, on the perceived value of the profession, and on satisfaction with employment conditions. This result, however, is quite unexpected given the employment
conditions, especially job security, in the Bureaucratic model where teachers usually enjoy civil
servant status, including early-career teachers. However, this security may explain why teachers
have a lower intention to quit, despite being less satisfied with their employment conditions.
Although the level of salary is comparatively low in this model, especially in France, Italy, and in
Portugal, our covariance analyses indicate differences between regulation models remain significant after taking into account the status of the teaching profession (and the individualist orientation
of countries). These negative effects could therefore indicate that despite a high level of job security, other dimensions of the Bureaucratic model may negatively affect the image of the profession
and that of employment conditions. One of these characteristics may be the professional advancement based mainly on seniority. This interpretation is consistent with recent findings that have
suggested the role of non-monetary incentives, such as the presence and level of career opportunities or the use of flexible compensation schemes, in teacher retention and teacher development
(Biasi, 2021). A second interpretation is linked to the form of professional democracy, which is
highly focused on unionism in the Bureaucratic model. Studies have consistently shown the central
role of teacher unions in the negotiation and contestation of teacher policies in these countries
(Dumay and Burn, 2022), but rarely considered the possible gap in teachers’ views between union
leaders, simple union affiliates, and non-affiliated teachers, which could also explain why teachers
on the ground, despite being highly represented in political decision-making, do not feel that their
profession has very much control over decisions (an item taken into account in the measure of
teachers’ feeling of value attached to the profession, see online Supplemental Material, item
TT3G54D). To conclude this point, it should be noted that the less positive image that teachers
have of their profession in the Bureaucratic model does not translate into less satisfaction with the
profession. Even more striking, teachers in the Bureaucratic model are significantly less likely to
intend to leave the profession than in the three other models, after controlling for the economic

Dumay et al.

219

status of the teaching profession. In other words, teachers in this model do not feel positively
regarded by society nor do they evaluate positively their employment conditions, but they do
remain more in the profession. This could be explained by intrinsic factors such as the commitment
to their job (Johnson et al., 2005) or by the level of specificity of their teacher training that makes
them poor candidates for jobs in other sectors.
Second, the results show quite the opposite pattern in the Market model, and to a lesser extent
in the Training model. Teachers in these models feel relatively recognized as professionals and
rather satisfied with their employment conditions but are more likely to report wanting to leave the
profession. This result is consistent with the observation of high rates of teacher attrition in liberal
systems such as the US or England (Ingersoll, 2001), literature showing that teachers’ working
conditions, due to the pressures of high-stakes accountability, are crucial in this respect. However,
the contrast between the Bureaucratic and the Market models observed here also suggests explanations in terms of the permeability of labor markets, likely more important in the Market model.
Mobility between sectors, in the Market model, could moreover be favored by the development of
short-track training and alternative certification programs in the last 20 years (Boyd et al., 2006;
Zhang and Zeller, 2016).
Third, teachers in the Professional skills model in systems such as Japan, South Korea, Shanghai,
and Singapore, are significantly less satisfied with their work environment than in the three other
models. Our results indicate indeed that for equal levels of economic status, cultural orientation,
and taking regional effects into account, differences remain between the Professional skills model
and the three others. This result is surprising, because this model is precisely articulated around the
idea that, unlike the Training model, learning the profession is not only acquired through initial
training but also through a process of professional development throughout the whole career, in
which the work environment plays a crucial role. Studies (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017) have
also pointed to these characteristics as foundational dimensions of the comparative advantage of
systems such as Singapore or Shanghai in the quality of teacher development and the performance
of educational systems. In these systems, teacher mobility between schools is driven by public
authorities to foster the professional development of teachers confronted with diverse work conditions and organizational contexts. This could be a simple explanation of the lower level of teacher
satisfaction with work environment in the Professional skills model given that some items measuring this construct (see online Supplemental Material) are proxies of teachers’ migration intentions.
However, another plausible interpretation could also lie in teachers perceiving their work environment as a source of stress and of professional and organizational pressure. This interpretation in
consistent with studies in occupational health and well-being showing average high levels of work
stress in countries such as Japan or South Korea (Kawakami and Tsutsumi, 2016). What our results
add to the comparative literature on “top performing” systems is that their comparative advantage
(in professional development of teachers and teaching quality) is possibly psychologically at a cost
for teachers unhappy within their work environments. Whereas the effect of work environment
stress is possibly strongly related to satisfaction with work environment satisfaction and career
attrition intentions in Professional skills model countries requires therefore further investigation.
It is important to note several limitations of our study. First, the low number of units at the
country level puts the study at risk of Type II statistical errors. However, we have chosen to work
with a sound theoretical classification of 16 countries, rather than taking the risk of misclassifying
countries due to a lack of data and knowledge that would allow us to put our results into perspective and, therefore, weaken the internal validity of our classification. Second, in relation to the
limited number of countries per model of regulation, our estimation of the relationships between
regulatory models and teacher outputs could be threatened by the fixed effect of some outlier
countries. The online Supplemental Material provides indeed evidence on the distinctive profile

220

European Educational Research Journal 25(2)

of some country cases. Australia for instance appears to have higher average levels on the four
dependent variables that could boost the positive perceptions of teachers in the Market model.
Singapore, and Shanghai for the value of the profession, and Singapore for satisfaction with
employment conditions could have the same effect for the Professional skills model. However, the
strongest effects of models of regulation (i.e. the negative effect of the Bureaucratic model on the
perceived value of the profession, satisfaction with employment conditions and attrition intentions,
and that of the Professional skills model on satisfaction with work environment) do not seem to
suffer from such “fixed effect” endogeneity issues given that on these dependent variables, country
cases in these two models have very comparable behaviors. Third, the effects of regulation models
have been argued to be confounded with country-level confounders. However, our covariance
analysis integrating three different types of country-level confounders confirm the importance of
differences between regulation models. The negative relationship between the Bureaucratic model
and the value of the profession or satisfaction with employment is maintained after taking into
account the economic status of the profession. The positive relationship between the Professional
skills model and the value of the profession is not threated by the collectivistic orientation of countries. The negative relationship between the Professional skills model and satisfaction with work
environment also resist to the three types of confounders. While regional effects seem to have the
largest impact on results, they remain difficult to interpret in terms of covariance with regulatory
models, as part of the explanation for regional effects is also linked to the endogenous effect of
regulatory models. Also, further analysis could include more cultural effects, but we lack the theoretical arguments to justify incorporating dimensions other than individualistic or collectivist orientation. Finally, the use of the TALIS data base implies that the picture we present here relies on
the voice of lower secondary school teachers. Moreover, our dependent variables are based on
TALIS items and constructs, which have been criticized for their limited capacities to measure
more finely indicators of teacher professionalism such as teacher knowledge and practices (Ulferts,
2021). Also, TALIS data are only survey data, and recent research has cautioned that labor market
outcomes such as those measured in TALIS do not fully correlate with actual attrition and turnover
(Grant and Brantlinger, 2023). It could also be added that understanding the effects of regulatory
models using TALIS data is limited in two other ways. The TALIS survey is primarily designed to
provide comparable information on teachers’ initial preparation and professional development
(Tahirsylaj et al., 2022). It therefore provides more limited information on teachers’ perceptions of
their careers, their profession, and their employment conditions. It may also tend to force comparability between teacher policy contexts through concepts such as teacher satisfaction, which may
take different meanings depending on the context (Wermke and Salokangas, 2021). Despite these
limitations, however, we believe that this study paves the way for others who will seek to shed light
on the complex effects of teacher policy systems on teachers, whether they consider their effects
on more objective working and employment conditions, or on their subjective relationship to their
career and profession (see Dumay, 2025).

Author biographies
Xavier Dumay is a Professor of Education at UCLouvain, Belgium. His research interests include the globalization of education and educational policies, new-institutional theory, global politics, and changing forms of
education. He is the coeditor of the World Yearbook of Education 2025-The Teaching Professlon In a
Globalizing World: Governance, Career, Learning; the Oxford Handbook of Education and Globalization
(2023); and The Status of the Teaching Profession: Interactions between Historical and New Forms of
Segmentation (2022).
Thibault Coppe is currently Professor of Educational Sciences at the Université libre de Bruxelles, Belgium.
His research interests focus on the professional development of (beginning) teachers and comparative analyses of policies affecting lateral entrant teachers. Through his research, he has developed a strong interest in
reflecting on the use of methods in the social sciences and in discussing the nexus between research and
practice.
Annelise Voisin holds a PhD in education from the University of Montreal, Canada. She is currently Professor
of education at the University of Liege, Belgium. Her research interests focus on education policies in a multi
scalar, comparative perspective, and the teaching profession.
