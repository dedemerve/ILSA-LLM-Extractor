The cultivation of higher-order thinking skills is fundamental within the evolving landscape of
artificial intelligence–enhanced learning environments. Utilizing data from the 2018 Teaching
and Learning International Survey (TALIS), this study examines the impact of institutional factors,
including teacher education, employment status, and professional development (PD), on
instructional quality targeting higher-order cognitive skills among U.S. teachers. The results
reveal that general pedagogical training significantly strengthens instructional practices for
higher-order thinking, while an excessive emphasis on subject-specific content knowledge.
Moreover, full-time teachers exhibit greater effectiveness than their part-time counterparts in
facilitating problem-solving and knowledge transfer. PD consistently has a positive impact on
instructional quality, with informal PD showing a strong and reliable effect on improving
teaching. The findings emphasize the necessity to re-evaluate teacher education and PD programs
that address the quality of instruction for cultivating higher-order thinking skills while leveraging
individual teacher strengths.

1. Introduction
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has profoundly reshaped contemporary society, creating new economic, social,
and technological demands. As AI systems rapidly transform every facet of daily life and work, the imperative for individuals to master
advanced cognitive and technological skills becomes increasingly urgent. Educational institutions therefore face mounting pressure to
elevate instructional quality to ensure learners are adequately prepared to meet the complex demands of this evolving landscape. High
instructional quality of cultivating cognitive activation is essential for equipping students with critical thinking, problem-solving, and
adaptability skills. In this context, it is imperative to align pedagogical approaches with the complex requirements of an AI-driven
society, which are the factors that are essential for advancing instructional quality and aligning pedagogical approaches with the
complex demands of AI.
Contemporary educational reforms increasingly recognize these cognitive competencies, encompassing critical thinking, problemsolving, creativity, and information literacy, as essential for fostering a high-caliber teaching workforce (Xia et al., 2023). The
cognitive skills are not only integral to academic success but also foundational in preparing students for functioning effectively in
AI-mediated environments, where routine tasks are increasingly automated and complex, non-linear thinking becomes indispensable
(Zhai & Nehm, 2023). Consequently, higher-order thinking is now positioned at the core of pedagogical strategies designed to prepare

The emergence of higher-order thinking skills (HOTS) including analysis, evaluation, synthesis, and creative problem-solving, has
gained central importance within contemporary pedagogical paradigms. Educators increasingly embed HOTS into curricula as a
strategic response to the complex cognitive demands posed by an AI-driven society, thereby equipping learners with the analytical and
evaluative competencies essential for navigating technologically mediated environments (Bhaumik et al., 2024; Kurniawan et al.,
2024).
Understanding how to effectively foster HOTS across educational systems is essential not only for enhancing teaching quality but
also for refining institutional frameworks that support teaching and learning. As policymakers increasingly emphasize the dual responsibility of educators to promote both academic achievement and equitable learning experiences (Darling-Hammond, 2024), the
need to embed HOTS within teacher education, employment structures, and professional development initiatives has become urgent.
The emphasis on fostering HOTS reflects a strategic response to the evolving imperative not only to elevate instructional quality but
also to advance and restructure institutional systems that underpin effective teaching and learning. The development of critical
thinking, problem-solving, and creative reasoning among all learners has become a central aim within modern education systems
(Wang et al., 2023). These cognitive competencies are essential not only for academic success but also for navigating the complexities
of an increasingly digitized and AI-driven society. The integration of HOTS into curricula necessitates instructional frameworks that go
beyond content delivery, requiring educators to adopt dynamic, learner-centered approaches that actively engage students in evaluation, synthesis, and innovation (Miterianifa et al., 2021).
While the importance of HOTS in educational settings has been extensively emphasized in response to the demands of an AI-driven
society (Lee et al., 2024), there remains a critical gap in the discourse concerning how to systematically enhance teachers’ instructional
practices to support HOTS development. This study positions educators not merely as implementers of curriculum, but as central
agents in educational transformation. Teachers’ professional growth, underpinned by strategically designed institutional policies and
comprehensive development programs, plays a critical role in narrowing the gap between educational policy objectives and actual
classroom practices particularly within the context of a rapidly advancing technological environment.
Although there is broad consensus on the importance of embedding critical thinking, problem-solving, and creativity within
educational curricula (Chen et al., 2021; Lu & Xie, 2024; Melati & Rasyid, 2023; Susilowati & Suyatno, 2021; Wang et al., 2023),
practical strategies for equipping educators to effectively cultivate the cognitive competencies remain insufficiently examined. The
successful integration of HOTS into pedagogical practice is closely tied to the professional competence of teachers (Bhaumik et al.,
2024; Jansen & Möller, 2022; Kim et al., 2018; Kim, 2025; Kurniawan et al., 2024). This teaching competence for HOTS is shaped
through rigorous initial teacher education, ongoing professional development, and stable employment conditions, which are the
factors that are essential for advancing instructional quality and aligning pedagogical approaches.
This study investigates the institutional and systemic frameworks required to support teaching practices that promote students’
higher-order thinking. A notable gap in the literature is the lack of concrete operational models and detailed implementation guidance,
which has hindered both the assessment and enhancement of instructional quality. The absence of clear regulatory structures creates
uncertainty that impedes the development and application of effective measures to improve teaching performance and address
persistent disparities across educational contexts. This study aims to provide a more defined and structured framework for evaluating
and improving teacher quality and investigates how institutional support and systematic approaches can be leveraged to develop
effective teaching practices. It seeks to offer insights into how educational policies and institutional structures can be optimized to
support the cultivation of HOTS, thus contributing to the broader goal of ensuring equitable access to high-quality education for every
student.
2. Instructional quality for higher-order thinking skills
Empirical research consistently demonstrates that the quality of teaching is strongly associated with students’ academic success.
Differences in instructional quality are widely acknowledged as critical determinants that shape not only individual student outcomes
but also the broader effectiveness of educational systems (Allen & Sims, 2018; Hanushek & Rivkin, 2012; Hanushek & Woessmann,
2012; Susilowati & Suyatno, 2021). Thus, continuously evaluating and refining strategies to enhance instructional quality is vital for
promoting meaningful student learning and achievement.
Despite the recognized relationships, there remains an absence of a cohesive theoretical framework that thoroughly articulates the
underlying dimensions of instructional quality (Mu et al., 2022). Even when theoretical references are structured, they often lack
clarity and fail to explain how such theories inform the conceptualization of specific framework elements (Praetorius et al., 2018). The
concept of instructional quality remains a complex and ambiguous construct, prompting ongoing efforts by policymakers and researchers to better understand and define its true impact within the educational context (Cochran-Smith, 2021).
The emphasis on HOTS within the framework of instructional quality is driven by the urgent need to prepare students for the
complex, rapidly evolving challenges characteristic of an AI-driven society. In contemporary educational and professional contexts,
mere mastery of factual knowledge and basic comprehension is no longer sufficient. Instead, there is a growing demand for individuals
who can analyze complex information, evaluate diverse perspectives, and generate innovative solutions to novel problems. HOTS,
encompassing skills such as critical thinking, problem solving, and the transfer of knowledge to new contexts, represent essential
competencies that empower learners to engage meaningfully with dynamic technological, economic, and social environments. These
advanced cognitive processes lie at the highest levels of the hierarchy and are integral to fostering intellectual autonomy and
adaptability. By focusing on HOTS within instructional quality, this study addresses a critical gap in educational practice: the need for
pedagogical approaches that move beyond rote learning to cultivate advanced cognitive capacities. This focus ensures that

instructional strategies are aligned with the overarching goal of developing graduates who are not only knowledgeable but also

At the core of this framework is the understanding that HOTS involve complex cognitive activities, including analysis, evaluation,
and creation in Bloom’s Taxonomy (Bloom, 1956). Cognitively advanced processes support the development of learners who are
critical, reflective, and adaptive, enabling them to transfer knowledge effectively to novel and unpredictable contexts. Teachers play a
pivotal role in designing, implementing, and evaluating learning experiences that nurture these competencies. Through cognitively
activating instruction, educators encourage students to articulate, clarify, share, and critically examine their ideas, concepts, and
problem-solving approaches (Lazarides & Buchholz, 2019; Mu et al., 2022). This is accomplished by engaging students in intellectually
challenging tasks, presenting cognitive conflicts, and exposing them to diverse perspectives, interpretations, and solutions.
HOTS represent a core component of effective education, directly influencing students’ cognitive development and academic
achievements. Unlike basic cognitive functions such as recall and comprehension, higher-order thinking skills involve complex mental
operations that challenge students to analyze, evaluate, and create. The foundational framework of HOTS finds its genesis within
Bloom’s Taxonomy, an enduring paradigm that delineates cognitive processes. Bloom (1956) conceptualizes the construct of six
cognitive activities with a hierarchical order. The dimensions of the cognitive process are classified into remembering, understanding,
applying, analyzing, evaluating, and creating. The cognitive activities of creating, generating, and designing from what students have
learnt at the top in the taxonomy are considered as higher order thinking.
This study focuses on specific components of higher-order thinking skills, namely transfer of knowledge, critical thinking, and
problem solving based on Brookhart’s (2010) conceptual framework. Brookhart (2010) categorizes higher-order cognitive activities
into these distinct domains, each of which is essential for fostering advanced learning outcomes and cognitive development. Nevertheless, the boundaries between the specific domains of HOTS are not always distinctly delineated. For instance, developing students’
critical thinking abilities often involves implementing educational approaches that simultaneously cultivate their problem-solving
capacities and encourage analytical reasoning (Anggraeni et al., 2023). While this study acknowledges the overlapping nature of
HOTS, the specific domains for HOTS concentrate on knowledge transfer, critical thinking, and problem-solving.
The components of instructional quality explored in this study are introduced to provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how teaching practices can effectively promote higher-order cognitive processes and meaningful learning outcomes among
students are essential objectives of effective instruction. Cognitive activation refers to an instructional practice designed to engage
students in advanced levels of thinking, thereby facilitating the development of a deep, integrated, and elaborated knowledge base
(Lipowsky et al., 2009). In the present study, the concept of instructional quality emphasizes the promotion of knowledge transfer,
critical thinking, and problem-solving.
Knowledge transfer refers to students’ ability to apply acquired concepts, skills, and strategies to novel and unfamiliar contexts.
This capacity is fundamental for fostering adaptability and for facilitating the integration of knowledge across various disciplines and
real-world situations, which is an essential foundation for lifelong learning, continuous professional growth, and adaptability in dynamic environments. Knowledge transfer involves more than merely recalling or reproducing learned information; it requires the
flexible application of existing knowledge to address new challenges and to construct new understandings. Through reflective transfer,
students actively consider the demands and contextual changes of different situations, enabling them to adapt and extend their
knowledge base beyond its original scope (Turulja & Bajgorić, 2020). This reflective process not only consolidates prior learning but
also stimulates the generation of new knowledge, encouraging innovative thinking and deeper conceptual understanding.
Furthermore, as students engage in knowledge transfer, they modify their cognitive frameworks in response to new environmental
demands. This dynamic adjustment strengthens their openness to change, fosters positive attitudes toward new experiences, and
supports the development of flexible cognitive habits (Bernardo & Presbitero, 2018; Sengupta & Ray, 2017). By maintaining diverse
representations of information and adaptive behavioral patterns, students cultivate greater cognitive flexibility, which is critical for
navigating complex and evolving academic and professional landscapes. Importantly, effective knowledge transfer requires students to
move beyond rote memorization, encouraging them instead to analyze the relevance of knowledge in different contexts and to
creatively reconfigure it to generate novel insights.
Critical thinking represents a foundational cognitive capacity that underpins effective learning and advanced intellectual development. It involves the systematic analysis of arguments, interpretation of evidence, evaluation of claims, and the formulation of wellreasoned judgments (Anggraeni et al., 2023). Through these processes, students are empowered to engage with complex and multifaceted problems, consider diverse and competing perspectives, and arrive at informed, ethical, and contextually appropriate decisions
(Anggraeni et al., 2023). Critical thinking thus fosters intellectual autonomy and reflective thinking, all of which are essential for
navigating contemporary societal and professional demands.
The construct of critical thinking can be conceptualized across multiple dimensions. First, it encompasses reasoning skills,
emphasizing logical analysis and coherent argumentation. Second, it involves reflexive judgment, highlighting the capacity to evaluate
evidence, weigh competing arguments, and make deliberate, self-regulated decisions. Third, it includes dispositional aspects, referring
to the attitudes and temperaments that predispose individuals to approach problems with open-mindedness, intellectual curiosity, and
a willingness to revise beliefs based on evidence.
Problem solving is a cognitive process that entails the strategic application, integration, and synthesis of knowledge to address
novel and complex challenges. This capability is essential for cultivating students’ resilience, creativity, and capacity for innovative
thinking competencies that are increasingly indispensable in rapidly changing academic, professional, and societal contexts. Problem
solving involves a deliberate and systematic use of cognitive skills and strategies to analyze problems, identify underlying causes,
construct and manipulate representations, interpret data, devise actionable plans, and ultimately generate effective solutions (Lu &
Xie, 2024). Through these processes, students develop the ability to approach problems analytically and creatively, enabling them to

The cultivation of HOTS among students is critically shaped by the proactive and intentional involvement of teachers in the design,

2013). Through such deliberate instructional practices, teachers can equip students with the intellectual capacities necessary to excel
in rigorous academic environments and effectively adapt to the evolving demands of an AI-driven society.
3. Institutional structures for instructional quality
Although the previous framework signifies the critical relationship between teaching quality and the development of higher-order
thinking skills in students, it also suggests that teachers’ efforts and effectiveness is limited by a lack of adequate resources, systemic
support, and PD (Kwangmuang et al., 2021; Retnawati et al., 2018). In this study, the framework for emphasizing the institutional
structures advocates for a systematic approach that includes institutional support consisting of teacher education, employment status
of teachers, and PD. Addressing the roles of institutional pillars signals the educational systems to better overcome the barriers teachers
may face in fostering HOTS, ultimately leading to improved academic outcomes for students. This framework provides a basic for
future research and policy development aimed at enhancing teaching quality and consequently students’ cognitive development.
The role of teachers is undeniably central to the effective implementation of the learning process. However, Retnawati et al. (2018)
emphasize, the responsibility for advancing educational quality does not rest solely on the shoulders of teachers; it is a collective
endeavor that involves all educational bodies. Yet, the current state of pedagogical strategies among teachers and the integration of
higher-order thinking skills into the instructional settings remains a significant concern. The need for comprehensive strategies extends
beyond the classroom, involving institutional support. The framework in this study seeks to explore the significance of institutional
pillars, comprising teacher education, the employment status of teachers, and PD, in enhancing teaching quality. It posits that while
teachers are the direct agents of education, the surrounding institutional environment plays a foundational role in supporting
high-quality teaching practices. This framework examines how institutional efforts such as teacher training programs and PD for
teachers aimed at improving teaching quality can contribute to more effective teaching and learning, in order to foster overall
educational advancement.
The effectiveness of teaching practices and the promotion of higher-order thinking skills are profoundly influenced by key institutional pillars that regulate the teaching profession. Institutions establish rules and procedures that govern educational interactions
and practices. Helmke and Levitsky (2006) emphasize that institutions act as architects of the rules, creating frameworks that operate
both explicitly and implicitly to guide behavior within organized contexts. Institutions shape the forms and structures of interactions
by specifying rules and procedures that govern behavior. In this study, the concept of institutional structures focuses on how institutional dynamics function as pillars of order and predictability in educational settings and how they influence teaching quality
through normative and regulative mechanisms.
Institutions provide a framework of order and predictability of how organized situations create positions, how individuals enter or
leave the positions, and the actions that are required, permitted, or forbidden within the roles (Crawford & Ostrom, 1995). In the
context of teaching quality, institutional structures outline the roles of educators, the standards for instructional practices, and the
expectations for student interactions. The structures provide some frames for a stable and predictable educational environment that
supports effective teaching and learning. To be specific, DiMaggio and Powell (2010) describe how institutions generate both formal
and informal rules that form the normative and regulative pillars of societal behavior.
Teacher education for preparedness is foundational pillars in determining the efficacy of teaching practices. Comprehensive and
well-structured teacher preparation programs provide educators with both the substantive knowledge and pedagogical skills required
for effective instruction. Robust teacher education initiatives develop the capabilities to implement instructional strategies that
promote critical thinking and problem-solving (Darling-Hammond, 2024). Such preparation is substantial for enabling teachers to
engage students in complex cognitive processes and support the development of advanced cognitive skills.
The employment status of teachers including aspects such as job security, contract types, and working conditions significantly
influences their ability to maintain and enhance teaching quality. Stable and supportive employment conditions are integral to sustaining effective teaching practices (Ingersoll et al., 2019). When teachers benefit from job security and favorable working conditions,
they are better positioned to focus on pedagogical improvement and professional growth. The stability in employment status allows
educators to dedicate more effort to refining their teaching methods and implementing strategies that foster higher-order thinking to
make positively impacts on teaching quality and student cognitive engagement.
Ongoing PD is another essential pillar that shapes teaching quality. Continuous learning opportunities are substantial for keeping
educators abreast of innovation instructional methods and pedagogical advancements (Borko et al., 2014; Gore et al., 2017). Institutions that prioritize PD create environments that encourage teachers to adapt their practices and adopt new strategies to enhance

Despite the recognized importance of the institutional mechanisms, the complex intersectionality among the institutional pillars
and their actual impact on teaching quality remains partially underestimated (Voisin & Dumay, 2020). The interaction between

This study conceptualizes and specifies the certain institutional pillars of regulating teaching professions teacher education for the

These institutional aspects of regulating the teaching workforce seek to promote quality of teaching at the institutional level.
3.1. Teacher education
Teacher education as one of the institutional frames in regulating teaching workforce aims to control the teaching profession for
preparedness. If functions as a gatekeeper, vested with the responsibility of molding aspiring educators into competent and effective
teachers, capable of meeting the demands of contemporary education.
Teacher education as part of institutional structures regulating a teaching workforce ensure the establishment and maintenance of
quality benchmarks. The structures ascertain that educators undergo comprehensive training aligned with contemporary pedagogical
theories, effective instructional methods, and subject-specific expertise. Specifically, teacher education equips pre-service teachers
with the necessary competencies to navigate diverse classroom dynamics adeptly, such as the necessary knowledge, skills, and
pedagogical techniques for internalizing the teaching competencies required for effective teaching (Darling-Hammond, 2024; Dunst
et al., 2019; Harvey et al., 2010; Hollins, 2011; Yusriadi, 2021). Rigorous training ensures that teachers possess the requisite pedagogical skills, content knowledge, and classroom management strategies to effectively engage students and facilitate meaningful
learning experiences.
Teacher education in the regulating institutions is responsible for designing and implementing curricula and teacher preparation
programs that align with the education systems. It crafts educational paradigms that not only meet the demands and exigencies of the
educational landscape but also harmonize seamlessly with embroider contours and dynamics of the educational apparatus (Barnes &
Cross, 2020; Menter & Assunção Flores, 2021). In doing so, these institutions navigate the confluence of educational theory and
practice with an unwavering commitment to not only preparing proficient educators but also aligning their pedagogical strategies with
the intricate architecture of the larger educational systems. This conscientious alignment is not a mere matter of superficial adaptation
but an attempt that necessitates an understanding of the complicated dimensions of educational policies, objectives, and contextual
factors.
The education systems are in a constant status of flux, influenced by societal changes, evolving student needs, and technological
advancements. Teacher education facilitates the incorporation of innovative teaching methodologies, technological integration, and
inclusive practices. It cultivates pedagogical skills and transcending the transference of knowledge (Barnes & Cross, 2020; Darling-Hammond, 2017). Teachers are not just conveyors of knowledge but orchestrators of transformative learning experiences. Pedagogy
requires a delicate balance between creativity and evidence-based practice in that teaching is a dynamic process that necessitates
constant refinement, adaptation, and innovation. The institutional element in teacher education becomes a cornerstone for fostering
preservice teachers’ own teaching methods and realizing their impact on diverse student populations.
3.2. Employment status of teaching
Another factor of the institutional structures governing the regulation of the teaching workforce is teacher employment. Teacher
employment status functions as a regulatory institution by embodying the formalized structures and mechanisms covering the conditions under which educators are employed, retained, and progressed within the teaching professions. This institutional dimension
addresses the framework dictating career progression, tenure, and PD opportunities.
In general, institutions on teacher employment regulate that qualified and competent individuals enter the teaching workforce by
employing teachers with the requisite knowledge, skills, and pedagogical expertise in order to maintain some certain standards of
education. In particular, the regulation of teacher employment allows educational institutions to align the hiring practices with
broader educational goals and philosophies. Recruiting teachers who are aligned with the institution’s educational vision, values, and
objectives, the education systems govern the coherence in their educational practices and foster a conductive learning environment for
students.
The conceptual framework postulates that the employment status of teachers, intricately interwoven with their qualifications,
influences teaching quality. Given that stringent entry requirements and continuous PD mandates embedded within the employment
status, the institution of the employment status is related to regulating quality of the teaching workforce.
Institutional regulations pertaining to teacher employment also encloses provisions for career growth, PD, and retention strategies
(Bertron et al., 2023). One of the functions of institutions implements frameworks that support ongoing professional growth, offering

3.3. Professional development of teachers
In this study, the other element within institutions structures governing the teaching workforce is teachers’ PD. PD initiatives
ensure that educators have access to continuous learning opportunities, enabling them to enhance their pedagogical knowledge and
skills. By staying abreast of evolving educational theories, innovative teaching methodologies, and subject-specific advancements,
teachers can refine their instructional practices, ultimately improving their effectiveness in the classroom.
Teachers’ PD serve as one of the major institutions for regulating the teaching profession given its’ role of ongoing development in
shaping and maintaining the teaching workforce (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017; Gore et al., 2017; Postholm, 2012; Sancar et al.,
2021). The institution of PD for teachers emphasizes the dynamic and nature of teacher continued preparation and growth in the
education systems.
The institutional rationale for teachers’ PD signals the necessity of continuous learning and growth throughout a teacher’s career.
This ongoing process extends beyond initial teacher preparation, emphasizing that teaching is not a static profession but a dynamic and
evolving one (Darling-Hammond, 2024). Effective educators are characterized not merely by their initial qualifications but by their
ongoing commitment to acquiring new knowledge, skills, and pedagogical strategies. The perspective aligns with the notion that PD is
integral to the teaching profession and must be viewed as a lifelong endeavor rather than a finite phase (Hattie, 2010).
The paradigm shift advocated by the institutional approach redefines teaching as a dynamic that requires constant adaptation and
refinement. Educators are seen as lifelong learners whose professional journey involves regular updates to their practices in response to
new research, technological advancements, and shifts in educational standards (Borko, Jacobs, & Koellner, 2010; Sancar et al., 2021).
This approach highlights the significance of institutional pillar in providing and supporting teachers with opportunities for continuous
PD, ensuring they remain effective and responsive to the evolving needs of their students and the education system at large.
The institutional aims of teacher development are to sustain and drive education paradigms. Through teachers’ PD, teachers
become responsive to changes in curriculum, technology, pedagogy, and the diverse needs of students (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017;
Kyndt et al., 2016; Sancar et al., 2021). The nature of educational system is based on the constantly changing contexts and needs of
students, which requires educators to remain agile and receptive to transformation. Institutional support for PD becomes indispensable
to equip teachers with the tools necessary to navigate these shifts effectively by cultivating the ongoing adaptability.
The forms of PD among teachers can be categorized as formal or informal, depending on various situational factors. The factors
include the setting in which the learning occurs inside or outside institutional contexts, the learning style as reflected in the nature and
quality of the learning process, the ownership of decision-making regarding activities, and the degree of intentionality guiding the
learning experience (Boysen, 2024; Crans et al., 2021). However, the two learning forms are not binary (Billett, 2002; Kyndt et al.,
2016). Also, it is not possible to conclude what is the better way of teacher learning and what is the effective way of teacher professional competence development, as formal and informal learning exists on a continuum.
Both formal and informal learning opportunities are significant in promoting teachers’ professional competence (Kunter et al.,
2013). Formal learning and training procedures support shaping teachers’ professional knowledge and their teaching profession
(Fischer et al., 2018; Gore et al., 2017). In this regard, states often require that teachers attain an educational degree such as master’
degree and allow them to take graduate coursework by suggesting financial incentive (Hill et al., 2003). Informal learning, also, plays a
role in building up the elements of teacher professional competence, in ways of assimilation of information and evidence with
reflection and deliberative interpretation. These informal learning opportunities can involve interactions between colleagues
including conversation and feedback (Crans et al., 2021; Kyndt et al., 2016; Postholm, 2012; Shanks, 2023).
It is necessary to examine how teacher formal and informal learning opportunities associate with improving the quality of instruction, given a basic model of how teacher learning experience for PD makes a difference in student learning (Desimone, 2009;
Desimone & Garet, 2015). In order to explore the procedures of improving teaching and learning, this study investigates how the
instructional quality is related to teacher learning opportunities of PD.
4. Research questions
This study aims to explore the impact of various institutional factors on the quality of teaching, with a particular emphasis on how
different dimensions of institutional structures and supports influence teaching practices that promote critical thinking, knowledge
transfer, and problem-solving skills among students. The investigation is structured around the following research questions.
(1). To what extent are the individual characteristics of the teaching workforce associated with instructional quality?
(2). What is the relationship between the institutional aspects of teacher education programs and the quality of instruction?
(3). How does the institutional structure of teacher employment status correlate with the instruction quality?
(4). How do institutional practices related to PD for teachers relate to the quality of instruction?

5. Methods
5.1. Data and samples
This study utilizes the comprehensive dataset from the Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS) 2018, administered by
the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) (OECD, 2019). TALIS 2018 systematically collects detailed
information on teachers’ backgrounds, instructional practices, professional development, and institutional contexts across diverse
education systems worldwide (OECD, 2019).
The TALIS 2018 framework is designed around several key objectives, including policy relevance, value addition, indicator
orientation, validity, reliability, comparability, interpretability, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness (OECD, 2019). This comprehensive
design aims to equip countries with meaningful indicators to inform policy development and enhance teaching and learning environments. The survey instrument employed in TALIS 2018 encompasses multiple dimensions that address both institutional and individual levels. Specifically, it captures critical elements such as teachers’ professional characteristics, pedagogical practices,
educational backgrounds, initial teacher preparation, participation in professional development activities, instructional approaches,
self-efficacy, job satisfaction, school leadership dynamics, feedback processes, and overall school climate (OECD, 2020).
In the United States, the TALIS 2018 survey employed a stratified random sampling method to secure a representative and diverse
sample of teachers. This multi-stage sampling approach resulted in a total sample size of 2,560 teachers, with stratification criteria
including geographic location, school type, school size, and socioeconomic characteristics. To enable unbiased estimation of population parameters, the analysis utilized teacher weights included in the dataset. Furthermore, missing data were addressed using
multiple imputations, thereby enhancing the reliability and validity of the analytical outcomes.
This study utilizes teacher-reported items from the TALIS 2018 dataset to examine instructional quality and related constructs. Selfreported data may be subject to biases such as social desirability, self-enhancement, and selective recall, potentially affecting the
accuracy and objectivity of the information provided (He & Van De Vijver, 2015; Ponizovskiy et al., 2019). Despite the limitations of
using self-reported items, teacher-reported items remain a critical and appropriate data source for this study. They provide direct
access to teachers’ own perspectives on their instructional approaches, professional development experiences, and perceptions of
institutional contexts. The insights are essential for exploring the relationships outlined in the research questions, particularly those
focusing on teachers’ experiences in the instructional structures and teaching experiences.
5.2. Analytical approach
The analytic approach for this study involves several stages, beginning with the presentation of weighted descriptive statistics and
progressing to multilevel regression analyses. Initially, weighted descriptive statistics are calculated for all variables included in the
analysis. This step provides an overview of the data distribution and variability. For continuous variables, such as educational
attainment, and PD indicators, and teaching quality measures, summaries include means, standard deviations, and ranges. For categorical variables, such as gender, contract status, and teacher employment status, frequencies and percentages are presented. These
descriptive statistics are calculated after addressing missing data using multiple imputation techniques.
Missing data are addressed using multiple imputation, a robust statistical method designed to handle incomplete datasets. Multiple
imputation involves generating multiple complete datasets through imputing plausible values for missing observations based on
observed data patterns. These datasets are analyzed separately, and the results are pooled to produce estimates that account for the
uncertainty associated with missing data. This approach reduces bias and preserves the statistical power of the analysis. The rationale
for using multiple imputation is its ability to handle data that are missing at random (MAR) or even under some violations of this
assumption, ensuring that the conclusions drawn are valid and generalizable.
Following the descriptive analysis, multilevel regression models are employed to account for the hierarchical structure of the data.
This dataset includes data from 165 schools, with teachers nested within schools. Multilevel regression models adjust for this nested
structure by accounting for the clustering of teachers within schools. Standard errors are adjusted to reflect the 165 clusters defined by
the variable "idschool."
To address the specific research questions, four distinct models are utilized. Model 1 controls for individual characteristics,
including gender and educational attainment, to investigate their impact on the quality of teaching, specifically in terms of critical
thinking skills (Research Question 1). Model 2 incorporates institutional aspects of teacher education and preparedness to assess their
relationship with teaching quality (Research Question 2). Model 3 includes variables related to employment status and contract types,
examining how these factors influence teaching quality (Research Question 3). Finally, Model 4 evaluates the effect of teachers’ experiences in both formal and informal PD on teaching quality (Research Question 4).

6. Measures
6.1. Instructional quality for HOTS
The dependent variables indicative of instructional quality in fostering HOTS are conceptualized through three primary teaching
practices, which are critical thinking, knowledge transfer, and problem-solving. Critical thinking is evaluated through examining the
degree to which teachers involve students in tasks necessitating analytical and evaluative reasoning. This construct is operationalized
through a teacher-reported item stating, “I give tasks that require students to think critically.” Knowledge transfer is assessed based on the
frequency with which educators contextualize learning by linking it to real-world or work-related scenarios, as reflected in the item, “I
refer to a problem from everyday life or work.” Furthermore, teaching practices for problem-solving are gauged by the extent to which
teachers encourage students to autonomously develop strategies for addressing complex tasks. This dimension is captured through the
item, “I ask students to decide on their own procedures for solving complex tasks.”
The comparatively low Cronbach’s alpha coefficient among the three variables (α = .526) may be attributed to the multidimensional nature of the items, which appear to reflect distinct aspects of a broader pedagogical construct rather than a single, cohesive
dimension. When measurement items are conceptually divergent, the internal consistency of the scale is diminished, yielding lower
Cronbach’s alpha values (Cortina, 1993).

The three themes of instructional practices are operationalized as categorical variables, with responses ranging from 1 to 4: “Never
or almost never” (1), “Occasionally” (2), “Frequently” (3), and “Always” (4). All items used to measure the teaching practices are based
on teacher-reported data, wherein instructors reflect on their instructional strategies in the target class by responding to the prompt,
“Thinking about your teaching in the target class, how often do you do the following?” This self-reported approach provides a consistent
framework for evaluating the frequency and effectiveness of instructional methods aimed at activating higher-order thinking skills
among students.
6.2. Teacher education
Teacher education for preparedness is operationalized through several key indicators, each focusing on different aspects of
preparation. Content of subject as content preparation assesses how well teachers are prepared for teaching the content of the subjects
they are responsible for. It evaluates the extent to which teachers feel equipped to deliver subject-specific material effectively.
Pedagogy for subject, as pedagogical preparation for subject-specific instruction, measures the preparation related to pedagogical
strategies specific to the subjects taught. It gauges how well teachers are prepared to apply pedagogical techniques tailored to the
content areas they instruct. General pedagogy indicates teachers’ preparedness in general pedagogical strategies and principles. It
includes an evaluation of their overall pedagogical skills and methods applicable across various teaching contexts. Classroom practice
examines how well teachers are prepared for implementing classroom practices pertinent to the subjects they teach. It evaluates
teachers’ readiness to manage and facilitate effectiveness classroom environments and interactions.
Each of the indicators is rated on a scale from 1 to 4, where 1 represents “Not at all,” 2 indicates “Somewhat,” 3 denotes “Well,” and
4 signifies “Very well.” Responses are categorized accordingly to provide a comprehensive understanding of teachers’ preparedness in
the teacher education systems.
6.3. Employment status of teaching
In evaluating teacher employment status as the independent variables, this study considers two key dimensions, which are
employment status as a teacher and current employment status in working hours. The first dimension, employment status as a teacher,
classifies teachers based on their type of employment contract. Teachers are categorized as follows: those with permanent employment
are assigned a value of 1, while those on a fixed-term contract for more than 1 school year or a fixed-term contract for 1 school year or
less are both assigned a value of 0. This classification enables a clear differentiation between teachers holding permanent contracts and
those with fixed-term contracts within the school, providing insights into their employment stability and tenure.
The second dimension, current employment status in working hours, evaluates teachers’ working hours in relation to full-time
status. Teachers are classified into two main categories: full-time, defined as working more than 90 % of full-time hours, and parttime, which includes those working 90 % or less of full-time hours, with additional breakdowns into subcategories of 71–90 %,
50–70 %, and less than 50 % full-time hours. For analytical purposes, the working hours data has been recoded into a binary variable.
In this recoding scheme, a value of 1 represents full-time status, while a value of 0 indicates part-time status. This binary recoding
simplifies the data analysis and facilitates the examination of the relationship between employment status and working hours,

6.4. Formal and informal PD for teachers
The independent variables for measuring formal and informal PD programs of teachers are designed to capture diverse participation in various PD activities. Each variable is framed to address specific types of PD over the preceding 12 months, ensuring clarity
for respondents and accuracy in responses. The binary response format (Yes = 1, No = 0) simplifies the data collection process and
facilitates straightforward quantitative analysis, making it easier to assess participation rates across different types of PD.
In terms of formal PD, to assess involvement in traditional, face-to-face PD, the following question was posed: “During the last 12
months, did you participate in any in-person courses or seminars as part of your PD?” This variable is operationalized to capture
engagement in in-person educational activities designed to enhance professional skills and knowledge. Such activities include
workshops, seminars, and training sessions conducted in physical settings. Secondly, to evaluate participation in digital PD, the
question asked was: “During the last 12 months, did you engage in any online courses or seminars for your professional growth?” This
variable identifies involvement in online educational programs aimed at PD, including webinars, online training sessions, and elearning modules. Lastly, to measure engagement in formal qualification programs, the following question was used: “During the last
12 months, did you participate in any formal qualification programs, such as enrolling in academic courses, obtaining certifications, or
pursuing degree programs designed for PD in education?” The variable captures involvement in structured educational programs that
lead to academic degrees, certifications, or formal qualifications specifically aimed at enhancing professional competence in the field
of education.
In order to assess participation in informal PD through observation, the question asked was: “During the last 12 months, did you
engage in observation visits to other schools to learn from their teaching practices and educational approaches?” The variable records
engagement in visits to other educational institutions with the purpose of observing and learning from their teaching methods,
classroom environments, and educational strategies. In addition, to evaluate involvement in peer and self-observation and coaching,
the following question was used: “During the last 12 months, did you participate in peer and/or self-observation and coaching, which
includes observing colleagues’ teaching practices, receiving feedback from peers, or self-reflecting on your own teaching methods?”
The variable addresses activities where teachers engage in observing their peers or themselves to improve their teaching practices. This
includes receiving feedback from colleagues and engaging in self-reflection aimed at PD.

8. Discussion
8.1. The institutional impacts of teacher education on instructional quality
The findings of this study address the critical importance of teacher education in promoting high-quality instructional practices that
enhance students’ HOTS. Specifically, general pedagogy emerged as a consistently positive and significant factor in fostering critical
thinking, knowledge transfer, and problem-solving abilities among students. This suggests that comprehensive pedagogical preparation, emphasizing instructional strategies, classroom management, and student engagement techniques, shapes a foundation in
equipping teachers to facilitate cognitively demanding learning activities. In contrast, subject content knowledge demonstrated a
significant negative association with the quality of teaching for problem-solving. This counterintuitive finding suggests that an
exclusive focus on deep content expertise may not directly translate into enhanced student problem-solving skills. Rather, it highlights
potential limitations when teachers rely heavily on content mastery without effectively integrating pedagogical approaches that
promote student-centered, inquiry-based learning.
The results contribute to the ongoing debate concerning the preparation of specialists versus generalists in teacher education.
Specialists, with their in-depth subject knowledge, are often presumed to be more effective in content delivery (Phillips, 2008).
However, the negative association found in this study implies that content expertise alone may not suffice in fostering complex
cognitive skills. In contrast, generalists benefit from a broader pedagogical foundation that prioritizes flexible, adaptive instructional
strategies across subjects, potentially making them more effective in promoting higher-order thinking (Jurasaite-Harbison & Rex,
2010).
In terms of classroom practice, although existing literature strongly emphasizes the importance of practical experiences, such as
observing and engaging with effective teaching practices, as critical components in teacher education (Depaepe et al., 2018; Nissim
et al., 2016), the current study’s findings suggest a more nuanced reality.
The misalignment between previous research and this study points out the need for a re-examination of teacher education.
Cochran-Smith et al. (2017) argue that exposure to effective pedagogical methods in authentic classroom contexts enables pre-service
teachers to internalize these approaches and subsequently apply them to promote students’ cognitive development. Similarly,
Grossman et al. (2009) demonstrate that hands-on experience in diverse educational settings bridges the gap between theoretical
knowledge and practical application, thereby enhancing teachers’ capacity to foster HOTS. However, the absence of a significant
relationship between classroom practices and the development of HOTS in this study raises critical questions regarding the
12

effectiveness of current instructional strategies. This discrepancy suggests that, despite robust theoretical frameworks and practical
training, actual classroom practices may not fully integrate or operationalize strategies known to enhance higher-order cognitive
outcomes. Given that is imperative to prioritize the ongoing assessment and enhancement of teacher education programs
(Darling-Hammond, 2024; Menter et al., 2021), the results emphasize that engaging in ongoing evaluation and refinement of teacher
education programs, as well as fostering discussions about their relevance and effectiveness, will encourage a culture of continuous
improvement.
8.2. The institutional influence of employment status of teachers on instructional quality
This study examines the influence of teacher employment status on instructional quality. Existing research highlights the significant effects of different employment statuses, such as tenure-track positions versus temporary contracts, on instructional approaches
and professional engagement (Ingersoll et al., 2019). Tenure-track positions, typically characterized by greater job security and
institutional support, have been shown to foster more sustained and innovative pedagogical practices. The stability afforded by these
positions can strengthen teachers’ professional identity and encourage a deeper investment in long-term instructional goals and
continuous improvement (OECD, 2022; Sancar et al., 2021; Sutherland et al., 2010). In contrast, individuals employed under temporary or contract-based arrangements frequently experience heightened job insecurity (Guarnaccia et al., 2018). The precarious
conditions can undermine teachers’ motivation and diminish their capacity to deliver high-quality instruction consistently. Moreover,
limited institutional support and the transient nature of such contracts may impede the development of meaningful teacher-student
relationships and hinder the implementation of comprehensive, student-centered instructional strategies.
This study also reveals a noteworthy negative association between permanent employment status and the transfer of knowledge in
certain models. This finding suggests that while tenure-track or permanent positions offer job security and institutional support, they
may also introduce constraints that can affect specific aspects of teaching practice. One possible explanation is the heavier administrative and extracurricular workload often assigned to permanent teachers, which may reduce the time and energy available for
refining and innovating classroom instruction (Magtalas, 2024). These responsibilities can include committee work, mentorship obligations, and institutional service duties, which, although valuable, may divert focus away from direct instructional improvement and
individualized student support (Jerrim & Sims, 2021; Magtalas, 2024).
The findings indicate that merely securing stable employment conditions does not automatically translate into enhanced
instructional practices aimed at developing students’ analytical, evaluative, and creative skills. Instead, it underscores the importance
of creating comprehensive institutional environments that not only offer job security but also actively support teachers.
Given the absence of a significant relationship between employment status and instructional quality for HOTS, future research and
policy initiatives should prioritize the development of supportive institutional practices that provide all educators considering their
employment arrangements with the necessary resources and professional learning opportunities to effectively promote HOTS in
diverse classroom settings.
8.3. The roles of teachers’ PD for instructional quality
PD is widely recognized as a foundational mechanism for enhancing teachers’ competencies and, consequently, improving the
quality of teaching (Blömeke et al., 2022; Darling-Hammond, 2024). The findings of this study move toward the differential yet
complementary roles of formal and informal PD in various dimensions of student learning, including critical thinking, knowledge
transfer, and problem-solving abilities.
Formal PD, typified by structured programs such as advanced degree courses and certification programs, is notably associated with
enhancing students’ capacity for knowledge transfer. Through formal PD, teachers acquire specialized content knowledge and
evidence-based pedagogical approaches that facilitate the effective translation of theoretical concepts into practical understanding
among students (Postholm, 2012; Sancar et al., 2021). These structured learning opportunities provide teachers with frameworks that
support explicit instruction, systematic scaffolding, and contextualized application of knowledge, strengthening students’ ability to
transfer learned concepts across varied academic and real-world contexts.
Informal PD, which encompasses self-directed learning, collegial interactions, and non-structured reflective activities, has
demonstrated a significant association with the development of students’ critical thinking skills. Teachers engaging in informal PD are
more inclined to adopt innovative instructional strategies, encourage open-ended inquiry, and foster a classroom culture that prioritizes analytical reasoning and independent thought (Abramenka-Lachheb et al., 2021; Shanks, 2023). This learner-centered orientation directly cultivates students’ capacity to evaluate information critically, pose questions, and engage deeply with complex
problems.
Crucially, both formal and informal PD are significantly related to fostering students’ problem-solving skills, which are central
indicators of instructional quality. Effective problem-solving instruction requires teachers to integrate robust content knowledge with
adaptive teaching practices that respond to students’ evolving needs (Tawfik et al., 2021). Formal PD equips teachers with theoretical
and methodological foundations, while informal PD cultivates flexibility, creativity, and responsiveness in instructional delivery. The
complementary integration of these PD modalities enables teachers to design learning experiences that promote analytical reasoning,
strategic thinking, and collaborative problem-solving among students, thereby enhancing overall instructional effectiveness.

8.4. Teacher characteristics and instructional quality
Teacher characteristics matters. Teachers are among the most essential components within any school context, as effective teaching
serves as a catalyst for school improvement (Ko et al., 2024). In particular, teachers’ individual characteristics play a substantial role.
(Burroughs et al., 2019; Wayne & Youngs, 2003). The findings of the present study suggest that female teachers are more likely to
encourage and foster critical thinking among students. This tendency may be attributed to differences in teaching implementation
(Marpa, 2020) and distinct instructional attitudes and priorities (Amjad, 2023). The results align with previous studies indicating that
teacher gender serves as a critical predictor of instructional approaches and classroom practices (Amjad, 2023; Gremmen et al., 2016;
Martin & Sass, 2010; Marpa, 2020; Wong et al., 2015).
The results of the present study also demonstrate that teachers’ educational attainment has a significant effect on instructional
quality, especially in fostering critical thinking and problem-solving skills among students. Previous research has similarly demonstrated that cumulative teacher qualification indices are positively associated with students’ attainment of higher educational degrees
(Lee & Lee, 2020; Lee & Mamerow, 2019).
The findings of the present study reinforce the argument that teacher characteristics are critical determinants of instructional
quality; however, their effects manifest differentially across various instructional domains. This nuanced perspective underscores the
complexity of teaching effectiveness and highlights the need to move beyond a one-dimensional understanding of teacher attributes.
Recognizing and leveraging these differential effects are essential for designing effective teacher development initiatives and ultimately improving student learning across diverse educational contexts.
9. Limitations
A key limitation of this study is its reliance on teacher-reported data to assess the development of students’ HOTS. While self-reports
can provide valuable perspectives on teachers’ instructional practices, they are subject to biases stemming from subjective interpretations and individual variations in self-perception (He & Van De Vijver, 2015; Ponizovskiy et al., 2019). Such biases may affect
the accuracy and completeness of the data in capturing the extent to which students’ cognitive activities related to higher-order
thinking are actually activated. To address these concerns, future research should incorporate a wider range of assessment tools
beyond self-reported measures. Performance-based assessments, student portfolios, and systematic classroom observations offer more
objective and comprehensive evaluations of higher-order thinking skills (Wagner et al., 2016). Including students’ perspectives and
experiences could also provide deeper insights into how HOTS are fostered in practice.
Another limitation concerns the methodological focus on selected aspects of HOTS, which may not fully capture the complex and
multifaceted nature of these cognitive abilities. Higher-order thinking encompasses a broad range of skills, including analysis, synthesis, evaluation, and creative problem-solving. By focusing narrowly on specific variables, this study may have overlooked other
critical dimensions of these skills. Future research should develop more comprehensive assessment frameworks that reflect the full
spectrum of higher-order thinking. Expanding the scope of investigation in this manner would not only strengthen the validity of the
findings but also provide a more comprehensive understanding of how HOTS are cultivated and manifested in varying learning
environments.
10. Conclusions
This study advances the understanding of how institutional structure mechanisms critically shape instructional quality and,
consequently, student cognitive outcomes. The findings demonstrate that teacher education, employment status, and participation in
PD exert significant influences on enhancing instructional quality, in fostering students’ critical thinking, knowledge transfer, and
problem-solving abilities. Moreover, this study addresses the importance of teacher characteristics, notably gender-related differences
in instructional approaches. Female teachers are found to be more inclined to promote critical thinking, highlighting the need to
consider individual teacher attributes alongside broader institutional factors.
The findings in this study highlight the imperative of integrating teacher-specific characteristics with systemic institutional supports to effectively elevate instructional practices and optimize student learning. Specifically, this study emphasizes the need for reevaluation teacher education and PD programs that address specific instructional domains and leverage teacher characteristics to
optimize student learning.
