Abstract
Research evidence is mixed on the consequences
of ability grouping policies, but most research has
found an overrepresentation of disadvantaged
social demographics in low-­ability groups. However,
researchers have neglected to explain why ability
grouping policies vary between countries. Departing
from welfare regime theory, our study aimed to
explain schools’ ability grouping polices. For data, we
used the principal survey from PISA 2022 sampling
approximately 9000 schools in 34 countries. As
the explanatory variable, we used welfare regimes,
and as the outcome, we analysed schools’ ability
grouping policy (a) within the school or (b) within the
class. For analysis, we reported descriptive statistics,
correspondence analysis and multilevel ordinal
regression (adjusting for school demographics). In
support of our predictions, we found large differences
in ability grouping policies within school between
welfare regimes (social democratic welfare regime vs.
liberal regime). For ability grouping within the class,
we found only meagre differences between social
democratic regime compared with conservative
regime. In contrast to our predictions, ability grouping
within the class seems to be driven by school
demographics rather than welfare regimes. As a
sensitivity check, we compared welfare regimes with
production regimes as in Varieties of Capitalism (VoC
or production regime theory; Liberal vs. Coordinated

Key insights
What is the main issue that the paper addresses?
This study presents an inquiry into the origins of schools’ ability grouping policies
(between or within classes). Specifically, the study seeks to explain how ability
grouping policies in schools vary between countries based on two explanations:
welfare regimes and production regimes.
What are the main insights that the paper provides?
Welfare regimes (social democratic vs. conservative) account for country differences
in schools’ ability grouping policies between classes. Production regimes (liberalvs
vs. coordinated market economies) account for similar results. However, school
demographics account for variation in ability grouping policies within classes.

I NTRO DUCTI O N
All educational systems need to respond to student population diversity. Students enter
schools with different demographics that correlate with differences in educational
achievements (e.g. test scores, grades and assessments). One response to student diversity
has been organisational (i.e. dividing students into ability groups; Jerrim et al., 2024). The
terminology for such division of students varies across countries: tracking, educational
stratification, ability grouping, sorting, streaming, setting, banding or differentiation (Reichelt
et al., 2019). In this article, we use the term ability grouping. Even though ability grouping
varies between countries, it almost seems like universal practice (as a social fact). More
widely, ability grouping started in the 1960s when compulsory education began to be divided
up between academic/vocational streams and special education (Rix & Ingham, 2021).
Ability grouping involves offering students different curricula, as well as assigning students to different educational tracks (e.g. vocational and academic tracks); for example,
some students choose or are selected into academically more demanding programmes,
which focus on the general skills required for postsecondary education, whereas other students choose or are selected for vocational or technical programmes, which focus on the
practical skills useful in the labour market (Reichelt et al., 2019; Solga et al., 2014).

Ability grouping also involves offering students different levels within the same school
(e.g. comprehensive schools) and at different ages. Ability grouping can occur (a) within
class, (b) between classes within the same school or (c) between schools within the same
country (Organisation for Economic Co-­operation and Development [OECD], 2023, p. 127).
There is a wealth of research on the consequences of ability grouping and who are placed
there (Jerrim et al., 2024; McGillicuddy, 2021; Rix & Ingham, 2021; Spina, 2019). However,
instead of asking questions about (a) the placement in or (b) the consequences of ability
grouping, we propose a novel research problem and thus identify a research gap.
This study presents an inquiry into the origins of ability grouping (i.e. the precursors of
ability grouping in schools). Specifically, why do ability grouping policies in schools vary between countries (i.e. the research gap). Therefore, we shift the unit of analyses focus from
the individual to schools and countries.
To address this research problem and fill the knowledge gap, we propose shifting the attention from individuals to schools and countries. Inspired by political sociology, we estimate
and explain the relevance of the type of welfare state (i.e. welfare regime) to inter-­country variation in ability grouping in schools (i.e. the research contribution; Esping-­Andersen, 1990).
However, we recognise that welfare regimes have been challenged by alternative explanations at the country level such as Varieties of Capitalism (VoC aka production regime
theory; Graf, 2009; Hall & Soskice, 2001). Varieties of Capitalism asserts the importance of
production regimes (i.e. type of market economies) to explain educational polices, including
ability grouping. Therefore, we supplement our welfare regime analysis by assessing the
predictive importance of production regimes.
We argue that welfare regimes offer a partial answer to the problem. However, production regimes (VoC) offer a supplementary explanation (i.e. complementary and not rivalry). Having outlined the gap, contribution and argument, we state our aim and research
questions.

Aim and research questions
Departing from Esping-­Andersen's (1990) welfare regime theory, this study aims to explain
schools’ ability grouping policies (a) within school into different classes and (b) within the
classes in 34 countries using data school survey from the Programme for International
Student Assessment (PISA) 2022 data (n ≈ 9000). Our research questions are as follows:
RQ 1: How do schools’ ability grouping policies into different classes within the school
vary by welfare regime?
RQ 2: How do schools’ ability grouping policies within the classes vary by welfare regime?
As an alternative explanation to welfare regimes, we consider VoC (i.e. production regime
theory). Therefore, we pose a third research question:
RQ 3: To what extent does VoC's production regimes (Liberal vs. Coordinated Market
Economy) offer an alternative explanation of schools’ ability grouping policies (a) between and (b) within classes?

Ability grouping: research on the positive and negative consequences
Research evidence on the consequences of ability grouping is mixed. Although some researchers (the proponents) have shown that ability grouping has a positive impact, others
have found the opposite (the sceptics). Ability grouping proponents emphasise the benefits
for both low-­and high-­achieving students.

First, ability grouping may be beneficial from a teacher's perspective. Homogeneous
classes reduce teachers’ workload, are generally easier to teach, allow teachers to focus
on developing and preparing a single lesson, and allow all students to use the same
teaching material (Taylor et al., 2017). In mixed-­ability classes, teachers need to prepare
and deliver material for students at rather different levels in classes. This means a much
greater workload for the teacher, and it impacts teachers’ capacity to prepare lessons
and may in turn impact their capacity to support students at the top and bottom ends of
the class. Therefore, ability grouping gives teachers the opportunity to challenge high-achieving students and to give more support to low-­achieving students owing to reduced
workload (Hornby & Witte, 2014; Slavin, 1990). In addition, teachers can also use ability
grouping as a flexible practice (i.e. allocate students in/out of ability groups). According to
proponents, flexible ability grouping is easier to implement in the early stages of education
(Matthews et al., 2013).
Second, ability grouping may be beneficial from a student's perspective. For example, low-­ability students can work in a slower pace with less challenging tasks, whereas
high-­ability grouping students can work at a higher pace with more challenging tasks,
and the latter will not be disrupted by their low-­achieving peers (Bäckström, 2023; Jerrim
et al., 2024).
In contrast, the critics underscore the negative consequences of ability grouping from
a student's perspective. First, the critics of ability grouping suggest that, in homogeneous
classes, students have greater opportunities to share learning experiences with higher-achieving peers, and the latter may act as role models (Hornby & Witte, 2014; Slavin, 1990).
The second criticism concerns the labelling and stigmatising consequences of ability
grouping—that is, being labelled a ‘low achiever’ and thus causing these students feel ‘stigmatised’ and aware of their weak skills, which they then internalise (Hornby & Witte, 2014;
McGillicuddy & Devine, 2020). Once labelled and stigmatised in early education (e.g.
primary education), students get stuck in low-­ability groups, particularly within class (in
contrast to not placing them in any group; Dunne et al., 2011). Therefore, labels and stigmas deprive students from enjoying school subjects during primary education (Boliver &
Capsada-­Munsech, 2021; i.e. students become ‘alienated’). This alienation mechanism of
ability grouping may in turn explain the correlation between socioeconomic disadvantage
and inequalities in educational achievements and thus life chances.
Subsequently, alienation leads to student disengagement. Therefore, early ability grouping practices reinforce student behavioural disengagement in the classroom (Van Houtte
& Stevens, 2015). Although causality may be bidirectional, ability grouping may be used to
penalise behavioural disengagement and thus reinforce labels and stigmas.
Concerning the third criticism, socioeconomically disadvantaged students have been
overrepresented in the placement in ability grouping, which in turn has unintended consequences. Examples include segregation according to students’ race and backgrounds,
restricted educational opportunities for students in low-­ability classes, and limited postsecondary pathways (Johnston & Taylor, 2023; McGillicuddy, 2021; Rix & Ingham, 2021;
Spina, 2019). For socioeconomically disadvantaged students, sorting and grouping processes tend to be socioeconomically, not just academically, selective. Separating students
according to their ability results in racial, ethnic and class segregation, which reinforce
stigmas and labels. Gifted programmes, selective schools and high-­ability tracks typically
serve students from privileged backgrounds (Mickelson & Everett, 2008; Solorzano &
Ornelas, 2002). Minority students and those from low-­income families are overrepresented
in low-­ability tracks and special education (Cipriano-­Walter, 2015). Therefore, educators
stigmatise and label Black, Brown and economically disadvantaged students who may fall
behind academically for a variety of socioeconomic reasons, such as low expectations
(Jaremus et al., 2020; Spina, 2019).

However, empirical evidence for the (a) positive or (b) negative consequences of ability
has been mixed to negligible. In addition, researchers seldom address between-­country
variation (i.e. comparative).
Using cross-­national comparative data, TIMSS 2015, Jerrim et al. (2024) reported a null
result. The researchers investigated the association between class ability grouping and student outcomes. No evidence was found that either ability grouping or mixed-­ability grouping
is favourable in developing students’ self-­confidence, enjoyment or test scores. The researchers concluded that students with a socioeconomic disadvantage have been overrepresented in ability grouping; however, the negative consequences of ability grouping have
not been empirically supported in most studies.
Collectively, these findings (a null correlation) agree with previous studies. Researchers
have consistently reported a null or negligible correlation between ability grouping and educational achievement. Therefore, the ‘problem’ with ability grouping concerns overrepresentation and not (necessarily) achievements and self-­confidence. From a policy perspective,
researchers discourage ability grouping owing to (a) concerns about overrepresentation and
(b) the lack of support for favourable outcomes compared with mixed-­ability grouping (Jerrim
et al., 2024; see also Boaler, 2014; Slavin, 1990).
Summing up, we know that research on the consequences of ability grouping is mixed to
negligible (i.e. unresolved). However, researchers have consistently reported the misplacement of socioeconomically disadvantaged students (especially among ethnic minorities).
The correlation between social demographics and ability grouping underscores the policy
issue from a social justice perspective and therefore the need to study where ability grouping policies come from.
However, only a limited amount of research has attempted to explain the emergence
of ability grouping in the first place. Existing research on ability grouping has focused either on consequences of schools’ ability grouping for individuals or the correlation between
school demographics and ability grouping. In addition, most research on ability grouping has
been conducted based on single countries (e.g. Johnston & Taylor, 2023; see also Artiles
et al., 2005). Therefore, comparative research may offer new insights.
Taken together, researchers have neglected the opportunity to account for the importance of country-­level explanation. In other words, research has neglected ‘macro-­level’
explanations such as differences in country policies based on countries’ history and norms.
Therefore, researchers need to shift focus from the individual to the school level, as well as
from the school to the national level, which is a change of perspective from demographic to
institutional explanations, such as types of welfare state regimes or its alternative production
regimes.

Theory of welfare regimes
The theory of welfare regimes refers to various social policies found in most developed
democratic countries. The welfare state modifies the impact of the market by providing
some minimum guarantee (mitigating poverty), covering a range of social risks (security)
and providing certain services (e.g. healthcare or child and elder care) at the best standards
available. Welfare states create social norms regarding good education. Consequently,
welfare educational arrangements enable opportunities for all students to achieve curriculum
goals.
Welfare states differ with respect to the level of ambition and the mix between these
aspects. Esping-­Andersen's classification is based on two studies published in 1990 and
1999. Esping-­Andersen's (1990) typology distinguishes between three basic models of welfare state regimes. The social democratic model has high levels of gross domestic product

type). Welfare provision is characterised by universal and comparatively generous benefits,
a commitment to full employment and income protection, and a strongly interventionist state
used to promote equality through a redistributive social security system. Social democratic
welfare states give priority to the state in order to reduce the dependency of individuals on
both market and family, thus favouring equality (Esping-­Andersen, 2009).
The liberal welfare regime favours market-­based solutions. The state provides minimal
welfare or subsidises private welfare schemes. The benefits are modest and often involve
strict entitlement criteria. These benefits are usually means-­tested and typically associated
with a welfare stigma.
The conservative welfare regime favours social rights based on status differences (e.g.,
hierarchical) and thus tolerates inequalities. Instead, corporatism serves as an organizing
principle (i.e., cooperation between state, employers and workers). In addition, the family
and church play an important role to promote social cohesion (e.g., Christian democratic
policies based on subsidiarity, preserving traditional family roles).
Finally, Esping-­Andersen (1990) classified nations according to their predominant regime
properties, even though they might have some properties of other welfare regime types.
Scandinavian countries may be predominantly social democratic, but they are not free from
liberal and conservative elements. Neither are the liberal and conservative pure regime
types. Both have social democratic impulses.

Education in the welfare regime
Education is regarded as an integral part of the welfare state, and different welfare state
regimes are expected to frame and influence the general direction of educational politics
in different ways. Researchers have seldom studied the intersection between education
and the welfare state (Niedlich, 2021). Welfare states and educational systems share
several similarities. Although educational systems and welfare states coevolve, educational
systems have their own unique histories that contribute to their peculiarities. Let us give
some examples:
The social democratic welfare regime underscores the importance of policies for early
intervention—for example, preschool every weekday from early morning to evening and
preschool staff that are highly qualified to ensure a strong start for all children and preparedness for school. Therefore, investing in children will yield returns for the individual's life
chances and for society at large. Esping-­Andersen et al. (2012) highlighted that childcare
and early education policies may not only raise average achievement levels but may be of
special benefit for less-­advantaged children, particularly if high-­quality programmes with
high-­quality staff are provided. The positive effects persist beyond schooling into adulthood.
Conservative and liberal welfare states also have a tradition of childcare, including preschools. However, the service remains limited to mornings or a few days a week
(Esping-­Andersen, 2009).
In the social democratic welfare regime, polarisation—skills dispersion—is also minimised (e.g. Finland; Esping-­A ndersen, 2009) and intervention is initiated throughout the
life course (e.g. adult education). Lifelong learning is a central issue in welfare states regimes according to Esping-­A ndersen (1999): ‘skills are the single best source of escape
from underprivilege’ (p. 183). Moreover, the social democratic welfare regime is characterised by low stratification, whereas the liberal and conservative welfare regimes have

prevalent in the social democratic regime compared with other welfare regimes.
H2. Ability grouping policies within the class are more prevalent in the social
democratic welfare regime compared with other welfare regimes.
However, the morals (social values and social norms) of the welfare regimes stabilise over
time and may explain the durability of welfare politics regardless of the ruling party. Thus,
the social policies of the social democratic welfare regime do not vanish whenever social
democratic parties lose an election.

The Esping-­Andersen typology–country selection and regime-­type
classification
Esping-Andersen's classification of welfare regimes has been challenged by several
researchers. Critics claim that Esping-­Andersen's welfare regime typology oversimplifies
by neglecting complexities and nuances between countries. Common criticism includes
overlapping classifications of countries (e.g. the liberal and conservative typology) such
as Japan. Another source of criticism converses the need to expand the number of regime
types, such as (a) split the conservative welfare types into two—Southern and Conservative
types (Ferreira, 2008); and add (a) Eastern and Central European countries (e.g. post-communist regimes; Aidukaite, 2011; Fenger, 2007; Golinowska, 2009). The latter criticism
can be motivated by historical legacies such as the fall of the Berlin Wall and the historical
work of Mediterranean countries.
In summary, even with these sources of criticism, Esping-­Andersen's classification is
accepted as valid in most of the studies carried out in this field (Güler, 2019). Disagreement
only exists concerning additional types (e.g. Southern and Central and Eastern European
countries). Despite this criticism, we adopt the research strategy of Esping-­Andersen (1990,

Alternative explanations: Comparing welfare regime theory to
Varieties of Capitalism
Welfare regime theory has been challenged by alternative theories to explain educational
outcomes. Here, we briefly discuss the main alternative explanation, Varieties of Capitalism
(i.e. production regime theory) (Hall & Soskice, 2001).
Varieties of Capitalism originated in political science and focuses on governance of the
economy (read: ‘efficiency’). It outlines two regime types (aka production regimes): Liberal
Market Economy (LME) vs. Coordinated Market Economy (CME) and not three (or four)
types as in welfare regime theory. Therefore, basically, LME and liberal welfare regimes
overlap, whereas CME subsumes the remaining regime types in welfare regime theory
(Esping-­Andersen, 1990).
According to VoC, CME and LME influence firms and schools owing to shared histories
of economies. Owing to their shared histories, firms and schools have developed a shared
understanding (i.e. policies; e.g. ability grouping in schools). Let us briefly outline the distinguishing features of LME and CME (Hall & Soskice, 2001).
In LME, market competition dominates, and firms and schools compete on the liberal
market. Firms and schools compete with one another. Negotiations between firms and labour unions (collective bargaining) display low coordination and are decentralised. Conflicts
between labour unions and firms occur regularly. Therefore, firms and unions only agree on
short-­term deals but seldom agree on long-­term ones. The lack of coordination also characterises the educational system. LME emphasises general skills as opposed to vocational
skills or firm apprenticeship. Therefore, the firms’ demand for ability grouping in schools
diminishes (Graf, 2009; Hall & Soskice, 2001).
In CME, firms and schools coordinate together with labour unions and their government.
Firms organise into employer associations. Negotiations between firms and labour unions
(collective bargaining) display high coordination and are centralised. Labour unions and
firms cooperate and engage in agreement with firms (e.g. Japan, Sweden and Germany).
Long-­term deals between unions and firms make firms willing to invest in workers’ training
or apprenticeship. Therefore, the educational systems display a high degree of coordination
with firms. In addition to firms, labour unions encourage vocational skills and apprenticeship
to ensure the long-­term employability of citizens. Therefore, schools promote vocational
specific skills to match firms’ skill demand. Subsequently, schools often combine vocational training and apprenticeship with ability grouping, such as ‘tracking’ (e.g. Germany;
Graf, 2009; Hall & Soskice, 2001).
However, proponents of welfare regime theory have critiqued VoC (Korpi, 2006). First,
VoC overemphasises coordination and underemphasises conflicts. Therefore, VoC outlines
an employer-­centred theory that neglects the power struggle between employers and workers. Second, welfare regime theory and VoC overlap. To reiterate, welfare regimes overlap
with labour markets (Esping-­Andersen, 1990, 1999; Kolberg & Esping-­Andersen, 1991).

Third, in social democratic regimes, coordination depends on norms of fairness owing to

citizens (e.g. high-­quality preschools, adult education and retraining programmes—aka active labour market polices). Highly skilled workers ensure employability over the life course
(i.e. increased opportunities), which reduces risks (e.g. layoffs and offshoring of companies).
For example, vocational training occurs later in the educational system, often in combination

We agree with the criticism of VoC (Korpi, 2006). However, despite the criticism, VoC has
a unique advantage, as it offers a nuanced explanation of the importance of the educational
system compared with welfare regime theory (Graf, 2009). Based on VoC, we hypothesise
that schools’ ability grouping policies depend on the production regime as an alternative
explanation:
H3. In CME, schools have a higher probability of endorsing ability grouping
policies than in LME.
This is because tracking aligns and reinforces CME's demand for vocational skills and
apprenticeships. Therefore, we consider VoC (Graf, 2009; Hall & Soskice, 2001) as an alternative explanation that supplements welfare regime theory (Esping-­Andersen, 1990).

We organised the Methods section into three parts. First, we describe and justify the data
(PISA school survey). Thereafter, we continue by outlining the selection of outcome and
explanatory variables as well as adjusting variables (school demographics). Finally, we
discuss and justify the strategies for data analyses.

Data
For our study, we used the PISA 2022 data supplied by the OECD (2022, 2023). The PISA
2022 study used a complex random sample to administrate test scores (e.g. reading, mathematics and science). Beyond administrating test scores, PISA has collected survey data on
15-­ and 16-­year- ­old students,1 parents (or guardians) and schools (principals). Of the three
surveys, we focused on the school survey, which is based on the principal reports. This
means that the school survey used the school as the unit of analysis based on principals’
responses.
We focused on the school survey for several reasons. First, the PISA school survey enabled us to compare countries by welfare regimes using high-­quality data. Second, the PISA
school survey contains two variables on ability grouping based on the school policy. Third,
the relevant unit of analysis for our research problem concerned countries and schools, not
students or their parents. Fourth, schools have been an under-­researched unit of analysis
in educational research (often treated as ‘context’ and thus an explanatory variable); however, we insist that schools’ ability grouping policies constitute a ‘social fact’ and thus can
be examined as an ‘emergent’ outcome variable (in agreement with sociology of education;
OECD, 2022, 2023).

Data analyses
First, we analysed the data using descriptive statistics such as bar plots of conditional
proportions/percentages (Agresti, 2010). To supplement the bar plots, we also used simple
correspondence analyses (Greenacre, 2017). Briefly, simple correspondence analyses
visualise the frequency of co-­occurring categorical responses based on their chi-­square
differences (Person's residuals; Greenacre, 2017).
Second, we estimated a series of regression models. For simplicity, we treated the outcome variable as ordinal (i.e. order categories and thus assumed unequal scale steps).
Thus, we opted for an ordinal regression model (Agresti, 2010; Christensen, 2018). Ordinal
regression models estimate ‘log odds ratio’ and a series of ‘cut points’. These estimated
parameters may be transformed into estimated probabilities of a specific response option.
The estimated probabilities have a simpler interpretation than the average of the 1–3 scale
(Agresti, 2010). Specifically, we may interpret differences of more than 10 percentage points
as substantive.
However, to account for the fact that schools have been clustered into countries, we
allowed the intercepts to vary by country (aka ‘intercept as outcome’). Allowing for varying
intercepts implies a multilevel ordinal model (Agresti, 2010; Christensen, 2018): schools at
Level 1 and countries at Level 2. We included welfare regimes at Level 2 (country) and
school demographics at Level 1.
In agreement with the guidelines of the American Statistical Association (Wasserstein &
Lazar, 2016), our analyses emphasise the strength of the associations and their uncertainty
(e.g. confidence intervals and standard errors). Such guidelines agree with what educational researchers for a long time have referred to as ‘practical significance’ (Kirk, 1996)
or ‘indexes of magnitude’ (Cohen, 2013). Thus, we de-­emphasise ‘statistical significance’

Sensitivity check of alternative explanations: comparing welfare
regime theory to VoC
Here, we wish to compare welfare regime theory against its alternative explanation, VoC.
Therefore, we conducted a sensitivity check in this subsection.
To test for alternative explanations, we re-­estimated the models using a dummy variable
for VoC. We coded the dummy as LME vs. CME.
To compare models, we used Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information
criterion (BIC). AIC favours predictive accuracy and tolerates more complex models. BIC favours predictive simplicity and thus penalises complex models. We focused on AIC because
we embraced complex models (‘rich parameterisation’ as analogous to ‘thick descriptions’).
Therefore, AIC has greater correspondence with sociological theory and educational research.
BIC also assumes that we know the true model, which we seldom do (Weakliem, 2016).
However, we did not fit the Welfare regimes and the VoC into the same model. Theoretically,
VoC and welfare regimes represent two alternative explanations. Therefore, the two theories
correspond to two different models (welfare vs. VoC) per response variable (i.e. ability grouping
between vs. within the class). Statistically, fitting welfare regimes and the VoC variable in the
same model would result in perfectly correlated predictors (owing to overlap). Perfectly correlated predictors results cannot be estimated with classical regression models (the inverse of
the predictor matrix does not exist), which violates the assumptions of the regression model.
When comparing the two models in Table 4, welfare regime theory and VoC result in
similar stories about the data. Similar to welfare regime theory, the VoC models predict that
schools in LME less frequently endorse ability grouping between classes within the school
than schools in CME, accounting for other predictors in the model.
Inspecting Table 4, we interpret the model for ability grouping between classes as follows.
Accounting for school predictors, the model estimated an average negative difference between
LME vs. CME of 85% in the odds for endorsing the highest response option, compared with
two other response options. However, we cannot compare the size of coefficient between logit
models. Therefore, we interpret the difference in probability terms by turning to Figure 4.
In probability terms, we found that schools in LME have a lower probability (an estimated
average of 44 percentage points) of responding to the highest option of ability grouping
between classes compared with schools in CME, after adjustments. This difference in estimated probabilities is large.
The difference between LME and CME concerns the statistical significance for ability
grouping between classes. However, the VoC model does not predict a statistically significant difference between LME and CME regarding ability grouping within the class. Therefore,
H3 holds for ability grouping between classes but not for within classes. However, we cannot
judge a statistical model based on statistically significant coefficients.
Instead, we should consider the model fit to compare the theories’ predictive accuracy.
In Table 5, we inspect the AIC and BIC. Lower values indicate better fit of the model, either
with the data (BIC) or with ‘new’ data (AIC). The AIC suggests that the models have about
the same predictive accuracy (i.e. no difference). We interpret the result as follows: that the
two explanations would perform about the same if tested on a new data set. In other words,
the two theories both have merits. Although we favour welfare regime theory, we cannot rule
out VoC as an explanation for between classes within the school.

D I SCUSS I O N A ND CO NCLUS I O NS
In this section, we outline the conclusions. Thereafter, we discuss the significance of the
contribution and its implications for educational research. Finally, we address the study's
limitations and the possibilities for further research.

Conclusions
For our purposes, we used the PISA 2022 school data (principal questionnaire) from the
OCED to analyse roughly 9000 schools in 34 countries. We analysed schools’ policies for
ability grouping to different classes within the school and within the classroom. Our study
yielded the following conclusions as answers to our research questions:
1. Ability grouping to different classes within school occurs at a lower estimated probability in schools in a social democratic welfare regime than in schools in a liberal
welfare regime. These findings remain robust after we adjust for school demographics.
Again, we find support for our theoretical expectations (H1).
2. Ability grouping policies within class seem to be more related to school demographics
than to welfare regimes. Specifically, socioeconomic composition and special needs of
the school's student population seem to be more reliable than those of welfare regimes.
Only after we adjust for school demographics does ability grouping within class occur at
a higher estimated probability in schools in the social democratic welfare regime than
in schools in conservative welfare regimes. A similar result holds for the liberal vs. the
conservative welfare regime (H2).
3. Similar to welfare regimes, schools in LME had a substantively lower probability of endorsing ability group policies between classes than CME. This result supported hypothesis H3
regarding schools’ ability grouping between classes. However, similar to welfare regimes,
production regimes do not explain within-­class ability grouping. Therefore, VoC offers an
alternative explanation of a school's ability grouping between classes but not within classes.

& Taylor, 2023; Rix & Ingham, 2021; Slavin, 1990; Steenbergen-­Hu et al., 2016). On the one

Theoretical and empirical implications of welfare regimes theory for
educational research
We now turn to our findings’ implications for educational research in general as well as
special needs education, sociology of education and political sociology. As a theoretical
implication, we show that our results support the theory of welfare regimes (aka the ‘power
thesis’, Esping-­Andersen, 1990). Compared with other welfare regimes, social democratic
welfare regimes encourage social policies that provide equal life chances to all children by
offering all children equal educational opportunities.
Welfare regimes’ importance extends beyond the political economy to its moral foundation,
illustrated by the social democratic moral ethos: education for all, in other words, equal life
chances for all citizens by redistribution (Svallfors, 2013). Avoiding ability grouping implies that
all children gain access and exposure to the same (equal) knowledge. Therefore, children of all
social classes gain the same chances, access and exposure early in the life course. Equal access to knowledge means an investment in children in the early life course, which gives them
opportunities and protection from risk over the life course. For example, improved life chances
strengthen children's capabilities for, for example, life-­long learning re-­education, re-­training,
cultivation, etc., thus helping them cope with risks (e.g. risks of unemployment, mental health
issues) over the life course (Esping-­Andersen, 1990, 2009; Esping-­Andersen et al., 2012).
As an empirical implication, our study expands the scope of application of welfare
regimes within political sociology to the intersection of educational research, special
education needs and sociology of education. Sociologists of education and other educational researchers have for a long time studied the outcomes of ability grouping and its
demographic correlates (Jaremus et al., 2020; McGillicuddy, 2021; Rix & Ingham, 2021;
Slavin, 1990; Spina, 2019).
As mentioned above, researchers have neglected to identify and explain inter-­country
variation in ability grouping policies (i.e. there is a knowledge gap in this regard). Our study
helps fill the knowledge gap by shifting the focus from individuals to schools and countries.
Most educational research focuses on individuals, such as teachers, students or parents.
Specifically, special educational needs researchers have mainly investigated individuals as
the unit of analysis. Therefore, special educational needs have often been restricted to studying individual's special needs as opposed to what schools do and why. Such methodological

schools and countries as a unit of analysis.
Schools set ability grouping policies, but these policies may be influenced by the educational system or, as we show, the welfare regime. Again, welfare regimes represent clusters
of social policies (Esping-­Andersen, 1990, 2009; Esping-­Andersen et al., 2012). We contend
that social policies and educational policies may have a strong agreement, such as in the
case of ability grouping within schools (i.e. at the school).
Schools (management and administration) adapt to their social contexts, such as the
educational system, but also welfare regimes (Esping-Andersen, 1990, 2009; Esping-Andersen et al., 2012). Therefore, schools adopt policies in response to the social context
(i.e. welfare regimes). In turn, school policies govern (i.e. set standards) but do not determine
teachers’ work and students’ life chances. Therefore, although welfare regimes may govern
what schools do, their importance for what happens in the classroom remains uncertain.
Therefore, our result implies a clear support for the long-­standing arguments in sociology
of education (i.e. a weak correlation between policy and classroom but a strong correlation
between schools and policy; Meyer & Rowan, 1977).
Educational researchers (Grek et al., 2009; Lingard, 2020), including special needs education researchers, have for a long time recognised policy's importance (Berhanu, 2010).
Educational policy governs educational practice. Therefore, educational research has a long
tradition of case studies of educational policies (Lingard, 2020; Ozga, 2021). Again, we help
show how welfare regimes may explain variation in such policies (Esping-­Andersen, 1990).
By implication, our findings may guide educational researchers who aim to contextualise
policies (e.g. policy sociology; Grek et al., 2009; Lingard, 2020). Similarly, our study implies
support for the call to shift the unit of analysis away from individuals in special educational
needs research to schools (Berhanu, 2010).
However, our results show the need to move beyond schools and recognise that they
cluster into countries. Instead, country variation can be explained by welfare regimes.
Therefore, our study implies that political sociology can inform educational research and
thus help explain variation in schools and the conditions that govern children's life chances
(Esping-­Andersen et al., 2012). Having discussed our study's theoretical and empirical
implications, we now turn to the policy implications. In agreement with the social democratic ethos (Berhanu, 2010), we emphasise that schools offer children of all social classes
places to meet. In schools, all children can study together, play together, work together,
exercise together, eat together and learn together. Ability grouping to different classes
within the school obstructs meetings between children of all social classes. At the same
time, schools and their teachers need to respond to diverse student populations. In response to a diverse student population, schools display a social need for some type of
ability grouping.
Consistent with a social justice perspective, schools (and their teachers) still need them
in the classroom as the ‘lesser evil’. Therefore, ability grouping within the class functions as
a practical solution and hence an alternative (‘substitute’ or ‘replacement’) to ability grouping
to different classes.

regimes as a complement
We now turn to the alternative explanation of our results (VoC, i.e. production regime theory;
Hall & Soskice, 2001). The result of the sensitivity check of alternative explanations suggests that welfare regime theory and VoC result in the same substantive conclusions. The
two theories had the same predictive accuracy (based on AIC but not BIC). Therefore, we

recognise that VoC offers a viable alternative explanation of our results. Owing to the overlapping between the theories, we regard them as complementary.
Varieties of Capitalism does offer a richer theory of why educational systems matter
(Graf, 2009; Hall & Soskice, 2001). In CME, the greater coordination between the educational system and the labour market does explain a greater need for ability grouping between
classes within the school. Ability grouping between classes within the school increases the
coordination between labour markets needs and the educational systems’ supply of CME,
meaning that CME benefits from sorting students based on ability groups and subsequently
into tracks with different vocational foci. For example, low-­ability grouping prepares the student for vocational training and apprenticeship in firms whereas high-­ability grouping prepares students for higher-­education studies.
Again, we consider VoC appealing for educational researchers owing to the rich theorisation of the educational system's importance (Graf, 2009; Hall & Soskice, 2001). However,
despite the appeal, we favour welfare regime theory over VoC. Unlike welfare regime theory,
we contend that VoC overemphasises the employer's perspective, which over-­emphasises
coordination and underemphasise conflicts (Korpi, 2006). Therefore, we consider the two
theories overlapping and complementary. This leads us to recommend further research on
the two theories in educational research to examine their predictive accuracy on schools´
educational policies.
Another outlook for future research concerns the inter-­c orrelations between social
policy, educational policy and school average competencies. Welfare regimes account
for differences between schools’ average competence in PISA (Peter et al., 2010), and
ability grouping policies have been cited as an intervening variable (Allmendinger &
Leibfried, 2003). Therefore, we consider these inter-­c orrelations a promising research
topic.
Finally, VoC and welfare regimes did not explain ability grouping within the classes.
Instead, sociodemographics seem to account for most of the variation, a result that agrees
with previous research.
However, further research is still necessary to understand ability grouping policies stigmatising/de-­stigmatising and labelling/de-­labelling mechanisms. To advance the research,
researchers may consider the mechanisms in other units of analysis (i.e. beyond individuals;
e.g. collective stigmatisation or labelling, territorial stigmatisation of socioeconomic deprived
neighbourhoods), such as how subnational welfare regimes (or production regimes) contribute to educational policies at the school level and therefore intervene as de-­stigmatising and
de-­labelling of sociodemographics (Artiles et al., 2005).

Limitations and future research
Finally, we turn to the limitations of the scope of study. Scrutinising the limitations helps
us better understand the conditions under which we draw our conclusions and their wider
scope. Our first and second limitations concern the explanatory and outcome variables,
respectively. The third and fourth concern the scope of the study (stages and the Global
South).
First, classifications of countries into welfare regimes (or production regimes, as in VoC)
may result in misclassifications and hence measurement errors (Esping-­Andersen, 1990).
Measurement errors tend in the explanatory variables to be more serious than those in the
outcome variables. However, we know from previous studies that classification of countries
into welfare regimes does vary by study, yet findings demonstrate a remarkable robustness
of welfare regimes’ predictive validity across these despite discrepancies in classifications
of individual countries (Güler, 2019).

Second, we measure the ability grouping policy and not the ability grouping practice (i.e.
the activity). Disagreement between the policy and practices may occur at school or in the
class. However, by estimating the probability of policy outcome, we still learn something
valuable about schools’ propensity to enforce ability grouping. Next, understanding is valuable as such, and we agree with educational researchers who claim that policy governs all
educational practice.
Third, our analysis did not address how schools’ ability grouping policies vary between
educational stages (e.g. early vs. late selection). Therefore, further research is necessary
on how schools’ ability grouping policies vary between educational stages, depending on
welfare regimes or production regimes.
Fourth, explanations centred on welfare regimes applied to welfare states. Welfare states
tend to be consolidated democracies and affluent. Applying the theory of welfare regimes to
the Global South, such as African and Asian countries (beyond Japan), has been notoriously
difficult (Fenger, 2007; Ferreira, 2008; Golinowska, 2009). Similarly, VoC fails to resolve the
problem, as it neglects dependencies between the global North and South. Instead, we need
alternative theories (e.g. world system position) or theoretical elaborations about welfare regime
types (e.g. developing welfare regimes) to make scientific progress. This posits an excellent
opportunity for future research (i.e. explaining variation in ability grouping in the Global South).
