ABSTRACT

Keywords:
Gender math gap
Semiparametric Oaxaca Blinder decomposition
Gender stratification hypothesis

The gender stratification hypothesis maintains that higher levels of societal gender equity predict smaller gender
gaps in mathematics achievement and attitudes. Using data from the Program for International Student
Assessment (PISA) across 56 countries, this study aims to provide a thorough empirical test of the gender
stratification hypothesis. We employ a novel two-stage empirical strategy to investigate the link between societal
gender equities and gender differences in mathematics achievement and attitudes. In the first stage, we use a
semiparametric Oaxaca-Blinder (OB) method to decompose the gender gaps in each country into a part that is
explained by gender differences in observable characteristics and a part that remains unexplained. In the second
stage, we examine the relationships between the unexplained parts of the gender gaps and country-specific
gender equity measures. The results highlight the importance of gender equity in the labor market in explaining
cross-country variation in the gender math gap. We find that lower gender wage gap is significantly associated
with a smaller unexplained part of the gender math gap favoring boys. In terms of the gender gaps in math
attitudes, the results yield mixed support for the gender stratification hypothesis.

1. Introduction
What accounts for the gender achievement gap in mathematics? The
literature offers two principal explanations for the existence of the
gender math gap. The proponents of biological theories argue that
gender differences in brain composition (Cahill, 2005), hormone levels
(Davison & Susman, 2001), or spatial ability (Kucian, Loenneker,
Dietrich, Martin, & von Aster, 2005) produce the gap.1 The other body
of research attributes the gender math gap to a complex variety of sociocultural factors rather than biological differences. Theories relying
on the sociocultural explanation point out various societal factors as the
cause of the gender math gap, including differential treatment by teachers (Dee, 2007; Carrell, Page, & West, 2010), stereotype threat
(Spencer, Steele, & Quinn, 1999; O’Brien & Crandall, 2003, Stoet &
Geary, 2012), differential parental investment or expectations (Jacobs,

1991; Fryer & Levitt, 2010; Bharadwaj, Giorgi, Hansen, & Neilson,
2016), attitudes towards competition (Niederle & Vesterlund, 2010;
Shurchkov, 2012), and societal gender inequity (Baker & Jones, 1993;
Guiso, Monte, Sapienza, & Zingales, 2008).2 Studies that emphasize the
role of societal-level gender inequities in the formation of the gap are
based on the gender stratification hypothesis (Baker & Jones, 1993).
This hypothesis proposes that gender differences in educational and
economic opportunities can affect numerous socialization processes
that shape mathematics achievement and attitudes. Baker &
Jones, 1993, (p92) argue that:
If male students are afforded the possibility of greater future educational and occupational opportunities as a function of their performance in mathematics, then they may try harder, teachers may

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 elaborates on the gender stratification hypothesis and provides a review of
the empirical literature on this hypothesis. Section 3 describes the data
sets used in the analysis. Section 4 introduces the semiparametric OB
decomposition employed to investigate the gender gaps in mathematics
achievement and attitudes across countries. Section 5 presents results
from the empirical analysis with a discussion on robustness checks, and
Section 6 concludes.

Using data from the Program for International Student Assessment
(PISA) across 56 countries, this study aims to examine the relationships
between societal gender equity and the gender gaps in mathematics
achievement and attitudes. To this end, we employ a novel two-stage
empirical strategy in which the first stage involves decomposing the
gender gaps in each country into a part that is explained by gender
differences in observable characteristics and a part that remains unexplained. In the second stage, we investigate whether the unexplained
components of the gap are systematically related to country-level gender
equity measures. Our study makes two main contributions to the empirical literature on the gender stratification hypothesis. First, unlike
previous studies that either use the raw gender test score gap in
mathematics (e.g., Baker & Jones, 1993; Else-Quest, Hyde, & Linn, 2010)
or implement regression-based decomposition techniques to identify the
unexplained part of the gap (e.g., Dickerson, McIntosh, & Valente, 2015;
Guiso et al., 2008), we use a semiparametric Oaxaca-Blinder (OB) decomposition to analyze the gender gaps in mathematics achievement and
attitudes. The raw gap provides limited information because it does not
account for gender differences in observable characteristics. Differing
from regression-based decomposition techniques, the semiparametric OB
does not require any estimation of outcome equations. To account for
individual heterogeneity, we estimate the counterfactual outcome for
each female student separately using a propensity score matching technique. Second, cross-country variation in gender gaps in math attitudes is
often overlooked in the previous empirical tests of the gender stratification hypothesis. Using the same decomposition method, we also examine the link between societal gender equity and the unexplained parts
of the gender gaps in math self-efficacy, intrinsic motivation to learn
math, and instrumental motivation to learn math. Additionally, we investigate the role of societal gender equity in explaining cross-country
variation in the gender test score gap in reading to explore heterogeneity
in gender achievement gaps across domains.
The semiparametric OB decomposition results based on the 2012
PISA sample show that the unexplained parts of the mean gender gaps
in mathematics achievement and attitudes that remain after accounting
for gender differences in observed characteristics are statistically significant and in favor of boys in most countries. Consistent with the
gender stratification hypothesis, the results indicate that greater gender
equity in access to tertiary education and lower gender wage gap significantly predict a smaller unexplained part of the gender math gap
favoring boys. To capitalize on cross-country and time variation in the
gender math gap, we use the 2006, 2009, 2012 and 2015 PISA waves.
Our findings from the panel data analysis also suggest that gender
equity in the labor market plays an important role in explaining the
cross-country variation in the gender math gap. In addition, we provide
evidence that gender pay equity is positively linked to not only girls’
relative math achievement but also their relative perception about how
useful math might be in their future careers.3 However, our societal
gender equity measures are not significantly associated with gender
differences in intrinsic motivation to learn math. The results also provide mixed support for the gender stratification hypothesis when we
focus on the gender gap in math self-efficacy. Moreover, our empirical
analysis underscores the importance of using specific domains of gender
equity in addition to composite indices of gender equity in testing
gender stratification hypothesis to identify the societal domains

2. Literature review
The gender stratification hypothesis, proposed by Baker and
Jones (1993), suggests that higher levels of societal gender equity
predict smaller gender gaps in mathematics achievement and attitudes.
Pointing out the connections between anticipated future opportunities
and current academic performance, Baker and Jones (1993) argue that
girls tend to perform worse than boys in math and hold more negative
attitudes about math due to gender stratification of occupational and
educational opportunities. Consistent with the gender stratification
hypothesis, the expectancy-value theory developed by Eccles (1994)
provides insight into mechanisms behind the links between societal
gender inequities and the gender gaps in mathematics achievement and
attitudes. According to Eccles’ (1994) model, expectation of success and
subjective task value directly influence academic choices.4 People do
not engage in tasks or activities that hold little return in value. Gender
differences in subjective task value might be shaped by cultural milieu.
For example, the scarcity of women role models in math related careers
may cause girls to believe that career options appropriate for or
available to women do not require mathematics competency. Consequently, they may think that math is not valuable or useful which, in
turn, leads their math interest to stay low and their math skills to remain undeveloped. A lack of gender equity in educational and economic opportunities may also affect girls’ confidence in their own math
abilities through various socialization processes. For instance, parents
may have higher expectations regarding math for their sons than for
their daughters because they believe that math is for boys and is less
important for their daughters’ future. Similarly, gender stereotypical
beliefs of teachers, shaped by societal gender stratification, may result
in gender differences in math attitudes and skills.
The empirical tests of the gender stratification hypothesis offer mixed
findings. Using the Second International Mathematics Study (SIMS) microdata on 77,000 eighth-graders from 19 countries, Baker and
Jones (1993) find that greater gender equity in higher education and
labor market predicts a smaller gender math gap.5 In their well-publicized study, Guiso et al. (2008) examine the link between cross-country
differences in gender equity and the gender mathematics test score gap
using data from the 2003 PISA. They use the following four indicators to
assess national level gender equity: (i) the World Economic Forum’s
Gender Gap Index (GGI), which reflects economic and political

Else-Quest et al. (2010) meta-analyzed the 2003 TIMMS and the
2003 PISA datasets to test the gender stratification hypothesis. They
examine gender differences in math achievement as well as math attitudes. In addition to composite indicators of gender equity, they also
use more specific indicators of societal gender equity, including women’s share of research positions and girls’ access to primary, secondary
and tertiary education. Their study supports the gender stratification
hypothesis in many cases.7
In the developing country context, Dickerson et al. (2015) investigate
the determinants of the gender mathematics test score gap across 19
African countries. Like Fryer and Levitt (2010), they find that the crosscountry gender math gap is not significantly correlated with the level of
national gender equity, as measured by the GGI. Their results indicate
that almost half of the cross-country variation in the gap is explained by
differences in women’s role in society as proxied by the fertility rate in a
country. Using the National Assessment Educational Process (NAEP) data
from the United States, Pope and Sydnor (2010) show that there is a
large and statistically significant variation in gender gaps in standardized
test scores for eighth-graders scoring at the top percentiles across states
and census divisions. The authors find that in areas where men and
women are viewed as more equal, gender disparities are smaller in both
stereotypically male-dominated tests of mathematics and science and
stereotypically female-dominated tests of reading.
Using an epidemiological approach, Nollenberger, RodriguezPlanas, and Sevilla (2016) focus on second-generation immigrant children who were born and raised in their respective host countries.8
These second-generation immigrants share host country institutions
and environment; however, they differ in terms of their ancestry. Using
the 2003, 2006, 2009, and 2012 PISA data sets on 12,027 secondgeneration immigrant children from 45 different countries who reside
in 12 host countries, Nollenberger et al. (2016) find that lower gender
equality in the country of ancestry is associated with a higher gender
mathematics gap for second-generation immigrant children, suggesting
that culture matters for the gender math gap. Following the same approach as in Nollenberger et al. (2016), Rodriguez-Planas and
Nollenberger (2018) find that country-of-ancestry gender social norms
can explain not only gender differences in the PISA math test scores but
also gender differences in the PISA reading and science test scores,
emphasizing the relevance of general gender stereotypes as opposed to
math-specific stereotypes on girls’ relative test performance.
Our paper differs from the existing studies in two important aspects.
First, we apply a propensity score matching method to identify the unexplained part of the gender math gap that remains after accounting for
gender differences in observed characteristics in each country. This

semiparametric decomposition does not specify any functional form assumptions on outcome equations and makes it possible to account for
heterogeneity across individuals by estimating counterfactual outcomes
for each individual separately (Heckman, La Londe, & Smith, 1999;
Imbens, 2004). To test the gender stratification hypothesis, we relate the
unexplained part of the gender math gap to the domain specific indicators
of societal gender equity. The previously reviewed studies either use the
raw gender math gap or implement a regression-based decomposition
technique to measure the unexplained part of the gap.9 Second, taking
advantage of the fact that the 2012 PISA contains information on both
mathematics achievement and attitudes towards mathematics, we also
examine the relationships between cross-country variation in the unexplained parts of the gender gaps in math attitudes (i.e., math self-efficacy, intrinsic motivation to learn math, and instrumental motivation to
learn math) and societal gender equity. The two studies that are closest to
our paper are Else-Quest et al. (2010) that investigate the cross-country
variation in the raw gaps in math attitudes using data from the 2003 PISA
and 2003 TIMMS and Rodriguez-Planas & Nollenberger, 2018that focus
only on 4,521 second-generation immigrants living in 9 host countries and
use only the GGI to assess gender equity in the country of ancestry when
examining the gender gaps in math attitudes.
3. Data and descriptive statistics
3.1. Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) data
PISA is a standardized international achievement test in reading,
mathematics, and science for 15-year-old students, implemented on a 3year cycle by the Organization for Economic Co-operation and
Development (OECD). Each PISA wave focuses on one particular subject
and provides a more detailed information regarding this subject. The
2003 PISA was the first assessment in which the major domain was math.
Our math-focus analysis is mainly based on the 2012 PISA for the following two reasons. First, information on the Gender Gap Index (GGI)
and its sub-indices, which are our measures of societal gender equity, is
available starting from 2006. Second, we are interested in gender differences not only in math achievement but also in math attitudes. Since
2006, the 2012 PISA has been the only wave that contains data on a wide
range of variables used to construct indices on self-reported beliefs regarding math. PISA follows a stratified two-stage sample design. Schools
having 15-year-old students are sampled as the first stage unit. In the
second stage, students are randomly selected with equal probability
within schools. In the econometric analysis, we use the final student
weights that incorporate both the school weights and the within-school
student weights. Employing item response theory, PISA reports plausible
values to present each student’s test performance. We take the average of
those plausible values to measure the students’ test performance.
Our sample coming from the 2012 PISA consists of 220,333 students
from 56 different countries.10 The descriptive statistics are presented in

mathematics for students and their future careers.14 The higher the
indices are, the more motivated a student is to do well in mathematics.
OECD (2013) describes how each of the three attitudinal indices is

wide range of student and family characteristics that may affect academic achievement. In our student-level analysis, we control for the

3.2. Indicators of societal gender equity

4.1. The semiparametric OB decomposition

We use the GGI as a composite measure of societal gender equity.
The GGI, which has been reported annually by the World Economic
Forum since 2006, assesses national gender equity in educational,
economic, political and health domains (Hausmann, Tyson, & Zahidi,
2012). The GGI and its four sub-indices range from 0 to 1, with larger
values indicating higher status of women in society. Table A.3
presents the four sub-indices and the components of each sub-index.
The GGI is the unweighted mean of the four sub-indices while each
sub-index is the weighted mean of the variables within the
sub-index.21
Else-Quest et al. (2010) underscore the importance of using specific
domains of gender equity that are more relevant to math achievement
when testing the gender stratification hypothesis. Therefore, in addition
to the GGI, we utilize the following eight indicators of gender equity in
education, economic and political domains: (i) the ratio of female-tomale in net primary school enrollment, (ii) the ratio of female-to-male
in net secondary school enrollment, (iii) the ratio of female-to-male in
gross tertiary school enrolment,22 (iv) wage equality between women
and men for similar work (converted into female-over-male ratio), (v)
the ratio of female-to-male in labor force participation rate, (vi) the
ratio of women to men among legislators, senior officials and managers
(vii) the ratio of women to men among technical and professional
workers (viii) the ratio of women to men in parliamentary positions.
Focusing on specific domains of gender equity also allows us to explore
potential mechanisms suggested by the gender stratification hypothesis.
It is important to note that these domain-specific indicators of societal
gender equity are used in the computation of the GGI sub-indices. We
relate the 2012 PISA data to the 2012 GGI data when we investigate
gender differences in math achievement and attitudes across countries.23

the Global Gender Gap Report 2012.

We use a propensity score matching method to decompose the mean
gender gaps into an explained and an unexplained part for each country
individually. Propensity score matching is not only employed in the
evaluation literature to estimate average treatment effects (Rosenbaum
& Rubin, 1983; Black & Smith, 2004; Frölich, 2004; Sianesi, 2004;
Smith & Todd, 2005), but also applied outside the treatment evaluation
context to decompose the gender wage gap (Frölich, 2007), the test
score gap across genders (Gevrek & Seiberlich, 2014), and the test score
gap across countries (Botezat & Seiberlich, 2013).
Following Frölich (2007), we implement a semiparametric version
of the twofold Oaxaca-Blinder (OB) decomposition to investigate the
gender gaps in mathematics achievement and attitudes. The OB decomposition methodology (Oaxaca, 1973; Blinder, 1973), which is
primarily used to investigate discrimination in the labor market, has
been recently applied in the economics of education to examine the
test score gap between countries (Ammermüller, 2007), schools –private versus public– (Duncan & Sandy, 2007; Krieg & Storer, 2006),
boys and girls (Sohn, 2012; Gevrek & Seiberlich, 2014), and ethnic
groups –indigenous versus non-indigenous– (Sakellariou, 2008;
McEwan, 2004).

The main identifying assumption requires that conditional on the
distributions of observable characteristics, the potential outcomes are
stochastically independent of gender: Y0, Y1⊥G|P(X). To justify this
assumption, we control for a large set of covariates available in the PISA
data set, including family background characteristics and school fixed
effects. Gender differences in innate ability could plausibly lead to
gender differences in test scores. Even if boys (girls) are innately more
capable than girls (boys) in math (reading), it is reasonable to assume
that the underlying innate ability distributions do not differ substantially across countries, suggesting that gender differences in innate
abilities cannot explain cross-country variation in gender test score gaps

called the overlap assumption (Hahn, 1998; Heckman, Ichimura, &

2010). It rules out the phenomenon of perfect predictability of gender
given X, i.e., 0 < Pr(G = 1|X ) < 1. To satisfy the overlap assumption,
we restrict the estimation of the explained and unexplained parts to the
common support sub-population.

5.1.3. Gender test score gap in reading
The gender stratification hypothesis suggests that gender achievement gap is more pronounced when society is more stratified based on
gender, and thus has more gender-related inequality of opportunities.
Previous empirical tests of the gender stratification hypothesis focus
mainly on the gender gap in mathematics. Pointing out the importance
of analyzing the links between gender gaps in other subjects and gender
social norms Rodriguez-Planas and Nollenberger (2018) state that
“Evidence that the effects of culture expand beyond math suggests that
gender social norms are affecting female performance more broadly.” To
investigate the relationship between societal gender equity and the
gender test score gap in reading, we implement the same two-stage
empirical strategy in which the first stage involves decomposing the
gender gap in reading in each country into a part that accounts for
differences in observed characteristics between girls and boys and a
part that remains unexplained.
Table 6 shows the semiparametric OB decomposition results for the
gender test score gap in reading. Girls, on average, outperform boys in
reading in all 56 countries. The explained part of the gap is positive and
statistically significant in 42 out of 56 countries, suggesting that gender
differences in observed characteristics predict an advantage for girls
over boys in the average reading scores in most countries. Similarly, the
unexplained part of the gap is in favor of girls and statistically significant in all countries. It varies across countries greatly, from 11.12
score points in Belgium to 49.08 score points in Finland. Moreover, the
last column of Table 6 shows that the component of the reading test
score gap accounting for the difference in mean test scores between
girls who are matched with boys and those who remain out of common
support is not statistically significant at conventional levels.

5.1.2. Gender gaps in attitudes towards mathematics
We also examine the cross-country variation in gender differences in
math attitudes to provide insight into mechanisms behind the relationship between gender math gap and national level gender inequities. The gender stratification hypothesis predicts that gender differences in math attitudes are smaller in more gender egalitarian
countries. To assess gender differences in students’ motivation in
mathematics, we focus on two forms of motivation to learn mathematics: (i) students are intrinsically motivated to learn math because
they find it enjoyable and interesting. To represent this construct, we
use the PISA index of intrinsic motivation to learn math; (ii) students
may learn math because they perceive it useful to their future education
and careers. To explore this possibility, we use the PISA index of instrumental motivation to learn math.
Table 3 shows the semiparametric OB decomposition results for the
gender gap in the index of intrinsic motivation to learn math. The
gender gap is negative and statistically significant in 49 out of 56
countries, suggesting that boys, on average, have higher levels of intrinsic motivation to learn math than girls in most countries. The explained part of the gap is statistically significant and in favor of boys
(girls) only in Austria (Finland, Indonesia, and Kazakhstan). Column 3
of Table 3 indicates that the unexplained part of the gap exhibits a male
advantage in 49 countries, ranging from 0.07 units in Bulgaria to 0.45
units in Switzerland. Malaysia is the only country where the unexplained part of the gap is in favor of girls. We can make a number of
conjectures about the male advantage in the unexplained part of the
gap. It may be a result of differential teacher attention to boys and girls
in the classroom (Carrell et al., 2010; Dee, 2007). It could, for instance,
be the case that mathematics teachers discriminate against girls due to
stereotypes about female inferiority in math, causing boys to show
more positive math attitudes.

to account.41 First, using Hofstede's (1980) concept of power distance,
which is a cultural dimension describing the extent to which social
groups are segregated in society, Else-Quest et al. (2010) argue that
high power-distance cultures (PDCs) tend to have less gender-equal
social structures, discouraging between-gender interactions. Thus, individuals from high PDCs are more likely to engage in within-gender

social structures that encourage between-gender comparisons. The increased between-gender comparisons in more gender-equal countries
may lead girls to hold more negative attitudes about mathematics,
causing gender differences in math attitudes to be more pronounced.
Their second explanation points to the importance of human development which is closely related to women’s empowerment. According to
Maslow (1954) hierarchy of human needs, feeling confident about one’s
math skills can be classified as a higher need that can be met in better
economic, political and educational environment compared to a basic
need. Else-Quest et al. (2010), (p124) note that “Girls who live in a society where they face major inequities because of their gender may be too
consumed with meeting their most basic needs to think about such things as

how anxious they feel about math or whether they feel good about their math
skills.” Consistent with this theoretical perspective, one might expect
gender differences in mathematics self-efficacy to be larger in countries
with higher level of gender equity.
Fourth, it is worth highlighting that the estimated coefficient of GDP
per capita is statistically significant and negative in all specifications
presented in columns 2-4 of Table 7. The richer the country, the larger
the unexplained part of the gender gap in math attitudes favoring boys.
This pattern might be explained by the two hypotheses proposed by
Else-Quest et al. (2010) given the fact that low power-distance countries are often more developed and more gender-equal countries.

6. Conclusion
This study extends the literature on the role of societal gender
equity in explaining gender differences in math achievement and attitudes by applying a novel two-stage empirical strategy. In the first
stage, we use data from the Program for International Student
Assessment (PISA) across 56 countries and employ a semiparametric
Oaxaca-Blinder (OB) method to decompose the gender gaps into a part
that is explained by gender differences in observable characteristics and
a part that remains unexplained for each country. In the second stage,
we examine the relationships between the unexplained parts of the
gender gaps and country-specific gender equity measures.
The semiparametric OB decomposition results based on the 2012
PISA indicate that the unexplained parts of the mean gender gaps in
mathematics achievement and attitudes considerably vary across
countries and exhibit a male advantage in most countries. We find that
greater gender equity in access to tertiary education and lower gender
wage gap are significantly associated with a smaller unexplained part of
the gender math gap favoring boys, as proposed by the gender stratification hypothesis. Our findings from the panel data analysis based on
the 2006, 2009, 2012 and 2015 PISA also highlight the importance of
gender equity in the labor market in explaining cross-country variation
in the gender math gap. In terms of the gender gaps in math attitudes,
the results yield mixed support for the gender stratification hypothesis.
For instance, we find that greater gender pay equity significantly predicts a smaller unexplained part of the gender gap in instrumental
motivation to learn math while gender differences in math self-efficacy
tend to be larger in countries with higher level of gender equity,
measured by the GGI. We use only the 2012 PISA to analyze gender
gaps in math attitudes due to data limitations. Future research should
explore the link between gender differences in math attitudes and societal gender equity measures by using different international data sets
to shed more light on these findings. Moreover, we relate the unexplained part of the gender reading gap, which favors girls in all
countries, to our societal gender equity measures. The results reveal
that in countries where the labor market is more gender-equal, girls’
relative advantage in reading widens.
The cross-country analyses that aim to investigate the relationships
between the gender gaps and societal gender equity measures are descriptive in nature rather than causal due to potential omitted variable
bias. However, in the absence of experimental data, they are crucial to
explore the role of sociocultural factors in shaping gender differences in
math achievement and attitudes because these factors can vary substantially across countries and if they influence the gender gaps in math
achievement and attitudes, this should show up in cross-country variation in the gender gaps.
