Abstract
Students from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds generally have worse 
academic outcomes than their more advantaged peers. However, some resilient stu­
dents beat the odds and achieve academic success despite socioeconomic adversity. 
Identifying the factors that promote resilience is of critical theoretical and practical 
importance. Hence, this study aims to examine the different personal and social-
contextual factors that predict resilience. We utilized the 2018 Program for Interna­
tional Student Assessment (PISA) data from Hong Kong and focused specifically 
on the 1,459 students in the bottom socioeconomic quartile. Of these, 251 were 
identified as resilient students as they demonstrated a high level of achievement 
despite being from disadvantaged backgrounds. Machine learning (i.e., random for­
est classification) was adopted to understand the relative importance of 30 different 
personal and social-contextual factors in classifying students into those who are 
deemed resilient versus those who are not. Eight top variables that best predicted 
resilience were identified, including the use of meta-cognitive strategies, joy of 
reading, teacher-directed instruction, perception of difficulty of the PISA test, sense 
of belonging to school, discriminating school climate, self-efficacy, and perceived 
teacher’s interest. This study sheds light on the factors that underpin resilience, 
providing important theoretical and policy implications.

1  Introduction
Academic resilience refers to the phenomenon whereby socioeconomically disad­
vantaged students beat the odds and achieve academic success (Agasisti et al., 2018, 
2021; OECD, 2011). Understanding the factors that underpin academic resilience is 
critical, which could provide informative evidence to bridge the achievement gaps 
caused by socioeconomic disparities (OECD, 2011). This is especially pertinent 
as approximately 1.2 billion children under age 18 are living in poverty (UNICEF, 
2021).
Past studies have identified different factors that facilitate resilience, such as 
self-efficacy and self-concept (Cheung, 2017), academic interest and perseverance 
(Thorsen et al., 2021), school engagement (Finn & Rock, 1997), supportive learning 
environments (Padron et al., 2014), and disciplinary climate among others (Agasisti 
et al., 2021). These findings suggest that resilience is a complex phenomenon that is 
multiply determined (Kumpfer, 1999).
However, most existing studies have only investigated the role of a small subset 
of variables in facilitating resilience (e.g., Cheung et al., 2014; Das, 2019; Li, 2017; 
Wills & Hofmeyr, 2019). Focusing on only a few factors may give researchers an 
incomplete picture of the most critical variables that predict resilience. For example, 
questions such as which factors are more important predictors of resilience remain 
unanswered. Hence, there is a need to explore a more comprehensive set of predictors 
to understand the diverse factors that underpin academic resilience.
Capitalizing on the flexibility of machine learning in data analysis (Breiman, 
2001a) and the public availability of data from the Program for International Stu­
dent Assessment (PISA), this study aims to examine the role of different personal 
and social contextual factors in helping socioeconomically disadvantaged students 
achieve academic resilience. Research endeavors in this direction are imperative as 
investigating the set of most important factors could provide solid evidence for prac­
titioners to find the most cost-effective intervention targets.
2  Literature review
2.1  Defining resilience
Resilience is an umbrella term referring to a dynamic process wherein individuals 
display positive adaptation in the face of obstacles and adversity (Greenberg, 2006; 
Luthar et al., 2000; Rutter, 2012). More specifically, it has been broadly defined 
as “the process of, capacity for, or outcome of successful adaptation despite chal­
lenging or threatening circumstances” (Masten et al., 1990, p.426). There are two 
interconnected factors related to resilience (Masten, 2001). The first one is adversity, 
which refers to individuals’ chronic exposure to challenging conditions where the 
risk factors hinder their normative development, such as socioeconomic disadvan­
tage (OECD, 2011), parental unemployment (Moreno-Maldonado et al., 2020), and 
maladaptive symptoms of mental health disorder (Boden et al., 2018). The second 
one is adaptive outcomes that individuals achieve despite the presence of adversity,

such as socio-emotional adaptation (K. Wang & Kong, 2020) and academic success

As an umbrella concept, resilience has been widely researched in a variety of fields, 
such as clinical psychology (e.g., Davydov et al., 2010), psychiatry (e.g., Barzilay et 
al., 2020), business (e.g., Linnenluecke, 2017), and education (e.g., Agasisti et al., 
2021). In this study, we focus specifically on academic resilience in the educational 
domain. M. C. Wang et al. (1994) defined academic resilience as “the heightened 
likelihood of success in school and other life accomplishments, despite environmen­
tal adversities brought about by early traits, conditions and experiences” (p. 46). In 
educational research, socioeconomic status is one of the strongest determinants of 
academic achievement (see Harwell et al., 2017; Sirin, 2005 for reviews). Socioeco­
nomic status could influence students’ academic achievement through a variety of 
channels (Yeung et al., 2022). For example, students from low socioeconomic status 
families tend to live in poor home environments, attend worse schools, and have 
fewer learning opportunities. Hence, they are more likely to fail in school than their 
peers (Coleman et al., 1966). Despite this general trend, a subset of socioeconomi­
cally disadvantaged students beat the odds and achieve academic success. This group 
of students is known as academically resilient students (OECD, 2011; Rudd et al., 
2021). In this study, OECD’s (2019a) operational definition of resilient students was 
adopted, which pertains to socioeconomically disadvantaged students in the bottom 
25% of socioeconomic status but score in the top 25% in terms of academic achieve­
ment in their region. This classical person-centered approach enables researchers to 
explore the determinants of academic resilience by investigating the differential role 
of factors in predicting resilient and non-resilient subsamples (e.g., Agasisti et al., 
2018; Cutuli et al., 2013).
2.2  Conceptual framework: Predicting academic resilience with an integrative 
framework
Many studies have been devoted to exploring the factors that could buffer disadvan­
taged students against risk and help them achieve academic success. These studies 
have highlighted the crucial roles played by personal and social-contextual factors 
(e.g., Das, 2019; Masten, 2001; Rutter, 2012).
Personal factors pertain to motivational, cognitive, behavioral, and affective 
competencies (Kumpfer, 1999). Students with a high level of motivation, academic 
engagement, and positive dispositional attitudes are more likely to achieve resil­
ience. For example, Martin and Marsh (2006) focused on the role of motivation and 
engagement in predicting academic resilience. Their study identified the importance 
of self-efficacy, planning, and persistence in promoting resilience. They also found 
that anxiety and lack of control undermined it. Thorsen et al. (2021) found that resil­
ient students were higher in perseverance and interest in school. In another research, 
Borman and Overman (2004) employed a series of multivariate analyses of variance 
and found that resilient students had higher levels of academic engagement, self-
efficacy, and self-esteem. They also held more positive attitudes towards school than 
their non-resilient peers.

Meanwhile, researchers also suggested that academic resilience is influenced by 
the social-contextual environment (Masten & Coatsworth, 1998). There is a burgeon­
ing body of evidence indicating that supportive learning environments make a differ­
ence in academic resilience. For example, Borman and Rachuba (2000) found that a 
supportive school environment such as a safe and orderly school climate and positive 
teacher-student relationships tend to increase the likelihood of being resilient. Cefai 
(2007) explored the role of the classroom process in promoting resilience and found 
that positive teacher behaviors such as supporting students, expressing the beliefs 
that students had the potential to succeed, and adopting positive instructional strate­
gies could increase the likelihood of academic resilience. In another study, Agasisti et 
al. (2018) revealed that orderly classes where teachers provided well-paced instruc­
tion and where students focused on learning are particularly critical for socioeco­
nomically disadvantaged students to improve their achievement.
These prior studies show that academic resilience is not formed within a vacuum 
but is embedded in and shaped by multiple personal and social-contextual factors 
(Rutter, 2012). However, many studies only “explain a small but important percent­
age of variance in academic resilience, there are other unidentified explanatory fac­
tors” (Cappella & Weinstein, 2001, p.767).
Lee and Shute’s (2010) integrative framework could serve as a useful theoreti­
cal lens to understand the different factors that facilitate resilience. This framework 
entails the importance of the joint influence of a diverse list of personal (e.g., behav­
ior, affect, attitude, and cognition) and social-contextual (e.g., classroom and school 
learning environments) factors on academic achievement. The core tenet of the inte­
grative framework is that “achievement is influenced by not only cognitive, or not 
just affective or motivational, or contextual factors, but all of these major factors 
working in concert.” (Lee & Shute, 2010, p. 198).
There are two advantages to applying this framework in resilience research. First, 
as an integrative framework that embraces a comprehensive view, it provides a means 
of integratingdifferent variables f into one model and enables researchers to reveal 
the complex determinants of resilience. Second, the integrative framework focuses 
on the personal and social-contextual factors, which are potentially malleable.Prac­
titioners could identify the most cost-effective intervention targets by comparing the 
relative importance of multiple factors that impinge on academic resilience. How­
ever, in resilience research, as Kumpfer (1999. p.184) acknowledged, “because it 
is a daunting task to simultaneously test multicausal models, most of the predictive 
research cited in this review includes only parts of this framework in a single study.” 
Most empirical studies only examined a small part of the variables of this integrative 
framework. Our study aims to address this gap by simultaneously examining a broad 
range of personal and social-contextual variables that facilitate resilience, which is 
made possible through the public availability of PISA data.
2.3  Analytical approaches: classical statistics and machine learning
Past studies on resilience have predominately relied on classical statistical approaches 
(e.g., logistic regression). These studies treat resilience as a binary outcome (resil­
ient vs. non-resilient) and provide the odd ratios for identifying factors impinging

on resilience. For example, Das (2019) classified socioeconomically disadvantaged 
students into resilient and non-resilient groups according to their reading arithmetic 
tests and found the critical role of structural factors (e.g., private schools and avail­
ability of educational support at home) in promoting academic resilience. In another 
study, Cheung et al. (2014) grouped socioeconomically disadvantaged students into 
two groups based on their residual reading performance and found that joy of read­
ing and metacognitive strategies could increase the likelihood of becoming resilient. 
Despite the widespread use of classical statistical approaches in resilience research, 
this approach suffers from several key limitations.
First, the use of a logistic regression model with many predictors might lead to 
overfitting problems, which means a complex model with many variables makes an 
accurate estimation in the training dataset but does not generalize well to novel data. 
This is because the inductive inference in classical statistical approaches is achieved 
through the evaluation criteria of null hypothesis significance testing (NHST), and 
relies on thep-value (Krueger, 2001). Furthermore, classical approaches rely heavily 
on the “goodness of fit” between the model and sample. An overfitted model could 
potentially be judged as a "good" model using classical methods even if it would not 
generalize well to a new dataset due to potential sampling or measurement errors 
(Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017).
Second, multicollinearity problems will ensue if there are a huge number of cor­
related predictors in the logistic regression model. The model does not appropriately 
partition variance explained by each variable. The standardized regression coeffi­
cients of variables may not provide informative information to compare the relative 
importance of predictors (e.g., Lavrijsen et al., 2021).
Machine learning could complement classical statistical approaches (Breiman, 
2001a, 2001b). First, machine learning focuses on prediction by extracting data 
patterns without making inferences (Bzdok et al., 2017). In this respect, machine 
learning results do not rely on significance indices (e.g., p-value) as its purpose is to 
display what ‘does’ happen rather than making a significant inference. Furthermore, 
the inherent cross-validation procedure in machine learning can alleviate the problem 
of overfitting by examining the predictive accuracy of models in out-of-sample data.
Second, machine learning approaches could compare the relative importance 
of different predictors without being beset by multicollinearity problems (Yarkoni 
& Westfall, 2017). This approach is particularly important when the predictors 
are highly correlated, which often occurs in large-scale educational assessments. 
Although machine learning has been utilized in education and psychology research 
(e.g., F. Wang et al., 2022; Sinclair et al., 2021), to our knowledge, this approach has 
not been used in resilience research.
2.4  The Hong Kong context
This study was conducted in Hong Kong, which is a highly unequal city and saw 
a rapid increase in income inequality (Lui, 2018). Social inequity in Hong Kong is 
tied to educational inequity. Hong Kong’s education system is competitive and strati­
fied, where socioeconomic status heavily influences the quality of schools in which 
students enroll (Chiu & Khoo 2005; Chiu & Walker 2007). Students from socioeco­

nomically advantaged families are more likely to attend high-quality schools with 
the best educational environment ( H. Wang et al., 2021; Kwan & Wong, 2014) and 
would benefit more from their initial advantages, thereby exacerbating the educa­
tional inequality. For example, socioeconomically advantaged students are more 
likely to be surrounded by high-quality peers whose positive behaviors, motivations, 
and attitudes might strengthen their academic advantages through social contagion 
(King, 2020; King & Mendoza, 2020). In comparison, socioeconomically disadvan­
taged students who interact with low-ability peers are more likely to be influenced by 
maladaptive motivations and behaviors.
On the other hand, despite the educational stratification and socioeconomic dis­
parities, Hong Kong is one of the highest-performing education systems, achieving 
educational equity and quality (Lee & Manzon, 2014). In PISA 2018, the mean read­
ing achievement of Hong Kong students was 524, ranking fourth in 76 participating 
countries and economies. The average difference in reading achievement between 
socioeconomically advantaged and disadvantaged students is 59 points, far below 
the average of 89 in OECD countries. More than 15% of socioeconomically disad­
vantaged students in Hong Kong were resilient students, ranking fourth worldwide 
(OECD, 2019a). These figures indicated the success of the Hong Kong education sys­
tem in providing high-quality and equitable educational opportunities for all students.
Despite the unequal development of the social and education system in Hong 
Kong, the educational outcome is of high quality and comparatively equitable com­
pared to other contexts. This paradox leads us to ask what factors contribute to socio­
economically disadvantaged students’ academic success. Several studies attempted to 
unlock this secret. For example, Cheung (2017) revealed that strong beliefs in one’s 
ability and a low level of mathematics anxiety could help socioeconomically disad­
vantaged students achieve academic success. Sandoval-Hernández and Białowolski 
(2016) found that high educational expectations and subjective values protected dis­
advantaged students. However, these studies mainly focused on a few explanatory 
variables and were unable to comprehensively examine the role of multiple factors 
that could predict resilience. Hence, this paper addresses this gap by adopting a more

2.5  The present study
To better understand the factors that promote resilience, we examined a range of 
personal and social-contextual factors drawn from the Hong Kong PISA dataset. We 
used Lee and Shute’s (2010) framework to organize the different variables that might 
impinge on resilience. The first aim of this study was to provide a holistic understand­
ing of the relative importance of different personal and social-contextual factors in 
predicting academic resilience. The second aim was to identify the top variables that 
best predicted academic resilience to inform educational practice. The conceptual 
model is displayed in Fig. 1. Machine learning, specifically random forest classifica­
tion, was used to classify students into resilient and non-resilient ones.
3  Methods
3.1  Data and participants
The data were drawn from the PISA 2018 Hong Kong database. PISA is a trien­
nial international comparative study designed to gauge the competencies and skills 
of 15-year-old students to meet real-life challenges. It not only measures students’ 
literacies in reading, mathematics, and science, but also collects multiple indicators 
concerning personal and social-contextual factors. 
A total of 7,037 Hong Kong 15-year-old students participate in PISA 2018. 
According to the operational definition of resilient students (OECD, 2019a), only 
socioeconomically disadvantaged students who are in the bottom 25% of Economic, 
Social and Cultural Status (ESCS) were selected as the research sample. In PISA 
2018, ESCS was operationally defined as financial, social, cultural, and human 
capital resources available to students, which was measured by the highest parental 
occupation (e.g., What is your father’s main job?), parental education (e.g., What 
is the < highest level of schooling > completed by your mother?), and the availabil­
ity of home possessions that indicate material wealth or educational resources (e.g., 
How many of these are there at your home: Cars) (OECD, 2020). Compared with 
a single indicator such as family income and parental education level, ESCS is a 
comprehensive, stable, and high-level descriptive indicator that strongly influences 
students’ learning opportunities and outcomes (Avvisati, 2020). Hence, numerous 
studies have used ESCS as a key indicator of students’ socioeconomic status given its 
greater reliability and validity compared to other narrower socioeconomic measures 
(e.g., Gjicali & Lipnevich, 2021; Lee, 2016; Marquez & Long, 2021). We follow 
this empirical precedent in the current study. The Appendix 1 in the Supplementary 
Materials includes sample items for measuring ESCS..
The final sample comprised 1,459 students. The average age was 15.73 (SD = 0.29). 
Most students were in grade 10 (57.8%) and grade 9 (28.6%), with the rest in other 
grades (13.6%). There were 57% males and 43% females.

3.2  Measures
Three criteria guided the selection of independent variables: (1) they should be avail­
able in the PISA 2018 student questionnaire (OECD, 2019b); (2) they can be mapped 
onto Lee and Shute’s (2010) framework. The targets were personal (i.e., learning 
behavior, affect, attitude, and cognition) and social-contextual factors (i.e., learning 
environment); and (3) they are significant predictors of academic resilience in previ­
ous studies. After reviewing the PISA student questionnaire, 17 personal variables 
and 13 social-contextual variables were identified as the independent variables. These 
variables were scaled and derived by the PISA working group using item response 
theory. The high reliability and validity of these variables have also been verified 
(OECD, 2020).
The dependent variable was a binary variable to signify whether low-ESCS stu­
dents achieve academic resilience. Low-ESCS students whose reading achievement 
in the top 25% were defined as resilient students and coded as 1, while the rest were 
non-resilient students and coded as 0.
All variables used in this study have been listed in Fig. 1. The detailed descriptions 
of each variable and sample item can be found in Table 1.
3.3  Data analysis
Missing data. The proportion of missing data for all variables was small, ranging 
from 0 to 11.9% (i.e., effort given in PISA). Missing data were imputed five times by 
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) multiple imputations using SPSS 26.0. This 
method leads to less biased estimates than other methods such as pairwise deletion, 
listwise deletion, and mean substitution (Peugh & Enders, 2004). However, the “ran­
domForest” package does not have any provision to analyze each of the five imputa­
tion datasets and combine the results. Hence, we selected the first imputation dataset 
for our subsequent analysis.
Preliminary analysis. Descriptive statistics such as mean and standard deviation 
were generated to understand the students’ scores on personal and social-contextual 
variables. The correlations between personal and social-contextual variables and aca­
demic resilience were also demonstrated. Furthermore, a series of t-tests were com­
puted to explore differences in personal and social-contextual factors among resilient 
and non-resilient students.
Primary analysis. Random forest classification, a specific type of machine learn­
ing model, was used for the primary analysis. The package “randomForest” was 
used in R (Liaw & Wiener, 2002). The purpose of this model was to rank the rela­
tive importance of all independent variables and identify the set of top predictors in 
predicting academic resilience. The predictive accuracy of this model was assessed 
by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve 
(AUC). ROC is the graph showing the performance of a classification model, with 
the horizontal axis being specificity (proportion of correctly classified non-resilient 
students) and the vertical axis being sensitivity (proportion of correctly classified 
resilient students). The ROC can be numerically reflected by AUC, which represents 
the ability of the model to accurately identify resilient and non-resilient students.

An AUC value higher than 0.6 is regarded as sufficient (Šimundić, 2009). Mean­
while, the relative importance of variables was assessed by the mean decrease in 
accuracy (MDA). Larger MDA indicates greater importance as removing the variable 
decreases the predictive accuracy of the model.
We used ten-fold cross-validation with five repeats to identify the most important 
set of factors (known as feature selection in machine learning literature) of academic 
resilience. Previous studies have indicated ten-folds are more likely to lead to less bias 
and moderate variance, providing a good estimate of model performance (Marcot & 
Hanea, 2020; Kohavi, 1995), and it was commonly used in the existing research (e.g., 
Tennenhouse et al., 2020). With this procedure, the sample was randomly divided 
into ten folds. Nine of them were designated as the training set for model estimation; 
one was the test set for model cross-validation. The optimal prediction performance 
was determined by averaging each fold’s result. This procedure evaluates the model 
performance with the minimum optimal solution by sequentially reducing the num­
ber of predictors and calculating the mean decreased mean square error. One can 
determine how many optimal predictors could best explain the outcome by seeing a 
substantial drop in mean squared error (MSE).
Detailed descriptions of the random forest classification can be found in the Sup­
plemental Materials (see Appendix 2). Readers who are interested in this approach 
can refer to Breiman (2001a, 2001b), Biau and Scornet (2016), and Grömping (2009).
Supplementary analysis. We undertook a supplementary analysis using the classi­
cal logistic regression strategy to compare how the findings obtained from machine 
learning would be different from findings from traditional statistical models.
4  Results
4.1  Preliminary analysis
Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics. There were 251 (17.2%) resilient students in 
Hong Kong. The correlation coefficients between explanatory variables and academic 
resilience ranged from − 0.20 (discriminating school climate) to 0.28 (meta-cogni­
tion: assess credibility). T-tests (t = 30.81, p < 0.01) indicated that the reading achieve­
ment of resilient students (Mean = 641.03, SD = 36.78) was significantly higher than 
non-resilient students (Mean = 476.01, SD = 83.17). Meanwhile, the T-test and cor­
responding Cohen’s d value showed that resilient students significantly scored higher 
than non-resilient students in many of the personal and social-contextual factors.
4.2  Primary analysis: Random forest classification
In the first step, we constructed a random forest classification model with all 30 
exploratory variables to provide a comprehensive picture of their relative importance 
in predicting academic resilience. Figure 2 descends the relative importance of each 
predictor based on the MDAs. The variable with a larger MDA indicates it is more 
important than other variables as it contributes more to model accuracy. For exam­
ple, meta-cognition: assess credibility is the most important variable because of its

largest value of MDA. Furthermore, the value of the AUC was 0.81, indicating the 
high accuracy of this model in distinguishing resilient and non-resilient students. 
The accuracy value (i.e., the proportion of correct predictions) of random forest in 
predicting academic resilience was 0.83 (95%CI =[0.79,0.87]).
To identify the set of most important variables predicting academic resilience, we 
undertook a ten-fold cross-validation analysis. The results indicated that the marginal 
reduction of MSE was minimal after the eighth variable (see Table S1 and Figure S1 
in Supplementary Materials), indicating that the top eight variables were sufficient 
to predict resilient students. As shown in Fig. 2, the top eight variables were (1) 
meta-cognition: assess credibility (i.e., reading strategies for assessing the quality 
and the credibility of the reading sources), (2) joy of reading (i.e., intrinsic motivation 
for reading), (3) teacher-directed instruction (i.e., teacher are the ones who transmit 
the knowledge and controls the learning processes in the classroom), (4) perception 
of difficulty of the PISA test (i.e., students’ perceived difficulty with reading test), 
(5) sense of belonging to school (i.e., perceptions of being accepted, valued, and 
included in their school), (6) discriminating school climate (i.e., teachers’ attitudes 
towards stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination), (7) self-efficacy (i.e., individ­
ual’s perceived capacity of performing specific tasks), and (8) perceived teacher’s 
interest (i.e., the teacher’s interest in teaching as perceived by the students). The 
results showed that all variables positively predicted academic resilience, except for 
the perception of difficulty of the PISA test and discriminating school climate, which 
negatively predicted academic resilience.
4.3  Supplementary analysis: logistic regression
The accuracy value of logistic regression in predicting academic resilience was 0.68 
(95%CI =[0.64, 0.73]), which was lower than our machine learning result which was 
0.83. Hence, we use the machine learning approach as our main analysis, but inter­
ested readers can refer to the Supplemental Materials (see Table S2) for the logistic 
regression estimates.
5  Discussion
The overall aim of the present study was to examine how personal and social-con­
textual factors facilitate or hinder academic resilience in the Hong Kong context. The 
present study ranked the relative importance of 30 variables measured by PISA 2018 
and identified eight top predictors of resilience among Hong Kong students.
First, capitalizing on the machine learning approach and the integrated set of vari­
ables provided by PISA 2018, our study extends previous research by providing a 
holistic understanding of how multiple personal and social-contextual factors jointly 
predicted academic resilience. Lee and Shute’s (2010) framework, which assumes 
that student learning is determined by several different personal and social-contextual 
factors, provides a solid theoretical underpinning for our study.
Compared with past studies that only focused on a few variables, we captured a 
more comprehensive picture of academic resilience by integrating variables drawn

from several different theoretical perspectives into one model, such as metacognition 
(meta-cognition: assess credibility; Flavell, 1979), intrinsic motivation (joy of read­
ing; Ryan & Deci, 2000; perceived teacher’s interest, Schiefele & Schaffner, 2015), 
self-efficacy (perception of difficulty of the PISA test and self-efficacy; Bandura, 
2001), and teaching strategy (teacher-directed instruction; Caro et al., 2016). Our 
findings supported that academic resilience is a complex phenomenon that should be 
researched based on different theoretical insights; focusing on a few variables might 
provide fragmented findings.
Second, in terms of the role of personal factors in predicting academic resilience, 
our results indicated that students who use sophisticated learning strategies and have 
greater motivation are more likely to be resilient. These findings corroborate previ­
ous research as these factors could improve students’ self-regulation, persistence, 
and willingness to engage in their studies (e.g., Borman & Overman, 2004; Cheung, 
2014; Murayama & Elliot, 2012; Thorsen et al., 2021).
The most important personal factor is meta-cognition: assessing credibility 
(ranked 1st). When students use metacognitive strategies, they are more likely to 
perceive the input and output information as meaningful and get cognitively involved 
in it, which contributes to better learning outcomes (Vrugt & Oort, 2008). In a study 
focusing on academic resilience, Cheung et al. (2014) found that socioeconomically 
disadvantaged students with adaptive metacognition are more likely to achieve aca­
demic resilience in the Asian context.
Joy of reading (ranked 2nd) is rooted in self-determination theory and is defined 
as the most autonomous type of motivation, indicating that students learn reading for 
their inherent interests and enjoyment (Ryan & Deci, 2000). When intrinsically moti­
vated, students are more likely to proactively engage in learning activities, obtain 
high academic achievement, and increase the likelihood of academic resilience 
(Cheung et al., 2014; Taylor et al., 2014; Thorsen et al., 2021).
The academic self-efficacy variables of perception of difficulty of the PISA test 
(ranked 4th) and self-efficacy in reading (ranked 7th) are identified as critical vari­

help socioeconomically disadvantaged students obtain academic success (Cheung,

Third, social-contextual factors also play an integral role in predicting academic 
resilience. Our result showed that an inclusive and supportive environment contrib­
utes to academic resilience (Borman & Rachuba, 2000; Cefai, 2007). The social-
contextual factors of teacher-directed instruction (ranked 3rd), sense of belonging 
to school (ranked 5th), and perceived teachers’ interest (ranked 8th) were identi­
fied. These findings corroborate existing research showing the importance of the 
environment for students’ optimal development. For example, a meta-analytic study 
conducted by Stockard et al. (2018) showed that well-designed instruction could 
facilitate students’ academic success. Korpershoek et al. (2020) found that a sense of 
belonging to school was positively associated with greater motivation, better socio­
emotional functioning, and higher academic achievement. In another study, Schiefele 
and Schaffner (2015) found that teachers' interests could optimize instructional prac­
tice and motivation. Our results converge with these empirical findings highlighting 
the need to design optimal learning contexts for students.
Our results also demonstrated that a discriminating school climate could lead to 
a decreased likelihood of academic resilience. This finding corroborates previous 
research (e.g., Aldridge et al., 2016). A high level of discrimination at school signifies 
the absence of an inclusive environment for all students. Students who bear socio­
economically disadvantaged positions are more likely to perceive discrimination at 
school than their advantaged peers and get less support from their teachers. Hence, an 
inclusive atmosphere might be especially important for facilitating resilience.
The supplementary analysis indicated that the results of the random forest and 
logistic regression estimates are broadly similar, lending greater confidence in our 
results. However, there were also some minor differences. These differences are not 
surprising because random forest regression extracts patterns from data automati­
cally without explicitly presuming relationships among variables (e.g., non-linear 
relationship; Bzdok et al., 2017), while classical logistic regression is based on the 
linear assumption. Hence, one advantage of applying machine learning in research­
ing academic resilience is to identify some complex relationships that cannot be cap­
tured by traditional statistical models.
6  Theoretical and practical implications
The present study contributes to the literature in the following ways. First, this study 
demonstrates the utility of an integrative framework in resilience research, which 
enabled us to understand how different personal and social-contextual factors pre­
dicted resilience. This study addresses the limitations of past studies that only focused 
on a limited set of explanatory variables.
Second, this study enriches previous research by introducing machine learning 
in academic resilience research. Compared with the widely used classical statistics

F. Wang et al.
1 3
(e.g., logistic regression), machine learning is more appropriate for large-scale edu­
cational data with many participants and highly correlated variables. The application 
of machine learning contributes to academic resilience research by achieving sub­
stantive-methodological synergy, using new methodological approaches to answer 
substantively important research questions.  . On the one hand, machine learning 
could analyze a large number of variables without the problems of multicollinear­
ity and overfitting (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Hence, it enables the examination 
of how multiple personal and social-contextual factors jointly predicted academic 
resilience based on the integrative framework. Compared with previous research 
that only focused on a few variables (e.g., Thorsen et al., 2021), we enriched the 
academic resilience research by providing a holistic understanding of multiple per­
sonal and social-contextual predictors of academic resilience. Compared with previ­
ous evidence that “everything seems to work” (Hattie, 2009, p.1), this study extends 
previous research by showing that some predictors might play a more important role 
than others. For example, to our best knowledge, this study was the first to emphasize 
the most critical role of meta-cognition: assess credibility in predicting academic 
resilience. Meanwhile, our study showed better accuracy of random forest model in 
predicting academic resilience than classical logistic regression, which is consistent 
with the research conducted by Couronné et al. (2018). They compared the perfor­
mance of random forest and logistic regression based on 243 real datasets and found 
that random forest classification performed better than logistic regression according 
to the considered accuracy measures in approximately 69% of 243 real datasets. On 
the whole, our results support the increasing use of machine learning in educational 
research.
To date, machine learning has been widely used in neuroscience (e.g., Smith et 
al., 2013), politics (e.g., McAlexander & Mentch, 2020), and psychology (Depren 
& Depren, 2021), among others. However, its use in the educational context remains 
relatively limited. Our study shows the potential applicability of machine learning in 
the education domain.
Third, the present study contributes to the educational practice. The results of 
PISA suggested that only a small percentage of socioeconomically disadvantaged 
students reached top proficiency levels in reading. For example, only 2.9% of socio­
economically disadvantaged students were top achievers compared with their 17.4% 
of advantaged peers, and less than 2% of disadvantaged students were top achievers 
in 51 countries and economies (OECD, 2019a). Mitigating the achievement gaps 
caused by socioeconomic disparities is a critical issue in educational equity (Destin et 
al., 2019; King & Trinidad, 2021). The eight top factors found in this study could be 
potentially targeted by educational interventions. The insights from this study might 
serve to inform policymakers, administrators, and teachers who are interested in lev­
eling the playing field for disadvantaged students.

7  Limitations and directions for future research
There are several limitations of this study. First, only Hong Kong students were 
included in this study. Cross-cultural generalizability may be limited. Therefore, we 
encourage researchers to extend our analysis to other cultural contexts.
Second, the specific theoretical mechanisms that link the top predictors of resil­
ience to resilience need to be investigated. This study only focused on investigating 
the direct predictive effect of exploratory variables on academic resilience. There 
might be more complex relationships between variables such as mediation and mod­
eration, which are beyond the scope of the current study. Researchers may need to 
draw on other approaches such as experimental or causal modeling approaches to 
understand these theoretical mechanisms.
Third, we exclusively focused on personal and social-contextual factors as they 
are more malleable. Many studies have also revealed that structural factors (e.g., gen­
der, age, and school size; Cordero & Mateos-Romero, 2021) play a role in academic 
resilience. However, we purposefully excluded them as these factors are not easy to 
amend and cannot be viable intervention targets. Future studies are recommended to 
explore more on how structural factors predict academic resilience.
Fourth, the nature of the PISA design is cross-sectional, which prevents us from 
drawing causal conclusions. Hence, experimental or longitudinal designs can be car­
ried out to validate the effectiveness of the top factors identified in our study.
8  Conclusions
This study provides empirical insights into the factors predicting academic resilience 
in Hong Kong. Random forest classification was used to compare 30 personal and 
social-contextual factors relevant to academic resilience. Eight top factors that best 
predicted academic resilience were identified, including meta-cognitive strategies, 
joy of reading, teacher-directed instruction, perception of difficulty of the PISA test, 
sense of belonging to school, discriminating school climate, self-efficacy, and per­
ceived teacher’s interest. These findings provide evidence-based information for 
stakeholders to help socioeconomically disadvantaged students achieve academic 
success..
