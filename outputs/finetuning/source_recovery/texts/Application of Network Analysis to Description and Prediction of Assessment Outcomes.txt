ABSTRACT
With the use of computerized testing, ordinary assessments can capture both 
answer accuracy and answer response time. For the Canadian Programme for 
the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) numeracy and 
literacy subtests, person ability, person speed, question difficulty, question 
time intensity, fluency (rate), person fluency (skill), question fluency (load), 
pace (rank of response time within question), and person pace were 
assessed. Undirected Gaussian Graphical Model networks of the measures 
based on partial correlations were predictive of the measures as nodes. The 
population-based model extrapolated well to individual person estimations. 
Finally, it was shown that the “training” Canadian model generalized with 
minor differences to four other English-speaking PIAAC assessments (USA, 
Great Britain, Ireland, and New Zealand). Thus, the undirected network 
approach provides a heuristic that is both descriptive and predictive. 
However, the model is not causal and can be taken as an example of 
“mutualism.”

Introduction
With the advent of computerized testing, ordinary assessments can capture both answer accuracy 
(score) and answer response time (De Boeck & Jeon, 2019; Kyllonen & Zu, 2016). The response time 
(rt) information has proven useful for test design (Baldwin et al., 2021; Choe et al., 2018; Van der 
Linden & Xiong, 2013), motivation evaluation (Goldhammer, 2015; Lafit et al., 2019; Wise & Kuhfeld, 
2021), enhancement of accuracy measurement (Bolsinova & Molenaar, 2018; Van der Linden, 2009), 
defining “feeling of knowing” as pace (person rank of response time by question) (Thompson et al., 
2009, 2013), and test accomodations (Margolis & Feinberg, 2020; Rios et al., 2020; Sireci et al., 2005). 
Precision teaching (Kubina, 2012) and curriculum-based measurement (Cummings & Petscher, 2016) 
use fluency (rate or speed-accuracy tradeoff) as the primary metric to evaluate student progress and 
determine competence in the elementary and special education school setting. Fluency evaluation also 
has broader uses in such areas as risk assessment (Aichele et al., 2015; Deary et al., 2010) or skills 
training, e.g., medical procedural skills (Levy et al., 2016; Lydon et al., 2019).
Previous research has shown the above observables may be interdependent above and beyond 
inherent formative relationships. For example, more difficult questions tend to be associated with 
longer response times (Molenaar & de Boeck, 2018; Partchev & De Boeck, 2012), and person speed 
may be correlated with person ability (Goldhammer, 2015; Tikhomirova et al., 2020). In addition, 
person fluency (skill) and question fluency (load) function as the important mediators between the 
exogenous variables of person ability and person speed and question difficulty and question time 
intensity in the determination of fluency (Thompson, 2018).

Assuming the background cited above, it would be desirable to have a sparse representation of 
the joint distribution of the observed indicators. Conventionally, structural equation modeling 
(SEM) provides a general framework for modeling and analyzing such multivariate data (Klein, 
2016). Further, SEM can specify relationships between latent constructs and observed indicators 
as well as estimate interrelationships of dependent variables simultaneously. These relationships 
are typically characterized by comparison of the variance-covariance matrix of the model to the 
observed matrix. For the case of conjoint analysis of response score and response time, the 
bivariate item response theory (BGL-IRT) provides a good example (Molenaar et al., 2015). It is 
noteworthy that such modeling can subsume other modeling approaches within its framework, 
e.g., the hierarchical model of (Van der Linden, 2007) and the models of Ferrando & Lorenzo-Sev 
(2007a, 2007b) and Ranger & Wolgast (2019). However, it is important to observe that variance- 
covariance matrices capture only marginal associations between variables and are not an appro­
priate tool for capturing the true functional connections between them. For example, a “collider 
artifact” can exist when a substantial connection between a pair of nodes results from a common 
connection to a third party node even if the two nodes are not directly connected (Rohrer, 2018).
An alternate approach (Borsboom & Cramer, 2013) which can address such problems has been 
developed (network analysis) in the specific instantiation of Gaussian Graphical Models (GGMs). In this 
case, rather than modeling the covariance matrix as in SEM, the inverse of the covariance matrix is 
considered, generally transformed to the form of partial correlations. Thus, every GGM and SEM model 
are similar in that they imply constrained covariance structures (Epskamp et al., 2017). In GGM 
networks, models are depicted graphically with “nodes” representing the variables and “edges” repre­
senting partial correlations among the variables. The resulting networks can be either directed (path 
models) or undirected (pairwise Markov random fields). In addition, GGMs are often “pruned” or 
“regularized” to remove edges not deemed to be salient based on independent model consideration, e.g., 
L1 minimization (Friedman et al., 2008) or thresholding (Lafit et al., 2019). Such simplification is likely 
to facilitate generalization to new data as well as enhance model interpretability due to greater simplicity.
An advantage of the partial correlation approach is that the partial correlations measure the direct 
connection between two nodes by regressing out the effects of the other nodes in the network (Williams 
et al., 2020). This provides a firm foundation for eventual consideration of causal relationships in the 
network, e.g., a partial correlation of zero implies an absence of direct connection between two nodes given 
all the other nodes. However, when comparing directed and undirected models, directed models necessa­
rily behave as causal systems and typically involve assumptions that are hard to justify, e.g., absence of 
shared confounders or reciprocal influences. Acknowledging this problem, the “mutualism” paradigm has 
been advanced (H. L. J. Van der Maas et al., 2006; H. Van der Maas et al., 2017) i.e., undirected GGM 
networks as explanations in complex dynamical systems where nodes may affect one another.
In response to McFarland’s critique of the use of partial correlations (McFarland, 2020), (Kan et al., 
2020) provided a detailed description of the differences between traditional latent variable modeling 
and the GGM network analysis, emphasizing the distinction between the use of ordinary correlation 
matrices (SEM) and inverse matrices (GGM). Of special interest here, he showed that an undirected 
network model (“mutualism”) provided better fit than three other distinct latent variable models. 
While these results might be restricted to the particular WAIS-IV intelligence scales analyzed, it 
a priori seems reasonable that such modeling approaches could generalize to other psychometric 
contexts, e.g., the assessment analyses to be considered below.
Thus, partial correlation GGMs may provide an exploratory filtering procedure to select the most 
statistically robust information about the influence of a specific variable on the correlation structure of 
the system under analysis. In addition, because GGM edge partial coefficients can be considered as 
rescaled regression coefficients, GGM models can be justified as variable selection modalities for 
a series of regressions in which each node is “explained” by its attached neighbors (Haslbeck & 
Waldorp, 2020). With those regressions, it should be possible to evaluate if regression predictions 
approximate the raw data and evaluate the extent to which confounders (nonlinearity, collinearity, 
heteroscedascity, etc.) affect network interpretation.

Given this background, the goals of this paper are two-fold:
(1) To determine if undirected network analyses can provide coherent descriptive models for the 
major outcomes of assessments, and
(2) To determine if the resulting models are predictive of the major outcomes of assessments.
If these goals were to be met, the resulting heuristic could provide the platform from which more 
comprehensive comparative analyses would originate.
Materials and methods
All analyses were conducted in R computing environment (R Core Team, 2020). All available data for 
English speakers (Canada, USA, Great Britain, Ireland, and New Zealand) were downloaded from the 
Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC) public use files archive 
(Https://Webfs.Oecd.Org/Piaac/Puf-Data/SPSS/). The data were then subsetted to include only native 
speakers and multiple-choice questions for numeracy and literacy. In addition, only those individuals 
who completed all 46 of presented questions were included. Multiple choice scores were converted to 
binary 0/1 (incorrect/correct) and paired with the corresponding response times converted to seconds. 
Pace was calculated as the percentile rank (slowest to fastest) of person response time by question. All 
data and computer code used for this paper are publicly accessible (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9. 
figshare.14818104).
Consistent with PIAAC practice, scores were estimated by 2PL IRT in TAM (Robitzsch et al., 2020) 
with person ability (ability) and question difficulty (qd) taken as person-specific means and question- 
specific means of those estimates. Person speed (pspeed) and question time intensity (qti) were 
calculated from log-transformed response times by crossing person and question in linear mixed 
modeling (Bates et al., 2015; Davidson & Martin, 2013; Harald Baayen & Milin, 2010) and taking the 
median of the exponentiated predicted person and question response times.
Fluency was calculated at each point by dividing the predicted percent score at that point by the 
observed response time; the resulting values were scaled by multiplication by 100 for better 
interpretability. Person skill (skill) and question inverse load (iload) were calculated from log- 
transformed fluency by crossing person and question in linear mixed modeling and taking the 
median of the exponentiated predicted fluencies. The term iload is used to emphasize that as scores 
go up or response time goes down, fluency increases. It can be noted that the skill and iload 
measures are approximately collinear with data obtained by using means and medians to calculate 
fluency at the level of person and question (R2 = 0.90 and 0.99+, respectively). When indicated, data 
were ordered quantile (orq) transformed with the orderNorm function of bestNormalize (Peterson 
& Cavanaugh, 2020).
For Yen’s Q3 dimensionality analysis, the tam.modelfit function of TAM (Robitzsch et al., 2020) 
was used. For regularization comparisons, the biserial function of the psych package (Revelle, 2021) 
was used as well as the base R Pearson and Spearman correlation methods and the polychoric cor_auto 
function from qgraph (Epskamp et al., 2012) (derived from lavaan (Rosseel, 2012)). Finally, the Knight 
version of Kendall correlations (Knight, 1966) instantiated in the pcaPP package (Filzmoser et al., 
2021) was used. Partial correlation GGM network models were estimated with the qgraph package 
(Epskamp et al., 2012). Model fitting of the resulting networks was evaluated with the ggmFit 
procedure of qgraph. Estimation of groups within networks was done with the EGAnet package 
(Hudson & Ca, 2021). Ordinary least-squares (OLS) modeling of node interconnections used base 
R functions of glm for score and lm for continuous variables. Area under the curve (AUC) for score 
was evaluated in the ROC function of the EPI package (Carstensen et al., 2021). Collinearity of the 
resulting regression models was determined by the VIF function of car (Fox & Weisberg, 2019). 
Quantile-based regression with quantile sheets (Schnabel & Eilers, 2013) used the quantSheets 
procedure in GAMLSS (Rigby & Stasinopoulos, 2005).

Results
Measure analysis
Figure 1 displays the relationships of the parameters used in this analysis inverted back to their 
formative constructs. Except for the relationship of person speed to person pace (pseudo-R2 = 0.827 by 
asymptotic regression), all measures can be approximated as linear with ordinary least squares (OLS) 
coefficients of determination (R2) that exceed 0.90. The person variables display heterogeneity which 
will be addressed later by person-specific subsetting.
Dimensionality analysis
Figure 2 displays the empirical cumulative frequency distribution of interitem correlations (upper 
triangle) of the combined Numeracy and Literacy score subsets of the selected Canadian PIAAC data 
after item response analysis (2PL). The vertical lines (absolute correlations of 0.1 or 0.2) indicate 
cutoffs that might be considered “salient” by some authors (Christensen et al., 2017). Only 3.7% and 
0.13% of observed intercorrelations exceed those limits, respectively. As a consequence, the pooled 
data were considered as “essentially unidimensional” with respect to score and not further divided.

polychoric, and Kendall correlations were examined as alternatives. Given the moderate size of the 
data set, there is high statistical power to detect even small deviations from zero in the correlations.

Network description
For reference purposes, Figure 4 displays the unregularized Kendall correlations of the data

Figure 5 shows the GGM Kendall partial correlation network at the chosen 0.14 regularization

orq, speed.orq, skill.orq, and ppace.orq). As shown in Figure 6, person-specific node R2s cluster

Discussion
Measures
Previous work has established that scores and response times can be used to construct such person 
parameters as ability and speed. Similarly, questions can be characterized in terms of easiness (or 
difficulty) and time intensity (or time easiness). Taken together these six parameters or variants 
thereof provide the basis for modeling of assessment outcomes. The interaction of speed and score 
is typically assumed in terms of a correlation. For the data analyzed here, this Kendall partial 
correlation association in the orq domain is low (−0.09, Figure 4). Thus, the model depicted in 
Figure 5 has no direct edge between score and rrt.orq.
However, in some real-life assessment contexts, the relationship of score to speed, generally 
assessed in terms of fluency, is important to evaluate competence. Operational measurement of 
fluency is problematic when binary scoring is involved. For this paper, fluency was defined at the 
response level as the probability of a correct response divided by the observed response time at 
the response. Justification for this choice is seen in Figure 1 where person and question averaged 
values of fluency (skill and load) are seen to be consonant with independently assessed averages/ 
medians of score/response time at the levels of persons and questions.

The remaining variables of pace and person pace are based on ranks within question. This 
nonparametric transformation thereby minimizes the role of question. The operating assumption is 
that examinees should maintain a constant relative place in response time within questions if rapid 
recognition, uncertainty, or other influences are not influential. As shown in Figure 1, to a good 
approximation (pseudo-R2 = 0.827) this assumption is true. However, speed and ppace do not 
precisely align (overall nonlinearity and heteroscedascity is observed). Thus, pace tends to account 
for the deviation of response time from otherwise expected values, i.e., functions as a “residual” 
variable. This helps to explain why pace has such a strong edge to response time in the network model 
of Figure 5 but has weak collinearity (VIF = 1.66, Table 2) in the corresponding regression.
Regularization
Having constructed variables with close relationships to the raw data, continuous measures were 
standardized by the ordered quantile transformation in order to permit construction of GGMs. 
Because binary score was incorporated into the potential network analysis, various nonparametric 
methods were evaluated for threshold regularization potential. The RMSEA statistic was chosen as 
selection criterion because it is less influenced by sample size than many other measures (Jak et al., 
2021) . Kendall correlations allowed the highest threshold cutoffs.
Choice of RMSEA cutpoint for “significance” is problematic and will determine the number of 
edges to be considered in the network. The choice of RMSEA = 0.083 (partial correlation = 0.14) used 
here may be considered unduly generous (“mediocre fit,” (MacCallum et al., 1996)), but as has been 
noted above, this may be conservative in the sense that only one node (qte.orq) still displays high

Description of regularized network
The partial correlation network described in Figure 5 is informative. Many potential edges are 
absent. This absence implies a functional independence between the respective nodes at the 
threshold level imposed. However, this lack of relationship does not appear to have major consequences 
with respect to node prediction. In fact the imposition of partial correlation network thresholding can 
be considered as a form of variable importance selection. If adjoining edges are considered as predictors 
in OLS regression, fit of continuous org domain variables would generally be considered as satisfactory 
(R2 ranges from 0.86 to 0.97). All regression coefficients are “significant” by frequentist criteria; 
however, note the degrees of freedom are equal to or exceed 38,820 so that small coefficients are non- 
ignorable.
Collinearity within the variables is also a potential concern because of the multiple formative 
relationships within the dataset. However, observed VIFs are tolerable (<5.87) except for the qte.orq 
covariate. As would be expected, removal of the variable with highest VIF (iload.orq) reduces 
collinearity (maximum VIF = 4.67) with little effect on R2 (0.94 to 0.91). For either case, OLS fit 
would generally be considered acceptable. As already remarked, AUC of binary score was 0.854. 
This is considered “large” in comparison to Cohen’s d statistic and biserial correlation (Rice & 
Harris, 2005).

Person prediction from the population orq model
Given the assumption of population model utility, it is of interest to consider whether the model can 
be applied to the level of persons. Because of the person-specific subsetting that is involved in such 
person measures, the person measures of ability, speed, ppace, and skill are ignored in subsequent 
regressions. In spite of this limitation, as shown in Figure 6, person AUC and R2 fits distribute about 
the population mean R2. In fact, for rrt.orq and fluency.orq, person-specific fits generally exceed the 
population mean. This may reflect the reality that the overall data has deliberately not been purged of 
“outliers” and in contrast most individuals behave consistently. Consequently, the population model 
can be used to roughly approximate individual responses. More granular evaluations might be 
indicated to evaluate score and pace distributions because of substantial numbers below the popula­
tion means.
Data prediction after inversion from the orq domain to raw data
The results discussed above give some confidence that analyses within the orq domain provide 
useful insights. However, more confidence in such conclusions would be merited if they extra­
polate well to the original raw data metric. Fortunately, the orq transformation provides 
a predict function that provides a direct mapping from the org transform to the original data. 
This means that prediction may be done in the orq domain and predicted expected values 
inverted to the raw data domain. These raw inverted values can then be compared to the actual 
observed data values. Figure 9 compares OLS R2 values of orq-inverted expected data versus 
observed values in the raw data domain (score, seconds, proportions, and rates). Surprisingly, 
very little predictiveness is lost; some variables actually gain power by the raw data transforma­
tion (located above the identity line). Thus, fidelity to the orq-based originating network model 
is supported at the level of raw data.
While the inverted fits appear satisfactory from the raw data perspective, the viewpoint is restricted 
to population averages and cannot reflect the overall heterogeneity documented in Table 1. Quantile 
regression with penalized B-splines addresses this concern with plots of the orq-model inversed 
expected data versus the observed raw data. This independent spline-based assessment derived from 
the network model reveal that OLS linear fits closely track the 50th centile over most of the observed 
data range but that heteroscedascity has to be captured by explicit consideration of quantile distribu­
tions. In general, especially over non-tail domains, the quantile predictions maintain constant rela­
tionships, albeit with variation in effective slopes by quantile.

A comment is appropriate here. Most analysts working on related problems report their data fits in 
terms of optimization of statistics such as log-likelihoods or information criteria (Aikaike’s, Schwarz’s 
Bayesian, deviance information, etc.). To the best of my knowledge, these workers have never exposed 
their preferred data fits as expected versus observed results in the raw data metrics. This makes it 
impossible to evaluate if the results reported here are idiosyncratic or reflective of more typical behaviors.
Replication of network analysis in other English-speaking countries
The analysis reported above has been restricted to Canadian observations. If these results are 
considered as “training” data and the network evaluation methods used are considered as “gold 
standards,” then generalizability of the approach can be assessed by application to other similar 
PIAAC assessments. Here “similarity” is taken to mean other English-speaking countries. It has 
been demonstrated that cultural differences can affect test taking strategies (Avvisati et al., 2019) so 
this restriction is a minimal one. As summarized in Figure 8 and Table 3, overall skeletons are similar 
with reasonable correspondence between observed edge weights (correlations exceeding 0.95). With 
respect to edge presence, sensitivity and specificity are also reasonable (correlations exceeding 0.84). 
However, there are differences in edge presence by country (sums of false positives and false negatives 
range from 2 to 4 based on 19 Canadian edges). Thus, the network methodology captures similarities 
between different networks but also details where differences exist. These differences could provide the 
basis for further evaluation of differential node function, process analysis, background effects, etc. 
Such considerations are beyond the scope of this paper and are not explored further.
Caveats
There are numbers of concerns that must be recognized about the analysis reported here:
(1) The populations studied were adult computer-competent native English speakers. Potential 
sources of differential item function, such as gender, age, socioeconomic status, education, etc. 
(Goldhammer et al., 2017), were not evaluated. It is also appreciated that cultural differences 
may influence assessment outcomes (Avvisati et al., 2019).
(2) Cutoffs used for regularization determine network complexity. Given the moderate sample size 
studied here, most cutoffs assume a certain degree of arbitrariness.
(3) Person and question measures have specific operational definitions. Alternative parameteriza­
tions are feasible which would likely change the particulars of observed outcomes.
(4) The analysis is only descriptive and predictive, not causal.
Conclusions
Partial-correlation-based GGMs identify and describe salient interrelationships between measures 
available to assessments in which both score and response times are collected. Regression-based 
analyses derived from the GGMs give useful information about the magnitude and complexity of 
those relationships. Thus, network analysis provides a useful assessment summary heuristic.
