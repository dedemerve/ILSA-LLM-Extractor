from PISA and cognitive ability studies

In recent years, some educational researchers have questioned the theory of a robust relationship between test
scores in international large-scale assessments such as PISA and lagged economic growth. However, both the
proponents and opponents of such a human capital approach have mainly focused on earlier growth patterns
during the twentieth century. Hence, the current article analysed the relationship between PISA 2000 and
economic growth in 2010–2019 for 31 countries, and composite cognitive skills for 80 countries and their relationships with economic growth in 2010–2019. The results indicate that PISA scores or other cognitive skill
measures could to some extent predict economic growth between 2010 and 2019, even when controlling for a
significant number of variables. In a consecutive analysis, the relations were substantially associated with Middle
Eastern countries because many countries from this region experienced political instability and decreasing oil
revenues throughout this period. Hence, it is possible that regional and contextual relationships affect these
patterns.

1. Introduction
As Komatsu and Rappleye (2017, 2019) rightfully pointed out, the
role of the Organization for Economic Development and Co-operation’s
(OECD) Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) in education policy and claims about far-reaching predictive validity (e.g.,
see Kane, 2013, for a validity analysis) and extrapolating the scores’
predictive value in relation to other areas, economic growth in particular (e.g., Hanushek & Woessman, 2008, Woessman, 2012), is a topic
worthy of empirical investigation. Many education scholars have criticised the PISA tests for a variety of reasons, including partial lack of
validity and reliability (e.g., Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2016; Sellar &
Lingard, 2013; Sjøberg, 2015; Serder & Jakobsson, 2016) and high costs
(Sjøberg, 2015). A broader set of critique includes, for example, misinterpretations of data, as well as potentially harmful education policy
implications (Centeno, 2021; Feniger & Lefstein, 2014; Rappleye et al.,
2020; Tröhler & Maricic, 2021; Waldow et al., 2014). However, as
Hanushek et al. (2019) and Sahlberg (2007) have stressed, the PISA tests
measure student literacy in three broad and important subjects (mathematics, science, and reading), include a large amount of meaningful
data, and have a high degree of representativeness due to large sample
sizes. Be that as it may, PISA data aggregated to the country level could
potentially be used to infer meaningful relationships between for

example students’ test scores and economic growth.
A problem with earlier studies, whether they question the PISA–economic growth theory or not (e.g., Hanushek & Woessman, 2008,
2012; Kamens, 2015; Stromquist, 2016; Komatsu & Rappleye, 2017,
2019) is that they fully or predominantly focus on earlier growth patterns and as such in a somewhat misguided way. Moreover, there are a
host of other model misspecifications that need to be addressed (e.g.,
Angrist et al., 2021; Breton, 2015; Schoellman, 2012). As is well-known
in economic history and human capital theory (e.g., Becker, 1962;
Easterly, 2014; Lee et al., 1994; Rodrik, 1995), the relationships between educational attainment and achievement (i.e., grades or test
scores), labor market investments, and economic growth are very
complex, context-dependent, and related to different development
stages. Even countries as similar as South Korea and Taiwan have
experienced different “comparative advantages” with regard to their
economic growth and developmental trajectories (Easterly, 2014; Lee
et al., 1994; Rodrik, 1995). It is also common knowledge that for
example Norway’s high economic growth is chiefly attributed to its
large oil resources (World Factbook, 2023), although perhaps partially
mediated by human capital factors. Trade incentives and state interventions may have a substantial effect at some stages. Moreover,
post-Soviet nations like Estonia experienced their rapid economic
growth at much later stages than the East Asian “tiger” economies

tiger”, Ireland, has experienced growth conditions quite different from

Furthermore, economic growth may boost educational achievement at
one development stage but lead to a “wealth effect” (i.e., less diligent
students) in another. Other important economic growth factors include,
for instance, foreign direct investment (Wang & Soong, 2009), corporate
tax rates (Lee & Gordon, 2004), income inequality (Erman & te Kaat,
2019), a well-developed financial system (Levine, 1997), and an ability
to attract appropriate foreign workers (Kamens, 2015). This calls the
entire scope of multiple-country quantitative research into question,
even regarding later development such as the 2000s onwards.
Nonetheless, the more recent years of international large-scale assessments (ILSAs) and economic growth appear to be a more suitable
and reliable area of research than the nexus between economic growth
and human capital (i.e., the quality and/or quantity of education and the
labor force) between about 1960–1990, and even 1990–2015 (Komatsu
& Rappleye, 2017). First and foremost, virtually all nations in the OECD
and its partner countries have already experienced their first stages of
economic growth. The growth miracle in East Asia occurred between
about 1960–2000 (Easterly, 2014), the three Baltic states and other
post-Soviet countries have already experienced substantial growth rates
the 1990s onwards (Boman, 2020), and so has Ireland (Breznitz, 2012).
Thus, a fruitful area of research constitutes the relationship between
PISA 2000 and the economic growth trajectories in 2010–2019 (after the
financial crisis but prior to the covid-19 pandemic). While there have
been some analyses with more recent data sources (Angrist et al., 2021),
these studies have not focused on the same years and have not taken
advantage of cognitive ability studies (e.g., Boman, 2023). The current
article examined such relationships among 31 nations for which a substantial amount of data exists. In addition, data from both cognitive
ability studies and various international scholastic surveys complemented the study with 80 country-level observations.

The current study adds to this strand of both critical and supporting
literature but focuses on later test scores (PISA 2000) and consecutive
economic growth (2010–2019).
2.2. Theoretical and empirical rationale
The real or potential relationships between educational achievement
(i.e., ‘quality’) and educational attainment (i.e., ‘quantity’) and economic growth may seem self-evident. Which nation is not interested in
increased economic growth? However, it is possible to criticize the
economic growth agenda for a number of reasons (Krueger & Lindahl,
2001; Boman, 2020), but as regards the specific nexus between international student assessments like PISA and economic growth it is
important to be congenial with the theoretical and empirical rationale of
the research agenda. Hanushek and Woessman (2020) underline the
following three major reasons for how educational quality indicators
like PISA scores, which supposedly measure cognitive skills that are
important for real life contexts (e.g., OECD, 2017, 13), may boost economic growth:
First, education can increase the human capital inherent in the labor
force, which increases labor productivity and thus transitional growth
toward a higher equilibrium level of output (…) Second, education can
increase the innovative capacity of the economy, and the development
of new technologies, products and processes promotes growth (…)
Third, education can facilitate the diffusion and transmission of
knowledge needed to understand and process new information and to
implement successfully new technologies devised by others, which again
promotes economic growth (…). (Hanushek & Woessman, 2020, p. 171)
As was emphasised in the preamble of this article, there might be
several other factors that may explain economic growth in a better way
than test scores from various ILSAs, such as corporate tax rates, foreign
direct investment (FDI), and income inequality (whose empirical ties
seem to be quite context-dependent, see Erman & te Kaat, 2019).
Furthermore, reverse causality (i.e., economic growth may lead to more
schooling or better test scores) might be an issue, although Hanushek
and Woessman (2020) explain why that has not likely been the case
among those countries that have been analysed in earlier studies (e.g.,
Hanushek & Woessman, 2008). Moreover, as was not particularly
underscored by Komatsu and Rappleye (2019, 2017), many of the
cross-country research articles on the ILSA–economic growth nexus
consisted of merely 12–16 countries (e.g., Lee & Barro, 2001; Hanushek
& Woessman, 2020). In a world with some 195 jurisdictions, it appears
as if this small selection constitutes a case study (e.g., Seawright &
Gerring, 2008) or convenience sample rather than a representative
sample which may be used to infer general patterns. To the defense of
Lee and Barro and Hanushek and Woessman, however, only a limited
number of countries participated in the early ILSAs (Hanushek &
Woessman, 2020) and it is customary to remove some countries with
outlier characteristics from a sample (e.g., Seawright & Gerring, 2008).
Nonetheless, it implies that the ILSA scores–economic growth relationships do not constitute a general pattern but a rather particular such.
At this point, a meaningful compromise may be to focus on OECD
countries, as well as additional partner countries. The availability and
quality of data do to some degree decide how many countries that are
meaningful to include in an empirical analysis. The number of countries
and the timeframe may then impact the degree of ‘external validity’ (i.e.,
generalizability) of such a research endeavor (e.g., Kane, 2013).

2. Background
2.1. Related literature
Kamens (2015) examined the relations between early and later test
scores in ILSAs (International Large-scale Assessments such as IEA (International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement),
TIMSS (Trends in International Mathematics and Science), and PISA
between 1958 and 2011 and found that the test scores within countries
were rather weakly correlated with later test scores. However, the PISA
scores from different surveys are moderately to highly correlated. The
inconsistencies between the ILSA test scores within countries might
indicate that consecutive growth is not related to high test scores in
previous ILSAs. Stromquist (2016), akin to Kamens (2016), criticised the
models and findings which were proposed by Hanushek and Woessman
(H&W) in many of their studies due to methodological simplicity and
possibly misguided conclusions.
Komatsu & Rappleye (2017) re-analysed the data of H&W using
linear regression models. They found that there were no meaningful
relations between test scores in early ILSAs and lagged economic
growth. Moreover, they criticised H&W for relying too much on the
probability values (p-values) instead of the R2 values, which means the
statistical power of the regression models. Moreover, according to the
same authors, H&W had not properly examined potential reverse causality (i.e., that economic growth leads to higher test scores in this
context). In a follow-up study, Komatsu and Rappleye (2019) used the
Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies
(PIAAC) data to analyse the relations between cognitive skills of the
future labor force and consecutive economic growth. Their results were
corroborated by this alternative approach, which casts further doubt on
the findings of H&W. On the other hand, Heller-Sahlgren and Jordahl

In the first part of the current study, aggregated data on 31 OECD or
OECD-related partner countries which participated in the first PISA

survey (2000) were used. An average PISA score for mathematics, science and reading across the 2000 survey was created for all countries.
Thus, three values were combined into a single average score. The
dependent variable was GDP PC growth in percentage between 2010
and 2019. This gap is related to the notion that a substantial lag must be
present for earlier levels of cognitive skills to have an influence on later
economic growth (Kamens, 2015). Thus, it is not the temporal change in
test scores that could logically predict economic scores (i.e., increasing
test scores predict larger growth, decreasing test scores predict smaller
growth), simply because the high-performing group of PISA participants
within a given country could not have an effect on economic growth
when they are approximately 15 years old. Instead, the supposedly
high-performing students should, according to human capital logic,
affect the economic growth rate, via the participation in the labor force,
at a later time (Komatsu & Rappleye, 2019, 2017), when they are
somewhere between 25 and 35 years old.
Because 2020 and 2021 were severely affected by the Covid-19
pandemic these two years were not included in the data, although
many countries were affected by Covid-19 in similar ways and thus may
have experienced a similar type of temporary economic decline. It is also
notable that 2010 constitutes the first year when the global financial
crisis had ended.

3.3. Independent variables
The main independent variable was PISA scores in 2000, whereas the
other predictor variables were related to the earlier literature on economic growth theory and research. As such they constituted control
variables and are as follows: the level of income inequality defined as the
Gini coefficient (a Gini coefficient of 100 % indicates maximum
inequality), corporate tax rates, labor force participation, years of
schooling, and foreign direct investment (FDI inflow) as a percentage of
GDP. In addition, prior GDP per capita in 2009 was included in order to
control for previous growth and economic development (e.g., Kamens,
2015).
The logic behind using PISA scores from the 2000 survey as predictors for later economic growth is that those students who took the
PISA test in 2000 have entered the workforce 10–15 years later and may
have more or less impact on the economic growth when they are adults.
Therefore, PISA scores from the 2003 and 2006 surveys were not
included. Higher PISA scores would predict higher economic growth,
according to the human capital theory as suggested by Hanushek and
Woessman (2008; see also Kamens, 2015; Komatsu & Rappleye, 2017).
Hence, countries such as Finland, Canada, Ireland, South Korea, and
Australia may have had high economic growth at a later time that
matches the assumption in a temporal sense.
All data on GDP per capita, and GDP PC growth, were collected from
IMF (imf.org 2024) for consistency. GDP per capita was log transformed
in SPSS. The other data sources were collected from KMPG (2021),
Macrotrends (2022), and OECD (2021). Most of the descriptive data are
displayed in Table 1.
In addition, a composite cognitive ability score (i.e., results from
various cognitive ability studies, in tandem with the results from various
ILSA surveys) for in total 80 countries were used in an alternative model
with the same outcome variable in robustness checks. These data are

3.4. Bivariate relationships
In the first step, a correlation matrix for the dependent and independent variables was created. This was, in part, to examine if the independent variables were intercorrelated, which may indicate
multicollinearity (e.g., see Cohen, 1988; Dohoo et al., 1997), and as a
first step prior to conducting the multiple regression models. These results are displayed in Tables 3 and 4. As expected, cognitive skills are
highly correlated with PISA scores, and both PISA and cognitive skills (i.
e., scores based on cognitive ability studies) are correlated with GDP per
capita (2009) and democracy to different extents. That indicates that
PISA scores and cognitive ability scores should not be included in the
same regression models and that both seem to measure a similar
cognitive skill construct (Rindermann, 2007; Warne, 2022).
3.5. Regression models
Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model were conducted,
similar to many earlier studies in this strand of research (e.g., Komatsu &
Rappleye, 2017). Independent variables that had an intercorrelation
above r = 0.70 were not included in the same regression models,
regardless of the multicollinearity index scores. In accordance with
Komatsu and Rappleye (2019, 2017) attention was paid to the statistical
power of the models (i.e., the R2 values) and effect sizes and not just the
p-values. In concordance with, for example, Gustafsson (2016), the
adjusted R2 values were reported because of the relatively small number
of countries which are included in the analyses.
The analytical strategy was to include all predictors to identify their
effect sizes as well as the model fit statistic (R2). The lagged (time-series
regression) longitudinal model had the following constitution:

contextual factors.
Moreover, although the evidence is far from perfect it is also possible
that low-skilled immigration decreases GDP PC and average income to
some extent (e.g., Ruist, 2015). For instance, an ability to attract
high-skilled talent may increase growth (e.g., Boman, 2020; Kamens,
2015) and conversely, low-skilled migrants who are overrepresented as
welfare recipients impair the aggregated economic activity among citizens. That may happen in immigration intense countries like France, UK
and Sweden, perhaps largely independent of ILSA scores. On the other
hand, many ethnically homogenous nations like Finland and Japan have
experienced small growth in recent decades and may even take advantage of high-skilled migration. Nonetheless, future research may explore
such factors in more depth.

5.1. Limitations and directions for future research
This article has several limitations. First of all, only 31 countries
were included in the first step of the cross-country analysis. Although
some of the problems with regard to reliability and external validity
were alleviated by adding more countries in the second step of the
analysis, this second set of countries had another disadvantage, specifically a composite of both representative and non-representative
cognitive ability studies (Lynn & Becker, 2019). Furthermore, the
number of independent variables were limited. Moreover, some may still
consider the Becker and Lynn data set to be unreliable and misguiding
(Sear, 2022). Nonetheless, there are strong reasons to use it in regard to
some secondary analyses (Boman, 2023; Warne, 2022). As another
limitation it is important to stress that the growth trends did only cover
ten years, 2010–2019.
Because different time periods were examined, this study is also not
directly comparable to the research of for example Hanushek and
Woessman (2008), Kamens (2015), or Komatsu & Rappleye (2017).
However, the findings generally support the sound skepticism and,
indeed, criticism emphasised by Komatsu and Rappleye (2019, 2017)
and others while at the same time supporting the empirical findings of
Hanushek and Woessman (at least to a degree).
ILSAs such as PISA are important for a variety of reasons, including
the provision of a large amount of data and quite representative samples
from a large number of countries. Even though the representativeness of
samples and reliability of the scores might be questioned (e.g., Gustafsson, 2008; Rutkowski & Rutkowski, 2016), which also is the case
with annual GDP PC numbers (Easterly, 2014), this is not the main
concern. The problem seems to be misleading inferences and education
policy recommendations (Komatsu & Rappleye, 2017, 2019). Indeed,
human capital indicators such as PISA scores constitute one factor that
may or may not predict economic growth, but it does not, indeed, seem
to be a silver bullet as many contextual and exogenous growth factors
matter more (e.g., regional instability, foreign direct investment, previous economic growth or current level of GDP PC). Thus, more
cautionary interpretations seem required. While other education
scholars may have taken a more critical and interpretive approach in
regard to the PISA policy debate and similar policy orbits (e.g., Rappleye
et al., 2020; Sellar & Lingard, 2013; Sjøberg, 2015; Serder & Jakobsson,
