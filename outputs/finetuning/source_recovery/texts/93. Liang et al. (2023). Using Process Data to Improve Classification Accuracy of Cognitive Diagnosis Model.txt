Using Process Data to Improve Classification Accuracy of Cognitive
Diagnosis Model
Kangjun Liang, Dongbo Tu, and Yan Cai
School of Psychology, Jiangxi Normal University, Nanchang, Jiangxi, China
ABSTRACT
With the advance of computer-based assessments, many process data, such as response
times (RTs), action sequences, Eye-tracking data, the log data for collaborative problem-solv-
ing (CPS) and mouse click/drag becomes readily available. Findings from previous studies
(e.g., Peng et al., Multivariate Behavioral Research, 1–20, 2021; Xu, The British Journal of
Mathematical and Statistical Psychology, 73(3), 474–505, 2020; He & von Davier, Handbook of
research on technology tools for real-world skill development (pp. 750–777). IGI Global, 2016;
Man & Harring, Educational and Psychological Measurement, 81(3), 441–465, 2021) suggest a
substantial relationship between this human–computer interactive process information and
proficiency, which means these process data were potentially useful variables for psycho-
logical and educational measurement. To make full use of the process data, this paper aims
to combine two useful and easily available types of process data, including the mouse click/
drag traces and the response times, to the conventional cognitive diagnostic model (CDM)
to better understand individual’s response behavior and improve the classification accuracy
of existing CDM. Then the full Bayesian analysis using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
was employed to estimate the proposed model parameters. The viability of the proposed
model was investigated by an empirical data and two simulation studies. Results indicated
the proposed model combing both types of process data could not only improve the attri-
bute classification reliability in real data analysis, but also provide an improvement on item
parameters recovery and person classification accuracy.

cannot
be
used
to
evaluate

specific

nostic feedback (Templin & Henson, 2006;). In con-
trast with IRT models, CDMs can effectively capture
the complexities inherent in examinees’ cognitive
processes and structure and offer richer diagnostic
information to aid decision-making. Thus, several
cognitive diagnosis models utilizing item responses
and RTs models were developed (Peng et al., 2021;
Zhan et al., 2018).

As an auxiliary information, the number of actions
reflects the traces left by examinees in the computer

integrating the number of actions into the cognitive

variable (such as number of actions or RTs) can be
transferred through the correlation coefficient between
the person parameters: the larger the correlation coef-
ficient, the higher the mutual availability of informa-
tion and the greater the contribution to the parameter
estimation accuracy, and vice versa (Bolsinova &
Tijmstra, 2018; Ranger, 2013). So, it is possible to
improve the diagnostic accuracy of CDM by combin-
ing this kind process data when estimating the param-
eter of CDM. (2) The number of actions can help
educators deeply understand examinees and response
behaviors. For example, whether the examinees have
read the items carefully, whether the examinees have
comprehensive search information to answer item,
and so on. At the same time, with this type process
data, a more detailed classification can be done for
examinees with low ability. Such as, the examinee
with a score of "0" has a lot of traces left, which may
indicate that the examinee answers the item seriously

2. Joint cognitive diagnosis modeling for
response, response times and trace
(JCDM-RRT)
In this model, we consider three sources of informa-
tion in computer-based assessments. First, responses
provide us with information about the accuracy for
each test takers-item combination. Second, the RTs
result from the total time spent on the item by the
test taker. Third, the trace left by the test taker when
solving the problem, which is measured by the num-
ber of actions. A hierarchical modeling framework
will be developed that deploys separate models for the
responses, the RTs and the traces as measurement
models for ability and cognitive states (attributes pat-
tern), speed and trace, respectively.
Similar to previous studies, the hierarchical model-
ing
framework
with
two
related
structures
was
employed to combine the process data to the CDM to
improve its classification accuracy. Specifically, three
separate measurement models for responses, RTs and
traces respectively are specified at the first level, and
the joint distribution of the first-level model parame-
ters is defined at the second level. The dependencies
between the person and item parameters of these
models are constructed at the second level. The frame-
work is also shown in Figure 2.

The

In addition, we considered two MCMC chains with
20,000 iterations and the first 10,000 in each chain are
discarded as burn-in samples, the other 10,000 itera-
tions were retained for model parameter inferences.
The Gelman–Rubin convergence statistic ^R was com-
puted to assess the convergence of each parameter. ^R
< 1.1 indicates that the estimated value has converged
(Brooks & Gelman, 1998). In each replication, all ^R
were less than 1.05 generally, indicating good conver-
gence in the specified setting.

We used posterior predictive model checking (PPMC;

Specifically, we generated posterior predictive p values
(ppp values) of discrepancy measures for realizing and
replicating values separately for each type of observ-
ables (Ulitzsch et al., 2020). ppp values near 0.50 indi-
cate that there are no systematic differences (Gelman
et al., 2014). For item responses, we employed the
sum of the squared Pearson residuals as a discrepancy
measure. For logarithmized RTs, the sum of the
standardized error function was employed as a dis-
crepancy measure (Yan et al., 2003; Zhan et al., 2018).
For the Poisson model used for modeling the number
of actions, but the hierarchical Poisson distribution
model has a feature that is inflexible in that it restricts
the mean and variance to be equal (Edition, 2013).
The squared Pearson residuals or the standardized
error function cannot be used directly. Therefore, we
didn’t calculate posterior predictive p values (ppp val-
ues) for the number of actions.
We calculated the response and response time PPP

data set.

in real data example, the estimation times of MHO-
RDINA, JRT-DINA and JCDM-RRT were 1.91, 3.58;
and 6.5 hours respectively. In general, the estimated
time used by all model is acceptable, if the chain
length is shorter, the time required is even shorter.

to be aware of the conflict and to find ways to deal

This means that it is difficult for examinees to
obtain high scores by reading only one time. When
faced with multiple conflicting texts, examinees need
to read repeatedly to eliminate interfering information
in order to obtain high scores. Therefore, subjects
need to constantly switch texts and read each text
carefully, then left many traces (i.e. the number of
actions) during the exam.
Therefore, when the score is higher, the number of
actions of the examinees will be more. Next, the
results of this question are explained. According to
the examinee’s ability and traces, it is divided into
three categories. In the first category, if examinees
have a higher score and leave more traces, then we
think that the examinee seriously evaluates and care-
fully selects information when they are answering, and
weighs various solutions, scheme and choose the opti-
mal solution. In the second category, if examinee’s
ability is high but the trace is less, the examinee
answering process may be quickly filter invalid infor-
mation and accurately extract key information. The
third category of candidates has low ability and few
traces, it could mean examinees are not motivated
enough to take the test, and there is no comprehen-
sive search for topic information.

6. Conclusions and discussions
Process data have shown great potential in terms of
increasing test validity of the test scores and improves
test design, however, how to explore, discover and
extract useful information from such a huge and
irregular data ocean and then modeling them have
always been a big challenge. This research focuses on
two useful and easily available types of process data,
including response times (RTs) and the mouse click/
drag trace, and combines them to the conventional
cognitive diagnostic model (CDM) to better under-
stand individuals’ response behavior and to improve
the
classification
accuracy
of
conventional
CDM.
Large number of research (Bolsinova et al., 2017; Man
et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2021; Vandekerckhove et al.,
2011; Wang et al., 2020) had indicated the process
data of RTs can significantly contribute to the ability
estimate,
which
is
also
verified
in
our
model.
However, as a new type of process data in educational
and psychological measurement, the mouse click/drag
trace (the number of actions) records the number of
clicks, double-clicks, key presses and drag/drop and
reflects the traces left by students in computer-based
assessment. To our best knowledge, the mouse click/
drag trace hasn’t entered the researchers’ radar, espe-
cially for the framework of cognitive diagnosis assess-
ment. In our study, we make full use of this new type
of process data and combine it to the CDM based on
the following considerations: First, this information is
easily recorded in the logs of all computer-based
assessment; Second, the information has a standard
format and exists in the form of integers and it is pos-
sible to analyze by standard statistical methods; Third,
the number of actions represents the traces left by the
examinee
during
the
computer-based
assessment,
which
can
reveal
test-taker
response
behaviors.
Fourth, the result of our empirical application demon-
strated that the number of actions had significant cor-
relation with response (see Table 3 for details), which
indicated that this type of process data can contribute
to the accuracy estimation of person parameters.
Our empirical data analysis of PISA 2018 reading
assessment showed that the proposed JCDM-RRT
model had better attribute classification reliability
than previous CDMs. Further, we also observed a
strong correlation among accuracy, speed and trace
variables. Specifically, a positive correlation existed
between the ability parameters and trace parameters, a
negative correlation existed between the ability param-
eters and speed parameters, which suggested examinee
with
higher
scores
leave
more
trace
but
spend
less time.
To evaluate the advantage of the proposed model
which combines information of process data, two
simulation studies were conducted. In the first simula-
tion study, the parameter estimates from the empirical
research analysis were used as the true values of
model parameter. In the second simulation study, we
considered the sample size, number of items and the
correlation between person parameters (qhs, qhu) to
investigate the parameters recovery of the proposed
model under ideal conditions. The results of both two
simulation studies indicated that the proposed model
considering process data had higher correct attribute
classification rates, and leaded to more accurate and
precise estimation of the ability parameters, speed
parameters and trace parameters than the previ-
ous models.
To evaluate the feasibility of the JCDM-RRT model
in practical applications, we evaluated the estimated
times for three models, it is worth pointing out that
the MCMC settings were the same for all models in
all studies. Specifically, the estimated times (Unit:
hours) of MHO-RDINA model, JRT-DINA model,
and JCDM-RRT model were [0.170, 3.40], [0.255,
5.58], and [0.509, 11.91], respectively (see Tables S9
and S10 for details). The JCDM-RRT model incorpo-
rates process data (number of actions and RTs), there
are more parameters to be estimated. Therefore, the

JCDM-RRT model takes longer to estimate. But over-

At present, to better understand response behavior
and response patterns, researchers mostly only utilized
RTs data (Logan et al., 2011; Qian et al., 2016).
However, with advances in computer-based assess-
ment, the number of actions can also be easily
obtained. The proposed JCDM-RRT model provides
further detailed description about response patterns
and response behavior by borrowing information
from both the number of actions and RTs. Thereby, it
provides a more complete picture of the cognitive
activities that individuals undertake during a test.
Moreover, the number of actions and RTs can be
used to provide extra information to improve the
accuracy of the higher-order ability estimation and
individual correct classification rates, by borrowing
information from the person trace parameters and
speed parameters. Unfortunately, the improvement of
using the proposed model was not so larger than
expected. In addition, the improvement of ability esti-
mation and individual correct classification rates of
the proposed model is closely related to the correl-
ation strength of extra information (the number of
actions and RTs) and scores.
Although the inspiring results in the current study,
there are several limitations that can be addressed
with future investigation. First, the proposed model
examined only dichotomous responses stemming pri-
marily from correct/incorrect items, and it is neces-
sary
to
extend
it
to
polytomous
response
or
continuous response (Minchen et al., 2017). Second,
we assumed that each attribute is classified as a binary
variable (mastery vs. non-mastery), and polytomous
attributes can be considered in the future (Chen & de
la Torre, 2013). Third, it will be an interesting issue
to investigate whether the number of actions and
response times can be used to improve item selection
in computer adaptive test (van der Linden, 2018).
Fourth, the number of actions may be related to the
examinee test-taking effort and future research can
measure examinee test-taking effort based on the
number of actions (Wise & Kong, 2005). Fifth, in our
simulation study based on ideal conditions, we held
constant certain the number of attributes (K ¼ 5) and
the structure of the Q-matrix of the data generating
model, this may limit the generalization of the results
of this study. Finally, the study has proved that mouse
click/drag helps improve the attribute profile classifi-
cation accuracy in CDMs. This gives us reason to
believe that the number of actions may help to aber-
rant response behavior detection, improve test design
and item calibration, just like RTs can bring these
benefits (e.g., Lee & Chen, 2011; Meyer, 2010). We
think these would be an interesting research topic in
the future.
