Abstract
The main drive for this study is to look into potential predictors of educational
achievement using OECD’s original Pisa 2018 dataset. This paper explores (i)
access to ICT, (ii) physical characteristics of learning environment at school, (iii)
quality of teaching staff and educational material, (iv) student characteristics and
learning climate, and (v) political and economic structure of the country as potential
true predictors of academic achievement. Our sample covers 41 countries and data
used in our analysis are collected from 282,461 students distributed in 9,317 schools
worldwide. Multiple linear regressions are developed and analyzed using stepwise
estimation techniques. The results indicate that (i) availability of internet connection
and enjoyable pastime activity at home have positive impacts on student’s success,
(ii) a strong correlation exists between high test scores and student characteristics &
learning climate, i.e. students’ sense of belonging at school, students’ fear of failure
and disciplinary climate, (iii) there is a negative relationship between academic performance and teacher enthusiasm & support, (iv) student success is negatively correlated with income level and political and economic freedoms but positively associated with economic competitiveness of a country, and (v) the students in lightly
populated classes are more successful than those in overloaded ones and inadequate
or poor educational material deteriorates educational outcomes.
Keywords ICT · PISA · Secondary education · Educational achievement ·
Applications in subject areas

1 Introduction
Current form of competition at global scale forces countries to construct and maintain absolute and/or comparative advantages over others so as to sustain economic
development; and the quality of the education system is one of the factors that determine how a country performs in this fierce competition. Today, every economy
needs high quality skilled workers/employees to produce goods and services more
and more efficiently, and naturally such workers/employees earn much higher salaries than others with lower level of education. So, education in general and secondary education (that prepares students for higher education) in particular are vital for
countries, employers and employees.
Theoretically, secondary education can be seen as a production process as it
is a purposeful activity aimed at producing a product that meets needs using certain inputs. Within this framework, various characteristics of educational systems
(demand and finance structure, components of education production function,
assessment, comparative efficiency, provision etc.) are crucial areas to study. So far,
school characteristics/resources (Ammermueller, 2007; Calcagno et al., 2008; M. M.
Chiu, 2010; Collier & Millimet, 2009; Cyrenne & Chan, 2012; Graddy et al., 2013;
Hanushek, 1997; Ludwig & Bassi, 1999; Mostafa, 2010; Parcel & Dufur, 2001),
family characteristics (Carneiro, 2008; M. M. Chiu, 2010; Fuchs & Wößmann,
2007; Houtenville & Conway, 2008; Marks, 2008; Mertoğlu & Aydın, 2012; Parcel
& Dufur, 2001; Pong, 1997), gender (Bahar, 2010; Freitas & Leonard, 2011; Fuchs
& Wößmann, 2007), neighborhood effects (Cyrenne & Chan, 2012; Lindahl, 2011;
Perl, 1973), socioeconomic status (Bahar, 2010; Rangvid, 2007), student characteristics (Freitas & Leonard, 2011; Fuchs & Wößmann, 2007), economic inequality (M.
M. Chiu, 2010; Ming Ming Chiu & Khoo, 2005), working (Darolia, 2014; Goulart
& Bedi, 2008), access to information and communication technologies (ICT) (Wittwer et al., 2014), academic self-concept (Erten & Burden, 2014), health status and
psychological stress (Freitas & Leonard, 2011), family-owned businesses (Gevrek &
Gevrek, 2010) and motivation (Castillo-Merino & Serradell-López, 2014) have been
among the variables studied as the prime predictors of academic achievement.
In this paper; academic/educational achievement, students’ success, academic
performance, educational outcome and similar other concepts mean the same
and are used interchangeably to avoid repetition of the same words in the text. The
paper aims to contribute to the existing literature by discovering the predictors of
academic success. We know very well that “correlation does not imply direct causation,” but regression analysis can indicate causation with vigilant application of
techniques and data. In fact, controlled experiments are considered the best tools
for detecting causal effects, but they are excessively costly and cannot be applied to
some research areas due to scale and scope related difficulties (for example, determinants of successful education systems). So, this paper explores (i) physical characteristics of learning environment at school, (ii) access to ICT, (iii) quality of teaching staff and educational material, (iv) student characteristics and learning climate,
and (v) political and economic structure of the country as potential true predictors of
academic achievement. The choice of variables is dictated by data availability.

The contribution of this paper to the current literature is threefold. At the outset, we add an up-to-date analysis to the literature. Additional analyses with most
recent data are important for replication, which is undeniably one of the keystones
of decent scientific exercise. Replication is even more vital in subject areas where
establishing causal relations through controlled experiments are extremely problematic and rare and, therefore, findings predominantly depend on correlational
analyses, as in the current topic. Correlational studies, by their nature, permit limited inferences about causality and hence require multiple iterations to ensure their
results are valid and consistent. So, this paper advances the knowledge on determinants of academic achievement. Second, this is one of the first papers to use the
OECD’s latest PISA dataset. Although PISA surveys provide the most extensive and
rich data on international education systems and limited number of papers provide
high-quality analysis using this data (for instance Barrera-Osorio et al. (2011), Feniger and Atia (2018), Giambona and Porcu (2018), Lewis (2020)), majority of the
studies just replicate the information in OECD reports on PISA results, rather than
really analyzing the raw data collected in PISA surveys. This paper fills this gap to
some extent by directly utilizing the data in PISA 2018. Finally, the studies using
stepwise multiple regressions to evaluate the impact of various factors on academic
achievement (e.g. Balogun et al. (1986), Cobb (1972), Goh and Moore (1978), Jafari
and Ahmadzade (2014), McCutcheon et al. (1992), Park and Bauer (2002), Tella
(2017)) are quite infrequent and this paper adds to this body of literature.
The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews recent applied work investigating factors related to students’ academic performance, specifies the research
question and hypotheses to be tested in the study. In the third section, methodology and data are presented. The fourth section gives study outcomes while the fifth
chapter explains the findings of the study, its academic and practical implications.
The last section summarizes the study and provides directions for future works.

2 Literature, research question and hypotheses
Any education system in any country is expected to improve the level of student
achievement. Therefore, many researchers from various disciplines have produced
a large literature on the relationship between educational inputs and outputs. The
non-applied studies in the form of anecdotal debate are not within the scope of this
paper. To contribute to the literature that has accumulated so far, this article attempts
to answer the following research question.
Which are the best predictors of academic achievement: (i) physical characteristics of learning environment at school, (ii) access to ICT, (iii) quality of
teaching staff and educational material, (iv) student characteristics and learning climate, and/or (v) political and economic structure of the country
The recent applied studies focus on the relationship between academic achievement and various indicators that are assumed to determine the level of academic success, such as academic self-concept (Hansen & Henderson, 2019), crime (Gimenez

H-1: Better physical school environment contributes to academic achievement.
H-2: Access to ICT improves student test scores.
H-3: High quality teaching staff and educational material advance student
achievement.
H-4: Positive student characteristics and learning climate enhance student attainment.
H-5: Politically and economically freer, more competitive, less corrupt and richer
countries perform better in PISA 2018.

We collected data on 30 explanatory variables but it is quite difficult to decide
whether or not to include all of them into our analysis as it may cause problems

need to overcome. In this study, t-tests and F-tests are used to construct the model.

4 Empirical analysis
In our analysis, mean test scores of 15-year-old students from 41 countries are
explained as a function of five sets of variables representing (i) physical characteristics of learning environment at school, (ii) access to ICT, (iii) quality of teaching
staff and educational material, (iv) student characteristics and learning climate, and
(v) political and economic structure of the country. The analysis starts with stepwise
regression by Stata using backward elimination, eliminating variables with p ≥ 0.2
and adding terms with p < 0.1. Based on stepwise regression results, three models
are estimated, one for each subject.
In multiple regression, the variance of the error term is assumed to be static. That
is to say, the distribution of errors is supposed to be the same all over the observation
sample, which is termed as homoscedasticity. However, in practice, heteroscedasticity exists, meaning that the variance of errors varies. Breusch-Pagan/Cook-Weisberg
test is the key statistical assessment for varying variance and checks whether the variance of residuals is a function of the predicted values ​​of the response variable and
whether the errors are normally distributed. We ran this test for our three models,
and high p-values ​​indicate that we failed to accept the null hypothesis of homoscedasticity in our model. The easiest and most common approach to solving this problem is to run regressions with robust standard errors that use a different technique
to analyze the standard error of each coefficient, resulting in a diverse confidence
interval and p-value for each coefficient. However, the projected coefficients are
identical as before. In short, robust standard errors are used in our analysis to control
for heteroscedasticity. Table 2 shows the outcomes with dependent and independent
variables, significant coefficients, robust standard errors (shown in parentheses after
coefficients), number of observations, F test, prob > F, ­R2, root MSE results and the
results of the test for heteroscedasticity. Let’s pay attention on each of these dimensions one by one.
“Prob > F” is the p-value of the model, indicating whether ­R2 differs from 0. In
our models, this value is very close to zero, indicating a very good fit. ­R2 displays
the total variance of outcome variable explained by predictor variables. In our analysis, predictor variables explain around 90% of the variance in PISA mean scores,
which is again an indicator of good fit. Root MSE is the root mean squared error,
that is, the standard deviation of the regression. When it comes closer to 0, we get a
better model. In all our models, root MSE is less than 0.05. Dividing the coefficient
by its standard error gives us t-value, which tests the hypothesis that the coefficient

5 Discussion of the results
Although some variables are dropped in the course of stepwise regression and some
others are not significant even at 10% level, the results from our analysis are consistent with Hypotheses 2 and 3; that is, access to ICT seems to improve student
test scores and high-quality teaching staff and educational material advance student
achievement. For Hypotheses 1, 4 and 5, we have mixed results in terms of direction
of the relations between explanatory variables and test scores. In the first subdivision below, the results from the stepwise estimation of three multiple linear regressions (see Table 2) are debated comprehensively. In the next sub-segment, the paper
concentrates on the “super” predictors of academic achievement that explain variation in test scores of all three subjects. In the final part, the policy repercussions of

5.2 Best predictors of academic achievement
Having discussed the direction of the effects of statistically significant variables on
student success, now we focus on their magnitude. The analysis here is on the socalled “super” predictors of academic achievement that explain the variation in all
subject areas. Figure 1 presents the data in Table 3 visually while Fig. 2 highlights
the impact of “super” predictors.
Even a quick look at Figs. 1 and 2 reveals that we have 11 “super” predictors in
our analysis, namely average class size, time spent on the internet at home on a typical weekend day, percentage of schools with inadequate or poorly educational material, index of students’ sense of belonging at school, index of disciplinary climate,
index of students’ fear of failure, index of teacher enthusiasm, index of teacher support, freedom index, the global competitiveness index, and GDP per capita.
In our sample, average class size ranges from 18.9 to 43.9 and our findings suggest that if number of students in a class is reduced by 5; reading, mathematics and
science scores increase by 3.9%, 3.4% and 3.9%, respectively, holding all other variables constant. The variable on time spent on the internet at home on a typical weekend day ranges between 125.5 and 256.9 minutes and about a 26-minute increase
in this variable means 2.7% increase in reading and mathematics scores and 5.4%
increase in science.

5.3 Policy repercussions
Undoubtedly, our findings have many policy repercussions but, due to space limitations, we focus on top-7 policy guidelines in this sub-section.
First of all, each year billions of dollars are spent on education systems worldwide and there is a definite need for comparative and uniform measurement of their
outcomes and effectiveness, which in turn requires organized micro level data. At
the present time, besides some partial data from transnational sources like PISA and
TIMSS, micro level international data is unavailable. Even the coverage of PISA
data is not uniform and varies significantly from one year to another, which makes
applied analysis across time very difficult, if not impossible. For instance, despite
an initial sample data from 79 countries, the working sample in this study was condensed by nearly one half as data on some variables are not available on every country in PISA 2018 dataset. International educational micro data providers should produce uniform data for all countries in the sample; that is, data on the same set of
variables should be collected each survey year and for each country. In addition, better data are needed in terms of quality (including more variables), quantity (including more students and countries), and frequency (including surveys at least every
two years).
Second, students in overcrowded classrooms were found to accomplish less than
those in underpopulated classrooms, and there was a negative correlation between
student success and the indices of teacher enthusiasm and teacher support. In line
with this finding, countries should take the necessary measures to increase the quality as well as the number of teaching staff. The third recommendation concerns the
availability of internet connection and leisure activities (such as video games). It has
been seen that the internet connection and the student’s having fun activities have
encouraging bearings on student achievement. Therefore, strategies should aim to
upsurge internet accessibility and leisure activities. Fourth, it has been found that
insufficient or inadequate educational material distorts educational outcomes, so
policy makers need to develop policies to improve the quality and quantity of educational materials.
The next policy advice relates to solid connection between high test scores and
student characteristics and learning climate, like students’ sense of belonging at
school, disciplinary climate and students’ fear of failure. Since these variables and
academic success are strongly correlated, necessary steps should be taken to ensure
that these variables are realized at optimal levels. Therefore, schools and classrooms
should be designed to increase students’ sense of belonging while creating and
maintaining an optimal climate of discipline and fear of failure.
The sixth policy guidance is about the most unforeseen fallouts from this paper.
Astoundingly, the conclusions suggest two separate negative associations: one
between student success and freedom index and another between academic achievement and GDP per capita. In line with this finding, policymakers should not take

it for granted that freer and more prosperous environments inevitably contribute to
educational attainment. On the other hand, economic competitiveness of a country
seems to be an important predictor of better educational outcomes. So, the final policy guideline is that education systems perform better in a competitive economy.

6 Conclusion
This paper constitutes one of the very few papers that utilize OECD’s most recent
PISA dataset to explore the determinants of academic achievement. The paper questioned whether physical characteristics of learning environment at school, access to
ICT, quality of teaching staff and educational material, student characteristics and
learning climate, and political and economic structure of the country are correlated
with educational outcomes; and, tried to measure the size of this correlation, if
present.
A single dataset cannot entirely include all variables affecting the education system, and the PISA 2018 dataset is no exception. In addition to variables in our dataset, a pupil’s examination mark may be a function of some other missing variables,
potentially causing bias that may technically be positive or negative. Although it is
the greatest potential limitation, nothing indicates that the limited nature of dataset
used in this paper weakens the analyzes or fallouts.
There are still many unanswered research questions and an enormous research
gap in this important area, highlighting the need for more hands-on work. Five
key recommendations for future work can be listed as follows. First, more work
is needed with expanded data (in terms of quality, quantity, and frequency) and
resources to better capture and discover new determinants of academic success.
Although, this paper sets forth the existence and direction of specific linkages
between student achievement and some variables, it does not explain why they exist.
Therefore, the second main track on which future research may go forward may be
the effort to explain why teacher enthusiasm and teacher support deter student success. Third extension may be another academic undertaking to determine the optimal type and duration of leisure-time activities that maximize student achievement.
The fourth contribution of future work may relate to discovery of ideal levels of
discipline and fear of failure in classrooms. Finally, future researchers should question why students in more competitive countries perform better while those in freer
and wealthier ones do less, and try to develop tools to address underperformance in
developed countries.
